=== BEAT 5117: Quiet Work ===
The building manager's schedule had slipped forty minutes, which was a gift and not something Rook had planned for. She had planned for a twelve-minute window in the secondary stairwell, a six-minute gap at the Level 9 panel, and a controlled exit through the Level 2 loading dock. The margin she built into jobs like this was five minutes per stage. The forty-minute gift belonged to the building manager and to whatever argument had developed at the service entrance over a disputed materials delivery, and she spent it standing very still in a maintenance alcove on Level 11 while the building's systems ran without her.

The alcove smelled of thermal paste and the specific staleness of recycled air from a filtration unit that had not been serviced recently. Z3 construction had a consistent character in the service corridors: marble and electrostatic glaze in the lobby, copper railing fittings in the client-facing stairwells, and then past the access doors — insulation wrap rated three grades below the specification visible on the permit placard, cable conduit runs that had been extended and re-extended with mismatched hardware until the original installation was invisible under the layers of repair. The gap between appearance and infrastructure was not a secret. It was just not the face the building showed.

The railing on the landing below her was structural steel, painted over a primer coat that had never properly cured. She could see it from three meters up: the iron surfacing through the finish coat at the weld joints in rust-orange patterns, the material declining to stay covered. Old construction had that quality — the tendency to tell you what it was made of if you looked at the right places, the skeleton insisting on itself under the skin.

She counted ceiling tiles while she waited. Thirty-two, standard drop-grid, every third one fractionally off-plumb from thermal cycling over the decades. The imprecision was not structural. It was cosmetic. She noted the distinction.

The contract electrician arrived on the landing below at eleven past. Approximately fifty years old, heavyset, grey stubble. He had the safety gloves tucked into his back pocket rather than his tool belt, which meant he was either between tasks or between floors. He stood at the landing rail and looked down the stairwell — not searching, just the automatic sight-line check of someone who had learned to know what was behind him in service corridors. Then he settled in.

His posture was the specific stillness of a man taking a break he was not supposed to be taking: relaxed enough to be convincing to a passerby, alert enough to be gone in four seconds. She did not find this threatening. She found it professional. The kind of attentiveness that developed not from training but from doing the same work in the same buildings long enough to understand which risks were real and which ones weren't.

He left the landing at eighteen past. She gave him two additional minutes and then moved.

The Level 9 panel was Civic-grade hardware installed in a private Z3 commercial facility, which was a technical contradiction. Civic-grade panels belonged in the public infrastructure hierarchy — water regulation nodes, neuretics relay junctions, civic grid substations. They required a higher-clearance threading key than commercial equivalents and were certified to a different access standard entirely. Installing them here meant either a legitimate civic contract that the building's own records didn't reflect, or acquisition through channels the installer preferred not to document. Either explanation produced the same result: whoever had ordered this installation had wanted to believe the vault was more secure than standard commercial hardware.

They were not wrong that it was more secure. They were wrong about by how much.

Rook had pulled the threading key two days prior from a gray-market broker Vox used for infrastructure-adjacent work. The transfer had taken forty-two seconds. She had not asked what the broker's source was and the broker had not offered.

The client's files were exactly where they were supposed to be: a five-year procurement chain documenting a materials acquisition routed through three shell subsidiaries and terminating in a capital flow the client did not want appearing under their name in the GLMZ civic audit trail. She found them in the correct directory, threaded a full copy onto a half-centimeter data wafer, reset the access log entry to the state it had been in before she'd touched the panel, and was back in the stairwell by nineteen forty-four.

Four minutes inside her buffer. Clean.

She descended on the west stairwell. The structural steel railing was cold under her palm — not reinforced composite, just material that had been doing the same job for six decades and had developed a settled, weight-bearing honesty about it. Solid in the way that did not advertise itself. Her footsteps were quiet in the half-compression sole pattern she wore for interior work, a layering that absorbed impact without the rigidity of tactical hardware. She moved like someone going somewhere unremarkable.

The Level 2 exit opened through a loading dock to a service lane. She confirmed the latch engaged behind her. The lane ran east for twenty meters before joining the public street.

Outside, the Z3 evening was mid-transition between business and dinner traffic — the pedestrian density still high but directional, people with destinations, carrying the particular air of those whose address had already resolved the question of where they belonged. The lake was visible at the end of the block through the gap between two tower footprints: a flat grey strip in the last of the day's light, the water going dark at the edges while the center held the sky's final reflections.

She stopped for three seconds and looked at it.

The payment arrived while she was standing there. Φ 4,200 confirmed, nineteen fifty-two. Two minutes after her delivery acknowledgment. She had expected five.

She did not count it. She had not counted a payment like that in three months. She noted this again, as she had noted it each time, without particular significance.

Then she went to the diner.

---

It was the fourth time — fourth diner, fourth window over the same water — and she had not planned any of them. The pattern had developed the way patterns developed when you were not managing them: something happened once, then twice, and by the third time the path was worn enough that you took it without the friction of deciding. She was not sure when the path had started. She was not sure the when mattered.

The booth was wooden, the outer edge worn to a specific smoothness where hands caught it on the way in — the material memory of accumulated arrivals, the texture that took years to earn. She chose the seat with sightlines to both exits and clocked the choice as a choice, which was a distinction she had started making recently. The body doing its job. The mind watching. A gap between reflex and decision that she was not entirely sure what to do with, but that seemed worth keeping.

She ordered something hot without reading the menu carefully enough to tell you what. The server was young, trained in the precise attentiveness that the Z3 diner economy produced — visible enough to be useful, invisible enough not to interrupt. He brought her order and filled her water and left her to the window.

The window was single-pane. No electrostatic coating. The old kind that fogged at the corners in cold weather, softening the lake's edges into something that looked painted rather than real. She noted this as a cost decision — electrostatic glaze was within reach for an establishment operating at this rent tier, but the margin ran on deferred upgrades and steady trade, and some costs got deferred indefinitely. The fogging was not a problem. The lake was the lake regardless of what layer it was seen through.

The water at this hour was the color of old iron. Cold and dark grey, rolling in slow long-period swells that came uninterrupted from sixty kilometers west, the November weather system using the open expanse as a runway. The surface showed it — not violent, just relentless, the kind of movement that didn't escalate and didn't stop, that simply continued as long as the pressure behind it continued. Three cargo barges worked the middle channel under running lights, heavy and deliberate in the water with their loads. She tracked their positions without deciding to. Distance, bearing, relative speed. The arithmetic that ran itself once you had logged enough sightlines.

She was fine.

Three months in and this was still the thing she kept noticing, because it was not what she had expected to notice. Not an absence where something difficult had been. Not relief, or at least not only that. A presence: a settled quality in her chest like a weight that had found its load-bearing position and was doing the structural work from there without announcement. She had been fine before in the operational sense. Functional, precise, capable. That fineness had always pointed forward at the next thing. This was different. This was fine like the building's iron skeleton was fine — not decorative, not performing, not dependent on the surface holding.

She did not know exactly when it had shifted. She had spent some weeks trying to locate the moment and had stopped, because the moment turned out not to be the point. The point was the condition. The condition was present.

What she could locate was the change in the arithmetic.

She had kept a count since she was nineteen. It had started as money — rates and hours and amounts owed and amounts received, the economic grammar of surviving in the zones where survival was a calculation before it was anything else. Over time, without a single explicit decision, the count had taken on other items: jobs, locations, faces, specific rooms where outcomes had gone one way instead of another. After Tidewell the count had started including names. Not as debt. Not as liability. As names — present in the ledger the way a barge was present on the water, moving at its own speed, carrying what it carried, going somewhere.

After Crimson the count had stopped running backward.

It still ran. But it ran forward now, into time she had not yet entered. That was a different kind of arithmetic than the one she had maintained for fifteen years. She was still learning what the forward notation required of her. She was, provisionally, not in a hurry.

At the booth behind her, two people were discussing a renovation contract in Z5 — load specs, a permit window, a subcontractor who had missed two consecutive deadlines. The conversation had the texture of a problem being solved rather than complained about, two people who had defined the failure and were working toward the fix. One of them laughed briefly at something — a short, genuine sound, not performing amusement — and both returned to the problem without ceremony.

The server refilled her cup at twenty hundred without asking. She acknowledged this with a nod. He left.

She thought about the electrician.

He had been doing his job carefully in a building full of people doing various kinds of work. Most of that work was visible — documented, present in the civic record, checkable by anyone with the access to look. One person's work tonight was not. She had reset the log. She had taken care to ensure the building did not know she had been there. She was not sure, anymore, that this made her structurally different from the electrician. She was sure it made her different in position — the part of the building that did not appear in the inspection report because it had been correctly hidden, not because it was absent.

Both of them had done their jobs. The building would keep standing.

Before Tidewell this thought would not have arrived. The electrician had been a timing variable and that was all he had been. She was not sure when timing variables had started being people whose evenings continued after she cleared the building — boots by a door somewhere, dinner, the specific weight of a problem being worked on in a diner booth. She was not sure she needed to locate the transition. It had occurred. The count was the evidence.

She left the diner at twenty-one twenty.

The November wind had come up off the lake while she was inside, the iron smell of cold fresh water over old rock — heavier than ocean, more mineral, the scent of geological time moving through a city still working out its relationship to the water it had been built beside. She turned her collar up and walked toward the transit node with the wind at her back.

The job had been data, not people. The payment had arrived prompt. She had sat by a window over the lake for the fourth time in a series she had not planned and did not, particularly, intend to stop.

The count ran forward. The lake was the same.

She did not know yet what tomorrow's job would require.

=== BEAT 5131: The Offer ===
The contact came through Ferris at seven the next morning, which was early for Ferris. Rook noted this before she opened the channel.

Ferris operated out of Z3, primarily — a mid-tier fixer with solid relationships in the collector and procurement markets, a record clean by the standards that mattered in this business, meaning nobody Rook knew had a specific grievance against them and nobody she trusted had any evidence of side-dealing. She had taken four jobs through Ferris in the past two years. The shortest had run three days; the longest, nine. All four had paid what was agreed, closed without significant incident, and required no follow-up conversation. By the standards she applied to fixers, this made Ferris excellent.

The early contact was still unusual.

"Art recovery," Ferris said. They delivered briefs the same way every time — unhurried, level, information presented in whatever order was most logically useful rather than in dramatic sequence, as if the whole of the job existed already and was being translated rather than introduced. "Sculpture. Piece called Headcount, located at a Z3 collector's market — the freight depot conversion on Meridian north. Stolen from the original buyer seven weeks ago by a consignment courier who subsequently went to ground. The piece surfaced twice on secondary market feeds before the current location. Anonymous client. Twelve thousand."

Rook was in her Z5 flat. She had been awake for forty minutes. Coffee in hand, east-facing window putting morning light at an angle that required her to shift her position to read anything without squinting. She had not shifted. Some friction she maintained because removing it required effort she preferred to direct elsewhere.

"Sculpture," she said.

"Physical and digital. The piece has an embedded data layer — standard artist-registration architecture, provenance chain, certificate format. Physical housing is ceramic composite, roughly half a meter on the long axis. Not fragile, but not casual handling either." Ferris paused in the precise way they paused when they had said the first frame and were allowing the listener to orient. "The data layer is described as the value."

"To who?"

"Anonymous," Ferris said again. Not evasion — definition. Fixers at Ferris's level maintained the boundary between what they knew and what they were authorized to relay, and they maintained it consistently rather than situationally, which was the difference between a fixer you could rely on and a liability in decent clothing.

"Twelve thousand for a courier recovery job," she said.

"Twelve thousand," Ferris confirmed.

The number was real money for a job described as straightforward. Courier recovery — locate the piece, acquire it from its current position, deliver to a specified drop point — ran at standard rates somewhere between four and six thousand depending on complexity, security environment, and timeline pressure. Twelve thousand was approximately two and a half times the upper range for a job Ferris was describing as clean. The overpay was information. It meant one of two things: the client had resources to spare and was buying certainty and speed, or the client had specific reasons to want this done by someone whose approach produced results without collateral attention. The two explanations were not mutually exclusive. They rarely were.

"Two prior locations," she said.

"Z6 secondary market and a Z4 holding facility. Both confirmed accurate at time of report. The piece moved each time before the client's previous recovery attempts completed." Ferris was quiet for a moment. "You'd be the third attempt."

She turned this over once. Two failed recovery attempts before her meant either the piece was being actively moved — a holder who was trying to sell and understood they were being traced — or the prior attempts had been slow, under-resourced, or had signaled their approach in a way that prompted the holder to relocate. The current location at a Z3 collector's market argued for the second interpretation. A Z3 freight depot conversion was not where you kept something you were actively flipping; it was where you put something on display for buyers who would come to you, on your terms, at your price. The collector had the sculpture and was waiting for a better offer than anyone had yet made.

"Timeline," she said.

"No stated deadline. The client wants recovery before the piece changes hands."

"Delivery format?"

"Physical piece, intact, to a Z3 drop point. Coordinates on contract execution. No extraction of the data layer required — the physical piece transfers everything."

She noted this: not extract the data, return the housing. The data and the housing were the same object. You could not have one without the other. She filed this without responding to it.

"No personnel engagement parameters?" she said.

"Not in the brief. Clean recovery authorization. No destruction, no escalation."

She set her coffee down. The morning light had shifted fractionally, the angle changing as the sun moved, and she could read the corner of the room clearly now without relocating. Small adjustments in position. Some friction resolved itself if you waited long enough.

Simple work for good money. The sentence was technically accurate. It was also the sentence that preceded the jobs you examined afterward.

The thing that snagged was not the money and not the anonymity and not the two prior failed attempts, though all three were worth noting. It was the data layer. Ferris had described it as standard — artist-registration format, provenance chain, certificate architecture. That vocabulary was correct for art pieces with embedded documentation. The GLMZ collector market had run on that certification format for thirty years, and most pieces above a certain value carried the layer as a matter of course. There was no particular reason to read the description as anything other than accurate.

Except that the overpay was twelve thousand for what the brief called a simple courier recovery. And the two prior attempts had failed, which meant someone capable of authorizing recovery operations had tried twice. And the anonymous client had apparently preferred making a third attempt over writing the loss off against whatever they had paid for the piece originally.

You did not pay twelve thousand Quanta and make three attempts for a provenance chain.

The data layer was something else. She did not know what. She would find out when she was looking at it, and not before, and trying to reason about it from the outside of the job was not analysis — it was arithmetic without the correct variables.

"Send the contract," she said.

The file arrived in her neuretics queue within ninety seconds. She parsed it in two reads: standard recovery terms, Z3 delivery coordinates, payment in two tranches — six thousand on execution, six thousand on delivery of the physical piece in intact condition. A damage clause, standard for collector market work. A confidentiality provision covering the client's identity, which was expected for anonymous operations at this tier.

She executed. The first tranche cleared at seven forty-one.

She sat with her coffee for another five minutes and let the job settle into its operational shape. She had the brief. She had the money and the contract. She had a location: Z3 freight depot conversion, north end, a building that had been something structural and functional before the conversion market had moved through and repurposed it into a venue whose bones were more interesting than whatever occupied them now.

The collector who had the piece was not moving. That was the first useful fact. The second was that two prior attempts had confirmed the location but not completed the acquisition, which meant the collector had security and was not naive about their exposure. A professional display environment — cameras, staff, infrastructure. You did not approach this as an extraction. You approached it as a purchase.

She needed a buyer's cover. That meant she needed Lace. And it meant the job required at least one additional body on the perimeter, which meant she was making calls today.

She sent Ferris a brief acknowledgment — *Confirmed. Beginning site assessment* — and put the channel away.

---

Three hours later she was in a Z4 public records archive, pulling the permit history for the freight depot conversion. The building had changed hands twice in five years. Currently operating as a mixed commercial space: dealer showrooms on the lower two levels, private collection storage above. Single public entrance off the main street. Freight access lane on the east face for large deliveries. A civic maintenance conduit under the north foundation that had not been serviced, per the permit record, in eight years.

The maintenance conduit was information. Unused infrastructure had uses.

She paid for a coffee she did not drink and walked out into the Z4 midday pedestrian flow. The transit tower was visible to the northwest, the lake visible to the south in the gap between two older low-rise blocks — built in a period when Z4 was the edge of what Z3 would become, before the pressure had continued moving outward and left these streets in a specific kind of suspended animation between what they had been and what they had not yet become. The stonework on the lower building facades retained the iron-heavy finish of that construction era: dark aggregate, dense, the kind of surface that absorbed light rather than reflecting it.

The lake under the white midday sky was a different grey than the night before — lighter, more diffuse, the chop running from the south and complicating the surface without organizing it. She watched a cargo barge work the distant channel. Much smaller from here than it had been from the diner window. The distance flattened things.

Twelve thousand for an art recovery job with an anonymous client and two prior failures and a data layer that someone thought was worth the emphasis.

She was not going to understand what she was actually looking at until she was looking at it. This was fine. This was how most jobs worked — the brief gave you the surface, and the job gave you what was beneath the surface, and professional competence meant doing the work with enough attention that the gap did not surprise you into poor decisions. She had not been surprised into a poor decision in some time. She intended to continue this record.

She pulled up Lace's contact. Thought for a moment about how to frame the ask. Art recovery, Z3 collector's market, buyer's cover. The fee split she would propose was standard for a two-person operation of this complexity.

She sent the message and started walking toward the transit node.

The Headcount sculpture. The data layer described as the value. The anonymous client who had made three attempts rather than writing the loss off.

She would find out what it was. That was, in the end, the only way to find out what it was.

=== BEAT 5145: The Crew Problem ===
She did the building assessment on foot first, because foot was the correct methodology for this kind of target and because she was in Z3 already and had no particular reason to leave before she had what she needed.

The freight depot conversion was at the north end of the old logistics district, a block off the main arterial. It occupied most of the building footprint on a corner plot — a four-story structure with the heavy-beam industrial construction of the mid-2140s, the exterior stonework still carrying the dark aggregate finish that had been the aesthetic of that decade, iron-heavy and light-absorbing, a building that looked like it knew exactly what it was before anyone had tried to do anything else with it. The conversion work had added a glass-and-composite entrance pavilion on the street face and left everything else more or less intact. The bones showed. That was apparently the point.

She walked the block twice at a normal pace. On the first pass she counted four exterior cameras — two on the entrance pavilion, one at the corner, one at the freight access lane. On the second pass she confirmed a fifth on the east face that she had missed because it was mounted at an angle inconsistent with standard coverage geometry, which meant it had been placed by someone who understood that standard geometry had standard blind spots.

The fifth camera changed the perimeter calculation.

She found a coffee point across the street and ordered something small and stood near the window and watched the entrance for twenty minutes. Three buyers in and out during that time — two in the Z3 collector demographic, one in the Z4 adjacent bracket, all arriving alone and leaving alone and carrying nothing visible. The security at the entrance was a single staff member in the kind of professional-civilian clothing that announced security without performing it — no uniform, no obvious hardware, but the specific attentiveness of someone who was being paid to be attentive. Inside the pavilion, through the glass, she could see a second staff member at a reception position.

Two staff visible. Probably more not visible.

She tallied what the job required and the tally came out to four people.

She had already messaged Lace. The job needed a buyer — someone who could walk into the collector's market as a legitimate client with provenance documentation and interest in the Headcount piece specifically, someone who could move through that environment without creating the kind of attention that an extraction team created. Options were the professional substitute for prediction.

Four people. For a job the brief described as simple.

She finished her coffee and acknowledged that the job had not been simple from the moment Ferris used the word. Simple was descriptive. Simple was also aspirational. In her experience, jobs described as simple were usually simple in the brief and various other things by the time they concluded. The gap between the brief and the conclusion was where the work lived.

=== BEAT 5118: Vox at Four ===
Vox had her workspace in a Z4 building she had occupied for six years, which was long enough to make it hers in the ways that mattered — the air quality, the cable routing, the two reinforced locks on the interior door that she had installed herself during the first month and had never explained. She worked in a converted storage room on the fourth floor, no windows, the walls lined with hardware in configurations that changed frequently enough that the overall shape of the space was always familiar and the specific details never predictable. This was not accident.

Rook had been here enough times to have a sense of the room's rhythm: the way Vox left certain cable runs permanently attached and certain ones coiled and ready, the classification logic she applied to her equipment that was not organizational in any conventional sense but was, Rook had concluded, organizational in a personal one. The room was legible to Vox and nobody else, which was the point.

She arrived at nine the next morning. Vox let her in without ceremony and returned to the chair she had been in, which faced a workstation array showing four active feeds simultaneously — data streams of some kind, Rook could not read the content from the door, only the fact of four distinct streams running in parallel, each one updating in a slightly different rhythm.

"The collector," Vox said, before Rook sat down, "is Yusuf Tran-Okonkwo. Z3 collector for eleven years. Legitimate market activity — he registers purchases, he files provenance chains, he pays the civic transaction fees that serious collectors pay to maintain market standing. His collection runs to thirty-something pieces, mostly GLMZ-origin work from the last twenty years. Mid-market, good taste, no major flag activity." She moved one of the feeds to a secondary position and opened a different view. The safe house was a Z4 commercial unit on the second floor of a converted warehouse building, leased under a holding company that Vox had been maintaining for two years. Three rooms: an operational workspace, a secondary with basic facilities, and a storage room with a lock that required two separate key sequences. The operational workspace had a workstation array, a cleared table, good light, and the specific quality of air in rooms that were used regularly and maintained carefully — not comfortable, not institutional, the precise absence of both.

They arrived in sequence with a seven-minute stagger between vehicles. Vox was already there when Rook came in last — she had arrived first, which was Vox's practice when a data operation was the immediate next task. The workstation array was running. Four channels, the streams active.

The transport case was on the cleared table. Lace had carried it through without ceremony and set it down. Three-point latch, closed.

"Reading interface first," Vox said, by way of a general briefing that was also just the order of operations. She moved to the table and examined the case exterior, then opened it.

The Headcount sculpture was exactly what Lace had described and what the brief had said it was: ceramic composite, half a meter on the long axis, high-gloss dark glaze, the surface worked with the almost-typographic markings running in repeating bands along the length. Under the safe house's clean work lighting it had a different presence than it would have had under the collector's market exhibition lights — less dramatic, more factual. The way objects looked when nobody was trying to sell them.

Vox examined the south face. The reading node housing at the base: a port cluster in the standard provenance documentation format, recessed into the ceramic by a half-centimeter, protected by a dust cap that Vox removed with a thumbnail and set on the table. The port was clean. Good maintenance. Someone had cared for this object.

"The data architecture," Vox said, setting up her threading cable, "is going to tell us whether this is a flat file or an active schema. The provenance chain referenced a civic registry format, but that's the outer wrapper. What it wraps determines the retrieval protocol."

"Translation," Lace said. She was leaning against the secondary workstation with her arms crossed, watching.

"Either this is a document or it's a database." Vox connected the threading interface to the port. "A document you read once. A database you query."

The connection handshake took four seconds. The workstation array shifted: two of the four channels condensed and the freed bandwidth redirected to the active query interface. The data layer began to resolve.

Rook watched Vox work.

This was Vox's particular quality: she processed information at the speed the information came in, without the visible hesitation that characterized slower analysts or the performed urgency that characterized ones who understood they were being observed. She was reading the data layer as it opened, her attention moving across the interface display with the same systematic pattern Rook had seen in her workspace — not linear, not random, the logic of it invisible from the outside and clearly present in its results.

The data layer resolved.

Vox was quiet for eleven seconds. This was not a normal length of quiet for Vox during an active data operation.

"Fifteen years," she said.

"Migration patterns. Residency histories. Debt ledger cross-references. Heritage documentation filings." Vox's voice was level. She had arrived at level through the work, the same way she always did. "The GLMZ Gray Zone. All twelve zones, plus the buffer zones. Population movement, integration rates, financial dependency structures, ethnographic heritage cross-sections mapped to residency registration." She paused. "The heritage data is the significant category. It's coded to the civic grid's legacy classification schema — the framework used before the Meridian Charter harmonized the identity system in 2208. That schema hasn't been used for official civic purposes in eighteen years."

The room was quiet.

"Who would use it now," Ekow said. It was not a question.

Vox turned to look at him. "Organizations that maintained parallel heritage tracking systems when the civic schema was retired. The civic grid stopped, but the data schema is not proprietary — any institution with sufficient technical capacity could build their own registry against it." She looked back at the workstation. "I can count the organizations in the Gray Zone with that capacity on both hands. The Lotus Syndicate is one of them."

The silence extended.

"The anonymous client," Boiler said.

"Yes," Rook said.

Scout was sitting on the secondary workstation's edge, legs crossed, watching the ceiling at a particular angle that indicated she was processing rather than observing. "The Lotus commissioned a recovery job," she said, "to recover a data sculpture containing fifteen years of Gray Zone population data encoded to the heritage classification schema they use for their own registry work." A pause. "They're recovering their own data."

"Or someone stole it from them," Lace said. "And they want it back."

"Tran-Okonkwo bought it from a secondary courier," Ekow said. "He didn't know what it was — he knew it was worth more than market, but he didn't know the specific value. Data woven through the civic grid's systems so quietly, for so long, that it had been dressed as art. Somebody had been patient. Fifteen years of patience had produced this.

Rook thought about the anonymous client. Twelve thousand Quanta. Three attempts. Urgent enough to use an outside operator rather than an inside resource. She thought about what an organization with fifteen years of this data and an urgent need to recover it was planning to do with it.

She thought about Ekow's particular silence when she had named the schema format at the transit station. The weight behind the pause that had resolved into briefing language without naming what the weight was.

She looked at the transport case. The sculpture in its foam cradle. Φ12,000 worth of anonymous client's property, already copied, data layer opened. The contract with Ferris specified physical piece, intact, to a Z3 drop point. The contract did not say anything about the data layer being examined before delivery. The contract did not specify what happened if examining the data layer changed the calculus of the delivery.

"We're not delivering it tonight," she said.

Nobody argued.

"Ferris will need an explanation," Lace said.

"Ferris will get an explanation when I have one worth giving." Rook looked at the display. The heritage cross-section maps. The fifteen years of the Gray Zone's people, counted and categorized and held in ceramic composite dressed as art. "What else does the data connect to? The room held the shape of what that meant for a moment.

She looked at the copied data on isolated storage, the original sculpture in its case, the twelve thousand Quanta sitting in the contract account, and the anonymous client's drop coordinates waiting in a file she was going to leave unopened tonight.

The twelve thousand had been the point for approximately four hours. Then Vox had opened the data layer, and the twelve thousand had stopped being the point, and what had become the point instead was the question of who was building a target list over fifteen years and what they were planning to do with it now that it was complete.

That question did not have a drop point. That question required a different kind of approach entirely.

She took the sculpture from its cradle, turned it in her hands once — the worked surface, the almost-language markings, the ceramic weight of it — and set it carefully back. It was evidence now. It was also, she was beginning to understand, only one of several components in a plan that was not finished.

"We sit on the sculpture," she said. "Vox stays on the data. Tomorrow we find out who built this — and what they need to complete it."

She looked at Ekow. He was looking at the display. The heritage cross-sections. The fifteen years.

He said nothing. His right hand was very still at his side.

=== BEAT 5120: Adaeze Nnodu-Park ===
