=== BEAT 5122: The Protocol Named ===
The Z3 safe house was a basement space Rook had used twice before — not a fixed asset, a rotating address maintained by a contact she paid a retainer to on a quarterly schedule. The contact was reliable in the specific way that retainer relationships produced reliability: the address was there when she needed it, in the condition she needed it in, and the contact asked no questions because the retainer price included the value of not asking. The space was not comfortable. It had two chairs that matched and four that didn't, a table that had been level in 2221 and had been compensating for the building's foundation shift ever since, and a single overhead light on the backup circuit because the primary had a fault that the contact had never gotten around to repairing.

Rook had stopped noticing the fault two visits ago. The light it produced was adequate. The adequacy was enough.

Nari slept four hours. Rook did not. She used the four hours for the operational close-out that needed to happen before the next operational phase could begin — the extraction route review, the Thorn contact team's search expansion tracked via Vox's monitoring, the Z3 commercial dock's registration records checked through the harbor grid to confirm the boat's return was logged correctly. It was. The boat had returned to its registered mooring at 0150, the harbor maintenance record clean, nothing to flag.

She did the close-out. She closed the loops. She sat in the backup light and thought about Old Harbor until the thinking resolved into the operational question it needed to resolve into, and then she wrote down the operational question on a piece of paper and put the paper in her coat pocket next to her field kit and waited for Nari to wake up.

Nari came into the main room at 0547. She had slept in her clothes, which were the clothes she had been wearing when the crew arrived at her apartment, which were the clothes she had planned to leave in. Her hair was down and her posture was what it had been since the beginning: upright without being rigid, the specific composure of a person who had been holding themselves in a state of readiness for a long time and had not relaxed the posture even after the moment they had been ready for had finally arrived.

She looked at Rook.

"Now," she said.

"Yes," Rook said.

The crew was at the table. They had organized themselves without direction — Scout against the east wall with her threading equipment in sleep mode, Boiler in the corner with the uneven leg, Vox with the monitoring thread running at low priority and the rest of her attention on the room, Lace to Rook's left, Ekow to Rook's right but closer to Nari's end of the table. He had placed himself there without discussion.

Nari sat at the far end and put her hands in her lap.

"I'll begin with the structure," she said. "Because the structure is what makes the rest of things I need to say make sense."

She began with the Stems. The Lotus Syndicate's governing council — not the name the Syndicate used publicly, which was Lotus Community Leadership Council, but the operational name used internally, which was what she was going to use because she was done using their public names. Blood-purity gated membership: heritage verification, two-generation bloodline record with no exception procedure. She had been an adjutant to a Stem named Jang Yong-su for seven years. The adjutant role was administrative: she attended the meetings, she took the notes, she managed the record. She was present in the room for every vote that was recorded.

"I was present for the Protocol vote," she said. "In 2211. I was twenty-eight years old."

The crew was still. Not politely still — the specific stillness of people who had decided before the conversation started that their job was to receive the information, that the information's weight required reception rather than commentary, that the discipline of not interrupting was itself a form of respect for how long she had been carrying this.

She continued.

"They called it the Purification Protocol from the founding document," she said. "The name was not changed between internal and external records. The word 'purification' appears in the founding document's header. The document was drafted in 2186. I have not seen the original draft — I was not born in 2186. I saw the active version in 2211, which was the fourth revision. The fourth revision contained language describing the revisions' history. I read it. I memorized what I could."

"Why," Rook said.

"Because I understood, when I left, that my memory of it was the only copy I would be able to carry."

The room received this. Eight seconds of nothing, which was the silence of six people registering what it meant that a woman had spent fifteen years memorizing a document's key provisions because she had understood that the document was evidence and that the evidence existed only where she could keep it.

Nari did not acknowledge the silence. She continued.

The Protocol's development had four phases, all of them preparatory. Phase 0 was operational now — she learned this from Adaeze's information two months ago, not from direct knowledge; she had been out of the Syndicate's information flow since 2211. Phase 0 was the test deployment: a six-month period in which the Z6 civic grid cluster had been accessed through a cooperative arrangement with a Civic Systems Engineer, the neuretics registry queried for 200,000 Z6 registered individuals, the data extracted and stored in a Lotus-controlled archive. The Phase 0 deployment had been described to the GLMZ Civic Bureau as a routine system test. The Bureau had accepted this description because the Bureau processed twelve thousand routine system test notifications annually and had the staffing to review approximately four hundred of them.

"The partial rollback succeeded for the secondary nodes," she said. "Three nodes did not roll back. The 200,000 records are in Lotus possession now." She said this with the same level voice she had used for everything else, the voice of a person who had organized the facts she was carrying into a presentable sequence and was presenting them in that sequence, the emotion that accompanied the facts carried alongside them but not mixed into the delivery. "Phase 1 requires three components. I can describe them because I was in the room when they were discussed."

She paused. One breath.

"Demographic baseline — a comprehensive heritage cross-section of the Gray Zone's resident population, covering at minimum fifteen years of migration data. The founding document describes this as a population registry: a data set that allows Phase 1 operations to begin not by census, which would require authorization and leave a public record, but by cross-reference. If you have fifteen years of migration patterns, debt distributions, and heritage cross-sections in a civic-grid schema, you can identify every household in the Z4 through Z7 corridor that meets the displacement criteria without conducting a single official inquiry." She did not pause between items. "Neuretics registry access — the means of querying the GLMZ civic registry for individual heritage classification independent of the individual's consent or knowledge. The demographic baseline gives you neighborhoods and household distributions. The registry access gives you individual identities. Phase 1 operations can then target specific people, not areas." Another breath. "A deployment window — the founding document identified the GLMZ neuretics system's generation update as the relevant window. Article 14 of the Meridian Charter requires a thirty-day public review period for any large-scale registry modification. During the generation update, the Charter's review mechanism is suspended for the update's duration. The generation update is scheduled to begin in thirty days."

She looked at the crew.

She looked at all of them in turn, the specific survey of a person who had been carrying a thing for a long time and needed to see the faces of the people she was putting it in front of.

"I was twenty-eight years old when I heard this," she said. "I had been in this role for seven years. I had known for two of those years that the Syndicate's structure included — that the community services and the blood-purity doctrine were not separate departments of the same organization. That they were the same operation. That the clinics and the debt collection and the Phase 1 displacement plan were one plan." She looked at her hands. "I did not know what to do with that knowledge in a way that wasn't either surrender or martyrdom. I left. I filed the Protocol under when I die I will have done nothing and I waited."

"For fifteen years," Boiler said.

"For fifteen years," she said.

The room was quiet. Not a polished quiet, not the kind that required maintenance — the quiet that arrived when the weight of a thing had been set down and was now occupying the space it needed to occupy before anyone moved.

Boiler said, "Thank you."

He said it in his ordinary speaking voice, without ceremony, the way a person says something when they mean it and do not need to add anything to the meaning to convey the sincerity. Two words. He did not elaborate.

Nari looked at him. Something in her face changed — not opened exactly, but arrived at a different distance from closed. She had been carrying this for fifteen years and had imagined the moment she would put it in front of someone and they would understand what the carrying had cost, and in the imagined version the moment had been larger and more structured around the weight of what she had survived. The version in the basement room was a Finnish-Somali structural engineer saying thank you in his ordinary voice, and it was, in some way she had not predicted, exactly the correct version. Not the dramatic one. The true one.

"Yes," she said.

She looked at Rook.

"Ask me what you need to ask."

The questions were operational and Rook asked them in the register Nari handled best. The founding document: she reproduced the key provisions from memory, the specific language of the heritage classification criteria, the Phase 1 operational parameters, the displacement threshold definitions. She had not written them down in fifteen years. She had rehearsed them — not aloud, in the specific internal rehearsal of a person who needed to be sure the text was still there, the way you check a thing you are responsible for maintaining. The text was there. It had always been there. She recited it without error.

The vote record: seven to two, 2211. She named the voters. Jang Yong-su, for. Six others, for. Two against. She named the two against as well. Both had been moved from their Stem positions within eighteen months of the vote, reassigned to administrative roles with no council function. She did not comment on the timing.

The Phase 0 personnel: the administrator who had authorized the civic grid access. She named them by title and Lotus designation. She described the authorization chain: Root-level approval, distributed to the relevant Stem, executed through a Civic Systems Engineer contact. She did not know the engineer's name. She knew the Stem's name. She gave it.

Vox was writing everything down — on paper, the physical transcript, because the operational board was going to be analog until Rook decided it could be otherwise, and that decision had not been made yet. Her handwriting was compact and precise, the pages accumulating.

At 0712, Nari said: "The administrative authorization has Seul-ki's direct signature."

She said the Root's name without inflection, without emphasis, without adding anything to it that would make it more than the name. It was the name of the person who had authorized the Phase 0 deployment and who had authorized the founding document and who had been running the Lotus Syndicate for thirty years — the name of the person at the center of what they were in, which Nari knew and which was why she named her last rather than first, saving the center for last because it was the weight that everything else organized around.

Rook wrote it down.

At 0728, Nari said, "That's everything I have."

She looked at the window — the east-facing basement window, the Z3 street visible through the security glass in the early-morning light. The day beginning. The specific quality of morning light that said: the thing you have been waiting to do is done. You have arrived at the other side of the waiting.

She had waited fifteen years for this specific morning. Not for the extraction, not for the boat, not for the basement room with the uneven table and the mismatched chairs. For the morning that came after it — the morning where the document existed outside her memory, where the fourteen pages of transcript were in someone else's hands, where the weight was distributed rather than carried alone.

The transcript was fourteen pages.

Rook looked at it on the table in front of Vox. Fourteen pages of the Purification Protocol's history, scope, personnel, and operational timeline. Produced by a woman who had memorized a founding document at twenty-eight years old and had kept it intact in her memory for fifteen years while living in a Z6 apartment with a packed bag by the door.

The three components.

They had the demographic baseline. Vox had confirmed it when she read the Headcount data — the data layer was exactly what the Protocol's founding document described as the demographic baseline requirement. The sculpture was the first component, already in their possession, already taken from the client who had commissioned it.

The neuretics registry access was the second. Someone was working on that access right now, somewhere in the GLMZ civic infrastructure, somewhere they had not yet located. The person who had been coerced to provide it, or who was providing it willingly, or who had already provided the test cluster data and was being pressured to provide the full grid access now.

The deployment window was thirty days.

Rook picked up the paper she had written in the night — the operational question. She had written: What do you have when you take apart the load-bearing element without replacing it?

She looked at it for a moment. She put it back in her coat pocket.

"We'll let you rest," she said to Nari.

"I'm not tired," Nari said.

"Rest anyway," Rook said. "We have work to do and we'll need you when we're further along in it. What you just gave us takes time to use correctly."

Nari looked at her. The assessment — brief, pragmatic. She had waited fifteen years. She was not going to argue about rest.

"Yes," she said. She stood. She went back into the sleeping area without further comment.

The crew sat for a moment after she left.

The transcript was fourteen pages. The weight of it was in the room now. Outside Nari's memory, it was a different kind of thing — it was a document, it was evidence, it was the specific gravity of something that had been carried alone for a very long time and had just been set down in the middle of a table that had been level in 2221 and had been imprecisely level ever since.

Nobody rushed to speak. They sat with it for a moment, the way you sit with something that deserves to be sat with before the work of it begins.

Then Vox said, "I need about eleven hours with the Headcount data." She was already reaching for her monitoring equipment, the precision of someone who has identified what comes next and is organized around beginning it. "There's something I want to cross-reference."

"Do it," Rook said.

Vox began.

The overhead light ran on the backup circuit. The building above them was starting its morning. In the sleeping area, through the door that had been left slightly open, Nari's breathing settled into the rhythm of a person who was, after all, tired — who had carried something for fifteen years and had set it down and had, in the setting down, found out what rest was.

=== BEAT 5136: The Sculpture Connects ===
Vox worked through the morning and into the afternoon. She did not summarize her progress in the channel and nobody asked her to. The crew operated in the practiced patience of people who understood that analysis produced what it produced when it was done rather than when you needed it to be done, and that asking Vox to report before she was ready to report was the equivalent of asking Boiler to give you the load calculation before he had run it. The calculation ran when it ran. The result was worth waiting for.

At 1634, she looked up from the equipment.

"I need the table," she said.

Rook cleared the table. The crew moved their chairs. Vox spread four sheets of paper across the surface — not electronic output, not a display projection, physical paper with handwritten analysis and printed data extracts she had pulled from the gray-market archives and annotated. The four channels visible in the notation: each sheet was assigned a channel-color in the margin, the four-color coding she had been using since the rebuild, the narrower network's discipline applied to how she organized the information she retrieved through it.

She had been at four channels since the graze wound. Not because the rebuild had stopped at four — she could have continued. She had chosen to stop at four because four was the number where the information came in ordered rather than in volume, where she processed what she received rather than tracking what she had missed, where the network was an instrument rather than an environment. She had made the decision explicitly, after the rebuild, and had not revisited it. Four was the correct number. This analysis had been run through four channels and had produced, in eleven hours, a connection that she was confident was structural rather than coincidental.

She indicated the first sheet.

"The Headcount sculpture's data layer," she said. "Fifteen years of Gray Zone demographics in civic-grid schema. The specific schema is GLMZ Census Infrastructure Version 4.2 — which is the census schema from 2211, the year the GLMZ retired the official heritage classification field from the public census record. The field was removed from the public record in compliance with the Meridian Charter's data equity provisions." She paused. "It was not removed from the underlying civic-grid database. It was reclassified as restricted infrastructure data. The infrastructure version of the schema still contains it."

She let this sit for a moment.

"The Headcount data is collected and structured in the infrastructure version of the schema," she said. "Not the public version. The data contains the heritage classification field. It contains fifteen years of heritage classification data for the Z4 through Z7 corridor — migration patterns, household compositions, neuretics registration status, debt distribution by heritage classification, everything that the public census stopped counting publicly in 2211."

"Because in 2211 the public census retired the heritage field," Vox said, to herself as much as to the room. It was the thing she had not said yet, the thing that clicked the timing into place. "And the Headcount data covers 2211 forward. The sculpture starts where the public record stops."

"Fifteen years," Ekow said. He said it with the quality of a man completing a calculation he had already run and arriving at the same answer again.

"Exactly fifteen years," Vox said. "The sculpture's oldest data entry is dated two months after the Meridian Charter's heritage field retirement. The collection did not begin earlier. It began exactly when the public record ended." She moved to the second sheet. "Which is what the Protocol's founding document describes as the demographic baseline requirement. A data set that picks up where the official record stops. A population registry that can exist without official authorization because it is structured around a field the official record no longer acknowledges."

Scout said, "The sculpture was built for this."

"Yes," Vox said. "The sculpture was not commissioned to document Gray Zone demographics. It was commissioned to construct the Protocol's first Phase 1 requirement. The art commission was the data collection vehicle." She looked at the table, at the four sheets. "The commission was placed with the Gray Horizon Collective in 2213. The Gray Horizon Collective is a Z4 art installation and civic documentation group — the commission would not have appeared unusual to them. The brief specified data format: civic-grid schema. Structured data integrated into physical sculpture. Public installation in a Z3 collector's market."

"Publicly visible," Rook said.

"A public installation creates legal ambiguity about ownership," Vox said. "If the data is public — if anyone can read the sculpture's data layer — it is not a private registry. It is a public artwork with accessible data. The Meridian Charter's data privacy provisions do not apply to public artworks in the same way they apply to private databases." She paused. "The sculpture is both simultaneously. Publicly available, privately useful. The data is there for anyone who can read it. The only people who have the means to read it the way it needs to be read are the people who commissioned it."

Lace looked at the table. "An entire data collection methodology disguised as a public art installation," she said, and her voice had a quality Rook did not hear from her often — not quite contempt, something more specific than contempt. The quality of a person who had spent their career being the only one in the room who understood the gap between presentation and reality, encountering an institution that had been exploiting that gap at scale, systematically, for decades.

"The same methodology," Lace said, "at a different scale."

"Yes," Vox said.

She moved to the third sheet.

"The second component: neuretics registry access." She had an extract from the Z6 civic grid test cluster's administrative logs — gray-market, from an archive she was not going to name because the archive's provenance was its own thing entirely and naming it would add a complication the room did not need right now. "The Phase 0 deployment. Six months. The 200,000 Z6 registered neuretics records in Lotus possession. This is confirmed by what Nari described and by the administrative log signature." She indicated a line of notation. "The deployment was run through a Civic Systems Engineer. Grade 4. The access authorization required a Civic Systems Engineer's credentials — the neuretics infrastructure is Grade 4 minimum for direct registry queries, per the Meridian Charter's technical provisions."

"They coerced someone," Rook said.

"That's the operational implication," Vox said. "The test deployment required ongoing cooperation from a Grade 4 engineer. Six months. The scale and duration of the deployment is not consistent with a single-event breach — it required repeated access over the six-month window. Someone inside the infrastructure was providing it." She indicated the fourth sheet. "The deployment window. The generation update. Thirty days from the current date. The neuretics system's generation update begins in thirty days. During the update, Article 14 review is suspended. If the full neuretics registry backdoor — not the test cluster, the full grid — is in Lotus possession before the update begins, they can run the complete Phase 1 query set during the update window, and the results will be delivered before the review mechanism restores."

She stopped.

She looked at the four sheets on the table and then at the crew.

"The three components," she said. "Demographic baseline: the Headcount sculpture. Currently in our possession. Second: neuretics registry access. The Syndicate is acquiring it. The coerced engineer is providing the access pathway, presumably under continued pressure, with the full deployment timed for the generation update window. Third: the window itself. Thirty days."

The room was quiet for a moment that was different from the quiet after Nari's briefing — denser, more operational. The Nari briefing had been the weight of knowing what the Protocol was. This was the weight of understanding what the Protocol needed to work and which of those things the Syndicate already had.

"We have the first component," Rook said.

"We have it," Vox said. "It is sitting in the Z3 safe house in a sealed cargo case. The Lotus commissioned it, paid for it, and arranged its recovery. The fee they paid to Ferris, which Ferris paid to you, came from the Lotus's operational budget." She looked at Rook. "The Φ12,000 commission paid by the anonymous client was funding from the Purification Protocol's preparatory budget. The job we took to recover the sculpture was a job the Protocol authorized to recover its own first component."

Rook had known this since the Z3 collector's market. Not the full detail — not the data schema, not the Phase 0 connection, not the precise mechanism. But the shape of it had been visible since Vox opened the data layer and said this is not art. She had known it was connected. She had not known how precisely.

The precision was the thing.

"The sculpture is structured to Phase 1 requirements," she said. "Exactly."

"Exactly," Vox said. "The data format, the heritage field inclusion, the fifteen-year coverage starting from the Charter's retirement of the public heritage record, the civic-grid schema version — all of it is specified in the Protocol's founding document as the demographic baseline requirement. The sculpture is not analogous to the requirement. It is the requirement, produced on commission, installed in a Z3 market under cover of public art, and available for Lotus acquisition when they needed it." She began gathering the sheets, the four-channel notation, the gray-market archive extracts. "The sculpture's collector had no idea what he was holding. He thought he was sitting on a high-value art investment with a complex provenance question. He was sitting on the Protocol's first operational asset."

Ekow said, "Who organized the collection?"

"The commission brief came from a Z5 cultural development foundation," Vox said. "The foundation was established in 2212 — one year before the commission was placed. The foundation's incorporation documents list three directors. Two of them have no other institutional affiliations. The third is a former Stem assistant."

Nobody asked her to confirm the connection. The connection was confirmed.

"How long did we have the sculpture before the Thorn team ran the surveillance flag," Scout said.

"Park was watching it for three weeks before we arrived," Vox said. "He noted our entry and exit. He did not move."

"Because moving the surveillance would have cost them the sculpture," Rook said. "They needed us to take it first. They were waiting for the recovery operation so they could recover it from us."

She thought about the timeline of the Thorns reaching Nari's building. 2347. The same night as the market run, which had been — she checked the operational timeline in her head — three days prior. Three days between the sculpture recovery and the Thorn approach on Nari. The timing was not coincidental.

"Park flagged us at the market," she said. "The Thorn deployment on Nari was three days later. He ran a cross-reference."

"Adaeze's referral came through his network," Ekow said. "Not directly. But the referral chain exists — anyone looking at the right connections could map the route from Adaeze's legal cooperative to the crew she might call." He said this without affect, the acknowledgment of how Lotus intelligence worked when it was working correctly.

"He moved on Nari when he identified the crew," Rook said. "Not because the sculpture triggered it — because the crew that took the sculpture was also the crew with connections to Adaeze, who had connections to Nari. He wasn't chasing the sculpture. He was chasing whoever came for it."

The four sheets were stacked on the table. Vox had her equipment in standby.

The safe house was quiet around them. The uneven table. The mismatched chairs. The backup light running its steady inadequate output, sufficient and nothing more.

Three components. One in their possession. One in active development by the organization they had been unknowingly working for. One a countdown that was running whether they acted on it or not.

"Thirty days," Boiler said. He said it with the specific quality he brought to structural pronouncements — not fatalism, not alarm. The voice of a man who had run the numbers and was stating the result. "If the generation update begins in thirty days and the full registry access is delivered before it begins, the Phase 1 query runs during the update window before review restores."

"Yes," Vox said.

"And the Phase 0 data — the 200,000 Z6 records — is available now," he said. "Combined with the Headcount baseline."

"Enough to begin targeted displacement in Z6 immediately," Vox said. "Before Phase 1. The Phase 0 data set is smaller but it covers Z6 comprehensively. They do not need Phase 1 to begin Z6 operations. Phase 1 expands the scope to the full Z4 through Z7 corridor."

Boiler looked at the table. He looked at nothing in particular on the table, the gaze of a man running a structural analysis in his head that was not a structural analysis. He did not say what he was thinking. He did not need to.

"We need to think about what comes next," Rook said.

Nobody disagreed.

The afternoon light was visible at the top of the basement window — the Z3 street in its late-afternoon function, the city doing what it always did outside of whatever was happening in basements and harbor corridors and maintenance crawlways. The city did not know what they were sitting in. It was running the infrastructure, maintaining the services, keeping the lights on, and not asking what was underneath.

Vox put her four sheets in the operational file.

The Headcount sculpture was in the sealed cargo case in the corner of the room. The first component of a plan forty years in the making, currently inside a Z3 safe house, in the custody of the crew that had been paid by the plan's authors to retrieve it.

The lines were drawn. Vox had put them there. The crew had watched them connect.

=== BEAT 5150: No Clean Exit ===

=== BEAT 5153: What Survives ===
They were back in the Level 14 operations center by 1236. Priya had the go-bag under the bench. Boiler had the floor plan back in his notebook. Nobody spoke for a few minutes.

This was not unusual after an operation completed its primary phase. There was a specific quality to the silence — not debrief avoidance, not emotional withdrawal. The quality of people who had been running a complex technical operation for three hours allowing the operational register to exhale before the assessment began. The 200,000 Z6 registered neuretics user records — heritage classification, civic identity, neuretics access level — remain in those backup registers and remain accessible to whatever access pathway the Syndicate has established for the Phase 0 operation."

"The Phase 0 data." Rook looked at the documentation. She was running the arithmetic. "The Headcount sculpture's demographic baseline covers the Z4-Z7 corridor but the highest-density coverage is the Z5/Z6 buffer zone. The Phase 0 neuretics records are Z6. Lace was at the secondary bench.
