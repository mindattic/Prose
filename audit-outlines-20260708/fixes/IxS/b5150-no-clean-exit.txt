Rook stood at the table with the four sheets in front of her and the crew seated and looked at the window for a moment. Not through it — the basement window showed the Z3 street at ankle height, the late-afternoon pedestrian traffic moving past in fragments, the city's working rhythm visible in the passing shoes and wheels. She looked at the window without seeing the street. She was looking at the geometry of the thing.

Then she turned and looked at the crew.

"We're in a genocide intervention," she said.

She said it plainly, without the verbal scaffolding that made a statement easier to deliver. Plain was the correct register for it. The statement did not require softening and she was not going to soften it, because softening it would have been a lie about what it was, and she had learned — at some cost, across three jobs with this crew and whatever she had been before those jobs — that lies about what things were tended to produce operational failures at the worst possible moment.

Nobody in the room spoke.

Not because the statement was a surprise — she could see from the quality of the crew's stillness that each of them had been sitting with some version of this conclusion since the Nari briefing, some since earlier than that. Boiler had been building the calculation since the walk through Old Harbor. Ekow had been building it for twelve years. Vox had been building it since she opened the Headcount data. They knew what they were in. They had been sitting with knowing it while they waited for the room that named it.

She named it.

"The Lotus Syndicate has been planning a systematic displacement operation for forty years," she said. "The demographics for it are in that cargo case. The administrative access to execute it is being coerced from someone inside the GLMZ civic infrastructure who we have not yet located. The deployment window opens in thirty days. The Φ12,000 we accepted from the anonymous client was from the Protocol's operational budget." She paused. "We have been employed by the entity funding its own dismantlement. Whether or not that fact is going to remain true depends on what we do in the next thirty days."

Scout said, "The Ferris job was bait."

"Not quite," Rook said. "The Ferris job was a recovery operation the Syndicate needed completed. The sculpture was out of their possession and they needed it back. They commissioned the recovery because they needed it done. The fact that the crew doing the recovery was also connected to Adaeze's network, which connected to Nari, was a problem that Park identified after the fact. It wasn't designed. It was a coincidence that became a vulnerability."

"They were going to retrieve the sculpture from us," Ekow said.

"Yes. After we did the work of taking it from the collector. That was the plan." She looked at the sealed cargo case in the corner. "The collector had been sitting on it for three months, not selling it, waiting for the price to go up. From the Syndicate's perspective, the recovery crew was a tool for solving the problem of a collector who was holding out. Once we had the sculpture, they would have applied pressure to recover it." She considered this. "Park's surveillance flag was the first step in that process. The Thorn deployment on Nari was the second — he was moving to neutralize the connection between the recovery crew and Adaeze before the recovery crew became a problem. He was three days too slow."

"Because we moved on Nari the same day Adaeze called," Lace said.

"Yes."

The room sat with this for a moment. The operational picture, fully assembled. The Ferris job as the entry point, the Nari extraction as the intervention, the Lotus not knowing that the crew it had contracted to recover its demographic baseline was now in possession of that baseline and the testimony of the person who had witnessed its authorization vote.

"The anonymous client," Vox said. "They still believe we're holding the sculpture pending the exchange."

"They believed it until the Thorn deployment on Nari," Rook said. "At that point, Park's operational assessment would have flagged us as a complication rather than a tool. The client — whoever placed the Ferris contract on the Syndicate's behalf — now knows the recovery crew is not going to deliver the sculpture."

"They know where the Z3 safe house is," Scout said. It was not a question. It was a structural assessment of the situation's exposure, the kind of observation Scout made when she was building the operational picture in her head and the next step was to identify what they were exposed to.

"They have the Z3 address from the Ferris routing chain if they traced it," Rook said. "We move tomorrow morning. Vox has the secondary location."

"Ready," Vox said.

Rook looked at the table.

"The operational question," she said, "is not whether to intervene. We're already in it — we have the sculpture, we have the witness, and the Thorn deployment ran on Nari because of our connection to Adaeze. We have done the thing that puts us in the middle of this regardless of whether we intend to be. The question is operational: what does the intervention look like, and what does it cost, and what survives it."

She let the last part of that sit.

"The Syndicate runs eleven clinics in Z5 and Z6," she said. "The free clinics. The community markets — seven of them in the Z4 through Z7 corridor. The debt management structure that is simultaneously the mechanism of leverage and the mechanism of housing preservation for approximately forty thousand people in the buffer zone who could not maintain their addresses without the Lotus's debt restructuring services. The community building where Boiler has been working is dependent on the Lotus's load-bearing infrastructure investment." She looked at Boiler. "Tell them."

He did not hesitate. "The community center building in Z5/Z6 has a load-path dependency on the market infrastructure that was integrated in 2209," he said. "The market columns are structural supports for the residential above. The residential above is the community center's upper-floor operations — the childcare cooperative, the adult education rooms, the medical intake space that one of the free clinics uses as a satellite. If the market infrastructure fails — if the funding that maintains the market columns stops, if the maintenance access that keeps the structural joints operational is removed — the residential above becomes compromised within two to three years." He said this with the same precision he brought to any structural problem. "It is not a dramatic collapse. It is a slow failure. The building becomes unusable before it becomes dangerous. But it becomes unusable."

The room received this.

"The Syndicate is load-bearing," Rook said. "For the physical infrastructure. For the health services. For the debt structures that allow people to stay in their homes. For a community that built around the services and that did not build that way because of any conspiracy — that built that way because the services were there and the services were real and the community responded to real services the way communities respond to real services. The load-bearing was not an accident. It was the design. But the design does not change the weight." She looked at the table. "You cannot remove the load-bearing element without the structure it supports failing."

"Unless you provide an alternative load path," Boiler said.

He said it simply, the way he said structural solutions — as a statement of engineering fact rather than a rhetorical contribution. There was an alternative, in structural terms. You identified the load path. You designed the alternative that could carry the load. You transferred the load to the alternative before you removed the original. You did not remove the load-bearing element and then design the alternative. You designed the alternative first.

"Yes," Rook said. "Unless you provide an alternative load path first."

The room understood this.

"Three threads," she said. "The Headcount sculpture — we have it. The witness — we have her. The third thread is the access pathway. The coerced engineer who is building the neuretics registry backdoor. We don't know who they are yet. We have not located them. But they exist, and they are in the civic infrastructure somewhere, and they are running out of time in the same way we are running out of time, and there is a nonzero probability that they want out of the arrangement they are in." She looked at Vox. "Same probability that the civic infrastructure has a signal leak that we can find if we look for it."

"I have been looking," Vox said. "Nothing yet. I will keep looking."

"Three threads, one source, one plan," Rook said. "The source is the Lotus Syndicate. The plan is the Protocol. The question is not how to destroy the Syndicate — destroying the Syndicate produces the failure of the load-bearing element without a replacement, which produces community collapse in the buffer zone, which is harm done to the same population the Protocol is designed to harm. The question is how to make the Protocol impossible to execute without destroying the thing the Protocol is embedded in."

"Separate the silk from the iron," Lace said.

She said it quietly, not as a point she was making so much as a thing she was thinking aloud, the private notation of a person who was working through a problem in a language they knew well. Rook heard it. She did not respond to it directly.

"Nobody has to make that decision right now," Rook said. "What we have to do right now is find the third thread. The access pathway. The person inside the civic infrastructure who is building the backdoor. We find them before the Syndicate can close the deployment. If we find them, we have all three components of the Protocol under our control before the generation update window opens, and the conversation with the Syndicate happens from that position rather than from nothing."

"The conversation," Ekow said. He said it without editorial inflection, which was itself an editorial note.

"There will be a conversation," Rook said. "There are two possible ends to this. One: we destroy the Protocol completely and accept what that costs in terms of the Syndicate's structure and the community infrastructure that depends on the Syndicate's structure. Two: we give the Syndicate a choice — dismantle the Protocol publicly and fund the transition infrastructure that the community needs to operate without the Protocol's load-bearing contribution, or the documentation goes to public disclosure and the Syndicate loses the community trust that is the only thing making the load-bearing possible in the first place." She paused. "Option two requires the Syndicate to have something to lose. Which means not destroying it prematurely."

"Option two requires someone at the top of the Syndicate to be willing to make that choice," Ekow said.

"Yes," Rook said.

He looked at the table. He was thinking about something he did not say, the specific quality of a person who had knowledge relevant to the conversation they were in and who was deciding when to put it in the room. He did not put it in the room yet.

Rook did not push.

"No one has to commit to either option now," she said. "We don't have all three threads. We don't have enough information. What we do now is find the third thread. Everything else follows from that." She looked at the crew. "The Φ12,000 is not what we're working for. Everybody knows that. The Φ12,000 was a door. We walked through it. We're in the room now. The question of what we do in the room is going to take a minute to answer correctly."

Nobody argued with this.

Lace said, "How long do we have at this address?"

"We move at 0700," Rook said.

"And Nari?"

"She comes with us. She stays with us until we have a resolution. Her testimony is evidence only as long as she is alive to give it." She said this plainly, with the same register she had used for everything else. "She knows this. We discussed it."

Scout said, "The operational board goes analog when we move."

"Already planned," Rook said.

Boiler had been looking at the cargo case in the corner for the past several minutes — not at the case exactly, at the space the case occupied, the way he looked at a structural problem when he was building the load calculation in his head. He looked at it for another moment, then looked at his hands.

"The community building in Z5/Z6," he said. "The renovation I've been running. The childcare cooperative. The medical intake satellite." He looked at Rook. "If this goes wrong — if the Syndicate fractures and the infrastructure fails and there's no transition funding — those people don't have somewhere else to go."

"I know," Rook said.

"So the alternative load path has to exist before we remove the original," he said.

"I know," she said.

He looked at the table. He was not arguing with her — this was not a challenge to the plan, because there wasn't a plan yet, and he understood that. He was adding the weight to the accounting. Making sure it was in the room.

It was in the room. It had been in the room since the walk through Old Harbor, since the market column integration, since the conversation between him and Ekow on the street outside the debt collection office that shared a door with the clinic. He did not need to add it again.

"Right," he said.

The backup light was steady. The Z3 street above the window was moving through its evening function, the city's routine unchanged and unhurried. By Nari's account — intel she had not verified since she had last had access to the Protocol documentation — the generation update would begin in approximately thirty days, and the window that the Protocol had planned around for fifteen years would open.

Twenty-nine days.

Rook had not answered all of it. She had not answered any of it cleanly. What she had done was put the shape of the problem in the room, in the same room as the people who were going to have to solve it, and let the shape be there. The shape did not become smaller for being named. It became clearer.

Clarity was not comfort. Clarity was not a plan. Clarity was what you needed before you could have either.

She picked up the paper from her coat pocket. The operational question she had written at zero-three-hundred. She looked at it. She put it back.

"Get some rest," she said. "We move at 0700."

The crew did not ask about rest. They did what she said, which was the thing they had always done when the operational phase said wait. They waited. They slept when they could sleep. They ran their maintenance checks, their equipment reviews, their individual preparations. They did not talk about what came next because what came next was not yet visible.

In the cargo case in the corner, the Headcount sculpture waited. In the sleeping area, Nari slept. Outside, the GLMZ ran its systems.

Twenty-nine days.
