The crew dispersed on the morning of the fourth day.

It was not a formal dispersal — there had been no debrief scheduled, no moment at which the operation was officially closed, no ceremony of any kind, because the crew did not work that way and had not worked that way across any of the operations that had brought them to this particular Z3 safe house at this particular end. They had been together for four days since the council chamber. They had held the space together through the forty-eight-hour window and through the three days after, and now the holding was finished and they were leaving in the order in which they had things to return to.

Lace was first, two hours after sunrise. She moved with the ease of a person returning to her own life — or to the particular configuration of her life that existed between operations, which was also a performance, in the specific way that everything Lace did was both a performance and real simultaneously, the line between them having dissolved somewhere in the years of holding other people's faces over her own. She touched Rook's arm at the door, briefly, and then she was gone.

Vox said, to the room in general: *Four channels. If you need me.* She said this as a statement about her operational status and also, Rook understood, as a statement about herself — the four channels that were not what she had been and were what she had decided to be, the number held as a discipline rather than a limit. Then she was gone too.

Scout had left the night before, back to Z1 and Gerald and the structural data work that was her current life, or her current cover, or the thing that had become both. She had sent a single channel message before going: *Good run.* That was all. Two words was enough.

Boiler was last before Ekow.

He came and found Rook in the safe house's kitchen, which was the functional kind of kitchen that functional safe houses had: everything necessary and nothing decorative, the kind of space that had been designed to be used by people who were not going to stay long enough to have feelings about it. He poured himself something and stood with it, not in any hurry.

*You're going to be okay,* he said.

It was not a question. It was an assessment delivered with the specific weight of someone who had been doing structural assessments their entire professional life and had learned to say what they saw without hedging it.

*I'm going to be fine,* she said.

*Those are different,* he said.

She looked at him.

*Okay is — it worked out the way it should have. The structure did what it was designed to do and you can look at it and say yes, that's right.* He drank whatever he had poured. *Fine is it worked out some way and you're managing it. You're carrying the load manually instead of letting the structure do it.* He looked at her. *I'm asking about okay.*

She thought about it for a real moment. About the Protocol dissolved and the endowment funded and Priya in the cooperative's safe house and Nari in Ekow's arrangement and the hardliners dark somewhere in the city and Park Gi-su dark somewhere in the city and the 200,000 people in the Z6 registry and the flag on the Bureau's record and the twelve years of work ahead.

*Ask me in a year,* she said.

He nodded. *That's okay,* he said. She understood that this was both a statement about her answer and the answer to his question. He set down his glass, found his coat, and at the door turned and said: *The building in Z5 is coming along. If you want to see it sometime.* Not an invitation exactly — more like a statement about the future existing and having things in it. Then he was gone.

The safe house was quiet.

Ekow was still in the main room.

She had known he was still there. She had been aware of his presence the way you were aware of a large still object in a familiar space — not intrusive, not pressuring, simply occupying the room with the particular quality of stillness that very few people had naturally and that he had acquired through years of operational environments that required it. He was at the table where the physical board had been, where the three threads had been mapped and remapped over the third week of the operation, where the connections had clarified into a plan. The board was gone. The table was clean. He was not doing anything with the clean table. He was standing beside it in the specific way of someone who had been working in a space for a long time and is not quite ready to stop occupying it.

She came in and sat at the table.

He looked at her for a moment. Then he sat.

She had not seen him sit this way before — not operationally, not in the posture of a person prepared to stand back up. He sat the way a person sat when they were not going anywhere immediately and had accepted this about themselves, the body settling into the chair rather than perching above it. It changed something about the quality of the room.

He reached into his coat.

She had seen him do this twice before — or rather, she had noted it twice before, because noting operative gestures was automatic, the same way she noted exits and positions and the specific tells that accumulated toward a read. The first time had been in the first briefing, when he had been taking down patrol routes and had chosen to write them in a physical notebook rather than log them to his neuretics, which was a choice, and she had filed it as a choice. The second time had been at the stern of the boat during the Z6 extraction, in the dark, when the operation was still running and the silence of moving water was around them and his hand had gone to the place on his coat where the notebook was and had not quite touched it — the gesture of a person who is aware of something they are carrying, checking that it is still there.

Now he placed the notebook on the table between them.

He did not open it.

*I have a list,* he said.

She waited.

*Jobs I did for the Lotus,* he said, looking at the notebook rather than at her. *Over twelve years. Operations that went the way they went. People who didn't come through the other side of them.* He was quiet for a moment. *I don't carry it because I haven't found a way not to. I carry it because it's accurate. Because the alternative is to not have a record, and not having a record is worse — it's a different kind of carrying, the kind where you've stopped acknowledging the weight and started pretending it's part of you.*

She said nothing.

*I'm not looking for something from you,* he said. *Not absolution. You don't have any authority over the past and neither do I and I'm not confused about that.* He looked at the notebook on the table between them. *But the Lotus runs a record. Everything I did for them — it's in their system somewhere, in the operational logs under their authorization codes. My name is on their record of those operations and their record is run by them and it serves their institutional purposes and I cannot touch it.* He paused. *That is the only record where those operations exist.*

She understood where this was going.

*I want one name on a record that is not theirs,* he said. *Not run by an institution with an interest in what the name represents. One name, somewhere, held by someone who is not complicit in the reason the name needs to be on a record.*

The safe house was quiet around them. Street traffic outside, the ambient of the Z3 morning, ordinary and distant.

*Tell me,* she said.

*Twelve years ago,* he said. *Z6 border. A Thorn operation — I was the outside hand, the one they used when the operation needed to be outside the Thorn command chain, when they needed a result that couldn't be traced back through their own authorization structure.* He said this without inflection, in the same flat precise register he had used for every piece of operational intelligence over the three weeks. *There was a man. He was a community organizer. He had been running a debt-relief collective in the Z6 buffer zone — the kind of collective that organized against the debt instrument structures the Syndicate maintained in the zone, the kind that the Syndicate had an institutional interest in absorbing or removing. He had declined three times to align the collective with Lotus administrative oversight. He had written the declinations in formal community-cooperative language and had sent them through the zone's public organizing channels, which was the kind of thing you did when you wanted the refusal on the record rather than just in a private conversation.* He paused. *The fourth approach was the operation I ran.*

She was looking at him.

*He did not survive the operation,* he said. *He had a family. The collective continued for two more years, with different leadership, and then aligned with the Lotus administrative structure. The way those things went.*

He said the name.

*Berhane Haile.*

Quiet, precise, the name given the same weight as any operational designation, which was also the specific weight of someone who had been saying the name inside themselves for twelve years and who was now saying it outside.

*I know this does not change it,* Ekow said. *I know that the name being on a record outside the Lotus's system doesn't give him back to the people he was building the collective for. I know that you writing it down is not an act of justice — it's an act of record-keeping, and record-keeping is not justice, and I am not confused about the difference.* He looked at her for the first time since he had started speaking. *I know all of that. I am asking you to write down the name.*

She reached across the table and took the notebook.

He did not stop her. He did not look away.

She opened it to the first clean page. The notebook was worn in the specific way that objects were worn by consistent handling over a long period — the cover soft, the spine creased in the place where it was most often opened, the pages at the front showing the slight curved impression of having been read and reread. She found the first clean page.

She wrote: *Berhane Haile. Z6 border. Twelve years ago.*

She looked at it for a moment. Then she wrote: *Community organizer. Debt-relief collective.*

She closed the notebook. She put it on the table between them.

He looked at what she had put in front of him. His face was the same face it had been since she had known him — the flat, contained register of a practitioner who did all of his work internally and showed none of it — except that something in the containment had changed slightly, the way a physical structure changed when a load it had been carrying for a long time was redistributed rather than removed: still present, differently borne.

He picked up the notebook. He put it back in his coat.

They sat for a moment in the specific quiet that followed something that had been said and written and could not be unsaid or unwritten, the quiet that was not comfortable and was not meant to be comfortable, because comfort was not what had been asked for and offering it would have been wrong.

*Adaeze's cooperative has been doing case work in Z6 for twelve years,* she said. *Early-stage debt-relief organizing, from twelve years ago — she may have files on the collective. She may have his name already.* She looked at him. *It might be worth asking her, at some point. When the moment is right.*

He was quiet.

Then: *Yes.* The single word of someone who had thought about a thing for a long time and had not, until this moment, had a specific action to take toward it. *Yes. When the moment is right.*

He stood.

He picked up his coat from the back of the chair and put it on with the same economy of movement he brought to everything — no gesture wasted, nothing performed. He looked at the table where the board had been, at the clean surface and the absent threads, and then at the door.

She stood.

They walked out of the safe house together. On the Z3 street outside, the morning traffic was running: people moving with the purposeful patterns of a day beginning, Eigenlift transports gliding quietly in the middle altitude lanes, the gray-green of the lake visible between two buildings at the far end of the street.

He did not go south.

He did not go toward Old Harbor. He did not go toward the Z5/Z6 Lotus circuit, toward the debt collector nodes or the Thorn patrol routes or the gray-market drop points he had mapped on a whiteboard three weeks ago and navigated from memory for twelve years before that. He went north, which was the direction of the Z1 transit hub, which was where you went when you were going somewhere that was not the Lotus operational territory.

She watched him go for a moment.

He was in the crew. Neither of them had said this. It had been said by the direction he walked.

She turned in her own direction.
