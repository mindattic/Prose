Rook had been here enough times to have a sense of the room's rhythm: the way Vox left certain cable runs permanently attached and certain ones coiled and ready, the classification logic she applied to her equipment that was not organizational in any conventional sense but was, Rook had concluded, organizational in a personal one. The room was legible to Vox and nobody else, which was the point.

She arrived at nine the next morning. Vox let her in without ceremony and returned to the chair she had been in, which faced a workstation array showing four active feeds simultaneously — data streams of some kind, Rook could not read the content from the door, only the fact of four distinct streams running in parallel, each one updating in a slightly different rhythm.

"The collector," Vox said, before Rook sat down, "is Yusuf Tran-Okonkwo. Z3 collector for eleven years. Legitimate market activity — he registers purchases, he files provenance chains, he pays the civic transaction fees that serious collectors pay to maintain market standing. His collection runs to thirty-something pieces, mostly GLMZ-origin work from the last twenty years. Mid-market, good taste, no major flag activity." She moved one of the feeds to a secondary position and opened a different view. "The piece moved through a Z4 primary dealer eight weeks ago — sixty-two hundred, probably close to market for a piece of that category. The consignment courier who stole it worked for a Z4 logistics firm that has since let her go, which is how you handle a theft you don't want to file civic paper on."

Rook sat in the secondary chair — the non-Vox chair, the one that was always positioned for a visitor and never moved because the room had one correct visitor position and Vox had identified it. "The courier's gone to ground."

"Completely. I found one secondary feed mention of someone matching her pattern selling on a Z6 market three weeks ago, but I can't confirm it's her. May not matter. The piece is with Tran-Okonkwo and has been for four weeks, which means the courier sold it to him or sold it to someone who sold it to him." Vox scrolled through something. "He knows it was stolen. He bought it anyway. That's a civic violation he's not going to report."

"Which is why the client can't use official channels."

"One reason." Vox looked up for the first time since Rook had come in. The graze wound on her left forearm had been healed for two months — the skin closure complete, the slightly different texture that remained the only evidence of it, a narrow strip of new skin lighter than the old. She moved her arms the same way she had before, without compensation or protective instinct. The wound was gone. The healing had finished.

"What about the client?" Rook said.

"Anonymous is anonymous. I can't break the contract wall from outside." Vox paused, and the pause had a specific quality — not uncertainty but the gap before careful language. "What I can tell you is that the Headcount piece does not appear in any public record as a data-art format. It's registered with the GLMZ civic arts authentication system as a physical-digital hybrid in the standard provenance format. No category marker for embedded data beyond standard certificate architecture."

"But."

"But the piece's full certificate chain references a data schema format I don't recognize as a standard art attribution standard. I cross-referenced it against the GLMZ civic grid's published schema library. It's a match for civic demographic registry format. Population data. Migration, residency, heritage cross-sections."

The room was quiet for a moment. The four feeds ran their separate rhythms.

"Who produces that schema?" Rook said.

"The civic grid does, for official population census work. A few approved research institutions can request access. And anyone who has gray-market access to the schema library." Vox's voice was level. She was not performing calm; she had arrived at it through the work. "I can't tell you from outside the piece what the data is or where it came from. You'll need to open the data layer to know that."

"The client wants the physical piece intact," Rook said.

"I know. I'm telling you what the piece is. Whether you open it before delivery is your decision."

She filed this. The data layer was what it was, and she had been right that the brief had been incomplete, and now she had a clearer picture of the incompleteness without yet knowing what it pointed at. Demographic data in a civic registry schema embedded in an art piece called Headcount. Anonymous client. Twelve thousand Quanta.

"Any Thorn activity near the Tran-Okonkwo building?" she said.

"Nothing specific. Z3 north-end Thorn presence is low by historical standards — the Lotus doesn't have significant market interests in that neighborhood and the Thorns follow the money. You might pick up ambient presence near the border with Z4, but nothing that should affect a clean acquisition operation at the collector's market." Vox paused again, and the pause this time had a different quality — lighter, less weighted. "The building camera on the east face is a Meridian HD-7, which is a good piece of hardware. Scout should be able to thread the feed without significant effort. The model has a known handshake delay that creates a half-second window on forced reconnect."

"How do you know which camera model it is?"

"I looked at the building permit files." Vox allowed herself a small expression that was not quite a smile. "They listed the security hardware in the installation filing. Z3 permit office does not classify that data."

Rook considered the four feeds on the workstation. "You've been running at four for three months."

Vox glanced at the screens. "Yes."

"Not rebuilding to six."

"No." She said it simply, without the emphasis that would have made it a statement about something larger. It was a fact. She was letting it be a fact.

"Why four?" Rook asked.

Vox was quiet for a moment, which was not the same as being reluctant. Vox was quiet when she was choosing the right framing for something she had already thought through completely. "When I rebuilt the network after Crimson," she said, "I could have rebuilt to six. The contacts were there. The feeds were recoverable. The capacity I lost was capacity I could have replaced." She looked at the screens, at the four streams running in their separate rhythms. "I found that at four channels, I was reading things I hadn't been reading at six. The narrower the band, the more resolution you get within it. Specificity. When you're running six channels, you're covering territory. When you're running four, you're reading it."

Rook waited.

"I could have gone back to six," Vox said. "I chose not to. Four channels is not a limitation I'm working around. It's a methodology I'm working with." She looked back at Rook. "The graze was not the reason. The graze was when I decided, but the decision is not the same as the reason."

Rook absorbed this. There was something in the framing she wanted to keep — the distinction between the decision and the reason, the distinction between the accident that created the opening and the choice made in it. She did not say so. She kept it in the register that didn't have language yet.

"All right," she said.

"All right," Vox said, and the agreement was not about the channels; it was about the conversation being complete. They both understood this. Three months ago they had not been able to have a conversation that moved like this — that arrived somewhere and recognized the arrival and moved on. After Tidewell there had been a friction in their working relationship that she had not been able to fully diagnose: not mistrust, not grievance, something more like a gap between their respective operational registers that required active translation that neither of them had been doing correctly. She did not know exactly when the friction had resolved. She only knew it had.

The conversation was easy now. She had missed this without quite recognizing she was missing it, and now that it was present again she noted its presence with the same sideways care she applied to most things that mattered.

"Camera schedule on the entry pavilion?" she said.

"Standard rotation, twelve-minute cycle. There's a handoff between the pavilion camera and the corner unit that creates a four-second gap at the northeast angle, predictable interval." Vox sent the camera data to Rook's neuretics in a file. "Scout can work with this."

"I'll have her cross-reference." Rook stood. "I need the collector's buying pattern. How long he holds pieces before selling."

"Tran-Okonkwo holds for one to three weeks before listing on secondary market. He bought the Headcount piece four weeks ago, which means he's been waiting. Either holding for a specific buyer or holding for the price to come in." Vox pulled up a secondary file. "There's been one inquiry through a Z3 dealer network in the last ten days. I don't have the buyer identity. The inquiry was flagged to Tran-Okonkwo through a private channel."

"Someone else is interested."

"Someone else has expressed interest." Vox distinguished this carefully. "Whether they follow through is different. But the piece is live on the market and Tran-Okonkwo is not holding out of sentiment."

The operation window was narrowing. A collector with a pending inquiry and a piece he had been holding four weeks was a collector who might close before the crew was positioned. She revised the timeline in her head: two days was probably the outer boundary, not the estimate she had given Scout. She would need to compress.

"I'll confirm the team briefing tomorrow," she said. "Can you have the full building layout by tonight?"

"I have it now." Vox transferred a second file. "Permit records included structural schematics. Not real-time, but the building hasn't changed significantly in three years."

Rook paused at the door. The four feeds ran behind Vox's shoulder, each one carrying its distinct signal at its distinct rate — a specific kind of density, concentrated rather than spread, the difference between knowing four things deeply and knowing six things thinly.

"Thank you," she said.

Vox had already turned back to the screens. "Send me the crew schedule when you have it."

Rook let herself out.

In the corridor, she processed the Headcount data while she walked. Civic demographic registry schema. Fifteen years was the kind of timeline that produced a comprehensive population picture — migration patterns over long cycles, debt accumulation and resolution, heritage cross-sections built from neuretics registration data and civic filing records. That was not the data you embedded in an art piece to prove provenance. That was the data you embedded in an art piece to hide it somewhere nobody would look.

The anonymous client wanted the data back.

She reached the stairwell and started down. The concrete walls held the building's specific cold, the iron-aggregate chill of the old construction, the smell of fifty years of other people's passing through this same space. She kept her pace steady.

She would look at the data layer. She would make the delivery decision when she had done that.

Two days. Probably less.
