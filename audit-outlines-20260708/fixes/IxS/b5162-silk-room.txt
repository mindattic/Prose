The comms link was open from the moment Lace took her seat.

This was the operational protocol they had established: not active communication, not Rook in Lace's ear managing the room, but an open channel so that the clock was running in Lace's neuretics and the clock had ears. Whatever happened in the council chamber would arrive in Rook's channel eighteen seconds after it happened — the lag of the sub-level's signal routing through Scout's relay at the external camera interface, the cost of running a secure channel through an improvised path. Close enough to real-time that decisions could be made on it.

Rook sat in the eigenlift transport on the maintenance deck at 43 Velos Street and heard the council chamber.

The room's ambient was specific: concrete vault, nine meters across, the acoustic character of a space that had been built for meetings and that absorbed sound the way reinforced concrete absorbed sound — cleanly, without reverberation, the specific quality of a room where the walls were not going to echo the things said in it. Chairs on a hard floor. The sound of multiple people in a large room maintaining the particular silence of institutional waiting.

She counted the chair sounds. Eleven seated, two standing — Park and Ekow at the room's perimeter, she assumed, standing positions rather than seated at the council table. The Stems were seated. The Root was seated.

She had the operational feed on the transport's surface and the clock in the upper right corner and Scout's perimeter confirmation on the secondary channel and the open link to Lace's neuretics. She was running everything simultaneously from one block out and she was waiting.

She had been waiting since 0745.

At 0751, Lace's voice came through the channel — not to Rook, to the room.

"I'd like to raise something before the session's opening business."

Rook heard the room adjust. Not physical movement — the specific acoustic shift of a group of people directing collective attention toward a single point. The ambient changed. The ambient was now Lace.

She heard what she knew would come next.

"This session's opening business is what I'd like to raise," Lace said.

A silence. Not confused. Considering.

Then a voice Rook had not heard before: aged, precise, the cadence of someone who had been running meetings for decades and who received the unexpected with the same register they applied to everything else. "The agenda was distributed two days ago. The opening business is the Protocol's Phase 1 authorization confirmation."

"Yes," Lace said. "I know."

The voice again: "You have a question about the authorization."

"I have something to show the room," Lace said. "Before the authorization is confirmed, I want to show the room what the authorization is authorizing. Not as an objection — as a matter of record. So the record is complete."

Another silence. Longer.

Rook ran the clock. 0752 and forty seconds. The Thorn patrol rotation was at sixteen minutes out. The window was holding.

The aged, precise voice said: "Continue."

Rook understood, in the moment she heard the voice authorize Lace to continue, what had been in the founding documentation that she had assembled over three weeks: the same voice, the same cadence, the same specific conditional framing used when constraints were flagged — *as a matter of record, so the record is complete* — echoed in the authorization language on the Protocol's founding document, the community care paperwork, the civic grid charter compliance updates. The same structural signature across forty years of documents. She was hearing it live.

She was hearing Yim Seul-ki give Lace permission to speak.

She thought about what this meant. A woman who had been expecting the crew since Old Harbor. Who had moved the documentation to the council chamber. Who had set the room. Who had just heard someone she did not know request permission to show the room what the authorization was authorizing, and had said: continue.

Not because she was unprepared for this. Because she had decided that this was the room in which it was going to happen.

Rook ran the clock and listened.

Lace spoke for four minutes.

She spoke without notes, without the performance of prepared delivery, in the flat register of someone relaying factual information to people who were entitled to have it. She named the three documentation components: the Headcount sculpture, the neuretics registry firmware, the Phase 0 deployment data. She described what the crew had: the complete founding documentation, the Phase 0 operational records, the Stems' authorization vote record, the organizational signature analysis, the civic grid infrastructure authorization chain. She described where it was going: three simultaneous disclosure points, forty-eight hours from now, conditional release trigger, unless.

She said *unless* and stopped.

The room was very quiet.

Rook heard a Stem — not the Root, a different voice, younger, controlled but with the specific quality of control that came from not being fully prepared for a thing even when you had been told to expect it — say: "Who are you."

Lace said: "I'm the person who was sent to deliver this message. The message is what I've already delivered."

"You were authorized through the guest acknowledgment process."

"Yes."

"Your credential is documented."

"Yes."

A pause. Then the controlled voice said: "The credential belongs to a former adjutant's associate."

"The credential is documented," Lace said. "The message is what I've described."

Rook heard what was underneath this exchange: the room recalibrating. Not panicking — this was not a room that panicked, it was a room of people who had been in institutional positions for decades and who processed unexpected information with the deliberation of people who understood that speed in such moments was an error. The Stems were recalibrating. They were running, simultaneously, the assessment of whether the message was real — did the crew have what they claimed to have — and the assessment of what the message required of them if real.

They were also, and this was the part of the recalibration Rook had anticipated, running the assessment of how the message had arrived in this room. A crew of six people plus two protected witnesses had spent three weeks assembling the documentation for a forty-year plan. They had recovered the sculpture, extracted the defector, rolled back the firmware deployment. They had then walked into the inner sanctum through the front door.

The message had not been relayed from outside through a comms channel, which was how Rook had initially planned to deliver it. The message had been delivered inside the room, in front of the Stems, so that everyone at the council table had heard it and the authorization sequence for the Protocol could not be quietly deflected through a bilateral decision between the Root and an unknown external party.

This was Lace's judgment call. She had made it three nights ago when she looked at the operational plan and understood that a message delivered from outside could be managed — could be heard by the Root alone, processed privately, responded to through a channel that kept the Stems at the distance they had been maintained at for forty years, the same distance that had allowed the Protocol to run outside the oversight mechanism that the Stems were supposed to represent.

The message delivered inside the room, in front of the Stems, required the Stems to know that the Stems had heard it.

The room could not be made to unhear it.

Rook had understood the judgment call when Lace explained it. She was understanding it differently now, hearing the room recalibrate around the fact of the message. The Stems had just heard — together, in the council chamber, in the room where the Protocol had been authorized — what the crew had and where it was going. The Stems now knew what the Root was going to be offered. The Stems now knew that their forty-year involvement in the Protocol's authorization structure was part of the documentation package.

The authorization sequence for Phase 1 was not going to proceed this morning.

She heard the precise, aged voice again. The room had been adjusting itself toward the Root since the moment Lace stopped speaking, and now the Root was speaking.

"The documentation you describe," Seul-ki said. "I should tell you that I have been aware, for three weeks, of the crew that assembled it. I've been aware since the Headcount sculpture was recovered from the Z3 collector." A pause. "I would like to tell you something about that awareness."

Rook's hands went still on the transport's surface.

"The Thorns flagged the crew at the collector's market in Z3," Seul-ki said. "Not because we were monitoring the sculpture. The sculpture had been in independent custody for eleven months and we were monitoring it as a secondary priority — as a documentation asset, valuable but not in active use. The Thorns flagged the crew not for the sculpture. For the recovery methodology. Which was specific enough to suggest that the crew's next job would require extraction capacity in the Z6 buffer zone." A pause. "We had a defector whose extraction window was ninety-six hours from the date the sculpture was recovered."

Rook heard this land in her channel with the specific quality of information that reorders what came before it.

Three weeks ago at the Z3 collector's market. Park Gi-su's surveillance network. She had read the operational record at the time as: the Thorns observed the sculpture's recovery and monitored the crew as an unknown quantity with potential access to their documentation asset. She had read it as a defensive posture — the Syndicate protecting its data.

She had read it wrong.

The Thorns had not been monitoring the sculpture. They had been monitoring the sculpture's recovery methodology, because the methodology would tell them who was working in the Z6 buffer zone with the operational capacity to run a parallel extraction. They had been using the sculpture as a marker. Not for the sculpture. For whoever came to retrieve it, and what they intended to do next.

Park had been running a trace operation. From the moment the crew touched the sculpture in Z3, the Thorns had been tracking the thread back toward Lee Nari.

"He knew about Nari's extraction window," she said. Not to anyone specifically. To the record she was keeping.

"He has always known about Lee Nari," Seul-ki said in the channel — not responding to Rook, continuing her account to the council chamber. "Her location in Z6 was known to the Thorns within eighteen months of her departure from the Syndicate's administrative structure. The extraction window that your crew was working against was the Thorns' deployment window — the same window, from the same intelligence."

Rook ran the implications.

Park had known where Nari was for thirteen years. He had not closed the extraction window when the crew opened it. He had not intercepted the crew in the Z6 buffer zone when he had numerical advantage and operational cover. He had let them move Nari out of the buffer zone, through Old Harbor, to the Z3 safe house, and he had followed the documentation thread from there to the Spine operation and from there to this morning.

He had been in the anteroom for fifty-three minutes before the crew arrived.

He had come early because he wanted to be in position before the window opened. Or before he changed his mind.

Ekow had said that. She had not understood it then. She understood it now.

"The Thorns chose not to close the extraction window," Seul-ki said, "because I gave no authorization for it. I had been aware for some time that a thread existed that, if followed, would arrive at this room. I had made a decision that when it arrived, I would be here." A very slight pause. "I did not make the decision to facilitate the thread. I made the decision not to prevent it."

The council chamber was silent.

Rook held the channel and thought about a woman who had built the clinics and built the Protocol and built the machine that would come for her and had run it for forty years, who had sat in her administrative office forty-eight hours after the Spine rollback appeared in the civic grid log and had decided that when the end came, she would be in the correct room.

She had not given the Thorns authorization to prevent the crew from getting here.

She had set the room instead.

"Then we're in the right place," Lace said.

"Yes," Seul-ki said. "You are. Sit down."

A chair sound. Lace, sitting.

Rook heard footsteps — measured, unhurried, the specific economy of movement of a woman who had been walking through rooms like this one for a very long time. Moving from the center of the table toward the room's east end, toward the auxiliary entrance that Boiler's structural plans had identified in the east wall.

Then Seul-ki's voice, closer, at the peripheral of the channel's coverage: "Tell the crew's operational lead that I would like to speak with her directly. The anteroom credential will accept a visitor."

Lace said: "She's one block out."

"I know where she is," Seul-ki said. "I would like her here regardless."

Rook heard this and put her hand on the eigenlift transport's controls.

She had planned to be one block out. She had planned to be the clock. She had made a deliberate decision about the operational geometry and the decision had made sense and she had run it for ninety-six minutes and she was now being asked by a woman who had been expecting her since Z3 to come inside.

She ran the clock for three more seconds.

Then she came inside.
