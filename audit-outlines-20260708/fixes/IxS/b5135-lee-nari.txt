Rook's knock produced movement immediately behind the door — not the movement of someone woken or caught unawares, but the immediate response of someone who had been awake and had been expecting a knock. The sequence: footsteps to the door, a pause for the security interval, the door opening.

Lee Nari was forty-three and looked it in the way that meant she had been paying attention to her years rather than managing them. Medium height, precise posture, the kind of stillness that was not tension but the long-term result of living carefully in a space where certain kinds of movement had consequences. She was wearing clothes she had slept in or expected to leave in — the category that covered both conditions without distinguishing between them.

She looked at Rook first, then Ekow. Something in her face shifted when she saw him. Not recognition in the street-recognition sense — something more specific than that, the particular attention of a person who has been carrying a thing for a long time and has just encountered someone else who carries the same kind of weight.

"Adaeze sent you," she said. Her voice was even.

"Yes," Rook said.

She stepped back from the door.

The apartment was exactly the size it needed to be and no larger. Z6 residential, single-room with a separate sleeping area, the dimensions that the buffer zone building codes produced at the lower rental tier. The furniture was functional and sufficient — a table, two chairs, a shelf unit running the west wall. On the shelf: books, physical ones, the Z6 reading economy that ran on secondhand print because not everyone in these zones had mesh access and physical books did not require registration or access fees. A small kitchen unit. One window, Z6-standard frosted security glass with the privacy film that was standard in the eastern sector.

And by the door, on the floor to the left of the entrance: a bag.

Single-strap carry, mid-size, the civilian equivalent of a field pack. It was not positioned the way a bag was positioned when someone had left it there on the way in. It was positioned the way a bag was positioned when someone had placed it there with care and knowledge and specific intent — the strap looped over the lower door handle, the bag's weight settled and ready. Everything about it said: this has been here for a while.

The bag was not packed for tonight. The bag had been packed for three years.

Rook looked at it for a moment and filed it and did not comment on it.

Nari had not looked at the bag. She had not needed to. She knew where it was — had always known, the way you knew the location of every object in a room you had maintained in a specific state for a long time.

"How long do we have," she said.

"We leave in twenty minutes," Rook said.

Nari nodded. She moved to the table and sat in the chair that faced the door. Not because she wanted to sit — from the way she sat, she had been sitting most of the day, the stillness of someone who had been waiting and had found the particular posture of waiting. The chair was oriented to the door. This had also been true for a while.

"You've read my summary," she said.

"Adaeze briefed us on the outline," Rook said. "You'll tell us the rest when you're clear."

Nari looked at her with the measured attention of a woman assessing whether "when you're clear" was a condition being offered or being prepared for her to accept. The assessment was brief and its conclusion was pragmatic. She had waited fifteen years. She was not going to argue about sequencing now.

"Yes," she said. "When I'm clear."

She looked at Ekow.

He was standing near the door, in the position he took in every room. He was looking at her with an expression Rook could not read precisely, which was unusual. She could normally read Ekow's expressions by function if not by content. This one had a quality she had not seen on him before — not quite recognition, not quite something arrived at, something in the space between.

"You know me," Nari said, to Ekow. Not a question.

"By record," he said. "Not in person."

She absorbed this without asking what the record said. Whatever the record contained, she was familiar with the kind of record it was — the kind maintained by organizations that maintained records on people they had cast out, the kind accessible to someone working in close proximity to those organizations. She knew what his presence here alongside Adaeze's referral meant. She did not require him to explain it.

"Adaeze trusts you," she said.

"I know," he said.

"That's enough," she said.

Rook gave herself a moment to read the room. The physical record of fifteen years in this apartment. The books on the shelf were organized in a pattern that said daily use rather than display — certain volumes more worn than others, the reading material of someone who had been in the same place long enough for the books to reflect seasons of attention. The window with its security film. The table with the slight lean from a floor's imperfection. A photograph on the shelf beside the books, small format, a man and a woman in a context that read as an ordinary occasion — not a celebration, something more everyday, a meal somewhere, the kind of photograph you kept because it was an ordinary Tuesday and the ordinary Tuesday had mattered.

She noted the photograph without examining it.

Nari had been following her gaze. She looked at the photograph, then away. "Emre," she said. "He died six years ago. It was ruled an accident."

She said this in the same register she had used for everything else she had said since they walked in — level, precise, carrying no additional weight in its delivery. The sentence sat in the room at the same altitude as the other sentences. Her face held the same expression it had held before she said it.

Rook heard it. She filed it. She did not respond beyond a nod of acknowledgment — not because she didn't hear what was under the flatness, but because Nari had delivered it without the weight and adding the weight back would have been a commentary on how Nari chose to carry what she carried, and that was not Rook's commentary to make.

"You have a minute to take anything from the room," Rook said. "The bag is ready."

"The bag has everything," Nari said. "I thought I'd be ready in ten minutes when I first packed it. I started earlier than that." She said this without self-consciousness, the matter-of-fact quality of someone reporting a practical fact about a decision they had made years ago and had not regretted. She stood from the chair.

Ekow said, "Rook."

She turned. He was looking at her with the expression she couldn't read.

"Give us a minute," he said.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she looked at Nari, who was looking at Ekow with an expression that was not quite recognition and not quite something else — the attention of a person who had been carrying a thing for a long time and had just recognized someone else who was carrying the same kind of thing, and was deciding whether that recognition was a reason to say something or a reason to say nothing, and was arriving at the second answer.

Rook stepped out.

She stood in the corridor, which was Z6 residential in every dimension — narrow, the utility access panels on the east wall, the corridor lighting at half-power, the specific smell of a building at twenty-two hundred when most of the residents were asleep. Scout was at the stairwell end, watching the building entry floor. Boiler was one level down. Lace was on the street level with the egress route mapped.

The door was closed.

Rook stood in the corridor and did not listen. This was not discipline — she could not have heard through a door at this quality of construction. This was the closed door given as a gift, because Ekow had asked for it and had not needed to explain why he was asking, and she had understood without needing the explanation. Nari had fifteen years of something she had been carrying in this apartment. Ekow had twelve years of something he had been carrying somewhere else. Whatever was in the room between them was not for the crew.

She found herself looking at the utility access panels on the east wall. Standard Z6 maintenance architecture, the panel housings in the original installation ferrocement, the conduit labels faded but legible. Infrastructure that had been doing the same job for forty years without announcement. Old construction. You could feel the density of it if you pressed a palm to the wall — the specific resistance of material that had been in place long enough to have settled into its load entirely.

She pressed a palm to the wall.

The door opened at six minutes. Nari came out first — the bag over her shoulder, strap adjusted, weight settled into the carry position. The movement was practiced, automatic: she had tried the bag on enough times that the adjustment required no thought. It was ready. She had been ready.

Ekow came out behind her.

His face was the face she recognized.

She did not ask what had been said. She would not ask. The closed door was what it was and she had given it, and whatever the room contained stayed in the room. She was aware that something had shifted in the set of him — not changed, exactly, but shifted, the way a load shifts when it is redistributed rather than removed. Still carrying. Carrying differently.

"We move," she said.

She keyed the team channel: "We have the package. Heading for the primary extraction route. Scout, confirm the east crawlway access."

"Confirmed," Scout said. "Entry sequence loaded. The maintenance access is clear."

"Boiler."

"Ready," he said, from below.

She looked at Nari. The bag on her shoulder, three years of readiness made portable. She had been packed before the crew had taken the job. She had been ready before the twelve-hour window had been named. She had been ready, probably, since the day she had filed the Protocol document in the category of when I die I will have done nothing and had looked at that file and decided she was not done yet.

"This way," Rook said.

Nari followed without asking. She had not asked once. She had not asked when the crew arrived, not when Rook named the timeline, not when Ekow had asked for the closed door. She was a person who had been waiting for this moment for fifteen years and had packed her bag three years ago and had known, at some level below the knowledge she could name, exactly how it would begin.

Her Z6 address registration was under a different name. Jin Mirae. A cover she had held for three years, the civilian infrastructure of a departure she had been planning as long as she had been packed.

The corridor was quiet around them. The building asleep. The bag's strap settled on Nari's shoulder, the weight distributed the way she had learned to carry it over years of practicing departure in the same room.

They moved toward the stairwell.

Behind them, the photograph remained on the shelf — a man and a woman at a table, an ordinary Tuesday, the kind that mattered. The door to the apartment was closed. The room was as it had been, except for the absence of the bag by the door. That absence, in an apartment that had been maintained in a specific state for three years, was the most visible thing in it.
