Chapter 3 - The Cromwellian Conquest and the Tory Insurgency, 1649-c.1660s 50.0 Between 1642 and 1649, two-thirds of Ireland was governed not by the English Crown but by the Confederation of Kilkenny. The Confederation formed in the aftermath of the Rebellion of 1641, in which Catholic Irish forces had killed a significant number of Protestant settlers in Ulster, an event that hardened both sides for the decade that followed. Out of that rebellion, Irish Catholic nobles, clergy, and military leaders built something more durable than an uprising: a functioning government. It had a parliament, the General Assembly; an executive, the Supreme Council; and its own army in the field. Historians describe it as the only example of sustained self-government by the Catholic Irish on a national level before 1919. For seven years it collected taxes, issued laws, negotiated with foreign courts, and fielded soldiers across most of the island. It was a state, of a kind, ruling most of Ireland, answering to no crown. Its officers held ranks, its assemblies kept minutes, its diplomats corresponded with Rome and Madrid. None of that changed what it was fighting, in the end, against: an English Parliament that had just executed its own king and now turned its army, flush with victory in the civil wars at home, toward the reconquest of Ireland. The Confederation's seven years of government were the context into which Oliver Cromwell's army arrived in August 1649 - not a stateless population to be pacified, but an organized polity to be destroyed. [19] Everything that followed after 1649, including Edmund O'Dwyer's outlawed raiders in the Glen of Aherlow, was fought by people who had briefly had exactly what every figure in this book was ultimately after - a country free to govern its own ground - and would not simply accept that it was gone.

"Tory" - t¢ra¡, "pursuer, outlaw" - was already in use by around 1646 as a slur for dispossessed Irish Catholics turned bandit, the exact men described in this chapter's raids and bounty warrants. By 1680, during the Exclusion Crisis in England over whether a Catholic king could inherit the throne, the same word was thrown at English politicians who opposed barring him from succession - the insult likening them to predatory Irish rebels. Within a decade, the men being insulted had kept the name. "Tory" became the name of an English political party after 1689, and although "Conservative" formally superseded it around 1830, the older word never went away. It is still, today, the everyday name for the party that governs Britain. The state that cut passes through the Glen of Aherlow and priced Edmund O'Dwyer's head did not only remember how to fight men like him. It took their name for itself, and kept it. [20]

O'Dwyer and the men who rode with him wanted nothing the Confederation itself hadn't already tried to secure by treaty and parliament: the right to live free on their own ground. The word survived their defeat by three and a half centuries; the freedom it was coined to deny took nearly three centuries more to come back around.
