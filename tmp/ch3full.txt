50.0 Between 1642 and 1649, two-thirds of Ireland was governed not by the English Crown but by the Confederation of Kilkenny. The Confederation formed in the aftermath of the Rebellion of 1641, in which Catholic Irish forces had killed a significant number of Protestant settlers in Ulster, an event that hardened both sides for the decade that followed. Out of that rebellion, Irish Catholic nobles, clergy, and military leaders built something more durable than an uprising: a functioning government. It had a parliament, the General Assembly; an executive, the Supreme Council; and its own army in the field. Historians describe it as the only example of sustained self-government by the Catholic Irish on a national level before 1919. For seven years it collected taxes, issued laws, negotiated with foreign courts, and fielded soldiers across most of the island. It was a state, of a kind, ruling most of Ireland, answering to no crown. Its officers held ranks, its assemblies kept minutes, its diplomats corresponded with Rome and Madrid. None of that changed what it was fighting, in the end, against: an English Parliament that had just executed its own king and now turned its army, flush with victory in the civil wars at home, toward the reconquest of Ireland. The Confederation's seven years of government were the context into which Oliver Cromwell's army arrived in August 1649 - not a stateless population to be pacified, but an organized polity to be destroyed. [19] Everything that followed after 1649, including Edmund O'Dwyer's outlawed raiders in the Glen of Aherlow, was fought by people who had briefly had exactly what every figure in this book was ultimately after - a country free to govern its own ground - and would not simply accept that it was gone.
100.0 On 2 August 1649, before Cromwell had set foot in Ireland, the Parliamentarian commander Michael Jones broke a Royalist-Confederate siege of Dublin at the Battle of Rathmines, routing the besieging army in a single engagement. Cromwell landed roughly two weeks later, arriving to a war in which the decisive field battle had already been lost by the side he had come to finish off. He did not need to win Ireland in open battle; Jones had already done the work that mattered most. What remained was siege warfare, and Cromwell prosecuted it without pause. He took Drogheda by storm on 11 September 1649. The garrison was killed after the walls fell, a large proportion of the defenders put to the sword once resistance had collapsed. He took Wexford by storm on 11 October, and there the killing extended beyond the garrison to many of the townspeople as well. Both actions were justified at the time, and defended since, as customary siege law applied to towns that had refused terms - a legal argument that did nothing to reduce the number of dead inside either town's walls. The severity of Drogheda and Wexford did not end resistance in Ireland; it fed it. For the tory bands who would spend the following decade in the woods and bogs of Munster and Ulster, the memory of what had happened at those two towns was not abstract history. It was the reason given, again and again, for continuing to fight after the war itself was already lost. [9]
150.0 The Confederate Ulster army's ablest commander, Owen Roe O'Neill, died of illness in November 1649, weeks after Cromwell's landing, at the worst possible moment for the army he led. Command passed to Heber MacMahon, the Catholic Bishop of Clogher - a choice his own veteran officers argued against at the time, since MacMahon had no military experience comparable to the men he was now commanding. The argument proved correct. MacMahon led the Ulster army to destruction at the Battle of Scarrifhollis, on the River Swilly near Letterkenny, on 21 June 1650. He was captured shortly afterward and executed. The defeat broke the last coherent Confederate field army in the north. Kilkenny, the Confederation's capital and the seat of its General Assembly and Supreme Council, fell the following year. By 1652 both Catholic Confederate and Royalist resistance in Ireland had been crushed entirely. The transition was abrupt and total. A government that had run two-thirds of the island for seven years - with its own parliament, its own executive, its own army, its own diplomacy - simply ended. It did not evolve into something smaller, and it did not hand off its structures to whatever came next. What came after started from nothing: no parliament, no council, no treasury, no chain of command reaching above a single band in a single glen. The insurgency that followed inherited the Confederation's grievances and its dead. It inherited none of its institutions. [9][19]
200.0 What followed was the tory insurgency: scattered bands with no political structure at all, surviving on raiding and terrain alone. Edmund O'Dwyer, a minor Confederate officer before 1652, became a tory leader operating out of the Glen of Aherlow in County Tipperary once the formal war had ended, holding that ground with a remnant force that answered to no one above him. He and men like him survived by raiding convoys and cattle in the surrounding countryside, not to hold territory - they had none left to hold - but to keep themselves armed and fed once every other source of supply had dried up. A stolen herd of cattle or an intercepted convoy was not plunder in the ordinary sense; it was the difference between a band staying in the field through the winter or dissolving. Robert Dunlop's collected calendar of Commonwealth-era correspondence and court proceedings documents the state's side of exactly this problem in granular, bureaucratic detail: bounty warrants issued against named men, complaints from settlers about stolen cattle, and the steady, low-grade attrition of an occupying administration that could raise armies, levy fines, and garrison towns, but could never quite locate the men doing the raiding. The insurgency left almost no record of its own making. What survives of it survives because the government that hunted it kept careful books. [1][9]
250.0 The tories fought along wood-lined causeways and bog paths in the Glen of Aherlow that a mounted, conventionally equipped Cromwellian force struggled to move through at any speed. The government's answer, inherited from Elizabethan-era practice against earlier Irish resistance, was to cut broad passes through the woodland itself, removing the terrain advantage by removing the terrain. Charles Fleetwood, who succeeded Ireton as Commander-in-Chief in 1652 and oversaw the Act for the Settlement and the Connacht transplantation, went further, fining entire baronies where an attack had occurred, on the reasoning that a population living alongside active tories either knew who they were or was complicit through silence. The same records that document those fines also document the insurgency continuing for years afterward, unbroken by either the woodcutting or the collective punishment. Alongside both, the state relied on the bounty warrant: paperwork that named a man, priced him, and invited anyone at all - neighbor, cousin, stranger - to collect, with no trial and no soldier's status attached to him. This "discoverer" system paid for information rather than placing agents inside the tory bands, a far less reliable method than infiltration, but it is also, notably, the richest surviving source for the whole insurgency - these men are known largely because someone was being paid to catch them. Whatever tactical knowledge passed between one band and another passed by word of mouth alone, with no press, no organizer, and no way to preserve it once the people who held it died or were taken. Edmund O'Dwyer eventually chose exile over surrender to summary justice, taking his remaining men into French military service. He was killed in battle in France in 1654 - a negotiated way out of Ireland that still ended in death. [1][9]
300.0 **Then and Now**

"Tory" - t¢ra¡, "pursuer, outlaw" - was already in use by around 1646 as a slur for dispossessed Irish Catholics turned bandit, the exact men described in this chapter's raids and bounty warrants. By 1680, during the Exclusion Crisis in England over whether a Catholic king could inherit the throne, the same word was thrown at English politicians who opposed barring him from succession - the insult likening them to predatory Irish rebels. Within a decade, the men being insulted had kept the name. "Tory" became the name of an English political party after 1689, and although "Conservative" formally superseded it around 1830, the older word never went away. It is still, today, the everyday name for the party that governs Britain. The state that cut passes through the Glen of Aherlow and priced Edmund O'Dwyer's head did not only remember how to fight men like him. It took their name for itself, and kept it. [20]

O'Dwyer and the men who rode with him wanted nothing the Confederation itself hadn't already tried to secure by treaty and parliament: the right to live free on their own ground. The word survived their defeat by three and a half centuries; the freedom it was coined to deny took nearly three centuries more to come back around.
