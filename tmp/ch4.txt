Chapter 4 - The Williamite War and the Rapparees, 1689-91 50.0 In 1689 James II, the Catholic king deposed in England the previous year, landed in Ireland to fight for the restoration of his three kingdoms against his Protestant son-in-law, William of Orange. His Jacobite army moved north and settled into a siege of Derry, whose Protestant townspeople and refugees had shut the gates against a garrison they judged a threat and refused every summons to surrender through that spring and summer. The city held for roughly fifteen weeks, on short rations, until relief ships broke the boom laid across the Foyle to starve it out. The failed siege let William land an army of his own on Irish soil the following year. In July 1690, William's forces met and defeated the main Jacobite army at the Battle of the Boyne, on the river north of Drogheda. James did not remain in Ireland to contest the outcome further. He left for France in the defeat's immediate aftermath, and did not return. [30] Patrick Sarsfield, the war's central Irish commander, fought on after his king had already given up - for the last real chance to keep an Irish-held, Catholic-landed Ireland free of exactly the settlement that followed his eventual defeat.

Patrick Sarsfield left more documentary trail than almost any other figure of the Williamite War - treaty signatures, military commissions, a described wound and a named town of death - and still no grave was found for him for centuries; what marks his end today is a plaque on a church wall in Huy, standing in for a burial record that was never recovered. êamonn an Chnoic left nearly the opposite kind of trace. Most of what is said about his life rests on tradition rather than record, yet the lament attributed to him has survived in something close to continuous performance for roughly three centuries, and can still be sung today by people who could not otherwise date the Treaty of Limerick or name the county where he was born.

Between the two men sits the shape of what a culture actually keeps. A name and a tune outlast a body far more reliably than a grave outlasts a peace treaty, and the record that survives best is not always the one that was written down. Neither man got to keep the ground he fought for free; what survived instead - a plaque, a song - is what a country keeps when it loses the fight but not the wanting.
