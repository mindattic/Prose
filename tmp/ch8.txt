Chapter 8 - The Land War, 1879-82 50.0 Michael Davitt was born in Straide, County Mayo, in 1846, during the Great Famine, and before he was five years old his family was evicted from the land they held. They crossed to Haslingden, in Lancashire, in search of work, and there, at the age of nine, Davitt went into a cotton mill. He told the mill he was older than he was - over thirteen - so that he could be taken on, and he worked sixty hours a week. In May 1857, when he was eleven, his right arm was caught in the mill machinery and had to be amputated. The injury ended his work on the mill floor. He was afterward educated at a Wesleyan school in Haslingden, his schooling paid for by a local manufacturer, John Dean, who took an interest in the maimed boy and covered the cost of his education. He carried the missing arm for the rest of his life. [22] The eviction that started that whole chain, before he could even remember it, taught Davitt early what every other figure in this book eventually learned by harder means: a family without a secure claim on its own ground is at the mercy of whoever holds the lease, and the only real security is winning that ground back and holding it free.
Chapter 8 - The Land War, 1879-82 200.0 **Then and Now**

The word "boycott" is the concrete artifact this round of Irish history produced: coined from a single land agent's name in the autumn of 1880, it entered English within the year and passed into French, German, and most other European languages soon after, becoming the standing term for organized, public, non-violent economic ostracism directed at an identified target. No other word has replaced it.

The method survived the word. Coordinated, public, non-violent economic pressure applied to a named target - a company, a retailer, a bus line, a regime - remains a standard instrument of labor unions, consumer campaigns, and civil-rights movements in the twenty-first century, on every continent where organized dissent is permitted. It is the same tactic the tenants of County Mayo proved against Charles Boycott, built on the same premise: that a group which cannot win by force can still win by simply, and together, declining to do business. The word outlived the war it was coined in because it did what force in this book's other seven rounds so often couldn't: it let ordinary people make their own ground ungovernable to anyone but themselves, without a single shot fired.
