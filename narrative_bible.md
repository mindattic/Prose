## PART 3: NARRATIVE STRUCTURE

### The Bureaucratic Narrative

The story is told through documents. Not a single narrator's voice — a system of overlapping, contradictory, self-serving, and occasionally truthful records.

**Document types that constitute the narrative:**

- **Operator debriefs.** Kael's post-job reports, filed with his fixer. Terse, clinical, omitting anything that makes him look uncertain. What's NOT in the debrief is more important than what is.

- **Corporate memos.** Internal communications within corponations. The language is euphemistic, optimized, and reveals the institution's priorities through what it chooses to measure. A Tessera productivity report tells you more about the world than any character monologue.

- **Tribunal transcripts.** Corponation internal adjudication proceedings. Dry, procedural, devastating. The Tallow precedent (see `worldbuilding/case_ringo_v_tallow.md`) is the template: the horror is bureaucratic.

- **Surveillance logs.** Time-stamped records from corpo monitoring systems. Flat, observational, occasionally noting anomalies that the system flags but doesn't understand. What the camera sees is not the same as what happened.

- **Medical records.** Clinical documentation from corpo hospitals, black clinics, and ~~VCP facility intake logs~~ **[REDACTED — document access restricted per NeoCortex Industries v. Public Interest, docket NC-2097-4412]**

- **Personal communications.** Intercepted messages, journal entries, analog letters. The only documents in the narrative where someone is trying to tell the truth rather than file a report. Also the least reliable, because people lie to themselves more than they lie to systems.

- **Rogue AI artifacts.** Data patterns, network anomalies, and ~~authored documents~~ generated content from non-human intelligences. These may appear in the narrative without warning. They may appear AS other document types (a corporate memo that was actually generated by a Prowler-class rogue). The reader may not be able to tell. The filing system may not be able to tell. This is accurate to the world.

- **Corrections and retractions.** When the narrative contradicts itself — because sources disagree, because a filing was fraudulent, because the world changed between one document and the next — the contradiction is preserved. ~~The first version~~ The first version is struck through. The corrected version follows. The record shows the scar.

### Scene Transitions

Scenes do not transition through narrative logic ("meanwhile, across town..."). Scenes transition through FILING LOGIC:

- A cross-reference in Kael's debrief mentions a clinic. The filing system opens the clinic's records. The clinic's records mention a patient. The patient's file is opened. The patient is a Kessler-Dyne indenture worker whose employment contract is referenced in a corporate memo. The memo mentions a construction accident. The accident report cross-references a safety inspector. The safety inspector is a Torii Group contractor whose file reveals she's moonlighting as a fixer in the shadow economy. Her fixer contacts include Kael.

The circle closes. Or it doesn't. Sometimes the filing system follows a thread that leads nowhere relevant. That's accurate too. Not every document matters. The reader has to find the ones that do.

This is Discovery Hell by design.

### Error Handling

When the narrative makes an error — a character's name is wrong, a timeline doesn't match, a fact contradicts a prior filing — the error is handled bureaucratically:

~~Kael arrived at the clinic at 2240 local time.~~

**CORRECTION (filed 2100-Q2-14):** Per surveillance log GL-MKE-4400-CAM-7, subject arrived at 2237, not 2240. Prior filing based on operator debrief, which rounded. Debrief timestamp retained for record integrity. Surveillance timestamp now primary.

~~The clinic was operated by Helix BioSystems.~~

**RETRACTION (filed 2100-Q2-16):** The clinic's Helix BioSystems signage was a licensing agreement, not operational control. Actual operator: Kindred Medical Collective, an independent entity operating under Helix trademark license in the Gary-Hammond ungoverned zone. Helix BioSystems has filed a formal objection to being associated with the clinic's activities. Objection noted. Helix signage was on the building. The filing stands with annotation.

This is not a gimmick. This is how the world works. Every document in 2100 is contested. Every record is incomplete. Every correction reveals something about who wanted the original version on file and who wanted it changed.

## PART 5: NARRATIVE RULES

### What the Story Is About

See `worldbuilding/population_farming_machine_of_machines.md`, Section 3: "The Machine of Machines."

The story is about what it means to be a component in a system that is itself a component in a larger system, all the way up and all the way down. The corponation is a machine. The economy is a machine. The body is a machine. The mind is a machine running firmware that might not be its own. Is there an outside? Is there a gear that is not a gear? Or is it machines all the way down?

Kael's question — "Am I alone in here?" — is the personal version. The universal version is: "Is anyone?"

### Rules of Engagement

1. **No omniscient narration.** Every piece of information in the narrative comes from a source. The source is cited. The source may be wrong.

2. **No clean transitions.** Scenes shift through filing logic, not cinematic logic. The reader follows the paperwork.

3. **No silent corrections.** Errors are retracted, not fixed. The scar tissue of the narrative is part of the narrative.

4. **No supporting characters.** Every entity has a complete life. The narrative accesses it when the filing system requires. The entity does not exist to serve the protagonist's arc.

5. **Every facet speaks.** All six facets (WOUND, IDEAL, ID, SHADOW, MASK, GHOST) are active lenses for every sentient entity. Actions by one entity create modifiers that ripple outward to every entity they interact with. Kael's choice in the clinic creates a modifier on the teenager, the clinic patients, the Tessera procurement officer, and anyone whose file cross-references any of them.

6. **Cite your sources.** Cross-reference worldbuilding documents. The reader may drown. The truth is in the filing system somewhere. Finding it is the reader's job. Or perhaps: the realization that the filing system is too large to fully comprehend IS the truth.

7. **The machine of machines.** Every scene, every document, every character is simultaneously a story and a component in a larger story that is itself a component in a larger story. The narrative bible is a machine. The writer system is a machine. The worldbuilding archive is a machine. The reader is a machine. ~~This is a metaphor.~~ **RETRACTION:** This is not a metaphor.
