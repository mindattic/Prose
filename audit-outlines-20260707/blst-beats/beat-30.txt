The morning Ashgrave's engineers came aboard to take the coherence drivers, Teo was already at the core, because somebody from Candelaria needed to be standing there when the frame's control passed out of the co-op's hands for the first time in sixty years, and she'd decided months ago, without anyone asking her to decide it, that it would be her.

They were three technicians and a lead, all in Ashgrave grey, and they worked the way people work who've done a thing many times before and expect to do it many times again — checked her own last readings against their instruments without comment, confirmed the trim limits Almagre had been enforcing all along, and told her, when she asked, that the descent would run in stepped increments, the frame easing down in controlled decrements over the day rather than all at once, so that nothing aboard ever felt a drop it hadn't already felt a hundred times on an ordinary bad week.

The wall outside the core read three hundred twenty-nine meters when they started. Teo watched the first increment take it under that without any of the sound she'd half expected — no alarm, no shudder through the deck plate, only the Hum easing rather than rising, the frame settling into a shallower hold the way a hand settles when it finally sets down something it's been carrying too long. Three hundred twenty-four. Three hundred twenty. The number that had governed five meetings, one vote, and a year of Monday postings slid past without any of them in the room finding a single word to mark it with.

She left the core an hour in, once the lead confirmed the first stretch of the descent was running clean, and walked the spine the way she walked it every day, except today the spine was moving under her in a way it had never moved before, a slow, deliberate settling she felt mostly in her knees, the whole structure lowering itself toward a ground it hadn't touched since before it was built.

The households were mostly done packing by then. She passed doors standing open onto rooms half-emptied, crates stacked in corridors waiting for the ground-tier transports Ashgrave had staged below, residents she'd spent a year weighing in the abstract now hauling their own actual mass toward lifts, some of them nodding to her as she passed, most too occupied to notice her at all.

Wen's workshop stood open when she reached it, the lathes tagged and silent, a strip-crew advance team already working the bolts on the near one while Wen stood off to the side, arms folded, watching them do it without offering a hand either way. He caught Teo's eye for a moment as she passed the doorway and neither of them said anything.

Near the commons, she found Priya at the message board that had replaced the wrist display as the fastest way word moved through Candelaria that week, reading a confirmation notice from the clinic — the corrective procedure booked, the provisioning window closing on schedule instead of past it, the fund finally, plainly, closed. Priya wasn't crying. She was standing very still, the way people stood when a number they'd carried for months finally stopped needing to be carried, and Teo didn't stop to say anything to her, because the notice on the board said everything that needed saying and said it better than either of them could have.

The wall at the core read three hundred and four meters when Teo came back to it. Two hundred eighty. The increments were coming faster now, the technicians working through their sequence with the same unhurried competence they'd started with, Almagre's own governance folded quietly into theirs, enforcing the same trim limits it always had, taking no more notice of the day than it took of any other Tuesday.

She pinned the last schedule she would ever post on that wall a little after noon — not an altitude projection this time, nothing left to project, just the plain fact of the day's remaining increments and the hour the ground crews expected final contact — and stood back to look at it the way she'd stood back from every posting she'd ever made, forty-one households' worth of Mondays behind her.

By late afternoon the wall read under a hundred meters, and residents had started gathering along the outer ring the way they gathered for nothing else all year, watching through glass that had shown them the lake and the Gray Zone and the Spires for sixty years and would show them, within the hour, the ground itself close enough to name individual rooftops.

The frame's Hum had gone quieter with every increment since morning, not louder, exactly the way a healthy descent should sound, and as the last stretch began — the drivers stepping down together in the final sequence, the deck settling under Teo's feet with a give she felt all the way up through her knees — the Hum thinned past hearing entirely, the Hush arriving under everyone at once, the frame no longer needing to hold anything at all, and Candelaria touched down.
