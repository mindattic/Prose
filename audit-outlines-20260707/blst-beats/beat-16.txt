Kaja was at school, which meant the kitchen belonged to Priya alone for the two hours it took the descent shuttle to run its downside loop and come back, and she had learned, over the last month, to use those hours for the parts of this she didn't want her daughter watching her do.

The wrist display sat face-up on the table where she'd set it down an hour ago and hadn't touched since. Φ8,400. It hadn't moved. It wasn't going to move on its own, not while she sat there looking at it, not while she got up and rinsed the two cups from breakfast and set them in the rack the way she did every morning whether the number behind her had changed or not.

She'd learned the shape of the number by now the way she'd learned the shape of the flat's other fixed measurements — the width of the hallway, the height of the ceiling in Kaja's room where she'd once, years ago, marked her daughter's growth in pencil before growth stopped being the kind of thing you measured in inches. Φ8,400 didn't grow. It didn't shrink either, not from anything Priya did inside these walls. It sat there the way the ceiling sat there, load-bearing in its own quiet way, present in every room of her life whether she looked at it or not.

She dried the cups. She wiped the counter down, though it hadn't needed it. She thought, not for the first time, that there was a kind of housework that existed only to give her hands something honest to do while her mind sat somewhere else entirely, running the same subtraction it had run every day for three months: eight thousand four hundred, minus whatever the co-op's jettison quota gave her, minus whatever Ashgrave's payout might give her if the vote went the way she needed it to, minus the part of the number that never closed no matter how she arranged the other two.

The clinic's provisioning window for Kaja's age bracket didn't extend forever. She knew the exact month it closed, the same way Teo knew the exact rate the frame was losing coherence, and she'd stopped telling people the month out loud months ago, because saying it made the room go quiet in a way that didn't help either of them, and because she'd noticed, the first two times she'd said it, that the quiet that followed was for her, not for Kaja, and she didn't want anyone's sympathy standing between her daughter and a number that had nothing to do with sympathy.

She picked the display up, finally, and didn't touch the number itself — didn't open the ledger behind it, didn't run the subtraction one more time on the actual screen instead of in her head, because she'd learned that running it on the screen never once returned a different answer than running it in her head, and doing it in her head cost her less. She turned the display face-down instead and set it back on the table, and the gesture, small as it was, felt like the only kind of control she still had over the number: not changing it, just choosing, for an hour, not to let it look back at her.

Kaja's room was down the hall, door open the way Priya kept it even when her daughter wasn't in it, and she stood in the doorway a moment, looking at the bed made the way a twelve-year-old makes a bed when nobody's checking, the cochlear fitting kit still sitting on the shelf where the clinic had left it after the last provisional adjustment, cheap-bracket components doing their best against a growth window that was closing faster than the components could keep pace with. Kaja didn't complain about the sound the way Priya expected a child to complain about anything wrong with her own hearing. She'd learned to describe it instead, precisely, the way she described everything — a kind of doubling on certain frequencies, a lag on consonants that made her own name sound, to her, like it belonged to someone standing just behind her. Priya had asked her once whether it frightened her. Kaja had thought about it and said no, it just made her tired, the way walking uphill made your legs tired even when the hill wasn't steep, and Priya had not slept well for three nights after hearing her daughter describe her own hearing that way, calmly, like a fact about weather.

She went back to the kitchen. The display sat face-down where she'd left it, Φ8,400 turned toward the table instead of toward her, and she understood, standing there with the cups dried and put away and nothing left in the small flat that needed her hands, that she had built almost the whole of the last three months out of exactly this kind of waiting — not idle, not passive, just adjacent to a number she couldn't move by staring at it, doing the only thing available to a person in her position, which was staying upright and present and unbroken until the day arrived when someone else's vote decided whether the number closed.

She didn't turn the display back over before Kaja came home. There would be time enough for the number to look at her again tomorrow. Tonight she wanted the kitchen to be only a kitchen, and for an hour, before the shuttle brought her daughter back up the spine, she let it be exactly that, and nothing more, and nothing less.

She thought, standing at the counter with nothing left to wipe down, about the two men who'd made their positions known to the whole bloc without once flinching from doing it in public — Wen with his jigs laid out on a table, Ruslan with his six weeks stated plainly to Sigrun's face — and understood that her own position had been just as public since the first meeting, her number posted the same as anyone's, and that the difference between her and them wasn't courage. It was only that neither of them had a child on the other side of their number, and she had learned, these last months, that a child on the other side of a number changed nothing about the arithmetic and everything about how long a person could sit alone in a kitchen with it before needing to turn it face-down.

The shuttle's arrival chime sounded two decks up, faint through the flat's walls, and she straightened before it finished, already turning toward the door, already rehearsing the ordinary face she kept ready for the twenty minutes after school — not the face that had spent the last hour with Φ8,400 turned toward a table, but the one that asked about the day, about school, about anything at all that wasn't a number neither of them could do anything about tonight.
