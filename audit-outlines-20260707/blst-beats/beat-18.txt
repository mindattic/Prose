Teo reopened the ledger the night after the spine walk, the same file she'd left half-built weeks ago, DESCEND on one side and STAY on the other, and this time she didn't close it before she reached Priya's household, because Ruslan had put a number on the far side of STAY that the ledger hadn't had before — six weeks, a coherence curve with a name attached to its ending instead of just a rate.

She rebuilt the STAY column first, because it was the shorter one now. If the vote failed, the offer lapsed the way Ruslan had described it: not decayed, not renegotiated, simply no longer attached to anything that could still be sold, because by the time six weeks had run out on her own curve, Candelaria would have crossed three hundred twenty meters on schedule regardless of any vote, and a bloc on the wrong side of that line wasn't a bloc AshgraveMaterials or anyone else was still bidding on. Under STAY, Priya's household kept its existing jettison allocation — the goods already slated to leave under the ongoing ballast program, valued, posted, unchanged by anything Ruslan had said — and nothing else. Φ2,100. The same figure it had always been. No structural payout, because there'd be no structural sale. Only, on the far end of the column, the cost of an emergency relocation that wouldn't wait for anyone's convenience and wouldn't come with a check attached, a cost Teo had seen quoted on other blocs' final ledgers and had never once seen come in lower than what an orderly move would have cost the same household under DESCEND.

Under DESCEND, the shape was different, and harder to finish, because the honest version of it still had a hole in it that Teo had been avoiding for weeks. The bloc's proceeds — eight hundred forty-seven thousand, unmoving, the number Ruslan had reconfirmed on the gantry — split forty-one ways by the same proportional formula that had set every household's jettison quota in the first place. A household with a small allocation drew a smaller share of that split than a household with a large one; that was the formula, and Teo had built it herself, months before any of this, and had no grounds to distrust it now just because she didn't love where it currently pointed. Priya's allocation was modest. Her structural share, on the formula alone, wouldn't land at the flat average painted across every rough estimate anyone had thrown around in the commons — twenty thousand six hundred fifty, evenly split, was a number that assumed every household weighed the same, and no two households in Candelaria weighed the same, which was the entire reason Teo had a job.

She wrote both figures down anyway, side by side, because incomplete honesty was still worse than complete uncertainty: Φ2,100, guaranteed, hers already, whichever way the vote went, so long as the frame held long enough for the existing jettison program to run its course. And a second figure, DESCEND-only, dependent on a formula whose largest single input Teo still hadn't run.

Dagny's allocation sat at the bottom of every version of this ledger the way a keystone sits at the top of an arch — not because Teo had decided to weight it that way, but because the formula did that on its own, the moment a household's true mass got entered honestly instead of estimated. She'd been putting off running it since the night she'd first admitted, to no one but herself, that she suspected it would move something. Six weeks changed the deadline for admitting that. It didn't change the number, whatever the number turned out to be, and Teo had learned, doing this work as long as she had, that a deadline never made an unrun figure any less true. It only made the not-running of it a choice instead of an oversight.

She sat with the two unfinished columns a long time, the cursor resting over the one household she still hadn't entered, and thought about what Priya had said at the eigenlift core weeks ago — that the numbers here didn't pretend to be anything but numbers — and understood that she was, at this exact moment, the only person in Candelaria capable of making that true or false. She could run Dagny's allocation tonight and know, before anyone else in the bloc did, whether Priya's shortfall closed under DESCEND or merely narrowed. She could also, technically, not run it tonight, and let the Monday posting arrive with the figure already resolved instead of watched arriving.

She didn't choose the second option because it was easier. She chose the first because Ruslan's six weeks had made the ledger's honesty a matter of timing now, not just accuracy, and a schedule that only told the truth eventually wasn't, in her experience, much different from a schedule that lied.

She thought about the two columns the way she thought about trim on a bad night — not as an argument to be won, but as a load that had to go somewhere, whichever way the frame finally settled. STAY cost Priya nothing extra beyond what she already stood to lose to the jettison program, and it cost her everything else besides, because a bloc that crossed three hundred twenty meters without a buyer attached didn't hand anyone a check on the way down. DESCEND cost the bloc its structure and its address both, permanently, and paid for the privilege of choosing the manner of that loss instead of having it chosen by a coherence curve with no negotiating position at all. Neither column was kind. Teo had stopped expecting either of her columns to be kind months ago; she only asked them, now, to be accurate, and accuracy was the one thing she still trusted herself to deliver on a night when nothing else about the ledger felt settled.

She saved the file without finishing it, DESCEND still short one figure, STAY complete and unmoved and exactly as cold as it had always been, and went to bed telling herself she'd run Dagny's household in the morning, knowing, the way she always knew when she made herself that particular promise, that morning was simply tonight's honest deadline wearing a kinder name.
