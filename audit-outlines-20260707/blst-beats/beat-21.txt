The common space filled the way it always filled now, without anyone needing to be told twice, and Teo took the same stretch of wall she'd claimed at every meeting since the first one, close enough to hear everything, far enough that nobody mistook her for anything other than what she was. The wall outside read three hundred sixty-two meters. Nobody had needed to point that out either.

Sigrun didn't open with the number. She'd learned, across five meetings now, that the number could wait a minute longer than anyone's patience ever wanted it to.

"This is the last time I call this room together before the vote," she said. "I want to say that plainly, because I think some of you have started treating these meetings like they'll go on until somebody gets tired of holding them. They won't. There's one more piece of business tonight, and then there's the vote, and nothing between now and then that changes anything either of those two things say."

She looked down at her wrist display, though Teo suspected, same as always, that she didn't need to.

"The vote will happen three days from tonight," Sigrun said. "Same room, same hour, one ballot each, forty-one households. I'm setting the day now so nobody can say afterward that they didn't have time to sit with it. Three days is what we have left before I think holding it any longer stops being deliberation and starts being delay, and I'd rather call it what it is than let it drift."

Nobody spoke. Teo watched the room take the date the way it had taken every other hard fact these last weeks — not with relief, not with dread exactly, just the particular stillness of a bloc absorbing one more fixed point on a schedule that had been full of them for months.

"Before we get to that," Sigrun went on, "I'm going to read the offer again. All of it, word for word, the same as I read it the first night, because I don't want anyone walking into that vote three days from now working from memory of what was said instead of what's actually on the page. Some of you have read it yourselves a dozen times since. Some of you haven't looked at it since the first meeting. I want everyone in this room to hear the same version tonight, together, so there's no argument afterward about who heard what."

She read it the way she'd read Dagny's message weeks before — flat, unhurried, no emphasis added anywhere it hadn't already been written, the way a person reads something they want understood exactly as written and not as performed. AshgraveMaterials would purchase the bloc's structural mass for eight hundred forty-seven thousand QUANTA, fund a relocation to ground-tier residential housing for all forty-one households inside a thirty-day window, and distribute the proceeds by the co-op's standing allocation formula. The offer did not expire on its own terms and would not be renegotiated upward or downward regardless of how long the co-op took to decide. It would, however, cease to be available the moment the structure ceased to be airworthy enough to sell — a fact Sigrun read in her own words, at the end, because it wasn't in Ashgrave's letter but belonged, she said, in any honest reading of it. Six weeks. The frame's own curve, not Ashgrave's clock. She didn't dress that part up either.

She finished, and folded the display face-down against her palm, and looked out at the room the way she'd looked out at every room since the first meeting, waiting.

"Does anyone have a question about anything I just read?" she asked. "Not about how you're going to vote. Not about anyone else's number. About the offer itself, plainly, the way it's written. This is the last chance to ask before you're voting instead of asking."

Nobody raised a hand. Nobody spoke. Teo watched the silence hold the way it had held at the end of Dagny's message weeks ago, and understood it the same way this time — not agreement, not resignation, just forty-one households who had, by now, heard every fact this offer contained often enough that a question would have meant someone hadn't been listening, and everyone in that room had been listening for weeks.

"Then I'll take the silence as understanding, not as agreement," Sigrun said, "because those aren't the same thing, and I don't want either side claiming the other tonight. Three days. Same room. Bring your household's ballot and nothing else you feel obligated to bring."

Teo scanned the room while the silence held, the way she scanned a load before signing off on it, less out of suspicion than habit. Priya sat near the front, hands folded in her lap the same way Dagny's usually were, saying nothing. Wen stood at the door he'd claimed every meeting since the first, arms folded, saying nothing. Dagny hadn't come tonight either, same as the second meeting, and this time nobody turned to look at her empty chair the way they had the first time her absence had been noticed — the room had learned, by now, that her silence wasn't a message waiting to be read, only a woman who'd already said everything she intended to say and meant to leave it there until the vote made the saying unnecessary. A machinist from the third floor glanced once toward Household 12's usual seat and looked away again just as fast, and that was the whole of it.

The room broke apart slowly, the way it always did now, and Teo stayed against the wall a while after most of them had gone, looking past the emptying chairs to the door, and beyond it to the passage that led, eventually, to the wall where three hundred sixty-two meters sat waiting for Monday's repaint, patient, unargued with, the one number in Candelaria that had never once needed anyone's silence to be understood. Three days. She ran the figure the way she ran every figure now, out of habit more than need — three days at roughly a meter each, a number small enough not to matter against sixty years, small enough that nobody in that room had asked about it tonight, because for the first time in weeks, the altitude wasn't the number anyone in Candelaria was waiting on.
