Sigrun found her in the workroom the next morning, before the corridor traffic had picked up, and came straight to it the way she came to most things — no preamble, no small talk about the number on the wall, though she'd have passed it on her way in and it would have registered the way it registered with everyone by now, three hundred seventy-four meters and dropping.

"I want you to run something for me," Sigrun said, "that isn't on the schedule."

Teo set down the stylus. "Say it."

"Project the vote." Sigrun said it plainly, the way she said everything when she wanted a thing on the record as exactly what it was. "You have every household's mass. You have every household's stated position, or close enough — Priya's, Wen's, the ones who've said nothing yet but drew a certain way when the numbers came up. Run it the way you'd run a load distribution. Tell me where it lands before I have to stand in that room again and read an offer to forty-one people who are going to vote on it whether I'm ready or not."

Teo looked at her a moment before answering, not because she needed the time to think but because she wanted Sigrun to see that she'd taken the question seriously and not simply refused it out of reflex.

"No," she said.

"You could."

"I could build something that looked like a projection. That's not the same thing as being able to." Teo turned the display toward Sigrun anyway, the two open columns still sitting where she'd left them the night before, DESCEND and STAY, the household figures filled in as far as she'd gotten. "This tells you what each column costs. It doesn't tell you what forty-one people are going to decide a vote is worth to them, because that's not mass. I can't put a number on what Wen's shop means to him that would let me predict his vote any better than watching his face did three nights ago. I can't put a number on whether Dagny's forty years buys her silence or her yes. Those aren't columns. They're not mine to fill in."

"The households don't know that," Sigrun said. "They think your ledger already knows everything. Half of them are going to vote believing the outcome is decided before they walk in the room, because you've spent five years teaching this bloc that your numbers are never wrong."

"My numbers about mass are never wrong." Teo kept her voice level, because the alternative was to let it do something else, and she didn't trust what that would be. "That's what I have. Not the other thing."

"Then tell me honestly," Sigrun said. "If you had to guess."

"I'm not going to guess out loud to the co-op chair three days before a vote." Teo said it without heat, the way she'd have corrected a bad trim reading. "Whatever number I said, right or wrong, would stop being a guess the moment it left this room. It would become the thing everyone voted against or toward, whether I meant it that way or not. You're asking me to hand you a crystal ball, and I don't have one. I have a jettison schedule. It tells you what leaves the bloc and in what order once a decision's already been made. It's never once told anyone what to decide."

"You built the ledger that's sitting under every household's shortfall right now," Sigrun said. "You ran Priya's number. You ran Wen's. People are already treating those figures like weather reports. I don't see the difference between what you've already given them and what I'm asking for."

"The difference is that I only measured what's true today," Teo said. "Priya's quota is true today. Wen's exemption is true today. Neither of those numbers guesses at Thursday. A projection of the vote would be guessing at Thursday and calling it Monday's arithmetic, and I've spent five years making sure nobody in this bloc can point to a figure of mine and say it was dressed up as something it wasn't. I'm not going to spend that now, three days out, on a guess I can't stand behind."

Sigrun didn't answer that immediately. She looked instead at the wall display a moment, at the two columns still glowing where Teo had left them from the night before, DESCEND and STAY, and Teo watched her read them the way everyone eventually read that display: looking for the number that would tell her what to feel about tomorrow, and finding only what things weighed.

Sigrun was quiet long enough that Teo thought she might push again. She didn't. She looked at the two columns a moment longer, the way a person looks at a door they've already decided not to open, and then she straightened, the posture Teo had started to recognize as the one she wore into rooms.

"That's the most useful answer you've given me all week," Sigrun said. "I don't like it. It's still useful."

"I'd rather it be useless and honest than useful and wrong."

"I know." Sigrun almost smiled, and didn't quite finish the motion. "That's the job you have. Mine's worse. I have to stand in the room and let people believe whatever they want about what your silence means, and I can't correct them, because correcting them would be its own kind of guess."

She left without asking again, and Teo sat with the columns a while after the door closed, thinking that Sigrun had come closer than anyone yet to naming the exact shape of what Teo did every Monday — not predicting anything, only refusing to, in a voice steady enough that the refusal read, to anyone who wanted it to, as a kind of answer anyway.
