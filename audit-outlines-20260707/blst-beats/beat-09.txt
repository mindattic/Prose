Her quarters held fifty-one kilos, and she knew the number the way she knew her own weight standing on a scale: not sentimentally, just accurately. Two changes of work clothes, a spare set of boots, a tether-platform photograph she hadn't looked at in a year, a kit of hand tools she'd carried down from the transit lines before she'd ever heard of Candelaria, a bedroll that had outlasted three postings. She'd run her own allocation the first week she'd taken the job, the way she'd run every other household's, because it would have embarrassed her to post other people's numbers without knowing her own. Fifty-one kilos. It had never changed, because she'd never let anything into the room heavy enough to change it.

The ledger sat open on the desk that night the way it sat open most nights, two columns wide, DESCEND on one side and STAY on the other, and Teo worked through them the way she worked through every draft, checking one column against the twin figure in the next.

Under DESCEND: the vote passes, AshgraveMaterials pays Φ847,000 for the bloc's structural mass, forty-one households split the proceeds by the same proportional formula that set jettison quotas, relocation begins inside thirty days, the frame comes down under Teo's own trim plan instead of its own failing coherence, controlled the whole way to the ground.

Under STAY: the vote fails, the offer lapses, the frame keeps losing coherence at the rate it had been losing it since before Teo arrived, the bloc crosses three hundred twenty meters on schedule regardless of anyone's decision, tenancy law stops applying the moment it does, insurance voids the same day, and Candelaria becomes salvage-eligible to whoever gets to it first — not Ashgrave, not on Ashgrave's terms, not with Ashgrave's number attached.

She had built both columns from the same inputs: the same coherence curve, the same descent rate, the same forty-one households and their true masses. Every figure in DESCEND had a mirror figure in STAY, checked against the frame's actual physics and not against anyone's preference for how the physics should behave. That was the part she kept returning to, some nights later than others: the arithmetic didn't care which column won. It would resolve to a number either way, honestly, the same as it resolved every week on the wall. The only difference between the columns was which one arrived with a payment attached and which one arrived alone, the same distinction she'd already found sitting under Priya's Φ2,100 and Wen's exempted zero.

What the math couldn't do — what no version of the ledger, however carefully she built it, could ever do — was make the choice between the columns feel like anything other than a choice. And yet she understood, sitting with both columns lit at once, that this was exactly the effect her work produced whether she intended it or not. She posted the wall's number every Monday in a font that didn't argue. She built the household figures clean, checked twice, defensible, the same tone whether the number closed something or only measured a loss. People read her ledger the way they read a tide table, not a recommendation, and that was precisely why it moved rooms the way arguments couldn't: nobody had ever accused a tide table of persuading them. Her job, she understood now, turning the thought over slowly because she didn't love where it led, was not to tell forty-one households what to decide. It was to make one outcome look like weather and the other look like a choice, simply by reporting both in the same flat hand, and to let people mistake the first for inevitability because nothing about her voice ever told them otherwise.

She didn't think that was dishonest, exactly. Every figure in both columns was true. She'd checked. But truth arranged in two columns, side by side, in the same neutral font, was still an arrangement, and arrangements were never neutral no matter how honestly their contents had been checked. She had built the frame the room would argue inside of. She hadn't built the argument. She wasn't sure, sitting there at fifty-one kilos with the rest of the room's mass spread across the desk in numbers that weren't hers, that the difference mattered as much as she needed it to.

She thought about Priya's Φ2,100, and Wen's exempted zero, and Dagny's allocation two floors up that she hadn't run yet because she was, if she admitted it, putting it off — not because the number would be hard to calculate, but because she suspected it would be the one figure on this ledger that actually moved something, and she didn't like being the person whose desk that fact would first appear on.

She closed the file before she reached for that household. Not tonight. The wall would still want Monday's number whether she ran Dagny's allocation now or in the morning, and fifty-one kilos of her own life sat in the room around her, undisturbed, weighing exactly what it had weighed the day she moved in.

She looked at the tether-platform photograph a while, propped against the wall where it always sat, never hung, because hanging it would have meant deciding it belonged somewhere permanent, and she still wasn't sure it did. Fifty-one kilos wasn't nothing — it was clothes and tools and a bedroll that had kept her upright through three postings before this one — but it wasn't the kind of mass anyone would have to grieve on her behalf if it went over a rail tomorrow. She'd built her whole life to weigh that little on purpose, long before Candelaria, long before jettison ledgers were her job instead of someone else's. It had never once occurred to her, until this year, that the same lightness that made her good at this work might also be the reason she hadn't understood, until Priya's hand pressed flat against her own sternum in a room full of neighbors, what it cost a person to imagine any of their fifty-one kilos, or their four hundred, going over a rail they hadn't chosen.

She didn't write that down anywhere. It wasn't the kind of figure a ledger had a column for, and she'd learned by now not to force one where it didn't fit.
