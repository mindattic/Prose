Wen's household came up on the ledger the same afternoon, and it broke the formula the moment Teo tried to run it.

Every other allocation in Candelaria resolved the same way: declared mass, divided against the standard proportion, a figure for jettison, a figure for what stayed. Household 23 wouldn't resolve. Teo had known it wouldn't before she opened the file — she'd stood in that workshop twice for load readings and knew what sat bolted into that floor — but the formula didn't know anything until she fed it the declaration, and the declaration read the way it had read for forty years: two lathes, three dies, a bank of hand-built jigs, all of it entered under fixture mass, not household goods.

Fixture mass didn't jettison. That wasn't a judgment Teo made; it was a category the co-op had written into the trim charter before she was born, the same charter that classed a water main or a stairwell rail as part of the frame rather than part of a household's discretionary weight. Almagre enforced the distinction without exception, the way it enforced every trim limit Teo set — a bolted machine shop registered to the automaton the same as a load-bearing wall did, present in the stress model, absent from anyone's jettison quota. Teo could order every loose object in that household over the side tomorrow and Wen's lathes would still be sitting exactly where his grandfather had put them, because the frame itself, not Teo, had decided forty years ago that they counted as structure.

She entered the figure for the wall: Household 23, fixture-exempt, Φ0 in jettison-eligible goods. It was, on the ledger's own terms, the cleanest number she'd post all week.

It was also, she thought, staring at the zero a while longer than the number deserved, the least honest true thing she'd post all week, and she made herself sit with why before she moved on. The zero was correct for what it measured — Teo's authority, the jettison order, the narrow slice of the bloc's business that ran through her desk. It said nothing about the other authority sitting behind Ruslan's offer, the one that didn't ask Teo's ledger anything at all. If the vote passed, AshgraveMaterials wasn't buying households' discretionary goods one jettison order at a time. It was buying the bloc's structural mass outright — walls, mains, rails, and every bolted fixture riding inside them, Household 23's lathes included, priced for scrap the same as the girders around them. Wen's shop sat outside Teo's jettison quota and squarely inside Ruslan's purchase. Two different mechanisms, two different owners of the decision, and only one of them had ever asked Wen's permission for anything.

Wen knew it. Teo was certain of that the way she was certain of a load reading — she'd heard him say as much, three nights ago, without softening it: AshgraveMaterials doesn't relocate machine shops, they strip them for the metal and call it certified salvage. He'd said it plainly, to the whole room, and voted no anyway, on a shop his own charter protected from her ledger and nothing else protected from the sale. That wasn't a contradiction, not in trim terms. A man could hold two loads at once that pulled in different directions and still choose which one he built his stance around. Wen had built his around the shop, not around the quota that would never touch it, because the shop was the thing actually at risk and the quota never had been.

Teo posted the zero to the wall beside Priya's Φ2,100, the two figures sitting one above the other with nothing to say to each other — a household with almost no jettisonable mass and a shortfall no allocation could close, and a household with fixture mass the law wouldn't let her touch and an owner who knew the law wouldn't be the thing that saved it. Neither number lied. That was the part she kept turning over. Every figure on that wall was true, checked twice, defensible to anyone who asked, and the two truest numbers she'd post all week sat side by side saying nothing that helped either household past their own front doors.

She thought about telling Wen the zero outright — that his shop had never been hers to take, that the fixture exemption had always covered him — and decided against it, not because it was a secret, the wall would carry the same figure by morning regardless of who she told first, but because he already knew. He'd told the room himself what happened to fixture mass if the bloc sold. He didn't need Teo's ledger to confirm a fear he'd already priced correctly.

Almagre's confirmation line blinked once, routing the posting, and Teo let it. The automaton didn't know what it had just filed any more than a level knew what it measured. It only reported true. It had no opinion on fixture exemptions or on what a bolted lathe meant to the hands that had learned it, and Teo had stopped expecting it to. Its competence was the one thing in this whole business she never had to check twice — no automaton had ever lied to a room to spare a household's feelings, and none ever would, because it didn't have feelings to protect and no instruction telling it to protect anyone else's.

She thought about walking the figure over to Wen herself instead of letting him find it on the wall with everyone else, and decided against that too, for the same reason she'd decided against telling him outright: he already knew what the zero meant, better than she did, because he was the one who'd have to watch the shop leave if the room voted the way the money pointed. Explaining a true number to a man who'd already priced it correctly wasn't honesty. It was just noise dressed up as care.

Teo closed the file, and thought, the way she thought most things now, in trim: two households, two loads, and not one figure on the wall this week that would change what either of them was about to lose.
