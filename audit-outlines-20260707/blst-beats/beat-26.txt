The co-op's record required every ballot logged by household before the night was finished — not for the tally, which was already final and already announced, but for the permanent minutes, the same minutes that recorded every vote this bloc had ever taken back to before Teo had come aboard, and sorting them into that record fell, as most of the bloc's arithmetic fell, to her.

She worked through the yes stack first, calling household numbers to Sigrun in a low voice while Sigrun checked them against the roll, the two of them working the way they'd worked the count itself, methodically, without hurry, while the room emptied slowly around them. Twenty-three cards. She was most of the way through them — the widow from 8-D, the machinist from the third floor, a scattering of households she recognized more by their doors than their faces — when she reached a card folded exactly the way every other card in the stack was folded, no different, no more deliberate, and turned it enough to read the number on the back.

Twelve.

She sat with that for a second longer than the arithmetic itself required, not because the number surprised her — she'd stopped letting herself expect to be surprised by Dagny weeks ago — but because some part of her had been carrying the shape of this moment since the morning she'd posted the sheet with Φ4,800 on it, and now that the moment had actually arrived, it arrived exactly as unremarkable as every other card in her hand, indistinguishable from the widow's, from the machinist's, from anyone's.

"Household twelve," she said to Sigrun, and Sigrun checked the roll and nodded, and they moved on to the next card, and that was the whole of the ceremony the room gave to the single most consequential ballot in the box.

Teo finished the log an hour later at her own desk, alone, the driver readings waiting where she'd left them that morning, and pulled up the split formula she'd built weeks ago and left running as a projection every night since — the same formula, unchanged, that had set Priya's Φ2,100 and Wen's exempted zero and Dagny's Φ4,800 into the same forty-one-line column. She'd built it, back then, on an assumption: that if the vote passed, every household's true mass would enter the pool the way the ledger had always allowed for. Tonight the assumption had a fact standing where it used to stand alone. The vote had passed. Household twelve had voted yes. Dagny's allocation — the largest single figure she'd ever entered into a Candelaria ledger — was no longer a number she'd projected against a maybe. It was in the sale.

She ran the split one final time, the confirmed figures where the assumed ones had sat for weeks, and watched Priya's total resolve the way a level finally reads true once the last shim's been set under the leg nobody thought needed one. Added to Priya's existing Φ2,100 — hers regardless of any vote, guaranteed since the jettison program had started — the structural share from tonight's count put her past eight thousand four hundred, not by a margin that made the number feel inevitable, past it plainly, confirmed, no longer resting on the word if.

She didn't write anything into the ledger beyond the figures themselves. She hadn't, all year, and she wasn't going to start tonight, on the one entry that mattered most.

She found out later, from the machinist who'd stood near the door most of the evening, that Dagny had left the common space almost as soon as the tally was announced — not lingering for the piles to be sorted, not staying to hear her own household number read into anyone's log, gone before most of the room had finished absorbing the number Sigrun had given them. Teo pictured it without having seen it: Dagny rising from her chair the way she rose from every chair, unhurried, folding nothing, explaining nothing to the two or three people who might have tried to catch her eye on the way out, walking the same corridor she'd walked forty years' worth of times, back toward the door of Household 12, where the books still stood floor to ceiling and the bed still sat where it had sat since before Teo was born.

She hadn't stopped to speak to anyone. She hadn't stopped to explain herself to the room, or to Priya, or to Teo, who of everyone in the bloc had perhaps the best claim to an explanation and had already told her, weeks ago, standing in that same room among the books, that she wasn't owed one.

Nobody followed her. Teo, finishing her figures at a desk two decks away, hadn't needed to see it happen to know exactly how it had gone — a door closing, a room settling back into its forty years of quiet, a woman who had cast the single largest ballot in Candelaria's history and gone home afterward the same way she went home from anything, without a word for anyone who might have wanted one.
