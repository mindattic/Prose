Priya came to the eigenlift core the following day, which surprised Teo less than it might have a month ago. People found their way down to the core eventually, once they'd lived long enough with a number on the wall that they wanted to see where it came from. Most came once and didn't come back. Teo let her in past the outer housing without asking why, because the core wasn't restricted and there was nothing in it to hide — the coherence frame ran in plain view behind its inspection grille, a low structure of drivers and feedback rails that hummed at a pitch just above hearing when it worked hard, silent when it didn't, and it wasn't working hard today. The Hush held. Teo had checked twice on her way in, the reflex of five years.

"I won't take long," Priya said, though she didn't move toward the door, and Teo didn't push her toward it either.

She'd expected the visit to be about the number — Priya's Φ2,100, posted to the wall two days ago beside a shortfall Priya had already put in front of the whole bloc — but Priya didn't ask about the ledger at all. She stood near the inspection grille for a while, watching the drivers do nothing visible, and then she asked the question straight, the way she'd asked her question to the room the first night, aimed at no one else this time.

"If it were you," she said. "A daughter, a shortfall, a bloc that's sinking whether or not anyone votes on it. What would you do?"

Teo didn't answer right away, not because she hadn't thought about it — she'd thought about very little else since the first meeting — but because she wanted the pause to mean something honest instead of merely polite.

"I don't have a daughter," she said finally. "I don't own fifty-one kilos of anything I'd trade for someone else's shortfall, and I've never once had to weigh my own life against a number that size. Whatever I'd do in your place isn't a real answer. It's a guess dressed up as sympathy, and I don't think you came down here for sympathy."

"Maybe I did," Priya said, not unkindly. "Maybe I just wanted someone to say it plainly instead of everyone in that room being so careful with me."

"Then I'll say it plainly. I don't know what I'd do. I know what your number is. I know what it can and can't close on its own. I know what it takes to close it. None of that tells me what I'd choose if it were my daughter and my shortfall, because it isn't, and I think if I pretended otherwise I'd be lying to make you feel less alone, and you'd know it the moment I said it."

Priya was quiet at that, her hand coming up to press flat against her sternum the way it had at the first meeting, the gesture Teo had already learned to read as someone holding a weight in place rather than gesturing. "Sigrun would have told me the same thing," she said, "if I'd asked her instead. Everyone in this bloc has gotten very good at telling me it isn't theirs to answer."

"It isn't," Teo said. "That's not me passing on the question. It's the truest thing I can tell you about it. Nobody in that room can hand you a vote that closes your shortfall by wanting it enough on your behalf. Not me, not Sigrun, not anyone who cares about Kaja and can't do the arithmetic for you. The vote is real. It's going to happen with or without anyone's sympathy attached to it, and when it does, it'll close your number or it won't, and either way it'll be forty other households choosing, not one of us standing here right now."

"That's a cold thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say," Teo said. "I don't know how to make it warmer without making it wrong."

Priya looked at the frame a while longer, at the drivers running their quiet correction, at the grille that kept forty-one households from ever having to think about what held them up on an ordinary day. "I keep coming down here," she said, "because it's the one place in this bloc where the numbers don't pretend to be anything but numbers. Everywhere else, people dress them up. Here they're just true."

"They're just true," Teo agreed. "I can't promise you they'll be kind."

Priya nodded, once, and didn't leave right away after all. She stood a moment longer near the grille, watching the drivers hold their quiet correction, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped the way it had the night of the first meeting, precision instead of uncertainty.

"You said you don't own fifty-one kilos of anything you'd trade for someone else's shortfall," she said. "I don't think that's true the way you meant it. I think you'd trade all fifty-one and not notice the weight was gone, because you've spent this whole year learning what everyone else in this bloc is about to lose, and none of it is yours to lose, and I don't think that's nothing. I think that's its own kind of work."

Teo didn't have an answer ready for that, and didn't invent one. "Maybe," she said. "I still don't know what I'd do in your place. That part hasn't changed."

"I didn't come down here for that answer," Priya said. "I came down here to say the question out loud to someone who wouldn't flinch from it. You didn't. That's enough for today."

She let herself out past the inspection grille, and Teo stood a while after she'd gone, listening to the frame hold its silence, thinking that she'd given Priya the only honest thing she had to give and that it had cost her nothing to say and Priya everything to hear.
