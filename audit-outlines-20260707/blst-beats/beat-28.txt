The morning after the vote record went up, Teo opened a new file in the ledger — not a projection this time, not a split she'd run a dozen times against a maybe, but the actual wind-down schedule, the document that would replace the Monday altitude posting as the thing this bloc measured its remaining time against.

Thirty days. Ashgrave's relocation window, the one Sigrun had read aloud twice now, word for word, started counting from the morning after the tally, which made today day two. Teo built the schedule the way she built everything, in columns: household, current mass allocation, relocation date, receiving address at ground-tier, structural elements tagged for the strip crew that would follow once every household had cleared its own effects out from around them. Forty-one rows. She'd built plenty of documents this year with forty-one rows in them. This was the first one that ended, instead of continuing.

She ran the driver readings first, the way she ran them every morning, vote or no vote, ledger or no ledger, because the frame didn't know an offer had been accepted and wouldn't have cared if it had. Driver eleven was still running warm, same as it had for months, the Hum sitting exactly where it had sat at the gantry with Ruslan. She logged the figure into its own column, separate from the wind-down file, because the two documents measured different things now — one told her how the frame was aging, the other told her how much longer that aging was going to matter to the people living inside it.

Ashgrave's engineers would take over the coherence drivers on day twenty-eight, two days before the window closed, and step the frame down deliberately from there — not letting it fail on its own curve, not racing the six weeks Ruslan had named at the gantry, but bringing Candelaria to the ground on a schedule Ashgrave controlled instead of the frame. Teo entered that line the same way she entered every other line, without comment.

She worked through the households in order, confirming relocation addresses against the ground-tier assignments the co-op had received back from the housing authority two days earlier — the widow from 8-D to a unit three blocks from her sister's; the machinist from the third floor to a shared building with two other displaced blocs already settling in; Wen's household to an address with, Teo noted without remark, considerably less floor space than a machine shop required, a fact nobody had asked her to enter and she entered anyway, because the ledger's job was completeness, not comfort.

She kept going down the column — a household on the fifth floor to a unit near the freight terminal, close enough that the transfer cost would come out of the co-op's contingency instead of theirs; a household she mostly knew by the sound of their door, going to an address two zones over from anywhere they'd have chosen for themselves, if choosing had been any part of what this month allowed anyone. Dagny's line she entered last among the households proper, address confirmed the same as everyone else's, mass allocation the same Φ4,800 she'd posted weeks ago, no annotation added, no annotation needed.

She reached Priya's line and confirmed the closing figure she'd finalized two nights before, the one that no longer rested on the word if, and moved past it the way she moved past every completed figure now, without lingering.

She reached her own line last, the way she always did, because it had never once mattered in what order she entered anyone else's mass but hers had never mattered to anyone but her either.

Household allocation: fifty-one kilos. She typed it into the relocation column the same as she'd typed Wen's exempted zero and Dagny's four thousand eight hundred, and moved the cursor to the next field.

Then she got up from the desk, crossed the small room to the single shelf that held what she owned, and began.

It didn't take long. It had never been going to take long. She packed the books first — four of them, the only four she'd kept through three moves before this bloc — into the bottom of the single crate she owned, the same crate she'd carried up the freight lock the day she'd arrived. The two changes of clothes beyond what she wore went in next, folded the way she folded everything, without ceremony. The tape measure went in after that, then the load gauge, then the small kit of shims she used for trim work nobody else on the bloc had ever asked to borrow. The tools went in last, wrapped in the same cloth she used to wrap them for every load survey she'd ever run, corners tucked the same way every time, a habit older than this job and every job before it.

She didn't take anything off the wall, because there was nothing on the wall. She didn't sort through anything, because there was nothing left unsorted. The room gave up its whole contents in under ten minutes, the same room that had taken Dagny's household most of an afternoon to survey, months ago, before any of this had a vote attached to it.

She sealed the crate, checked the latch twice the way she checked every figure that mattered to her, and set it by the door, in the exact spot where she'd need to find it in thirty days without having to look for it, and then she went back to the desk and finished the schedule, forty-one households, one line each, hers no different in the column from anyone else's, and pinned the finished document to the commons wall before the morning traffic had properly started, the way she'd pinned everything else this year, without a word written anywhere on it that the figures hadn't already said for themselves.
