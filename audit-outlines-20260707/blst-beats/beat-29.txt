Four weeks out, the strip crew's provisional schedule went up on the commons wall two lines below the descent notice, cross-referenced against the household relocation dates the way everything on that wall now cross-referenced everything else, and Wen read his own household's line the same evening everyone else did, standing at the wall with no more privacy than anyone else had for it, and then he went down to the shop, because there wasn't anywhere else that evening had ever been going to end.

He didn't turn on the overhead bank, working instead by the bench lights he'd wired himself years ago, low and warm, the kind of light that had never once been about seeing clearly and had always been about seeing the machines the way he liked to see them, at the end of a day, when the shop belonged to him and to nobody else's schedule.

The shop smelled the way it had smelled every evening of his life that mattered to him — cutting oil, metal dust that never fully swept out of the corners, the faint ozone tang the grinder left behind hours after he'd shut it down. He'd stopped noticing the smell most days the way a person stops noticing their own house. Tonight he noticed it the way he'd noticed the calibration marks, deliberately, the way a man notices something he's decided to remember on purpose.

The lathes sat exactly where they'd sat for forty years, bolted through the deck plate his grandfather had reinforced before bolting anything to it, and Wen ran a hand along the near one's bed the way he ran a hand along it most evenings, out of habit more than inspection, though tonight some part of him was doing both at once without quite meaning to. Behind it, the bank of jigs he'd carried into the common space three weeks ago sat back on their shelf, returned the morning after, exactly where they'd always lived, six shapes he could have named blind by the weight of them alone.

He found the calibration marks by feel before he found them by eye, small notches filed into the bed at three points along its length, his grandfather's marks, then his own beside them a decade later when he'd recut the first set worn shallow by thirty years of the same measurements taken the same way. He'd told himself, cutting those second marks, that he was preserving something. Running his fingers over both sets at once now, ridged, precise, he thought that preserving had never been quite the right word for what he'd actually been doing. Keeping faith with something. That was closer.

Nobody had come to tell him there was still time, or a version of this that ended differently, or a fund somewhere that might yet close the distance between his fixture mass and a truck that could carry it somewhere else intact. Nobody was going to. He'd stopped expecting it sometime around the night he'd laid the jigs out on the vote table and watched the room look at them without arguing.

He moved to the second lathe, the one he'd learned on, and did the same thing there — hand flat against the bed, fingers finding the marks his grandfather had cut and the ones he'd cut himself later, tracing them slowly enough that if anyone had been watching they might have mistaken it for grief. It was closer to what he did every morning before starting a job, running his hand over a fixture to feel whether it had shifted overnight, except tonight there was no job coming after, and the checking had nowhere left to go once it was finished.

The tags had gone up that afternoon — small, official, one on each major piece, printed with a lot number and a destination he hadn't bothered reading twice — and he'd left them where the strip crew's advance man had stuck them, because arguing with a tag was even less useful than arguing with a jig, and he'd already learned that lesson from the other side of it. The mill in the back corner had one too, and the bench grinder, and the drawer bank where he kept forty years of hand tools sorted by a system only he and his grandfather had ever fully understood. He hadn't sorted through the drawers to see what might be small enough to carry out under the ledger's radar, into a crate the way Teo carried her own life in one. He'd thought about it, once, for the length of an evening, and decided against it before he'd finished thinking it through. A drawer of loose tools without the machines they were built for wasn't a shop. It was scrap that hadn't been melted yet.

He stood in the low light a while longer, between the two lathes, hands finally still at his sides, and didn't say anything out loud, because there wasn't anyone in the shop to say it to and he'd never been a man who talked to machines the way some of the old-timers claimed to. He simply stood there, in the particular quiet a shop holds when its work is finished for the night, and after a while he turned off the bench lights the way he always did, one bank at a time, and went up to bed, leaving the calibration marks exactly where his hands had found them, unlit, in the dark, still true.
