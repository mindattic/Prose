Priya waited until the common space had thinned to its ordinary evening traffic — a card game finishing near the door, the herb rack's grow-lights humming faintly under the ceiling fixtures, nobody paying either of them enough attention to notice one more conversation happening near the planters — before she crossed the room to where Dagny stood alone, examining a tray of something green that had never quite justified the water ration it cost. The wall outside read three hundred sixty-one meters. Two days now, not three, since Sigrun had set the date.

"I'm not going to ask you how you're voting," Priya said, before Dagny had turned all the way around, because she'd promised herself on the walk over that she'd say that part first, so there'd be no mistaking why she'd come. "I know you've already told Teo you don't want that question, and I'm not going to be the third person this month who asks it anyway and pretends I'm not."

Dagny looked at her for a moment without answering, the same stillness Priya had watched her hold through five household meetings now — not cold, exactly, just entirely unspent, a woman who'd decided a long time ago which parts of herself belonged to a room full of neighbors and which parts didn't.

"Then what did you come to say?" Dagny asked.

"I read the sheet Teo posted. The one with your household's line on it." Priya kept her voice level, the way she'd practiced it in the kitchen that morning, not because she didn't feel what she was about to say but because she'd learned, saying hard numbers out loud to Kaja's specialists for a year now, that the feeling traveled better in a level voice than a shaking one. "I know what forty years of your rooms comes to on Teo's formula. Four thousand eight hundred. I know it's the largest single figure in the whole ledger, bigger than the next three households put together, and I know that without it — without that specific number landing in the split the way Teo's sheet assumes it will — my daughter's shortfall doesn't close. Not partway. Not almost. It stays exactly where it's been sitting for three months."

She hadn't meant to keep going, but the room hadn't stopped her, so she did.

"I'm not telling you that to move you. I don't think there's a version of me standing here that could move you, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise to your face. I'm telling you because Teo told me once — not about this, about something else entirely — that she thought it was dishonest to let a person choose something without knowing the shape it lands in. I think she's right. And I don't think anyone's told you the shape of this one straight, the way I'm telling you now, instead of leaving you to work it out from a posted sheet the way I had to."

Dagny set the tray of green shoots back onto the rack, unhurried, and folded her hands in front of her the way she folded them in every meeting Priya had ever watched her sit through.

"You've told me," she said. "I heard you the first time you said it, and I heard the version of it that's been sitting on that wall for two weeks before you said it to my face. I don't need it said a third way to understand it."

"I'm not asking you to vote for my daughter," Priya said, and heard, as she said it, how close the sentence came to exactly the thing she'd promised herself not to do, and pushed through it anyway because it was the truest thing she had left to offer. "I know that's not a fair thing to ask anyone, and I'm not asking it. I just didn't want you casting whatever you cast without one person in this bloc having said the number to you plainly, the way you'd want it said to you if it were the other way around."

"Would I," Dagny said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I don't know," Priya admitted. "I don't know you well enough to know what you'd want. I know what I'd want, and I did the only thing I know how to do with that, which was come find you instead of waiting for you to hear it secondhand from someone at the card table."

Dagny didn't answer that either, not right away. She looked past Priya, toward the wall where the altitude number sat behind a scatter of residents who hadn't glanced at it in weeks, the way a fixture stops being noticed once a room has lived with it long enough, and when she looked back her face hadn't changed at all — not softened, not hardened, the same unspent stillness she'd carried into the room.

"I heard you," she said again, and it was, Priya understood, the whole of the answer she was going to get tonight, possibly the whole of the answer she was ever going to get before the vote itself made the question stop mattering. "Go home to your daughter, Priya. I'm not going to give you anything more than that standing in this room, and I don't think you came here actually expecting to."

Priya hadn't. She said as much, quietly, and left the way she'd come, past the card game that hadn't noticed either of them, past the herb rack's faint hum, out into the passage where the wall's three hundred sixty-one meters waited, patient and unmoved, the one number in Candelaria that had never once needed anyone's answer to keep counting down.

Behind her, in the thinning light of the common space, Dagny stood a while longer at the rack, not touching the trays again, giving nothing back to the room that would have told anyone watching which way she'd decided, if she'd decided anything at all.
