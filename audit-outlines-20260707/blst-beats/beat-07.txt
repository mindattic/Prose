The wall outside the commons space read three hundred seventy-seven meters that morning, freshly repainted, the number lower than yesterday's by exactly what the frame's coherence readings had promised it would be. Teo had painted it herself the night before. She didn't stop to look at it now. She already had next week's figure half-built in her head the way another kind of person carried a grocery list.

Her real work that morning was smaller than the wall and further from anyone's notice, though nothing about it was hidden: the household allocation ledger, open on the desk in her workroom, cursor sitting on the line for the household two floors down from Wen's — one working adult, one twelve-year-old, and not much mass past that.

She'd known the shape of Priya's household before she ever ran a number. You learned a household's mass from its water draw and its power draw long before its jettison quota came due, and Priya's drew like a household that had already trimmed itself to what it needed and nothing further — two beds, a kitchen fitted for meals that didn't waste, a closet that didn't hold a season it wasn't currently wearing. When Teo ran the standard proportional formula — declared mass against total household allocation, the same formula every household in Candelaria had agreed to sixty years ago and never once revised — the figure that came back was Φ2,100 in goods, eligible for jettison if an order ever reached that floor.

It wasn't a large number. Teo sat with it before she entered it into the column marked for posting. Against the Φ8,400 Priya had put into the room three nights ago, precise to the coin, in a voice that hadn't shaken, Φ2,100 didn't close a quarter of the distance. Even every jettisonable scrap in that household, sold at the generous end of the salvage schedule, wouldn't touch it. Teo checked the figure against itself twice, the way she checked every number that mattered, hunting for an error she half hoped to find. She didn't find one. Priya's own allocation, on its own, could never have funded the procedure. It never could have. The only arithmetic that closed that gap ran through Ruslan's offer and nowhere else — Φ20,650 a household, if the vote passed, riding on top of whatever a family's own jettison quota already carried out the door. Priya's Φ2,100 wasn't the number that mattered. It was the number underneath the number that mattered, the way a jig sitting under a lathe carries weight nobody notices until it fails.

She thought about the shape of a lever arm a moment longer than the calculation required — the way a small mass, placed at the right distance from the center, could tip a load that a much larger mass, placed wrong, never would. Priya's Φ2,100 sat close to the center, negligible on its own. The Ashgrave payout sat far out on the arm, and that was the whole of why it mattered and her own quota didn't. Trim didn't care about the size of a number in isolation. It cared where the number sat relative to everything else pulling on the same frame. She'd known that about steel for years before she ever thought to apply it to a family's shortfall, and she wasn't sure the application was fair to Priya, reducing a daughter's cochlear feeds to a moment arm, but it was the only vocabulary she owned that didn't flinch from the number, and she'd rather use an unflinching vocabulary than a kind one that lied.

Teo posted the figure anyway, because posting figures was the job, and a household's own jettison quota went up on the wall every week whether or not it changed a single vote in the room. Five years of doing this had taught her not to weight a number by how much she guessed it would cost someone to read. The wall didn't soften figures for the widow in 8-D any more than it softened them for Priya. It subtracted the same way every week and let whoever read it do their own arithmetic on top of hers.

She thought, entering the figure, about the difference between a number that closed something and a number that only measured it. Priya's Φ2,100 measured a household's honest mass, nothing more — no judgment folded into it about whether that mass was worth throwing over the side or worth keeping. Making that measurement clean was Teo's job. Deciding what the measurement should cost anyone belonged to the room, to the vote, not to her. She'd told herself that more than once this year, the way a person repeats an instruction they aren't certain they believe.

What she didn't write into the ledger, because there was no column built for it and no honest way to build one, was the second calculation sitting underneath the first: if the vote failed, if Ruslan's offer went nowhere and Candelaria rode its own frame down past three hundred twenty meters on schedule, Priya's household would still owe its jettison quota. The bloc's own mass had to come down regardless of any sale — that was the frame's physics, not a vote's outcome — except then there would be no Φ20,650 riding in behind it. Priya would lose the same Φ2,100 in goods either way. The only thing the vote actually changed, for that one household, was whether the loss arrived with a payment attached or arrived by itself.

Teo closed the working file and opened the version built for the wall — stripped of names, just floor numbers and totals, the way public postings had always run in Candelaria. Nobody's shortfall went up there except the one a household chose, out loud, to say first. That had been Priya's choice three nights ago, not the ledger's. The ledger didn't editorialize. It only carried forward what people gave it to carry.

Almagre's status line blinked green in the corner of the workroom display, confirming the posting queued without needing to be asked — a small, unremarkable correctness, the automaton doing the one thing it was built to do and nothing past it. Teo barely registered it. She'd stopped thanking it for routine competence somewhere in her second year here, the same way she'd stopped thanking gravity.

She saved the file and stood, thinking that the wall would say the same thing this coming Monday whether Priya's neighbors decided her daughter's shortfall was their business or wasn't. It would read three hundred seventy-six meters by then. That number, at least, wasn't waiting on anyone's vote.
