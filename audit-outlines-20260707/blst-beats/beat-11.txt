The second meeting filled the common space the way the first one had, though Teo noticed the room held itself differently this time — fewer people standing near the door, more sitting through the whole of it, as if the bloc had already decided that this business was going to take a while and there was no sense standing through the wait. The wall outside read three hundred seventy-two meters. Nobody had needed to point that out to anyone tonight. Everyone had walked past it on the way in.

Sigrun opened without softening it. "Before anything else," she said, "Dagny Obuya isn't here tonight, and I want you to hear why from me instead of guessing at it among yourselves."

The room went still in the particular way it went still whenever Dagny's name came up, the stillness of a household everyone in Candelaria had an opinion about and almost no information on.

"She sent word this afternoon," Sigrun went on. "Through the co-op channel, same as any of you could have. I'll read it as she sent it, because I don't think it needs anything added to it." She glanced down at her wrist display, though Teo suspected she already had the message by memory. "'I have no position on the offer. I don't intend to state one before the vote, and I don't intend to be talked out of not having one. I will abide by whatever this bloc decides. Count my household as present in that sense, if nowhere else.'"

Nobody spoke immediately. Teo, standing against the same stretch of wall she'd claimed at the first meeting, watched the message land across the room in pieces — a few heads turning toward Household 12's usual chair, empty tonight, a few more turning toward each other instead, the private exchange of a look that asked a question none of them had an answer to.

Priya was the one who finally said something, though she kept it short. "Does that count as an abstention, or does it count as nothing?"

"It counts as what she said it counts as," Sigrun answered. "She'll abide by the vote. She's not asking anyone to decide on her behalf, and she's not offering an opinion for anyone to weigh. I'm not going to translate it into anything cleaner than that, because I don't think it is cleaner than that."

Wen, near the door again, arms folded again, said nothing at all, and Teo noticed that he didn't look surprised, the way a man doesn't look surprised by a reading he'd already priced in his head before it came back.

Teo understood the room's unease without needing anyone to explain it to her, because she'd done the same arithmetic herself the moment Sigrun read the message aloud. Dagny's household carried the largest mass allocation in Candelaria — forty years of accumulated goods, more than any other household by a wide margin — and whichever way that allocation moved would matter more to the payout total than any other single figure on Teo's ledger. A household that size choosing to say nothing wasn't the same as a household that size having no effect. It simply meant the effect would land on the day of the vote instead of before it, unannounced by the person carrying it, same as everyone else's ballot would.

"I'm not going to ask the room to speculate about what a household that isn't here tonight is thinking," Sigrun said, before anyone else could try. "That's not fair to her and it isn't useful to you. She'll vote when the vote comes. Everyone in this room will vote when the vote comes. Nothing before that is a vote, and I'd ask you all to keep treating it that way, because I've watched other blocs turn a hallway conversation into a headcount, and it never once made the actual decision easier when it finally arrived."

The meeting went on a while longer — questions about the relocation timeline, a clarification from Sigrun about the thirty-day packing window, nothing that changed the shape of what had already been said at the first meeting — and Teo found herself only half listening, running the same figure over and over without meaning to: Dagny's allocation, unstated, sitting somewhere north of every other household's on the ledger, waiting for a day that hadn't arrived yet.

A machinist from the third floor asked whether the vote could be delayed until the descent schedule showed a firmer number for the coherence curve. Sigrun told her no — the curve had been firm for weeks, the delay wouldn't change what the frame was doing, it would only change how many days the co-op had left to decide anything about it. A younger man near the back asked whether AshgraveMaterials would still honor its offer if the household count changed before the vote, someone moving out of the bloc on their own before any of this was decided. Sigrun said the offer was written against forty-one households and she had no reason yet to think that number would move. Nobody asked about Dagny again. Teo noticed that, too — the room had absorbed the message and set it down, the way a room sets down any fact it can't do anything about tonight.

She thought about walking up afterward and asking Sigrun whether she meant to send anyone up to Household 12 to press the point further, and decided she already knew the answer before she asked it. Sigrun wasn't going to press. Dagny's message had been plain enough on its own terms, and pressing a woman who'd said she didn't intend to be talked out of silence would only prove her right about why she'd gone silent in the first place.

She thought, watching Sigrun close the meeting without having asked anyone to raise a hand, that a household could say nothing and still be the loudest number in the room. She didn't say that aloud either. It wasn't hers to press, and the ledger, when she finally ran that allocation, would say it plainly enough on its own.
