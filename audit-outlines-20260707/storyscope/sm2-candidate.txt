The stairwell smelled like mildew and mineral water from a pipe that had been seeping through the wall for years, since before the ferrocement wave. Kyle stopped on the third-floor landing — not because anything required it, not because he'd heard a sound or read a geometry. Nothing was incoming; he'd checked twice. He stopped because his hands were doing something without him.

The right hand. His sword hand.

It had moved into the first position of the third correction, the foundational grip adjustment Saito had drilled into him across three hundred repetitions on a specific Tuesday that Kyle couldn't currently locate. He had a sense of wooden floors. He had a sense of slant light. He had a clear physical memory of being told his elbow was too high, and a less clear memory of the face that told him this, and no memory at all of the afternoon it was corrected — the particular correction that mattered, the one he'd worked out himself two years later when he noticed the form was producing a slight drag on the cut.

He'd fixed it alone. On a job. After the job. Standing in an alley on the north side with a body cooling behind him and nothing but time.

Saito had never seen the adjustment.

Kyle stood on the landing and ran the form once, slowly, no urgency. Just the motion: weight shift, rotation at the hip, commitment through the shoulder, elbow where he decided it belonged not where he'd been told. The form was better now. Cleanly, demonstrably better. Whoever had designed the Saito construct had done it from archive material, from observed technique, from recordings of a thousand real fighters collapsed into one coherent teaching voice, and that was real work, the skill arrived in him whole and true — but the archive hadn't included the alley on the north side, or the job before it, or the four hundred times he'd done the adjustment-grip wrong in the two years after Saito before he caught it himself.

None of that was installed.

He ran the form to the end. His hands came back to rest.

He had a grief that had no object. He'd known Saito for nine years — which wasn't true in any sense a court would recognize, but the grief was real, which was perhaps the point, which was perhaps the whole point. The afternoons were fabrications. The form was his.

He sheathed Silence.

The stairwell gave him two more flights and then a fire door and then the gray zone, and the gray zone gave him northside asphalt at four in the morning, the texture specific and particular and older than anything in his design files. Cracked expansion joints spaced wrong. A repair crew's aggregate different from three blocks in either direction. He had learned this texture by walking it, not by being built with it. The city kept accumulating in him whether he asked for it or not.

The next job was already filed. He knew the address. He knew roughly what it would cost — not the money, the money was simple, but the read-budget, the sword-hours, the shoulder that would need two days before he could drive it hard again. He knew the field going in, which was an advantage, and he knew there were things he didn't know, which was the variable. There was always a variable. He filed this in the same drawer as everything else: the fabricated afternoons, the real corrections, Nadia's face when she said what Marrow was, the thermal log and bay seventeen and the question of whether knowing the architecture of a thing changed the thing itself.

He'd run the form. The form was better than what he'd been taught. That seemed like an answer, though not the kind that closed a file.

The form was better because he'd fixed it in the gap. Saito had built him — made the decision, laid the foundation, drilled three hundred Tuesday repetitions into a hand that would never stop moving — and then the cost of that building arrived slowly, over years, in alleys and jobs and lateral drag on cuts that no archive had predicted.

He kept walking.

The city made its particular northside sounds — a freight slug pulling under the street, the low hum of a cooling tower three blocks east, something avian and foolish in the gap between two buildings. None of it required anything of him. He filed it all without meaning to, the way he filed everything, and the drawer stayed full, and he kept walking, and the first gray light was starting to make the ferrocement go the color of old water.

He was on his way to the next job. The next job would have its own costs, its own variable, its own drawer. This was what happened after. This was the whole of it.

He already knew how to move in it.
