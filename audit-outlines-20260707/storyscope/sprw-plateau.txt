Structures that read as large at document scale become geological at presence. The barrel follows the coastal ridge for several miles, half its length disappearing into the vegetation where the ridge curves, only the angle of it - the specific seventeen degrees off the horizontal, aimed at the equatorial sky - giving away that it is aimed at something rather than simply resting here.

He walks to the facility gate. The gate is unlocked. There is a note taped to the latch: Elias - I'm in the main hall. The handwriting is careful, not hasty. Someone who writes by hand regularly.

Tadesse Bekele-Sorensen meets him in the doorway of the main hall: a man in his fifties, Ethiopian-Danish by the cast of his features. He is wearing a facility uniform, the patch at the elbow done neatly in a slightly different fabric - not a rushed repair, a considered one. The kind of patch you apply when you intend to keep wearing the garment. He looks at Elias across the threshold for a moment - the look of a man who has had something confirmed - and then he says, with the quiet precision of someone naming an exhibit:

"Elias Macias. Zone 4 jobs. Cordon Freight, 2218."

It is not a question.

"Yes," Elias says.

"I wasn't sure you'd come." A beat. "I had the logs ready for two weeks, in case you did."

"I wasn't sure either," Elias says.

Something moves in Tadesse's expression - not quite a smile, but the acknowledgment of one. "That's the right answer," he says. "Come in."

Elias steps inside. The main hall smells of machine oil and the specific mineral quality of coastal air that has had thirty years to work its way into the concrete - salt, not unpleasant, the smell of a place that has been maintained. A wall of windows faces the barrel's near end. Tadesse crosses to the terminal without looking back, the confidence of a man moving through a space he knows in the dark. He has been alone here for twenty years. The presence of another person in this building is, Elias realizes, an event.

"You've been here the whole time," Elias says. "Twenty years."

"Twenty-two," Tadesse says. He pulls up the logs without turning around. "The contract renewed automatically. I renewed it manually, which wasn't required, but I wanted there to be a record that someone was choosing to be here." He pauses with his hand on the keyboard. "I didn't know who was on the other end. I still don't, entirely. But something was paying attention to precision, and I decided that was worth meeting."

He turns to look at Elias. "You're the first person who came in response to it."

The weight of that settles. Twenty-two years of careful maintenance, daily temperature logs the specification didn't require, a contract renewed on principle - and Elias is the first person from the other side of the arrangement who walked through the door.

In the main hall, before he pulls up the logs, Tadesse shows him the morning ritual first - not because Elias asked, but because it is what he does, and this is the first time in twenty years he has had a reason to explain it. Every morning he logs the temperature of the launch rail at three points: base, midpoint, crown. The specification requires quarterly checks. He logs daily. He has done this since 2204, because whatever was running the facility was paying attention to precision, and logging daily was the most precise thing he could do with his instruments.

He has never received a credit for it. He has never stopped.

"Do you think it noticed?" Elias asks.

Tadesse considers this seriously. "I think it measured everything it could reach," he says. "I can't say whether that is the same as noticing."

"Four hundred and twelve Φ. Retroactive, no line item." He nods once. "I knew what it was for. I decided not to ask."
