Her handset rings at 0917 the next morning. She does not recognize the number. She almost does not answer it.

She has not slept. The chair in Ledger's workspace is not designed for sleeping, which is why she used it -- if she is upright she will not sleep, and if she does not sleep she will not have to account for what she has dreamed, which seems like a reasonable trade. Ledger is in his own chair across the room, actually asleep, his shoes still on, his chin dropped toward his chest at the angle of someone who fell asleep in the middle of intending to stay awake.

The device is in its modified containment housing on the work table. It is still broadcasting. She has been watching the frequency counter for most of the night, not because it requires watching, but because it is something to do with her eyes.

When the handset rings she looks at it for four full seconds.

She picks up.

"Dr. Adeyemi-Kowalski," the voice says. It is a woman, middle-aged, a Northern European accent with something more complex underneath it -- East African, she thinks, after a moment. Finnish primary, Somali underneath. "My name is Petra Voss. I'm with the NSB Field Division in GLMZ. I'm calling because of a death report you filed two days ago."

The room is very quiet. The frequency counter ticks steadily. Ledger has not woken up.

"Yes," she says.

"The unaffiliated female patient," Petra says. "I'd like to talk with you about the circumstances. Before things go somewhere that neither of us can walk back from."

Stash is quiet for a moment. She sets down the not-quite-coffee from Ledger's unit, which has gone cold. She thinks about what she knows about the NSB Field Division. She thinks about what she knows about the four levels of protocol a call like this normally passes through before it reaches a field agent's direct line. This call came to her handset. Her personal number, which is not listed in the clinic's official filings.

"What do you mean by that?" she says.

"I mean that you filed a death report and removed a patient file and an indeterminate item from your clinic before two authorized operatives arrived to perform a standard asset recovery," Petra says. "And I mean that those operatives are not NSB operatives. They are contracted security from a private entity. And I mean that the situation is currently two tracks that have not coordinated with each other, and in my experience those situations go better when at least one of the parties is aware of both tracks."

Stash looks at Ledger across the room. He is still asleep.

She thinks about the word authorized. She thinks about the legal framework under which contracted private security can be described as authorized. She thinks about the fact that Petra has used that word deliberately, in the way of someone establishing a framing they know will be contested, to see how the other party responds to it.

"What do you want to talk about?" she says.

"In person," Petra says. "I'll suggest a location. Come without the device."

"What makes you think I have a device?"

A pause. The pause is not uncertainty. It is the pause of someone deciding how much to say and determining the answer is: enough. "Because the patient was Renata Emmei, and I have been looking for Renata Emmei for fourteen months, and the device she carried is what I have been looking for." Another pause. "I know what it contains, Dr. Adeyemi-Kowalski. Come without it."

The frequency counter ticks. The device broadcasts steadily into the dead channel, patient and consistent, doing what it was built to do.

She looks at the containment housing. She looks at Ledger, asleep in his chair with his shoes on. She looks at the window, which shows the gray Austin morning light coming through the ventilation slats, the permanent half-dark of the ungoverned corridor below thirty meters where the sun only reaches when it is directly overhead at midsummer.

She thinks about what it means that the NSB has been looking for Renata Emmei for fourteen months. She thinks about what it means that a field agent is calling her directly rather than sending a team to the clinic. She thinks about what Renata said: the first track and the second track and how the first track does not know about the second.

Petra Voss is the second track. Petra Voss is telling her there is something she does not know about the first.

"Give me the location," she says.

She writes it on the inside of her left forearm in the way she marks things she does not want to forget: one line, small, in the location she will see when she holds the handset. She memorizes it in the thirty seconds while Petra is reciting it, converts the zone-grid notation to a building she knows, confirms she can reach it in forty minutes walking through the Low.

She does not wake Ledger. She leaves him a note that says: meeting. back in two hours.

She puts on her jacket. She puts the medical bag over her shoulder with the device in its containment. Petra told her to come without the device. She has decided to go with the device because Petra does not tell her how to carry the device and because she does not go places without her tools.

She goes.
