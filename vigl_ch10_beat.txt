He had found the wheat bin in the eastern warehouse three days ago, and stayed a second night past when he should have moved, and would stay a third for the same reason he always did: moving carried its own risk, and staying carried another, and in nine weeks he had never once worked out which was smaller.

The grain gave off warmth as it settled — some slow chemistry in the kernels working itself loose, a fact he had no name for in any language but recognized on contact. It pressed against him at chest height, close on both sides. He felt the warmth and did not know what to do with the feeling of it, since registering a temperature and being warmed by one had stopped, somewhere in the last nine months, being the same operation.

Outside, the loading yard ran its morning cycle — heavy foot traffic thinning toward midday, the hoist-wheels still an hour off. He could read the time of day from the sound alone now.

He repeated the words once, not aloud.

Faeb. Fume. Morn. Solen.

Then the boards above the warehouse shifted.

He felt the weight of it before the sound reached him — timber taking a load at one discrete point, not the general creak of a building working against wind, not the hoist-wheels, which weren't due. Something applied. Something placed.

He held still.

The sound came a beat later: careful, which was its own kind of loud to anyone who had spent a season learning what care sounded like from the inside of a grain bin. Not a supervisor's tread — supervisors walked with the confidence of people who belonged. Not a work crew, who moved in clusters and didn't trouble themselves with quiet.

Single. Careful. Coming toward the section he was in.

In the dark, in the press of the warm grain, his hand — the black jointed thing that answered him a half-beat late and had answered him faithfully for seven months regardless — found the rifle. Something in the shell supplied the rest without being asked: safety off, round already seated, flat and immediate, the same way kal had arrived on the first day. He did not decide to know this. He simply did.

The barge was called the Houndstooth, and it smelled like every barge with a name like that. Orim had negotiated the passage himself, in a chandler's back room in Bressant, over a transaction he had conducted the way he conducted most transactions past his fortieth year on this river: stating a price, watching the other man's face for how much of an insult he could stand, and not haggling below that number even when he could have. A man learns, given enough decades, that haggling teaches the other party nothing about you worth having him know. Orim had stopped teaching strangers things about himself a long time before this river had learned his name in return.

Lyra had asked about the arrangement once, on the first day out of Bressant, and received an answer that answered nothing important. She had asked twice more before she stopped, on the morning of the second day, and Orim had noticed her stopping — he noticed most things people did without meaning to be noticed doing them. He said nothing about it. Silence, in his experience, taught a person to trust her own conclusions faster than argument ever had, and a woman who trusted her own conclusions was more useful to him, eventually, than one who trusted his.

He was at the porthole himself, most nights, watching the river with the long patience of a man who had watched rivers for longer than the young woman behind him had been alive to notice one. Darkness, in Orim's long experience of it, was not inherently stable, and a witness cost nothing and had on two occasions in sixty years saved his life, which was a poor rate of return for the trouble except that both occasions had been the kind that only needed saving once. Doyle had powered down against the far hull, boots still on, hands closed loosely around nothing, in the specific posture of a unit that had idled in places it did not trust long enough that the posture had stopped being a decision and become a default. Orim watched this the way he watched the casks: not because the information was urgent, but because a man who waits for urgency to start looking is a man who has already missed the useful window.

Lyra slept four hours, badly, favoring the shoulder that had taken the courier's groove into it decades before the infusions asked her body to be a different shape than the one that groove had been worn into. He watched her work the joint with her fingers in the gray part-light before she'd admit to being awake, the specific motion of a woman convincing a joint that rotation was still, technically, on offer. He said nothing about that either. There were things worth mentioning to a person and things better left to occur to her privately, and the border between the two categories was one of the few useful things sixty years bought a man that thirty did not.

The days on the Houndstooth established their shape by the second one, mostly because Orim built it that way and did not announce the building.

He catalogued the hold as he always catalogued a hold — touching the casks, reading the labels, weighing the bales in his hands and setting them down with the mild dissatisfaction of a man putting a thing away in a space built for something smaller — and, without any particular announcement, he began talking to Doyle.

It was not conversation in the sense two people generally mean the word. Orim named a cask's contents and asked a plain question about it — what the substance weighed in Doyle's own trade, what his people would once have used it for — and Doyle answered in three or four flat words, correct, complete, and no more than the question required. Orim nodded, moved to the next cask, and asked something else, small and concrete, in the manner of a man testing a lock with the easiest key first, before he tries the ones that matter.

He did this for the better part of a day before Lyra noticed what he was doing, and he did not tell her what he was doing after she noticed, because the question of why a Keeper of Orim's years wanted a manufactured soldier from an unnumbered Sphere talking about crate weights was not a question Orim intended to answer for anyone aboard this barge, including, on some mornings, himself.

By the close of that first day Doyle had answered nine of Orim's questions outright and let four go by unanswered, and Orim had learned, from the shape of what was withheld rather than what was given, that the man was disciplined in a way that had nothing to do with training.

He watched Doyle at the porthole again that afternoon, from the far end of the hold, at an angle that let him see both the glass and the side of the man's face without appearing to look at either. Lyra worked at her own notebook somewhere behind him; he did not need to see her to know she'd found her own angle on the same view. Two people watching a third from two directions, and none of them announcing it — a configuration Orim had built more than once in forty years, though never before out of simple appetite. He saw the distinction and did not examine it further.

The Veil River in its lowland reaches ran through country Orim had charted the way a man charts a debt he intends to collect eventually: checkpoint positions, road conditions in autumn, the taste of the water at the midpoint wells. He had made this passage more times than he had troubled to count — a man stops counting the things that no longer surprise him, and starts counting only the ones that might. The banks here ran soft and low, cut by old drainage channels into long straight fields the river bent around rather than through, poplar windbreaks standing in files along the fieldlines, a church tower showing gray above a stand of elm two bends ahead and then gone again behind the rise.

Doyle was doing nothing Orim recognized as useful. Not alarm. Not assessment. A sustained, absorbed attention, the kind a man pays to something he is trying to reconcile with something he already knows and cannot quite make sense of. His gaze tracked the poplar line as the barge went by it, and something in his face went still in a way that had nothing to do with rest.

He said nothing.

That was the whole of it, and Orim had seen enough men over enough decades to know that the whole of it was frequently the important part. The door came open behind Doyle's eyes for the length of a breath — Orim was old enough at this particular skill to see it happening in real time, though no better than anyone else at seeing what stood behind the door — and then it closed again, quick as it always did, before anyone could see what was there. Doyle had words for whatever he'd seen. He simply was not spending them. He watched the poplars fall away behind the barge's wake with the specific grief of a man revisiting a photograph he'd chosen not to pass around the room, and then he put his chin back on his forearms, and the moment settled wherever such moments settled, behind whatever door he'd just shut on it.

Orim made no note. He had long since stopped needing paper for the things that mattered to him, which was either a mercy or the reason he could not always say, afterward, what exactly it was he had wanted to know.

That night, the sound came first.

Third hour past dark, and Orim was already at the porthole — he slept less than most men expected of someone his age, and had stopped correcting the expectation decades before, since a man assumed to be resting is a man nobody watches. The vibration reached the hull before the eye caught anything: not through air but through water and keel-wood, a low rhythmic grinding old enough in his memory that he had the name for it before he'd finished waking fully into the thought.

"Rail yards," he said, without turning, when he heard Lyra sit up behind him. "A kilometer north. Night freight."

She came and stood beside him. The river ahead went dark, and then did not — a flash from the north moving south, blue-white, sharp-edged, nothing like fire, climbing and spreading the way a signal moves down a long corridor faster than anything is actually moving it. The flash lit a slice of the river — dock pylons, shallow-draft barges at anchor, flat black water — and went dark, and the next flash, further south, showed none of it, carrying the signal on.

Doyle came alert somewhere in the first pulses and reached the glass without crowding it, the same unhurried economy he gave anything that demanded his attention.

"Fire?" he asked, flat and certain, in the tone of a man naming the nearest thing he had available for comparison, and wrong.

"No," Orim said. "Halvenoire." A proper noun, three syllables, the kind of word a place invents only after it has needed to invent it many times over — Verlaine's ordnance proving ground, upriver past the third bend, where the arms foundries tested what the foundries built before any of it reached a soldier's hands. He heard Lyra's pen start behind him before she was likely fully aware she'd opened the notebook. A name Entos had thought worth minting specifically was a name worth having on paper; he had learned that rule from men considerably worse at survival than either of the two people currently standing in this hold, which was not, strictly, a compliment to anyone present.

Doyle repeated the word back once, exact — the shell rendered everything exact — and did not ask what it meant beyond what he had already worked out from light and sound. Orim named the fish stock upriver that fed on the display, and the guild that held netting rights past the third bend, and Doyle took both facts with the same unhurried care he gave anything about this world he had not yet had reason to learn — not the care of a man for whom speech came slowly, but the care of a man learning a country he intended to survive in.

The river went dark and stayed dark. The grinding faded north. Orim listened to Lyra close the notebook, then open it again to add a line he did not need to see to guess the shape of. There was always origin-Sphere documentation, somewhere, for a fact like that one. Entos documented everything. Orim had spent forty years inside that habit of documentation without once, publicly, admitting what he thought of it.

The manifest had come the first night, left on the cask nearest Lyra's head while she slept — by the barge master, ostensibly, though Orim's ability to move through enclosed spaces without disturbing anything in them was not, he would have said if anyone had asked, entirely incidental to the arrangement. Nobody asked.

He watched her read it in an order that told him something about her before she'd said a single word about the contents — not back to front, as commercial cargo was conventionally read, highest value first, but straight through, page by page, the way a woman reads who has decided that skipping ahead is how a person misses the thing that matters. He approved of the method without saying so. Approval, offered aloud, tends to change the thing being approved of; Orim had broken enough good methods that way in his first twenty years of watching people to have stopped doing it in his last forty.

She reached page eight and stopped moving in the particular way people stop moving when a number does not match the number they expected. Six Scry data blocks. Not an unusual count — the guilds had normalized data blocks into freight documentation generations back, arguing successfully that information was information, that the Vigil's older requirement, a Templar signature before every transfer, was administrative weight the commercial current could no longer carry. The Vigil had not had the stomach for that fight. Orim had watched it lose the fight in real time, at an age when he still believed institutions occasionally won the fights that mattered, and had revised the belief since, quietly, without troubling anyone with the revision.

The first block: Sphere 14, agricultural, contact duration 0.3 seconds. Standard. The third: a Sphere 9 dyeing method, 0.8 seconds, unremarkable. Orim let her read through both without comment — his habit with most things — on the theory — unstated, unconfirmed, and not one he would have defended if pressed — that a person arrives at a fact faster and holds it more firmly when nobody has handed it to her.

The fifth block stopped her outright.

Origin Sphere: [designation by number only, no catalogue entry]. Method: [contact, surface membrane, 1.4 seconds]. Data class: manufacturing, secondary. Subject: spring-steel alloying method (notation format: see attached sample). Observed at production facility. Transfer to: Henkin Consolidated Arms, Atrament branch.

The notation sample was attached — a half-sheet of heavy paper, folded once, a column of symbols Orim had seen cross this river a hundred times over twenty years and had taken, the same as everyone else on the Veil, for Calyx's standard freight notation, because no reason had ever presented itself to take it otherwise. He had stopped believing that particular reading some years before this crossing. He did not offer the revision to Lyra now. A fact a person is allowed to arrive at herself sits in her differently than a fact she is handed, and Orim had use, eventually, for facts that sat in a person firmly.

Doyle was three meters away, curled against the far hull, the faint uneven ticking of a shell cooling somewhere behind his plating, hands closed around nothing in the specific way of a unit that had recently held something and expected to need it again.

"Six," Orim said, from the porthole, when he judged she'd had long enough with it.

"I counted."

"The fifth and sixth are from the same sphere. The one without a number."

"Yes."

"The notation on the sample sheet." He let the sentence stop there, in the particular way he let sentences stop when he had already said the thing he meant and was only waiting to find out whether the other party had understood it. Most people took a moment. Lyra took less than most.

"How long have you known?" she asked.

"Since we found him." He turned from the glass wearing the expression he wore when he was about to say something that sounded like a joke and was not entirely one. "I've been looking at small things for sixty years. When a man reacts to a notation format, I notice. It's my gift."

His gift was Scrying, minor-class, contractual — which meant that at fifteen he had pressed his palm to a membrane he had not been told was alive until afterward, felt it give and warm under his hand like something holding its breath, and watched the small burn of contact close over and scar shut by the time he'd walked home that evening, a wound like any other, healed like any other, no mark left to show the guild recorder what it had cost him — and had done so eleven more times over the following six months, until the contractual minimum was satisfied. The Calyx guild paid his pension for it still. He had never volunteered whether it hurt. Nobody, in forty years, had asked him in a way that made the answer worth giving.

"It might be coincidence," Lyra said.

"It might." He agreed easily, the way he agreed with most things that weren't true, when agreeing cost him nothing and disagreeing would have cost him the rest of the conversation. "So might the rifle."

The rifle sat in the barge master's strong-chest two decks up. She'd had it looked at in Bressant's harbor quarter by two dockyard smiths Orim knew, sixty years of combined experience between the two of them, and both had told her, separately, without conferring, that they had never seen anything like it — that the metal had no name either man could put to it, that the notation on the receiver plate belonged to no system either recognized. The second smith had said, It's very precisely made, in the tone of a man who had expected to be unimpressed and had not managed it. Orim had arranged for her to hear that particular sentence from that particular man. He had not told her so, and did not intend to.

He had made the suggestion himself, three hours a day, delivered as a professional opinion and understood by all parties as an order. If you don't get him talking, he'd told her, you will spend the rest of this assignment guessing. I am too old to guess. It was true, as far as it went. It was also not the whole of the reason, and Orim had long ago stopped expecting himself to hand over the whole of a reason to anyone, including, some mornings, the version of himself that did the deciding.

The three hours were never consecutive. They assembled out of whatever the barge's schedule, Ardea's watch rotation, and Doyle's unpredictable idle hours allowed, and Orim listened to most of them from wherever the hold's angles let him listen without appearing to. Doyle gave Lyra the same designation and soldier's account he'd given at Bressant, refined only by repetition — rank, unit, the plainest facts of the taking — and past that, for the better part of the first day, very little.

She asked him where he was from. He said he had been a soldier.

She asked what he had done before that. He said he had been a soldier a long time.

It was not evasion in the sense of a lie. It was a man deciding, question by question, which door in the house he was willing to open, and closing every other one without apology. Orim had used variations of the same method himself, on rooms full of men, for forty years — some of whose doors he had opened by force rather than patience, in earlier decades, when he had believed force was occasionally the more honest tool. He did not offer this comparison aloud.

Doyle answered anything factual and present-tense without hesitation — the barge's heading, the hour, the weather. He answered anything about his own body and its function with the same flat completeness. He turned every question about before into a shorter answer than the question had asked for, and did it cleanly enough that it took Lyra two days to notice she was being managed. Orim had noticed by the second hour. He said nothing about that either, on the theory that a woman who discovers a thing herself trusts the discovery more than one who is handed it — a theory he had built an entire method on and had never once, in forty years, bothered to verify.

Orim watched her make Doyle a page, the first night — the barge's route out of Bressant, the two stops between the harbor and Atrament, the little she knew of what waited at each — in her own hand, on her own authority, without asking herself too hard whether it was standard practice to hand a found man from an unnumbered Sphere her own working notes. He did not tell her it was not standard practice. He had done stranger things himself for stranger reasons and had generally found the doing more instructive than the reasoning.

Doyle carried the page folded in his coat for two days before she noticed he'd kept it. He held it a long moment before looking up and saying, flat and toneless and entirely clear: "You made this for me."

Orim, from his corner, saw exactly what that sentence cost the room, on both sides of it, and put the observation nowhere in particular.

At the second hour past midnight, on the third night of the passage, Doyle came alert with a long creak of boards and boots on the planking, and Orim — who had not, strictly, been asleep — was at the lamp before the boards finished creaking.

Doyle sat against the cask nearest Lyra's corner. Not close enough to be a problem. Close enough to be an intention.

Orim left the lamp burning low. He had learned, forty years back, to leave a light burning whenever he expected an interruption of this particular shape, in whatever room he happened to be occupying at the time. Doyle looked worse in lamplight than he had in the grain bin at Bressant. The scoring along his cheek-plate had dulled from a bright fresh scrape to the flatter, oxidized cast metal took on after days of exposure, and the rim of his oval receptor had gathered a fine grit in its seams that no amount of wiping quite lifted. There was no age to put on him and no scale that would have held one. What there was, instead, was wear, and there was more of it on him now than there had been three days back — the kind of wear that does not come from distance travelled, but from a door being opened and shut too many times behind a face otherwise built not to show it.

Doyle held the manifest out, open to the page marked with a sliver of wood. Nobody had seen him take it. Orim put the quiet borrowing where he'd put the boots, which was nowhere Lyra could see him putting anything.

"I'll tell you four words," Doyle said. Flat. Exact. The voice the shell gave him, the one that made people mistake care for slowness the first week and never again after. "Not the rest of it. Not tonight."

Orim said nothing. He had found, across a great many years and a great many rooms, that the first silence after an offer like that one is worth more than any question a man could ask into it, and he was not inclined to spend the silence cheaply on Lyra's behalf, or his own. He nodded at her instead — a small motion, the kind that could have meant write it down or could have meant nothing, and let it mean whichever of the two she needed it to mean. She uncapped her pen.

"What's in the hold?" Orim asked, mildly, looking at the casks rather than at Doyle — a man asking a question he already half expected the shape of the answer to, careful not to seem to be waiting for it. "Something built for a purpose. Not this barge's purpose."

He was not asking about the Houndstooth's hold, and both of them understood this, and neither of them said so.

Doyle looked at the hold around them a long moment — the casks, the bales, the enclosed wooden structure of the thing — and, Orim judged, at something beyond it that was also enclosed, also structured, built around a function that had nothing to do with carrying river freight.

"Factory," he said.

Lyra wrote it in the left column, across from nothing, because there was nothing yet to put beside it.

Orim let the silence run. He had asked one question in sixty years that he genuinely did not know the shape of the answer to, and this was not that question, though he would not have told anyone which one had been.

Doyle was quiet long enough that Lyra nearly spoke. Orim caught her eye and did not shake his head — he had found, decades back, that stopping a person mid-motion teaches them less than letting the motion complete and fail on its own — but she read something in his stillness and held the pen instead.

"Smoke," Doyle said.

She wrote that too, and looked up, and found him watching the page rather than her — the particular attention of a man checking that a thing he'd said out loud had actually survived the trip to paper.

"What kind?" Orim asked. Small. Concrete. The same key he tried on every lock first, on casks and on men equally.

Doyle was quiet a moment. "The kind that comes from the process," he said. Then, after a pause long enough that Orim understood exactly what it had cost: "And the kind that's what's left when the process is finished with a place. I don't know which one I mean. Both, maybe."

Orim did not press it. There is a version of patience that looks identical to kindness from the outside and is not always the same thing on the inside, and he had stopped, decades ago, needing anyone to be able to tell the two apart by looking at him.

The third word took longer than the first two combined. Doyle looked through the porthole at the gray beginning to be something other than absolute dark — the interval between night and morning that had no clean name in most of the languages Orim had bothered to learn, and he had bothered to learn a great many.

"Morning," Doyle said, and something in his voice on that one word sat differently than it had on the other two, though Orim could not yet have said in what particular way — only that the difference stayed with him, something he intended to return to, and did not return to yet.

The fourth word came immediately after, flat, in the tone of a thing held in the mouth too long and needing, finally, to leave it.

"Gone."

Doyle sat with his hands on his knees, looking at the four words on the page, and Orim watched him decide something — a man's hands generally confess before his face does, in Orim's long experience of both, faces having had more practice at composure.

Factory. Smoke. Morning. Gone.

"That's the whole of it," Doyle said. "Tonight."

There was more sentence than that, and all three of them in the hold knew it. Four words handed over the way a man hands over a single photograph from a stack he has no intention of showing the rest of — enough to prove the stack exists, no more.

"A factory," Orim said, at last, looking not at Doyle but at the four words on the page — a man studying a lock, not the person he's asking to open it. "Yours."

Not a question. He left it room to be answered like one anyway — the same room he left most of his statements, because a door forced open teaches a man less than a door he believes he opened himself.

Doyle nodded once. Not resistance. The specific tension of a man confirming something he had been trying, in his own economy, to say for longer than this conversation had existed.

"You came from there," Orim said, very slowly, and did not point at anything, because he did not need to.

"Yes."

Orim let that sit in the hold a long moment, unremarked — giving the fact time to settle before deciding what to make of it. He had what he had come for. Whether Doyle understood that this had been, from the first cask question three days back, precisely the shape of thing Orim had been fishing toward — that was a separate matter, and one Orim did not resolve for him, that night or any other.

He didn't sleep again that night, and did not trouble himself over the lack of it; sleep had stopped being owed to him at an age well behind him now, and he had made his peace with the arrears.

Ardea came below at first light to count the provisions, looked at Lyra, then at Doyle — idled somewhere around the fifth hour, hands finally open — then at Orim, with the expression of a woman who had several questions and had correctly decided none of them were hers to ask.

"We'll see Atrament inside of four hours," Ardea said, to no one in particular.

"I know," Orim said, before Lyra could.

Ardea accepted this with the professional grace of a woman who recognized when the answer had already been decided by someone senior to her watch rotation, and went to count provisions.

Orim made the cup of something he made when he judged a person needed warming and did not want to make a performance of caring about it — not quite tea, not quite anything else — and handed it down to Lyra without comment. She drank it. He watched her decide, from the taste, that it was bad, the way everyone who had ever drunk it from him eventually decided, and said nothing to spare either of them the argument.

"Four words," she said.

"I heard." He had done rather more than hear them, though he did not correct the verb.

"You were supposed to be sleeping."

"I was listening. It's different." He settled his back against the hull. His joints cracked when he moved; he had stopped noticing the sound himself years before this crossing, though he understood it still unsettled people new to him. "Four words that go together like a sentence."

"The shape of a sentence. I don't have the rest of it yet."

"Factory," he said. "Smoke. Morning. Gone."

"Yes."

He was quiet a moment, weighing how much of the next thing was worth handing over, in exactly the manner he had spent three days teaching Doyle to weigh the same question, without ever once naming the resemblance aloud. "In my first contracted Scry session, the membrane I was put in contact with was the size of my palm. I was told it was a developed specimen. An old one. They didn't tell me what that meant until later." He looked out at the river. "I was given nineteen questions to hold in mind during contact. I managed twelve before the session was called. The guild recorder filed the results immediately; I was not permitted to review them." He paused, the pause doing most of the work the sentence around it declined to do. "Standard practice. I never found it strange until recently."

Lyra said nothing.

"The notation on the manifest," he said. "The same as his rifle."

"Yes."

"Someone documented that sphere very thoroughly." He did not say before. He did not say before it burned. He looked at the porthole, then at Doyle's idled shape, then at the manifest in her lap. "Thorough documentation. Filed in the commercial freight system. Sitting between grain and wine in every barge manifest going north for-" He stopped himself, the way a man stops on a step he has just noticed is rotted. "How long does a guild archive hold its records?"

"Sixty years," she said. "Older than that, they're at the central repository in Verlaine."

"Sixty years," he said. "At minimum." He turned back to the glass. "Good documentation," he said, in the tone he used for things that were not good at all — a tone he had been using, on and off, his entire career, for the same handful of things, and had never once, to date, used correctly in front of anyone who could have done something about it.

The sky changed before the smell did.

Orim noticed it first — he'd crossed this stretch of sky enough times that changes announced themselves to him the way a familiar face announces a new scar — the gray shifted in tone rather than temperature, a faint yellowish cast, as though the air between the barge and the horizon had been filtered through something warmer and sustained and industrial before it reached the glass. He said nothing about it. There was nothing useful to say about a thing everyone aboard was about to smell for themselves inside the half hour.

The smell arrived twenty minutes later. Coal first — not coal in storage, cold and damp, but coal that had burned long enough to pass through all its stages and settle into the char-and-mineral note of a furnace in continuous operation. Behind it, rendered fat, tallow reduced to its parts for candle and lubricant. Behind that, something sharper: hot metal, the mineral edge Orim remembered from the first time he'd made this passage, decades back now — Atrament's entry smell, industrial, the kind a man remembers without meaning to. He recognized it again. It felt the same as it always had. It did not sit with the same weight.

Doyle came up from his corner when the smell changed, at the porthole before anyone turned to look for him — not crowding, simply present, as he always was whenever something demanded it of him. The ticking under his plating had slowed. His face was doing the thing it did over the manifest, the door blown briefly open, closed again before anyone could see what was behind it.

The sound arrived before the stacks did. Orim felt it in his sternum first — he'd felt it on every crossing for longer than he cared to count precisely — a low continuous vibration, not rhythmic like the rail yards but constant, the acoustic signature of processes that do not pause for dark or morning or season. It separated, as the barge drew closer, into its parts: the percussion of the forges, regular as a pulse; a higher mechanical whine that had run long enough to find its frequency and settle there; and beneath both, a ground-note that was less a sound than a pressure against the ribs, the kind of thing a body registers before the ears do.

Then the stacks appeared at the river's bend — dark verticals against the yellowed sky, eight of them, then nine, then more as the bend opened onto the full waterfront. Taller than Orim remembered, or the same height and he was only reading them differently now, carrying different things than he had the last time. The smoke coming off them was the color of the sky itself, already absorbed into it, indistinguishable at any distance he could measure, moving south the same direction the barge was moving, so that no one aboard could have said with any confidence where the stacks ended and the sky began.

Doyle stood very still at the glass.

Orim did not need to see the man's face to know its shape. He had the words for the sentence already; he had built three days toward exactly this arrival, one cask and one small patient question at a time, and whether that building had been done in Doyle's interest, or in some other interest entirely — the Vigil's, Entos's, his own, some private reasoning Orim had kept to himself for forty years without once explaining it to anyone — was not a question Orim answered for himself either, standing at that porthole, watching the fifth word arrive before anyone had asked for it.

Lyra held the notebook up to Doyle, open to the four words, the way a person holds up a door they are not asking someone to walk through, only to notice.

He looked at the four words. He looked at the stacks. Something in him that had been carefully, professionally held for a long time went briefly uncontrolled — Orim watched it happen with the same attention he gave everything, and felt, for exactly as long as it lasted, considerably older than his years rather than merely as old as them — and then controlled itself again. He took the pen and, without being asked, wrote a fifth word beneath the others, small and precise and entirely legible.

Home.

Nobody asked him to explain it. Orim said nothing about it either, and found, not for the first time this crossing, that he was keeping more to himself than he generally allowed himself to keep.

The stacks kept coming. The smell thickened. Orim stood at the porthole of a barge called the Houndstooth, watching a city he had crossed to more times than he'd troubled to count arrive out of a yellow sky the same as it always had, and did not tell anyone in the hold — not Lyra, not Doyle, not, this particular morning, himself — which part of the last three days he had spent drawing the man out for his own good, and which part he had spent simply watching to see what the water gave up on its own, the same patient accounting he'd kept for most of the last forty years.

The Houndstooth put them off at the Atrament wharf an hour past dawn. Lyra marked the hour the way she marked everything: against the light, not the bell, because bells in a foreign port could not be trusted and the sun could. Ardea stayed at the customs shed to fight the barge master's manifest down to a figure the dockmaster would stamp without opening the main hold.

Lyra had watched her do this in six ports now. It was a discipline like any other — patience, a documented willingness to yield at the exact moment yielding cost least, and the specific skill of looking beaten while winning. Ardea was good at it. She would need an hour. Possibly more.

Lyra left Doyle at the edge of the customs square with an order in Entos he was learning well enough now to take orders in — watch, do not be seen to watch — and walked the harbor front alone — the same read she gave any perimeter before she trusted it.

This was not the Atrament she had heard men describe in taverns further south, the Forge Kingdom of ordered stone and gearwork precision. This was its back. The side that faced the river and dealt with the outside world, and so wore none of the interior's polish. Stevedores. Customs men. A fish seller working a flat boat off the pilings. The iron-filing smell she had come to associate with Atrament foundries, carried on air too cold for the season by a week she'd have put money on. Everything here was built for work and judged by nothing else. She approved of it, in the detached way she approved of a wall that did its job and asked no praise for doing it.

Orim was at the fish stall. He had not been aboard the Houndstooth. He had not, to her knowledge, been in Bressant. He was eating smoked river fish and watching the harbor with the loose, unhurried attention of a man with nowhere to be — a posture she had learned, over the length of this campaign, to distrust on sight.

"You should have been three days behind me," she said.

"I was," he said. "Then I wasn't."

He'd taken the western pass instead of the river — faster in the thaw, he said, and he'd spent a summer in these uplands sixty years past for reasons he did not presently care to itemize, and had kept the shortcut in reserve since. He said this the way a man reports the price of sardines. She judged it as she'd learned to judge everything Orim told her: true. incomplete. sufficient for present purposes. It was not trust. It was the closest thing to trust that worked.

He had also wanted a day in Atrament ahead of her arrival.

"For what?" she asked.

"To ask questions," he said. "People answer honestly in the day before a party arrives. Once you're standing in front of them, they remember you'll hear it, and revise."

She marked that too. Extremely useful. Possibly extremely unsettling. She didn't have to choose between the two.

Back at the square, Doyle had not shifted from where she'd set him — the same steadiness she expected from a picket holding position without being told twice. Three men had taken up the far end in the specific arrangement of watchers who have decided to watch something without yet deciding what they mean to do about it. Two wore the Atrament forge-mark on the left pauldron, army issue, stamping worn but present. The third had cut his away — the thing a man did in the Forge Kingdom when the army and he parted on terms neither side cared to put in writing.

She had seen the type in a dozen garrison towns. Men who'd walked out of one war and now stood at the seam of another, waiting to learn if it paid better than the last one had. She did not look at them straight on. She looked at Doyle instead, at the blank plane of his face, and found he wasn't looking at them either. He was watching a flat-bottomed river runner at the far end of the wharf, bow low, a crew moving cargo up from the hold in the patient, over-long rhythm of men working past the end of a shift that should have ended.

"What do you see?" she asked, arriving at his shoulder.

"Eleven," he said. A pause. "Not crew."

She counted for herself. Cargo hands, the manifest team at the gangway, the clerk with his board, and two men holding the forward dock lines who were dressed for nothing that required dock lines. Eleven, if you counted right. It came out right.

She looked back at the three men on the square. They were watching the same ship.

"Them?" she asked.

"Counting," Doyle said.

She marked the square, the ship, and the three watchers down as one problem — not our engagement, do not close with — and turned to find Orim already at her left shoulder. She had not heard him arrive. She let that go too, and moved on, because a woman who stopped to be unsettled by every unsettling thing on this road would never get anywhere.

"The fish stall was not purely for the fish," he said, in the tone he used for things he expected her to have already worked out herself.

"It faces the dock."

"A man who sells fish at dawn has seen everything that moved in the night. Oldest information exchange in any port that has one." He folded his hands into his coat. "Four ships ran the northern route this winter. One went missing in the passes. Verlaine's garrison logged it administrative loss and declined to follow up."

She knew the phrase. She'd read it once in a procurement review and hadn't followed it up either. She set that admission aside, one more thing about herself she did not care to examine twice.

"That ship?"

"Perhaps. The fish seller couldn't read the hull marks. He noted the paint, though. Fresh. Low, below the waterline. Put on in the dark."

Ardea reached them from the customs shed, stamped manifests in hand, wearing the look of a woman who'd paid four times a fair price and meant to write it off as a cost of doing business. She looked at Lyra, then the ship, then Doyle.

"What did I miss?" she asked.

"The fish stall," Lyra said. "And eleven men who aren't crew."

Ardea looked. "I count twelve?"

Four of them stood at the edge of a Forge Kingdom wharf that smelled of iron and river water, each arriving at a different number, none of the numbers agreeing. Lyra did not find this reassuring. A working count should hold across four separate pairs of eyes. When it didn't, that was itself the finding.

"We're not staying," she said.

"No," Orim agreed.

They left by the southern gate — not because it was safer, precisely, but because the twelve men weren't there, and because whatever the ship was or wasn't did not require an opinion from any of them. The highland road lay three hours north. It had been well-marked for three hundred years and ran exactly where they needed to go, which in Lyra's experience was the most a road ever owed you.

Doyle walked it without looking back. She had noticed that about him before. Men who look back are still deciding whether to be here. Doyle had decided some while ago, and simply hadn't mentioned when.

Nobody counted the ship again, or the men, or asked the fish seller if he'd sold to twelve after all. There would not be time, on this road or the ones after it, to follow every thread that frayed at the edges — and Lyra had already begun, without quite noticing it yet, the habit that would define the rest of her life: choosing which unraveling things she could actually afford to pull on, and letting the others fray.

The transit stamp Verlaine's border post had pressed into her papers three days south was, Orim informed her somewhere in the second hour, worth exactly nothing past the next valley. She had suspected as much and asked anyway, because a fact you've had confirmed twice is a fact you can act on without the half-second of doubt that gets people killed at checkpoints.

"The line moved," Orim said. "Then it moved back. Then it moved again, further than the first time. This valley's changed hands four times in thirty years, by the marker stones. The village at the crossing has been liberated" — he gave the word no particular weight, which was its own kind of weight — "by both Houses, more than once each. They believe neither anymore. Why would they? A garrison that frees you and a garrison that frees you back are, from where the village stands, the same event happening twice."

She understood this the way she understood any front: not as a line on a map but as a fact that expired. A stamp was proof of passage on the day it was pressed, in the place it was pressed, and nowhere else. Beyond that it was paper. She had crossed enough contested ground to know that the only durable fact on a shifting front was the shifting itself.

The road bore that out within the hour. Refugees moving both directions past each other without acknowledgment, which told her more than either group's papers would have. A cart with Verlaine markings painted over in a color that wasn't quite managing to be Atrament's. A boy of perhaps ten, watching them pass with the flat, watchful look of a child who reads which uniform is on which men, and only then decides whether to run.

And the deserters. More of them the further they went, drifting the verges in twos and threes, the way water finds low ground. She'd started sorting them without meaning to — the same triage she gave anything moving in numbers on a road she meant to keep using.

Some carried the stillness she recognized from Doyle on his worse days — men whose eyes tracked correctly and whose hands did not, or the reverse, a wrongness in the wiring that no drill she knew had a name for. Verlaine's product. Minds taken up whole into their war-constructs and returned, when they were returned at all, not quite refitted to the skull that had grown around them. Myrmidon strays. The word had come to her from Orim without her asking where he'd learned it, which she had also set aside and not asked about.

Others bore a different mark: a stiffness at the base of the skull, a raised ridge under the collar where something had been socketed and then, with more violence than care, unsocketed. Rig-pilots who had cut their own couplings and walked. Atrament's word for these, when the road bothered to have a word at all, was war-dogs — not affectionately.

She watched Doyle watch them. He did not slow. He did not speak. But something in the set of his shoulders eased by a measurable degree each time they passed a stray moving the other way, and tightened again once the distance closed back past recognition range. She understood this without needing it explained to her. A soldier reads rank on a stranger's collar before he reads the face under it. Doyle was reading something else on these men — not rank. Kind. He was among his own, on a road that had no country for either of them, and he knew it before she did.

She did not comment on it. She marked it as understood, no action required, and kept walking.
