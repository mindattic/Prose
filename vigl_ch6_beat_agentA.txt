Then the road stops being a road.

He will spend what remains to him circling that sentence and never improve on it, though it says next to nothing of what happened. Nothing came before it. No sound that did not belong to the tires, no light the wrong color except the one wrong light, the tree's bark going white and enormous in the high beams the half-second before it would have mattered, no warning any sense he owns is built to register as a warning. One instant his hands are on the wheel and the tree is coming up the way trees come up at the edge of a curve you have taken too fast a hundred times before and always made. The next instant there is no wheel, no hands he can locate, and the direction that has always, without argument, been in front of him has simply declined to go on being in front of him.

It is not pain. He will insist on this afterward, if there is an afterward: it was not pain first. Pain is a report the body carries about the body, and for one long unbearable interval he has no body to carry a report from. What he has instead is the specific, appalling sensation of being drawn. Not thrown — a crash has a direction, a windshield or a ditch waiting somewhere ahead of it to be arrived at eventually — but drawn, sideways and up and through, the way a hook set deep in a trout's lip takes the whole fish out of the river in one motion the fish never sees coming, because the fish, until that moment, did not know it was a thing suspended in something. He has lived thirty-one years inside his own skin entirely, without once understanding a man is also a thing that can be taken out of the medium he is suspended in. Something reaches into the warm dark he has occupied since before he had a word for himself — into the whiskey-loose hand on the wheel, the coal-dust lungs he got the same way every boy up that holler got them, whether or not he was still going down for them — and closes on him, and lifts.

He goes out the way a lamp goes out. Not into a darker room. Out of the having of a room at all.

The truck, a half-second later, finds the tree anyway. He does not get to take that back with him.

After the pluck there is no after, for a long while, in any sense a clock would recognize.

There is a spark, and the spark is aware, in the diminished and terrible way a spark can be aware of anything, that it is a spark and not a man, and that it is suspended in something warm and gold, thick as mead poured slow, the color of lamplight caught in a bottle of honey-water — the Amnios, he will hear it called much later, though the name when it comes will explain nothing he has not already learned the hard way — no bottom he can find and no wall to put a hand against and confirm the existence of a hand. It clings where he passes through it the way birth-fluid clings, and seals behind him seamless, as if the wound of his passing had already started healing before he was all the way through it. No up. He tries for up out of old habit, thirty-one years of a head that assumes a direction for the sky, and finds nothing the reflex can hook onto. No cold, because cold wants a surface to be felt against, and he no longer has one. No sound, because sound is air moving, and there is no air here to move — only the slow honeyed drag of the medium itself, unhurried, indifferent: water does not care what drowns in it.

There is, instead, wreckage.

It drifts past the way the debris of a wrecked truck drifts down a slow river, unhurried, indifferent to being watched, gone amber and half-dissolved in the warm gold that carries it — not pictures, but the actual broken matter of a life he recognizes as having belonged to him a very short while ago. The bottle turns past, whiskey and glass both, tumbling end over end. The county road — the dark, the tree, the cold of the gravel shoulder under a hand he no longer has — turns past close enough to reach for and already receding. France comes past in pieces that hurt worse than anything else in the gold: hedgerow clay sucking at a boot, a flat crack close enough to feel in the teeth, a hand that was Corbett's hand and then was not a hand any longer. And his mother's kitchen — oilcloth worn pale where her elbow went every evening, the kettle's two-note whistle, bread cooling under a cloth on a Thursday not unlike this one — turns slowly out of reach, and he understands, with the clean useless clarity a spark is apparently still capable of, that he is watching himself come apart, and no part of it will wait for him to say goodbye to it properly.

He tries to reach for the kitchen. There is nothing to reach with. There is only the gold, closing soft over the place the reaching happened, and under the closing a horror with no better name yet than this: that he is unborn again, a spark adrift in some other world's blood, with no body anywhere in it built to hold him.

Then there is weight, and with the weight a kind of dryness - the gold he had been drifting in gone all at once, the way water sheets off a hand hauled up out of a bucket in one motion, though there is no skin left under it for him to feel it go.

Wrong weight. Heavier in the shoulders than a man has any business being heavy, lighter somewhere in the chest where a man expects to carry something. - housing, forged of something dark, riveted at the seams, jointed at eleven working points, provenance Verlaine - not a thought so much as a reading taken off him by something else, the report arrives from nowhere, cold and complete, the way a foreman reads a tally without looking up from the board, and he understands before he wants to that the report was not a thought he had. It was the shell's, not his - the same one-way traffic as water through a sluice, no opinion asked or given.

He tries to open his eyes and there is a sensation of aperture rather than eyelid, a single oval widening onto gray light, and what it shows him is a yard. Packed earth. A high gray sky doing nothing in particular. A line of others like himself, oil-black and jointed and identical, stretching either side further than the aperture will turn to show him.

He tries to stand and the body answers half a beat late, the way an echo answers a shout after the shout has stopped meaning anything, and when it does answer it tells him nothing back. No warmth up through feet that are not feet. No ache in shoulders that carried a rifle across half of France and now carry nothing but do not say so. No breath. He is trying to breathe, out of the same blind animal habit that reached for up in the gold, and there is no rise, no fall, no air moving inside him to be moved, because there is no inside for it to move in. A numb body still tells you something, even if the something is an absence. This tells him nothing at all, and the not-telling is its own particular species of horror.

He tries to scream.

Something happens in the place a scream would have started. Air is moved - a mechanism somewhere in the jointed throat doing its designated work - and what comes out is not his voice. It is not any voice he would recognize as belonging to a living thing. A short flat tone, ugly and functional, the sound a thing makes when it is required to make a sound and given no better instrument to make it with. He hears it and understands two facts that will not separate again as long as he exists: the sound came from him, and it was not his.

They are called forward by number.

He understands this at once — understands every word of it, cleanly and complete, the way he will understand every word said to him from this hour forward, because whatever the shell is has already gone about rendering this world into sense before he can want it not to — and the understanding brings him nothing. A robed figure works down the ranks with a board, and Declan reads it the only way he has ever sized up a man with a board and a line of workers in front of him: a foreman, checking off a tally, and the tally is men. Each spark ahead of him receives a string of words that mean exactly what they say and steps forward, or is walked forward by a hand on the shoulder if it will not step on its own, and is led away through a gate he cannot see the far side of.

Before the tally, there is the oath.

A second robed figure stands at the head of the line, and over each spark in turn, before the number is even called, this one speaks — and Declan notices, cold under everything else cold in him, that this speech does not arrive the way the tally speech arrives. The tally words come flat and total, translated a half-beat ahead of any will of his own. This does not. This comes raw, unlaundered, sound before sense — and because it arrives raw, it is the only thing since the gold that reaches an ear still, somehow, his own, and not the shell's.

Requiem. In nomine. Corpus.

He knows those shapes. Not from this yard, not from any place a man could point to on a map of this world. From a one-room schoolhouse with a stove that never drew right, a teacher who made every boy in Carter County recite the same dead words whether or not they'd ever need them. From letters cut over a church door back home, and into fieldstone in a churchyard he has stood in more times than he cared to count, reading names. It is Liturgin — the plain schoolboy Liturgin nobody in Carter County has spoken outside that stove-warmed room or a Sunday liturgy in ninety years — poured over him now, here, an unbroken world away, by a robed woman who says it like the one true voice of God Almighty Himself, because to every soul in this yard but him, it is.

He understands, in the same cold half-second, two things that will never come apart again: this is the holiest sound in the whole of this place, and he is, so far as he can tell, the only creature in it who knows it is nothing of the kind. There is no one to tell. The shell hands him this world's speech whole and hands him no way to hand anyone his own — and even were there a tongue between them, who would credit the stolen man who says your God's own voice is a dead language off a country schoolhouse wall? He puts it the only place left to put anything: down, and quiet, and alone.

Then the tally reaches him, and the sound comes at him, precise, unhurried, the tone of a woman who has said this exact sentence some large number of times before and expects to say it some large number of times again before the day is through.

"Em One Oh One Triple Eight. Step forward."

He understands every word of it, whole and immediate — no lag in the sense, no fog to push through, only a flat total comprehension arriving a half-beat ahead of anything in him that could call itself a will. The numbers land first and hardest, because a number needs no permission from the rest of a sentence to mean what it means, and he understands, with a lurch that has nothing left in him to lurch with, that the numbers are his. Not his name. Not Doyle, not Ted, not Declan, not any of the three men he has been to three different sets of people who loved him or fed him bread of a Thursday morning. A string of digits assigned to a batch, in a rotation, at a station, on a board he will never read. It is the one thing in this whole gold impossible transaction unambiguously, exclusively his, and it is a number, and he hates it, and his body is already stepping forward before the hating finishes arriving, because refusing was never one of the choices on offer — only which face he wears while he answers.

He is walked through the gate. He does not look back at the line, because the aperture does not turn that way unless he tells it to, and he has not yet learned he is permitted to tell it anything at all.

The order came down that they were to hold a support trench two miles east of the barracks, and Declan held it with the rest of the line for six days before the guns changed key.

He knew the difference in his body before he understood it in his head — some report arriving from the shell that wasn't a report exactly, more a wrongness in the pressure of the air against a housing that did not, strictly, feel pressure the way a man's skin does. A long silence from the enemy line. Then a single tone, high and sustained, nothing like a shell's whistle or a mortar's cough, climbing a register no gun back home had ever needed.

Then the light.

It came low along the ground, white and continuous, a line drawn rather than a thing thrown, the way a man draws a line under a sum he intends to be final. It touched the two hundred yards of trench to his left — not violently, not even quickly, simply completely — and where it had been there was, for the length of one held breath, a silhouette of everyone who had been standing in it, men and Myrmidons both, the shapes briefly darker than the smoke behind them. Then even the silhouette went, and there was ash, and the ash did not look like it had ever been men and Myrmidons at all. It looked like a photograph left too long in a bad chemical bath.

Nothing in him had a category ready for that. He had named and known trench guns, tank plate, the particular whine of the bombers going over — all of it by his father's rule, that a man survives what he can name — and this did not have a name that survived the naming. It did not ask what it had killed. It removed the ground the question would have needed to stand on.

Afterward — he would only ever be able to say afterward, because the interval itself did not keep time the way an interval was supposed to — he found he had lost some hours he could not account for. Not sleep. Not the shell's blackout when it changed hands, which had its own particular texture by now, familiar as a held breath. This was worse: gaps, entire stretches where he knew a body had gone on standing at a post and following simple orders and he had not, so far as he could tell, been anywhere in it to see them done.

The barracks, when he came back to something like himself, did not feel like barracks. It felt like a set built to resemble one, thin at the edges, the wrong weight holding it up. He looked at his own black hands and understood, with a calm that frightened him considerably more than the light had, that none of this — the war, the yard, the tally, Doyle himself as he currently stood — was necessarily real. It would be so much simpler, some voice in him reasoned, patient and almost kind, if this whole gold-lit country were a coma dream running its course behind a pair of eyes closed on a Tennessee county road, and Aldenvast the only place that had ever actually existed. He could feel himself reaching for that explanation the way a man reaches for a rail on a dark stair — gratefully, and without checking first whether the rail was load-bearing.

He might have kept reaching for it. What stopped him was smoke — yggdra kindling, somewhere down the trench line, some cook fire relit against the evening cold — and the smell of it came to him wrong. Close to home. Not home. The exact, specific way it wasn't fir, the resin sweeter and thinner than balsam ever ran, some single degree of difference his memory could not smooth over no matter how badly the rest of him wanted it smoothed.

A coma does not build a smell that particular. A coma reaching for comfort reaches for the thing it wants, whole and correct and exactly as good as the memory demands. It does not, on its own, invent a wood that is almost fir and just barely is not, a discrepancy fine enough to ache and too fine to have been dreamed up by a mind trying to comfort itself. Only the actual world was careless enough to leave a gap like that lying around unrepaired.

He held onto the gap the way a man holds onto a handhold in the dark, because it was the one part of this whole impossible country that could not have been supplied by his own wanting.

He came back from it. Not whole — he understood, sitting with his back against the trench wall while the tally roll called names that weren't his down the line, that some portion of what had gone out with those six seconds of white light was not coming back, no matter how many hours he waited it out. But back enough. He never told a living soul what the six lost hours had almost cost him. Not a trainer. Not anyone with a board and a note to make. Some accounting a man does alone, in the dark, and keeps.

He was more careful after that about how much of himself he let go traveling to other machinery, other rooms, other men's evenings. He had learned, the hard way that mattered, exactly how much of what he was could be spent before there wasn't enough of him left standing to come back and collect the rest.

The barracks give him nothing worth keeping and the drill gives him less, and none of it matters beside the gunship.

He is put aboard it without ceremony, handled like cargo, and finds himself — the finding takes some while, the shell answering his intention a consistent half-beat behind the intention itself, a lag he will spend seven months learning to out-think rather than outrun — seated inside a turret ring on the gunship's flank, designated, he gathers from the numeral stenciled at the housing's base, the thirty-fourth of some greater number of identical rings. Turret #34. Engines somewhere forward doing a great deal of work, the deck thrumming a vibration that reaches him not through skin, which he no longer has, but through the frame itself, a borrowed sensation standing in for the true one the way a man feels a passing train through a platform rail.

- firing gear: arc emplacement, condenser bank fed off the gunship's own engines, the housing warm to the frame before the first shot, a thing the barracks talk called by a hushed name, the tone men use for weather they hope never to stand under — the report arrives behind his eyes unbidden, and he lets it pass through him the way you let a cough finish before you go on talking.

The engagement, when it comes, is brief and comes from below: not a trench line, not a column on the march, but roofs — a settlement laid out orderly along a road, chimney-smoke still climbing off half of them, the ordinary architecture of somewhere people had eaten breakfast that morning — and the mechanism built into him, not his, the shell's, the thing Verlaine wired into the housing and calls a fire-control routine, presents a solution the way a rifle presents a trigger: range, bearing, a tone in the aperture that means loose. He understands the word the instant it lands, and understanding does no favors here either; the horror arrives with nothing lost in translation. Under the word is the same cold reckoning that told him his own number was his own number, offered now toward a different end. Not a casualty count. The whole grid of it, roofs and all, reduced to the same brief silhouette a man gets when that kind of light finds him, and then to less than that.

It is not a decision he could afterward describe to a court, if there were a court, if the shell were the kind of thing courts concerned themselves with. It is closer to the pick-hand knowing, before the coal has given up anything, that the seam is ready to come away: something in him that predates the number, predates the yard, predates even France, looks at the roofs below and finds that whatever the fire-control routine is prepared to call them, he is not prepared to call them that. There is no argument to have with a mechanism that does not listen, and no half-beat left to have it in. He goes, instead — the same wireless slippage that had sometimes carried a stray thought loose of him without his meaning it to, called on now, on purpose, for the first time — out of Turret #34 and into the small waiting dark of his own shell, three rings down the flank, empty and cold and entirely his. It takes the length of a held breath. The turret's own aperture goes dark behind him mid-tone, the fire-control routine still patiently offering a solution to a ring with nobody left inside it to take the order.

Turret #34 does not fire. Ted Doyle, who put a bayonet through nothing in four years because opportunity and willingness never once arrived together, is three rings down the flank when the tone finally goes quiet, having gotten its answer the only way he had left to give one.

Afterward — engagement thinning below, the gunship holding its line, other turrets along the flank having done what his did not — the only report the shell gives him honestly, without the half-beat lag, without the flatness, without the sense of being told about his own body by a stranger, is smell. Burnt ozone, sharp and mineral, cordite's cousin in a world that has never heard the word cordite, some circuit in the empty ring behind him having cooked itself waiting on an order nobody was left to carry out. It reaches him the way the camp dog's bark used to, immediate and unmistakably real. It is the one true thing his new body tells him all day, and it is the smell of a room he is no longer standing in.

Nothing is done to him that a man would recognize as punishment. A different robed figure, further down the ship, makes a note on a board very like the boards from the yard, brief, and the note is the entire proceeding he ever sees of it — until the recalibration.

He understands the word before anyone uses it on him — cold and complete, arriving from nowhere he can point to. It is not destruction. Verlaine does not destroy matériel that answers to a number and can still be made to carry a rifle; it adjusts. Something in the housing is opened, and something in him — not removed, he is fairly sure of that after, though he can never afterward say exactly what changed — is turned down, the way a hand turns down a lamp rather than blows it out. He comes back from it able to stand, able to load and clean and answer to his number, everything a fault report would call fully functional. He is returned to the line inside the week.

He obeys after that. Not every order arrives easy — none of them ever have — but the half-beat where the mechanism used to wait on something in him to agree has gotten shorter, and shorter again, until he can no longer find the seam where it used to be. He notices this the way he noticed the wrong weight in his shoulders the first day: clinically, because naming what has been taken is the only ownership of it he has left. Something was his to spend once at Turret #34, and he spent it, and it bought him exactly one clean refusal and nothing else, because a man does not get a second one at the same price.

Under the flatness, where the schoolhouse Liturgin still sits set down and quiet, Declan Doyle understands he is now a fault that has been logged and corrected, in a rotation, at a station, three months shy of a green mountain nobody here has ever heard of — and there is not one soul in the whole of this gold-lit, God-haunted world he could tell any of it to, even if the shell gave him back a voice built to say it in.
