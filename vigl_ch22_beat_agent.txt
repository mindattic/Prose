Lyra put the strait at seven hundred meters, narrowest point, grey water, and didn't need the sergeant to tell her the smell under the naphtha was the thing the naphtha was burning for.

She had expected a wall. Entos called it a Gate - soldiers' habit of sizing any border down to a word small enough to carry. What she found was a shoreline: fortified towers on both banks, boats holding position against the current, bows angled into it, and between the water and the first course of stone, a burn crew of six working a black stain with two flamethrower rigs and the discipline of men who had run this same watch longer than they cared to count.

The stain moved. Black, light-eating, coming up out of the tideline in slow fingers that found the mortar joints and settled there.

"Ichor," Orim said. Unnecessary. She had read the word in six after-action reports and the color still hadn't prepared her.

The burn crew worked it by the book: short bursts, held low, one man on the line while a second covered the water at his back in case the stain reconsidered the fire as an argument. It went up clean - no smoke worth remarking on, a bright collapsing crackle, the stink of scorched rot under the naphtha - and where it burned the stone came up pale after, cauterized rather than healed. She watched a finger of it reach a coil of dock cable, watched the fire take the cable before it closed, watched the crewman's face register nothing at all. She took that in too.

"Fire's the only thing that takes it clean," the garrison sergeant told her, when asked.

"Cut it, it grows back doubled. Burn it, it stays burned."

He reported it with the flat unsurprise of a man reporting weather.

The officer had a name for the man leaning against the log-house's north wall, out of the wind, watching them with the specific patience of someone who has been asked to wait before and expects to be asked again. "Ser Vaen," he said, when Lyra asked. "Pallor. He escorts the assessment teams in, when there's an assessment team foolish enough to want one."

Vaen straightened off the wall at his own name, unhurried. Older than Lyra by a decade at least, built along the same lines the Gifted Ceremony built everyone along, though his had clearly been given longer to work — broader through the chest than she was, a scar closing one eyebrow at an angle that made him look permanently skeptical of whatever he was looking at, which today was her.

"Templar Ocipheus," he said. Not a question. Word traveled ahead of a Templar with her particular week behind her.

"I need an escort past the checkpoint," she said. "Not an assessment. Further in than an assessment goes."

He looked at her the way a man looks at a bill he already knows he can't pay and intends to pay anyway. "How far in."

"As far as it takes."

He was quiet a moment, weighing something that wasn't her authority — she had none left worth the name — and wasn't the pay, because there wasn't any offered. "I've buried six of my own on this ground," he said. "None of them past the first quarter mile."

"I know."

"You don't," he said, without heat. "Nobody who hasn't done it knows." He looked past her, at Doyle, at Orim, at Wren, cataloguing them the unhurried way a professional catalogues anything he's about to be responsible for. "But I've watched enough good people go in with a bad reason and come out fine, and enough with a good reason not come out at all, and stopped believing the reason has anything to do with it." He shrugged, the specific economy of a man who has made this decision before and knows exactly what it costs to make it again. "I'll take you as far as the ground lets me."

"Thank you."

"Don't," he said. "Thank the ones who come back."

Vaen was posted at the second tower, and she had taken the whole measure of him before she'd finished crossing the yard — the way you read weather closing in, except the weather in this case was standing on two legs and was easily twice the height of the tower door he'd have to duck through to use it.

Thirteen feet, near enough that counting the last inches felt like an argument not worth finishing. He'd braided his hair — russet-red, going copper where the light caught it — into a single rope thick as a mooring line, tied off at the small of his back so it wouldn't catch on anything that would never reach high enough to matter. His clothes were sewn from bed linen: dozens of sheets, by the look of the seams, stitched into something that could pass at a distance for a soldier's coat, because no loom in Entos wove a bolt wide enough to dress a man his size, and somebody — a quartermaster, a wife, a sister, Lyra never learned who — had solved that the only way it could be solved.

He was sitting when they arrived. Sitting, because standing put his head above the tower crenellations, and the garrison had asked him, politely, not to give the far bank a silhouette to shoot at. Sitting, he was still taller than Doyle standing.

"Little Sister," he said, and got up anyway to say it properly, unfolding in stages like a derrick going vertical. Not condescension. Recognition — one soldier naming rank to another — and under it something that would have been pity, if pity were a thing this man still had storage for.

"You're posted here?"

"My turn in the rotation." He said it without weight, reporting his own conscription like weather. "Champions cycle the Gate, House by House. A Loera corsair runs eight weeks each way from wherever you're called from, so nobody crosses for less than a year's duty — the travel alone would waste the posting otherwise. I'm eight months into mine."

Wren, who did not startle easily, had gone very still and was working out how much wider the doorway at Sal Vento would have to be, if it ever came to that. Doyle ran his three-height scan out of pure procedure and got nothing back in any of the three bands that matched what his eyes were reporting — the Champion registered, on the shell's own ledger, as unclassified, mass exceeds calibration. Orim didn't scry him. He didn't need a rod to confirm it.

"Eighteen infusions," Vaen said, before Lyra could ask, because everyone always wanted to ask and it was faster to answer first. "Near the top of what the Liturgy's ever signed a writ for. One a year, near enough, from my fourteenth to my thirty-second, and then they told me that was all my frame could carry without the bones going soft at the joints instead of thick. I stopped growing taller four years ago. I have not stopped eating."

He said this last with the particular satisfaction of a large man who has never once in his life been asked to leave food on a plate.

Lyra carried three infusions, and had believed, until this exact minute, that she understood the size of that gap. She did not. She asked him — because someone had to, because the question sat in the space between them like a held breath — whether it changed anything else. The mind. What a person was, under that much rebuilt bone?

"No." He said it simply, without the weight she'd braced for. "Whatever the infusions grow, they don't touch that. I think the same as I thought at fourteen. Slower, some might say" — and here something moved at the corner of his mouth that in a smaller man would have been called a grin — "though I've had thirty years of people saying it behind a hand they thought I couldn't see over."

He asked about her sister, having heard — everyone at every post between Ocipheus and the strait had heard by now — that the Athen-Moor girl carried a letter she hadn't opened. He asked without prying, the way a big man learns to ask everything, because a big man who pries gets treated like a threat and a big man who doesn't gets treated like company.

He told her, unprompted, that he meant to walk them across himself. Not the boat — the boat was for cargo and for men who needed the water between them and Sinter to feel like a margin. He meant to go in on foot, at the head of the column, because a man his size standing between a Sinterspawn breed and four exhausted travelers was worth more than any letter of passage the garrison could stamp. He'd walked escorts in before. None past the second waystation. He thought, this time, with eight months of the ground already under him, he might make the third.

Lyra let herself, for the length of that sentence, believe him. It felt like setting down a pack she hadn't noticed she was still carrying.

It happened forty minutes later. Too fast to take in, in the moment; too slow after, in memory, every second stretched to carry what it held.

The water went concave first — not a wave, the absence of one, a bowl of surface drawing down around a point forty meters off the near tower, like water pulling toward a drain before the drain's been found. What came up wasn't the long dark coiling mass the burn crew had trained their whole watch to expect. It came up jointed, plated at the head and thorax like something that should have fit in a net, if the net had been woven for a creature the size of a river barge — and behind the plated mass, uncoiling out of the water in lengths that kept arriving after the eye had already decided it was done arriving, a tail built of nothing so simple as bone. Rib on rib on rib, hooked and hinged, clutching at the air. The burn crew, already moving, already screaming for the flame lines, had no word ready for it. Someone shouted shrimp — the first small true thing a mind can hold when the large true thing won't fit — and it stuck, because soldiers name what they can bear to name and leave what they can't for later.

The fire found it and it screamed — the first sound any of them had heard a Sinterspawn make that wasn't pressure felt in the sternum, an actual voice, wet and enormous, and for one clean second Lyra let herself believe the fire alone would be the ending.

Then the tail came around, and it wasn't aiming for the tower. It was aiming for the one thing on the quay that stood a chance of stopping it before it reached the boats, and Vaen was already moving to make sure that guess was correct.

He went in with nothing but his hands and the flat of a blade too small for him, drawn more from muscle memory than any hope it would matter, and he was right that it wouldn't — the tail took him around the chest before the blade found anything to open, and the ribbed length of it closed like a fist, slow and total, no hurry in it at all, the unhurried certainty of something that had never once needed to hurry.

Lyra heard bone. Not his — hers, in the private register behind her sternum where his ribs and her ribs had briefly become the same fact.

He did not go down easy. Both hands found the seam where the plated thorax met the base of that terrible tail — the one soft place a creature that size hadn't thought to armor, because nothing before this had ever had the leverage to reach it — and he drove both thumbs in and pulled, forcing open something that was never meant to be opened by hand.

Lyra heard the spine go. A sound like a green branch breaking inside a drum, once, and then the whole jointed length of the creature's tail lost its grip in the same half-second it lost its argument with gravity, uncoiling off him in a boneless fall that took the last of its scream down into the water with it, and did not come back up to finish arguing.

It let go because the wound was more than even something that size could stand on. It let go too late for the man it had been killing, and took the rest of itself, wounded and still moving, back down into the dark the fire hadn't reached — which was not the same as beaten, and everyone standing on that quay understood the difference immediately.

Vaen didn't fall so much as arrive at sitting, the way he had been sitting when they'd found him, except now nothing in him unfolded back up. Lyra reached him in four strides that felt like forty. Up close, in the last of the light, he looked less like a monument than he had all afternoon — looked, for the first time since they'd met him, like a man, tired, glad of somewhere to sit.

What he said to her then, she has never once repeated, to Orim or to anyone standing on that quay who would have given a year's wages to have heard it. She knelt with him until there was no more use in kneeling, and then she stood, and she did not cry where the burn crew could see it, and she did not tell any of them what a Champion's last accounting sounds like when there's nobody left to report it to but the person he's decided to spend it on.

Something else moved beneath the settling grey while she knelt there — vast, unhurried, gone before the eye could be sure it had been there at all, a displacement felt in the boat hulls before it was seen anywhere. Nobody named it. There would be time to name it later, on the far bank, when naming it wouldn't feel like an invitation.

Nobody moved for three seconds that felt considerably longer. Then the far tower's lieutenant was screaming the burn crew back to the line, the boats were coming about, and the water — what was left of it disturbed — settled back to grey and gave nothing more away.

Orim didn't speak for a long stretch.

"Six," he said, finally, very low. "He told you he'd buried six."

"Seven now," Lyra said.

Then: "That's what it costs to make one of them bleed. And we still have to cross."

Wren had gone pale and still, staring at the place on the quay where a very large, very gentle man had been sitting a minute before.

Doyle ran his three-height scan again, low, middle, high, and logged nothing in any of the three bands that told him what he'd just watched.

Lyra's hand had found the seal of her sister's waystation letter before she seemed to know it had moved, and then didn't break it, because there was no line in her sister's notary hand that could have held what the quay had just taken from her.

They crossed at dusk, in a boat the garrison sergeant wouldn't meet her eyes to hand over, past the scorched black stain the burn crew was already back to working, because the Ichor didn't pause for what had happened here, and past a length of quay stone still faintly warm where a man twice anyone's height had sat down for the last time.

The far bank came up out of the grey water, dark, waiting, entirely unconcerned that it had just been introduced.

The water took the boat from underneath before anyone heard it coming.

Not a wave. A wave announced itself, gave you a swell to brace against. The boat simply stopped being where it had been, canted hard to port on something that had come up under the keel and closed. After that there was only noise — wood complaining, black water over the gunwale in a sheet, Wren already moving with no order given.

She saw the tail before she saw anything else of it — long, segmented, bone-pale where the crater-things had all run to black, whipping clear of the water and back under it again almost too fast to have been real at all, wrapped once around the keel and pulling.

"Out," Doyle said, not loud, which was somehow worse than loud. "Everyone out, now, this side."

Nobody argued with him. The boat went over a second later. The water took her breath out through her teeth and did not give it back for four strokes. In those four strokes there was no Vaen, no crossing, no case at her hip. Only up, and how far.

She came up swearing. Wren was already up, one hand locked in the back of Orim's coat, walking the old man backward toward the shallows. Doyle did not surface so much as arrive — the shell too dense to float, coming up out of the silt on its feet. She counted them. Four. She made herself say it aloud. "Four."

Whatever had taken the boat did not come back. It did not need to. It had already gotten what it wanted, which was apparently only ever the boat, and by the time all four of them had found footing in water shallow enough to stand in, there was nothing left on the surface to mark that anything had been there at all — no tail, no wake, nothing but four people standing waist-deep in the last of the light, breathing.

"Something in the water," Lyra said, when she had breath enough to say anything.

"Something with a tail," Wren said. "That's all I saw. A tail, and then not-a-boat."

Lyra put her hand to the case at her hip. Still there. She counted the four of them again anyway.

She didn't speak until the crossing was most of the way done, the grey water closing behind them and the garrison's lights already a smear on the bank they'd left.

Orim didn't ask her to. He had escorted enough of the newly dead-eyed and the newly widowed across enough black water to know that a woman holding a folded quiet like that would talk when the quiet stopped being hers alone to carry, and not one half-second before.

Not the words. Whatever passed between her and Vaen at the end, she gave no one the words, and Orim understood without being told that no one ever would get them. What she gave him instead was the shape of what they added up to. Vaen had meant to walk them in himself — on foot, at the head of the column, past the second waystation and, with eight months of the ground already under him, maybe the third. He'd said as much before the water went concave, in the flat, unhurried certainty big men reserve for anything already decided — not a plan still open for argument. He would have taken the worst of the crossing into a body built to take it, and the four of them would have gone into Sinter with an escort no House and no letter of passage could have bought them at any price the Compact recognized.

That was gone now, and it was, Orim understood — not as information but as loss, the limestone-deep kind that arrives in the body before the mind has caught up with it — very possibly the worse loss. Worse than Vaen himself, which was an obscene thing to let cross a mind and he let it cross anyway, because grief does not wait politely for a body to finish sorting out what it has actually lost.

They came up out of the shallows without the boat, the crossing behind them by more effort than eleven minutes should have cost and the last of the garrison's light behind that.

They moved up the bank by habit: Lyra forward, Doyle running his scan in three bands, the Calyx boy at the waterline watching the far shore with the expression of a man who had come to understand something about himself on the crossing and had not yet decided what to do with it. Orim did not move. He had come out of the water and stopped where the bank began.

The smell was wrong. Not as a survey report would describe wrong — anomalous atmospheric and biological conditions, the standardized language built to survive an auditor rather than the thing itself. Wrong in the way his body remembered from before the documentation had existed.

The rock below the tideline was old limestone, grey-blue, shaled in the pattern particular to this shore and no other shore he had stood on in fifty years of standing on shores. He had stood on it once before. He knew, without examining the knowledge, which part of himself remembered it and which part had read about it. The distinction was not ambiguous.

He had been a child. That had been Year Zero.

Lyra came back for him. She had the gift — one of several she had not been taught and would not acknowledge — of knowing which silences wanted breaking and which wanted acknowledging before they could be walked through. She stopped at arm's reach and waited.

"The Rod," she said.

"Quiet," he said. "Not the refusal at the Forge Hearth. That was a door closing." He did not look at her. "This is quiet the way a site goes quiet after it has been active long enough to stop needing to demonstrate what it is." He let that sit. "I am familiar with this shore."

She waited. She was very good at waiting.

"A powerful island nation of Sphere 31," he said, "not unlike Ocipheus in several martial respects, endured once a tragedy much like Sinter's. At the end of a long campaign of conquest, their enemy let the power of the stars fall upon them. Those who lived were haunted the rest of their days." The water made its small continuous sound against the limestone. "They had a word for such survivors."

He picked up the Rod's case from where he had set it down without noticing he had set it down.

"Hibakusha."

He said the word as a man finally sets down something he has carried the whole of a working life without occasion to use aloud. It was heavier outside the chest than inside. He had known it would be heavier. He had arranged, across the whole of a working life, not to find out.

The others were waiting at the treeline. He walked up to where they stood, looked at the zone ahead, and said nothing more about it — which was the most he had ever said to anyone on the subject, and which he had now finished saying.

Caer Dyn was Calyx's, not Sinter's - a forward post Calyx had held since before the Compact had a name for what it was holding against, close enough to the old border that the maps still argued about which side of the line it sat on, and far enough inside what used to be Sinter that nobody serious still made the argument out loud. Wren had known it would be there. He had not said how he knew, and Lyra had stopped asking him things he answered better by simply producing them.

The Balthier came down over the pass at Caer Dyn in five turns, not four - she counted them - she counted anything a ship did on final approach, because a ship that did the expected thing in the expected order was doing its job. She had watched Calyx groundcrew walk her out of a revetment cut into the hillside an hour before, the kind of dug-in shelter a garrison builds when it expects its aircraft to need hiding from something more than weather, and had not asked how long the ship had sat there waiting, or who had been paying to keep her fueled and answerable. Some debts of Wren's ran deeper than the ones he mentioned. The pilot flew by instrument report rather than judgment, trusting a hundred readings faster than a man could weigh them himself. At four hundred meters the ship stopped being flown. For the length of one breath it belonged to someone else entirely - the hum in the hull plating dropped a register, the yoke went still under no one's hands, and the small black shape strapped into the navigator's seat sat upright the way a soldier sits upright at reveille.

"M-101," Doyle said. To no one. Confirming himself - a man checking his own name after a concussion, to be sure the correct one had come back into the body.

He had been the ship for the descent. Now he was the shell again - the replacement, not the one Verlaine had built him. Acquired on the same terms a man acquires anything he did not choose: the alternative was staying gone. Wider in the shoulder joint than the old shell. The optical receptor sat off true by roughly half a degree, so that when he tracked a target the whole small frame overcorrected a fraction on the turn - a defect she had logged in the first hour on this shell and had not stopped logging since. He talked less than he had before the moor. She had not determined whether that was the shell's doing or his own, and she did not ask a man questions he had not chosen to answer.

Orim had the Rod's case out from under his coat before the ramp was fully down. He did not open it. He held it like a soldier holding a weapon he expects to need and has stopped trusting to fire.

The ship put down in a stone corridor between two peaks that had stood quarantine-adjacent six generations running, and had taken the quarantine's particular stillness onto itself - not the stillness of nothing moving. The stillness of things that had learned, across six generations, to move only when they judged themselves unwatched.

The gate stood a kilometer on.

They walked it.

The Vigil patrol kept a single stone building at the narrows, two rooms, a departure log bolted to a table in the format the Compact's commission had standardized in Year 1274. Five entries above hers in three days: an inspection team, a specimen collection detail, a maintenance authorization for the trail. The officer on the rotation was young. Verlaine-trained — she read that off him before he spoke, off the stance alone. He set his rifle down before he opened the log. Muzzle out, not muzzle in. Five seconds told her what she needed of a Vigil soldier, and those five seconds told her enough.

He looked at her transit authorization a long time.

"You want to cross," he said. Not a question. A man buying himself time to read a document a second time without admitting that was what he was doing.

"Yes."

"This designation code-"

"Year 630. Ocipheus Station. Vigil mandate." She waited while he found the clause. "The authorization predates the Compact's perimeter structure. It supersedes it. Fourth clause."

He read the fourth clause. He read it again.

"The last authorized descent on this access was Year 1342," he said. "Descent Corps salvage detail. Membrane specimen recovery. Four operators."

"Three returned," she said. "I've read the report."

He looked up at that — not at the number, which was in the log for anyone to find, but at the fact that she had reached for it before he'd finished offering it. He did not comment. He looked instead at her party: Orim with the Rod's case held against his ribs like something that might need to be put down in a hurry; Doyle with the rifle slung and the stillness of a man reading terrain rather than waiting on permission to; Wren at the back, watching the gate with the same habit he'd brought to everything since Caer Glas Moor — from the outside in, building the picture in pieces before he let himself look at the center of it. The officer's eyes stayed on Wren half a second longer than they stayed on the others. Perhaps because they were close in age. Perhaps because Wren was looking at the zone's threshold as if he expected to understand it from here, and was in the process of discovering that he would not.

He stamped the logbook. He stamped it a second time, which was not procedure.

"The commission will want a return report," he said.

"Yes."

"I mean the actual commission." He stopped there. He had started a sentence he decided partway through not to finish, and the second stamp was what he said instead of finishing it: that he did not believe the standard chain was where this descent belonged.

"I understand," she said.

He reached for the gate release, and then his hand stopped short of it — not a refusal, something worse, the arrested motion of a man who has just seen a reason he cannot yet name. He was looking past her, down the log, at a line she had not been shown.

"Year 1342," he said again, and this time the number came out wrong, careful — the tone of a man realizing, too late, that he should not have said it so freely the first time.

He turned the log so she could read it and pointed at the entry above the salvage detail — not below it, above it, dated the same week: a Vigil field annotation in a hand three commissions old. Perimeter designation revoked pending mandate review. Authorization holders to be detained at the narrows. He looked up at her, and the young steadiness had gone out of his face and left something closer to apology.

"Your clause supersedes the Compact's perimeter," he said. "It does not supersede this. This is Vigil-internal. Year 1342, Ocipheus authorizations were flagged for detention, and the flag was never lifted, because the office that could lift it stopped answering in 1351."

He set his hand flat on the log, not on the release. "I can stamp your transit. I cannot open this gate to a flagged holder without a countersign from a mandate authority that no longer exists. And the standing order for a flagged holder who insists-" he did not finish that either, but his eyes went, once, to the rifle he had set down muzzle-out, and came back. "I would have to call it in. And the moment I call it in, this stops being four people and a logbook."

The gate stayed shut. The zone lay on the other side of it, and the only road to the crater ran through a countersign a decade past the death of the man who could have given it.

Wren had been quiet through all of it — the particular quiet he went to when a problem had a shape he already recognized.

"You don't have to open it," he said.

The officer looked at him like a man wanting very badly to be able to agree with the sentence. "The gate doesn't open itself."

"It doesn't have to open at all." Wren nodded at the logbook, not unkindly. "You never touched the release. Nobody called it in. Four people were here, and then they weren't, and the gate never moved. Write it up as an equipment fault on your own patrol log if it eases anything — the Zone breaks things. Everyone already believes that about it."

The officer looked at the four of them for a long moment — at the case on Lyra's hip, at Doyle's stillness, at whatever he'd decided about a boy who talked like that about a Vigil-sealed perimeter — and set his hand back on the logbook instead of the release.

"I never saw you," he said. "Understand that's not permission. That's just what I'm going to tell the truth about, if anyone asks, because it happens to be the truth."

"That's all we needed it to be," Lyra said.

She put a hand on Wren's shoulder. He was warmer than he should have been, and shaking very slightly, and he did not look up at her.

He folded them through, one at a time, the gate's iron and stone passing around each of them the way water parts and closes over a hand pulled back out of it, and took the longest over the last of them. On the other side, the young officer was already bent back over his logbook, writing nothing down.
