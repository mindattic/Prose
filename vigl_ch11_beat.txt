The waystation came up out of the dusk — squat, stone, built by whichever House had held the ground last, with an eye to defense first and comfort as an afterthought that had mostly not survived the drafting stage. A signboard hung crooked on one chain, the paint long since weathered past reading either House's colors into it. That, Lyra thought, was probably deliberate, and probably the smartest decision anyone on this road had made all week.

Inside it was packed to the doorframe, and packed with exactly the traffic the road had been showing her all day, concentrated now into one low room with one fire and one bar and nowhere for any of it to disperse. Strays at one end, their stillness a held note under the general noise. War-dogs at the other, drinking the way men drink who've spent a great deal of effort making sure a coupling can never again be forced back into the base of their skull. Between them, everyone else — teamsters, a pair of Bressant traders, a woman who from her boots and posture had walked out of some third war entirely and wanted no part of these two.

Two Houses' worth of deserters, still technically at war with each other, drinking under the same roof because the alternative was the cold. Lyra read the room with the same eye she gave any ground before she committed troops to holding it: exits — one front, one through the kitchen she could smell but not see; ceiling height — low, which favored close work and disfavored anyone who liked room to swing; density — bad. Very bad. If this went wrong it would go wrong at arm's length, on all sides at once.

Ardea got them a table near the kitchen door, which Lyra approved of without saying so. Orim was gone within a minute of sitting, and she didn't chase him. He would surface with something. He always did.

Doyle sat with his back to the wall, which she had not told him to do. He had started doing it three weeks ago without her instruction, and she had stopped being surprised by it two weeks ago. She watched him watch the room the way the strays at the far end were watching it — not fear, nothing so simple as fear. Assessment. The habit of a man for whom every room had once, not so long ago, been a room he might be ordered to die in.

The spark, when it came, was exactly as small as sparks on grounds like this always were.

A war-dog three tables over — young, the coupling-ridge under his collar still raised and pink, cut recently enough that he was still finding out what his own neck did without the socket in it — stood too fast reaching for a second mug and put his elbow through the arm of a stray behind him, who did not flinch, because the stray's arm had not, for reasons Lyra suspected went back to whatever Verlaine had done to the wiring behind it, fully belonged to him in the way an arm should. The mug went over. The drink went down the front of the war-dog's coat, over the Atrament forge-mark he had, unlike his two friends outside the wharf that morning, not cut away — a sentimental holdover, she'd have guessed, from a service he hated and hadn't quite finished hating.

"Watch your-" the war-dog started, and turned, and saw what he'd hit the arm of, and stopped mid-word, because now he could see the stray's face and the stray's face had the wrongness on it that these roads had taught every soldier, deserter or otherwise, to read at a glance.

"Verlaine trash," he said. Not loud. Loud enough.

The stray did not answer with words. Something in his jaw set, the mechanical evenness of a reflex nobody had quite trained out of him, and his chair went back.

Lyra was on her feet before the chair finished moving. Not from alarm. She'd already seen how this room would go. A room this dense, with this traffic, at this density, did not survive one man standing with intent. It survived, if it survived at all, on someone closing the gap before the second man stood.

She did not reach the gap in time. That was the plain truth of it, and she knew it even mid-motion: too far, too dense, tables and bodies closing every gap she needed open. The war-dog's friend — older, steadier, the kind of steady that came from having done this exact thing in an actual engagement — was up and swinging a stool at the stray's head before Lyra had covered half the distance.

After that it was not a fight so much as a room deciding, all at once and without consultation, that it had been waiting all evening for permission.

She took the nearest hand that reached for her — a teamster, she registered, not even a party to the original grievance, just a man whose instinct in a collapsing room was to grab whatever moved closest — and did to it what regulation called a standing wrist lock and what her body did without her needing to name it first: rotated the wrist past the joint's honest range, applied weight through the elbow, and put him face-first onto the table he'd been sitting at a moment before. It cost him the wrist for a fortnight, not the arm, which she counted a mercy given how little time she'd had to be merciful in.

A second man came at her from the flank — a stray, moving with that same overcorrected evenness, throwing a punch that had all the force of a soldier's fist and none of a soldier's aim, because whatever had been done to him had not left him the timing to go with the strength. She stepped inside it rather than back from it — the correct answer to a slow, strong strike was always to close the distance, never to retreat from it — and drove her forearm up under his, using his own committed weight to fold him over her shoulder and down. His skull found the table edge on the way. She heard the sound it made and did not stop to assess the damage, because assessing damage mid-engagement was how you acquired more of your own.

Somewhere to her left the coupling-ridge war-dog and the original stray were on the floor, grappling in the specific graceless way of men who have both, at some point, been trained to kill efficiently and have both since lost whatever discipline made that training worth anything. She left them to each other for the four seconds it took to deal with the third man closing on Ardea's flank — a Bressant trader, drunk enough to have decided this was somehow his fight — a single strike to the side of the knee, not the killing angle, just the one that put a man on the ground wondering what had happened to his leg. He went down loud and stayed down, which was the desired outcome, whatever the noise cost her.

Doyle had not moved from the wall. She registered this in the same half-second sweep that took in everything else, and understood it for what it was: he was watching the strays, not the war-dogs, and he was not moving because whatever he was working out about his own kind was one she did not have time to interrupt.

The two men on the floor — war-dog and stray, forge-mark and wrongness — had stopped grappling and started actually fighting, which was worse, and it was this pair Lyra closed on last and dealt with first in terms of consequence. She took the war-dog's raised arm as it came up for a strike that would have opened the stray's face on the table leg, and did the thing regulation called a standing armbar and her instructors had called, with the particular grim humor of people who taught this for a living, the polite way to end an argument. Elbow past its honest range, weight through the shoulder, and the joint went with a sound like a green branch breaking underfoot — not loud, wetter than loud, the specific sound a body makes when it fails at a task it was never built to refuse.

He screamed. That was the honest cost of it, and she did not pretend otherwise to herself. A broken arm on a man who would need that arm to work, to fight, to hold a mug in a country that hadn't stopped being at war just because he'd walked out of it. She had traded that cost against the alternative — an opened face, possibly a dead man, definitely a longer fight — and she stood by the trade: not gladly. Correctly.

The room did not resolve into silence so much as into exhaustion. Fights of this kind did not end with one side victorious. They ended when there was no longer enough will left standing to continue them. Within another ten seconds it had guttered out on its own — men disengaging not because anyone had won anything but because the will to keep swinging had simply run out, the way water runs out of a cracked jug, no single dramatic failure, just an emptying.

She took stock of the cost, methodical and unsparing: two men down and likely to stay down past tonight — the war-dog with the arm, and the trader with the knee, who would walk, but not soon and not without complaint. A stray with a split scalp bleeding freely onto a floor already sticky with spilled drink, who accepted a rag from the barkeep without thanks or apparent awareness that thanks were customary. Broken crockery. One table with a leg gone entirely, canted against the wall like a man favoring an injury. No one had died. That was the whole of the victory available in a room like this, and she took it, because it was the only version on offer.

Ardea was already at the bar working the barkeep down to a figure for the damages, which struck Lyra, even mid-recovery, as the single most reassuring sight in the room — Ardea doing the exact patient, willing-to-yield-at-the-right-moment work she'd been doing at the customs shed that morning, as though a wharf negotiation and a tavern brawl's aftermath were the same discipline wearing two coats. In her experience they probably were.

Orim reappeared from wherever he had gone — the kitchen, she guessed, from the smell of woodsmoke freshly on him — and surveyed the wreckage with the same unhurried attention he'd given the harbor that morning.

"You'll want to know," he said, "that the boy with the broken arm was Verlaine-marked under the collar three months ago, before he cut and joined the Atrament line as a rig-pilot instead. Neither House would claim him now. He told me this himself, between the second scream and the third." He said it without particular sympathy, which was, she had come to understand, simply how Orim reported facts that did not admit of comfortable framing.

"And the stray?"

"Doesn't know his own name. Hasn't for eleven months. The others feed him and tell him where to walk. It is, I am given to understand, a common enough courtesy on this road that it has its own word among them." He did not supply the word. She did not ask for it.

She looked once more across the room — the strays reassembling at their end with the same unhurried stillness as before, as though the last two minutes had happened to some other room entirely; the war-dogs doing the same at theirs, minus one now on the floor with his arm bound crudely by a man who clearly had field experience setting them. Doyle still hadn't moved from the wall. His eyes, though, had found the reassembled strays and stayed there a moment longer than assessment required.

She did not ask him what he saw in them. She thought she already knew, and a commanding officer did not make a man report aloud what she could read plainly enough on his own face: that he was watching a room full of what he might have become, had the Verlaine program worked on him the way it was meant to instead of the way it had. That the men at that end of the room were not strangers to him in any sense that mattered. That there was, on a road with no country for either side of it, exactly one kind of company he recognized as his own, and it was this — silent, broken, minding each other's names for men who'd lost them.

She had, she realized, stopped saying the crossing-prayer somewhere around the second contested border. She could not now recall exactly which one. She set the absence aside along with everything else that no longer held — not as loss, precisely. Just a thing that had gone quiet, the particular quiet of a front that had stopped being anyone's to hold.

They paid Ardea's negotiated figure, which was, predictably, four times fair and treated by all present as the cost of doing business. They left before full dark, because a room that had already proven it could go over once could go over twice, and Lyra had no interest in confirming the theory.

The highland road took them on into the dark toward the Atrament interior, three hundred years well-marked and running, as ever, exactly where they needed to go. Doyle walked it as he'd walked the wharf road that morning — without looking back — and this time she understood the habit for what it plainly was, and did not need Orim to explain it to her first.

The sound was wrong before anything else was, and Lyra had it in her boots three hours up the highland road before her ears agreed to confirm what her feet already knew.

The rail line ran below them in the valley, curving south around the granite bluff before swinging north again toward the Schmalburg basin. The road they walked ran above it, and older — older than the rail, older than the Compact itself by the evidence of its own construction: dressed stone in courses, the mortar long gone gray and crumbling at the joints, the stone itself still seated true. This was the quality of the old work on the major approaches, built by people who had meant to move armies rather than goods, who had not planned for the day the armies would be replaced by freight schedules. The road held well enough under the weight of four people who were not a siege.

Not weather. The sky over the northern highlands sat flat white with high autumn overcast, the kind that holds no storm and only promises the cold will get worse before it improves. What she felt was a deep, continuous vibration coming from somewhere ahead and below, a sustained bass note the body registered before the ears would confirm it. She'd had it moving up through the stone under her boots for the better part of a quarter hour before she understood it wasn't her own fatigue.

"We're getting close," Orim said.

He was ahead of her on the road, moving in the steady, unhurried way of a man who had covered a great deal of ground over fifty years and made his peace with the pace it cost. He had not looked back. He had not slowed.

"You've been here before."

"I have crossed the Atrament border in eight directions over fifty years," he said. "The sound is the same in all of them. You stop hearing it after the second day. Then you leave, and you stop not-hearing it, and the quiet sounds wrong for a week." He stepped around a gap in the stone facing where a spring had colonized the joint. "Or so I'm told. I've never stayed long enough for the second part."

Lyra had her own crossing marked on paper three days behind them — the transit stamp from the Ocipheus liaison post, dated, countersigned, current as of the hour it was cut. She had assumed it would hold for the length of the valley. It would not. The border on the Compact's own maps sat a fixed line; the border on the ground sat wherever the last exchange of ground had left it, and that, by the wear on the verge markers, had been some weeks past. It would sit somewhere else again before the ink on her papers had finished drying in this cold. A transit stamp was a fact about a morning. It was not a fact about a war, and this was a war, whatever the Compact's filings called it on the days it didn't want to.

The smell came next. Not the smell of any one thing yet — the category of it. Metal worked past the temperature it wanted to be worked at. Stone asked to survive conditions stone was never built for. Something chemical underneath both that coated the back of the throat without being quite unpleasant, the smell of a place that had been doing one specific thing for a very long time and had stopped noticing that it did it.

Doyle breathed it once, testing, the way a man tests bathwater with two fingers before he commits the rest of his hand. He did not do it again. It was the Black Country on a still morning — a hundred chimneys agreeing on the same weather, coke-smoke and quenched iron, a smell he had walked out of an army recruiting office and straight back into eight years and one continent ago. He said nothing. Lyra had learned by then that the things worth his saying and the things he had words for did not reliably overlap.

Ardea walked at Lyra's other shoulder, scarf pulled over the lower half of her face against the highland cold, watching the road ahead with the specific posture of someone who has learned that cold does less damage if you decline to acknowledge it. Doyle fell in behind them both, watching the smoke — visible for the better part of an hour now, not rising from any single chimney but sitting over the next ridge as a general discoloration, a permanent grey-brown stain the overcast could not fully absorb.

The valley opened below them when the road crested the ridge.

Lyra stopped.

She was not a person who stopped for things. She had been trained out of it — the vigil's long-watch discipline, the Templar's field doctrine on maintaining movement in unfamiliar ground, seventeen years of applying both — and the discipline was sound, and she had followed it every day since. You took in the ground as you moved. You did not give the landscape a performance of your reaction.

She stopped.

Below the ridge the valley dropped in a series of terraces, and on the largest of them, a league distant, stood a fortification. That was the correct word for its shape — corner towers, curtain wall, the thickened stone of a structure whose builders had assumed threat from every direction at once. The walls had kept something out for a long time, or kept something in, which was often the same wall doing the same job twice.

It was also, at the same time, a factory.

She had read the Compact's language for this — integrated manufacturing installation, castle-form adaptive reuse, Compact Year 1070, ongoing modification per Hearth Table decree — and had built an image from the words: a factory grown up beside a castle, or fitted inside one, some transitional state where you could still find the seam.

There was no seam. The curtain wall was the factory wall. The corner towers had been cored out and fitted with furnace stacks that rose straight up out of what had been the battlements, the old parapet stone now serving as collar-rings for iron chimneys thick as well shafts. Smoke stood over three of the four in a column that leaned southeast in the highland wind, steady enough that it had made its own local weather. The wall itself had been cut through in six places she could count from the ridge, openings wide enough to drive a loaded freight wagon through — not windows, not gates, just apertures sized to whatever the process needed moved in and out of it. Raw material entered on the east face. Finished product left on the west. The wall did its job as a factory wall and made no pretense of doing anything else.

This was not a castle with a mill grown up around it. It was a forge that had once needed walls, and had simply kept them.

There was nothing apologetic in it. That was the part she had not anticipated. The architecture did not excuse itself. The castle had been a castle. It was now also a forge. Both were true of the same stone, and the stone had no opinion either way.

"Year 1074," Orim said. "The Hearth Table authorized the first interior conversion under the manufacturing expansion protocols. Before that it was a garrison. Before the garrison, a watchtower against the valley floor. Before that, something the Atrament record won't discuss, which usually means there was an argument about who built it and nobody won."

"How old?"

"The base courses read pre-Compact. Possibly pre-House. Someone was keeping watch from this ridge before the Houses existed." He looked at the stacks. "They keep a different watch from it now."

The sound resolved into its parts the closer they came: the repetitive thud of forging hammers at a cadence too even to be human hands; the deeper sustained note of furnaces at full heat; a rhythmic compression from somewhere inside the walls she couldn't place from outside. All of it running at once. All of it running without pause.

Below the fortification, in the yards between the outer perimeter and the approach road, the Heloths moved.

She had worked with Heloths at the Ocipheus Station — the vigil's courier models, lean single-rider constructs built to cross open ground without complaint. You developed a working sense of one if you rode it long enough, not fondness exactly, but familiarity with a machine built to do one thing well and decline everything else. The Heloths in the yards below were built for a different job: heavy-cargo variants, wide and low, the arm-housings fitted for cradle loads instead of saddle points, running the fixed routes between the factory mouths and the freight stage with the patient repetition of something calibrated to a task and content to run it until the maintenance interval said otherwise. Three stood motionless at what she recognized, even at this distance, as servicing stations.

Not all of it below her was Heloth. Between two of the cargo variants, drawn up in a ranked stillness at the yard's south wall, stood frames of a different make. Man-height. Man-proportioned, in a way no pure construct needed to be — articulated at shoulder, hip, and knee like a living body, not shaped by whatever a machine found convenient. She knew the type from the Vigil's own matériel briefings and had never before had it in front of her eyes: battle-rigs. Atrament's answer to the war it kept feeding. A Heloth was built whole, from the first rivet, to do a courier's job or a cargo job and nothing else. A battle-rig was built around something that had not started as metal. She did not know the process from this distance and did not, standing here, want the diagram. She knew the socketed housing at the sternum, sized for a spine, when she saw it, and she understood, on the ridge with the wind carrying the smoke southeast, what got made behind those six apertures and what it was made for. Verlaine took minds and gave them machine bodies to wear. Atrament took bodies — whoever's, wherever the supply was drawn from, and she set that question aside next to the others she could not yet answer — and fused them into frames built to take a blow a living skeleton could not survive twice. Two Houses working the same war from opposite ends of the same problem. This was where one half of it got built, at the rate of however many hammer-strokes a minute the sound below the ridge was counting out.

The stations glowed. Orange, low, bioluminescent — the ember-fungus, introduced from some other Sphere generations back, that grew wherever the Catalyst discharge left its residue and did not stop growing once it had taken hold. She had read about it in the ecological annex at Ocipheus. Reading about a fungus that lit a construct yard orange from the inside was not the same thing as standing above one and watching it burn low and steady on stone that predated the Houses.

"At night, from a ridge, you can read the whole yard by the fungus alone," Orim said. "Every station. Every wall the discharge spatters during the cleaning cycle." He paused. "That will matter later."

"When?"

"When we need to see the yards after dark," he said, in the tone of a man releasing information at the rate he judged useful and not one measure sooner.

The approach road ran through three settlements between the ridge and the Schmalburg basin, and none of them were towns.

The first was three long buildings and a dormitory block. "Hearth-stations," Orim said. "Numbered by production sequence, not geography. Station Twelve isn't near Eleven or Thirteen. It's the twelfth stage of the chain." He looked at the dormitory. "Same plan across the whole valley. Efficient use of the floor."

"It's very clean," Ardea said.

He looked at her.

"I was expecting it to be dirtier. Everything that size, making everything it's making. I expected more debris."

"Debris costs," Orim said. "Debris is raw material that has left the cycle before it should have. The Hearth Table worked out, four centuries ago, that the cheapest form of cleanliness is treating waste as escaped product. Nothing lands in that yard without a value assigned to it, and the value is recovered." He watched the clean yard slide past. "There are Atrament scholars who will tell you the arrangement isn't unkind at all — that it simply weighs collective output over individual comfort, and that the people living it understand the trade and accept it."

"Do they?" Ardea asked. "Accept it."

"The scholars," Orim said, "are not, as a rule, the people living it."

Ardea was quiet for a moment. "That's worse, somehow."

"Yes," Orim said, and did not elaborate, and Lyra understood that this was the whole of the answer he had.

At the second settlement they stopped briefly at the edge of a servicing yard, where a heavy Heloth stood under two workers running the discharge-clearing procedure on its back housing — the same one she knew from the Ocipheus maintenance cycle, scaled up. The ember-fungus washed the whole station orange, the workers visible in it as shapes more than as people. One said something short to the other. The other laughed, briefly, the ordinary currency of anyone doing a cold routine task in company.

Ardea watched them a moment longer than the pace of the road required, in the particular way she had of looking at things she meant to remember.

Then she moved on, and so did Lyra.

The staging ground was at the valley junction, where the approach road met the east-west freight road and the floor widened enough for a temporary military formation to sit comfortably: flat, sufficient, close to the eastern supply route without sitting on the rail line itself.

Six supply wagons under canvas. Three infantry columns standing at ease with their kit stacked at their feet, in the specific posture of men who had been told to wait and had been waiting long enough to develop opinions about the waiting. Two heavy Heloths moved between the columns carrying loads that were not exercise weight — she could read it in the gait, the small forward compensation of a construct carrying something denser than it had been trimmed for.

She counted the wagons twice. Six, both times.

The canvas was tied down under canonical property seals — the circle-and-line pressed in red wax, the Liturgy's mark for goods in transit under inter-House review. Property under that seal was subject to inspection only by the authority that had sealed it. Which meant it was subject to inspection by no one standing on this road.

She looked at the wagons. She looked at the columns. She looked at the direction the Heloths were carrying their loads — east, toward the highland approach, the route the Vigil rotation resupply used.

Seventeen years in the Ocipheus archive had put every disclosed Atrament production record filed under the Compact through her hands at least once. She knew the seasonal patterns: south to Loera for export, north to the Vigil line for resupply, held in the valley for the maintenance cycles. What she was looking at was neither. The Vigil line's own supply doctrine ran five sequential stages, and you did not open stage five until the first four were positioned and confirmed. Sealed wagons at a staging ground meant the beginning of nothing. They meant the end of something that had already been moving for a while — moving, she understood now, since well before any border stamp she or anyone else had carried across this line could have registered it. A front that relocated with the season made every notice provision in the Compact a formality already behind the ground it was meant to describe. That was not an oversight. That was the use of it.

She had been counting supply movement since the border without understanding the shape of it. She understood the shape of it now, standing at this junction, and it did not improve on understanding.

No Compact filing she had read disclosed an exercise here. No inter-House notice accounted for this supply chain. And the class of canonical seal on those wagons required countersignature from the Canon Immaculate — not the routine cross-border stamp, but the reservation kept for operations that needed the Liturgy's formal legal presence written into them before they happened.

She set it aside, next to the theft, next to the Relic's absence, next to Sanctorin's signature suspending her own field authority. Next to everything she did not yet have a whole shape for, whose edges were beginning, one at a time, to show themselves.

Doyle had stopped a little apart, watching the staging ground with the stillness she had learned to read as him turning something over he had no way to write down. His face carried, for longer than the usual three seconds, the look of a man for whom the present had briefly become a transparency laid over something he already carried. The columns. The equipment loads. The faces of men who had been standing at ease since some hour that morning they hadn't chosen.

Then he was watching the road again, and she did not ask.

He spoke rarely, and when he did it was clipped. Wait. No. That way. And one further word she was fairly sure meant I understand, though he deployed it in contexts that also seemed to mean I have seen this before and, on at least one occasion, this is the same thing wearing different clothes. It carried a great deal of freight for one word.

Whatever the staging ground had told him, that word would not cover it. She let it settle, and they went on.

The descent toward Schmalburg took the rest of the afternoon, the road switchbacking down through the lower terraces until the basin opened beneath them: a river confluence silted, over centuries, into a flat harbor bottom, the dockyard running the western bank in old garrison stone for what looked, from above, like half a league. Atrament's machine yards had grown to meet the dockyard wall from the landward side, filling every open ground between it and the rail line, so that the wall was now less a boundary than a hinge between two kinds of the same industry.

The Veil ran brown below it. Not mud-brown. Not storm-brown. The particular brown of something introduced rather than carried — the color of a river that had been receiving the valley's discharge for generations and had stopped pretending to be anything else. She had read the numbers on this once, in an annex, and nodded at them the way anyone nods at a statistic that is bad but not present. The river from the ridge was present.

There was construction on the dockyard wall. Not repair — new work: scaffolding at three points along the western face, dressed stone going up above the old coursing, the mortar lines even and fine in a way the garrison work below them was not. This required a hand the Heloths didn't have.

Below the scaffolding, along the wall's base, there were workers.

Not construct handlers. Not Hearth-station labor in its dormitory rotation. These moved dressed stone from the staging pile to the scaffold foot, ran the rope-and-pulley that carried the larger blocks up to the men setting the courses above, with the steady, unremarkable efficiency of people for whom the work had long since stopped being anything to remark on — not automatic, but habituated, the deep habituation difficult repeated labor eventually earns, the body finding the rhythm of the task while the rest of a person goes somewhere else and waits it out.

Lyra looked at them.

She looked at the dressed stone. She looked at the mortar lines, the fitment, the way the new courses would hold. She looked at the rope-and-pulley and the staging pile organized with the settled order of a site that had been running long enough to find its own logic.

She looked at the workers again.

She looked away.

She did not go looking for a word for it. She set it aside with the Heloths and the battle-rigs and the wagons and the columns — one more thing filed into a space that was getting fuller by the hour and had not, so far, produced anything she could act on before nightfall.

Orim made the decision for her when she opened her mouth to ask about entering the city that evening.

"No," he said. "Not tonight." He looked down at the dockyard, the wall, the road. "We watch it from here first. A city under pre-deployment develops an awareness. It watches who walks in." He shrugged the pack from his shoulder. "We go down when we know what it's watching for."

She considered arguing. She did not argue.

They made camp in the scrub above the road, a low fire on dry wood Ardea found wedged in a rock crevice where last season's growth had dried in place, small enough to throw no visible smoke. The cold at that height, once the sun had gone, required acknowledgment rather than refusal — it settled into the joints with the particular attention of a host who knows the guests have nowhere else to be.

They ate. They said little.

Then the dark finished arriving, and Schmalburg stopped being a city visible in its stonework and became a city visible in its glow.

Every servicing station in the dockyard's machine yards — she began counting, lost the thread, began again, gave up somewhere past a dozen as the farthest points blurred together — stood outlined in the same low orange bioluminescence, the fungus grown thick enough now to draw the whole yard's shape in light: the routes between stations worn thin where traffic kept the growth down, the walls that took the worst of the discharge spatter burning brightest of all.

In that light, the scaffolding on the dockyard wall was still visible. The workers were still there — not directly lit, but caught in the reflected orange rising off the stations below, shapes moving stone, the pulley system still turning, the work continuing past the hour Atrament's own hearth-stations rang their shift bells.

She watched the mortar going into the new courses, one stone at a time, patient, exact.

She still did not have a word for it.

"Tell me what you have," Orim said, without turning from the city.

She told him. The sequence from the border to the junction — the supply movements she'd tracked without understanding, the stage numbers, the canonical class on the seals, the Vigil doctrine she'd read seven years ago in an archive and hadn't thought of since, the frames on the yard floor that weren't Heloths.

He listened without interrupting. When she finished, he looked at the city a while longer.

"They were never looking for the Relic," he said.

"No."

"They knew where Calyx was taking it when they arranged the theft. The destination was part of the arrangement." He watched the ember-glow on the yards. "The theft gave the Canon a legal basis for moving sealed wagons into position for a Vigil-approach operation without inter-House notice. Recovering a Liturgy asset under canonical review required the Liturgy's field authority to go looking for it. That authority is what's sitting on the valley floor tonight."

"You keep calling it an asset."

"Because that's the word the file uses." He turned his cup once in his hands, not yet drinking. "It isn't accurate. An asset does something. The Oculus doesn't do anything — not the way a blade does something, or a wagon of Catalyst does something. It looks. Sinter's own Scriveners bonded it to its paired Orb in Year 630, an aperture that never closed by design, not by accident. Every scry I have ever taken heals shut behind me the moment I let it go — the skin seals, the sight is gone, and I keep whatever I managed to remember of it before that fades too. The Oculus never healed. It never stopped looking. Seven hundred and forty-one years of the same unbroken sight, still open right now, this hour, on whatever is growing down there and getting larger while it grows. And a scry reaches time as well as place, so the same window holds what came before it — Sinter whole and inhabited for six hundred and eighty-two of those years, and then the thing's own birth, in Year 1312, and everything it's been becoming since. The Canon didn't lose a weapon he can wield. He lost the one eye in Entos that was still open."

"Then why keep it in a vault instead of looking through it? If it only watches, watching is not a crime."

"Because a vault was never where you keep a thing safe," Orim said. "It's where you keep a thing from being used. The Liturgy has been the one authority both those armies down there will still accept, for three hundred years, precisely because it has never had to answer for what it's been filing under 'no action indicated' since the Event. Read what it's seeing and someone has to explain the silence. Easier, every time, to lose the eye that never closes than to answer for what it's still watching."

"It also required suspending the Templar assigned to the case," she said. "My transit documents would have crossed those supply routes eventually. I'd have read the stage-five sequence for what it was."

"As you have."

"I was meant to be delayed long enough that the deployment finished before I could report it."

He turned his cup slowly in his hands. "That's the charitable read."

She said nothing.

"The uncharitable read," he said, "is that you were meant to be holding the Relic when this resolves. A Templar with seventeen years of documented loyalty to the Canon Immaculate, found in possession of a stolen artifact recovered from the House the Liturgy's own deployment is about to implicate, is useful to have on the record." He paused. "I mention it because it's true, not because it changes anything we're doing."

"It doesn't change anything we're doing."

"Then we go on as we were."
