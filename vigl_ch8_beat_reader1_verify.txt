An asset write-off crossed her desk an hour before the WC-Eirene units came through the courtyard — routine registration, the ink still fresh, the kind of instrument she processed by the dozen without once looking past the rubric to what it retired. NORTHERN ROUTE RECONNAISSANCE — HULL DESIGNATION BALTHIER, OCIPHEUS STATION COMMISSION. STATUS: ADMINISTRATIVE LOSS, FLIGHT RECORD TERMINATED. No board of inquiry cited. No recovered wreckage logged, no crew accounted for, no successor hull requisitioned to replace her on the roster she'd been struck from. A ship simply stopped, on a Tuesday six years past, and nobody since had asked why loudly enough for the asking to reach a page.

She had learned, six weeks into watching one suspended transit authority hide behind eleven pages of unrelated adjustments, what an instrument that answered no questions was usually built to prevent. She copied the write-off anyway — that was the job, not the judgment — and set it in the outgoing stack, and did not write down the thought that followed it: that a vessel logged this cleanly missing had not necessarily gone down anywhere at all.

The WC-Eirene units came through the courtyard in the middle of the afternoon.

She had moved to the window to stretch her hand — the middle knuckle had stiffened again, earlier than usual — when she heard them before she saw them: iron in deliberate motion on stone, a low consistent percussion, lower than the Heloths on the city roads and slower and in a different register altogether. The gait of something heavier. Something not built for speed.

The first unit came through the gate, and she counted as the rest followed. Twelve. Taller than a tall man, iron housings built wide and low at the shoulder for impact absorption rather than deflection, the tracking assembly that served as a head set lower than a man's head and canted slightly forward, rotating through the slow deliberate arc she recognized from the evaluation summaries: active acquisition, registering the space around it — she had rendered that phrase in a dozen instruments and only now, watching the arc happen in the actual stones, did she hear what a strange, bloodless way it was to describe a machine deciding what in a room might need to die. The handlers walked alongside rather than ahead — she had filed enough summaries to know why. A handler in an active unit's forward registration field created a compliance conflict the unit resolved by following rather than being followed. Walking alongside was correct procedure. Maintenance speed — not operational pace, not inert — was correct procedure. Twelve units crossing a courtyard in file, led by handlers doing everything by the book, was not, on its own, alarming. She took it in with the same reflex she brought to instrument volume and lead-sheet weight and the Canon's cadence. She missed nothing. That was the office.

The volume had tripled in six weeks. She had filed the first evaluation summary under a provisional designation, origin notation sphere-42; by the third summary the provisional flag had been dropped, quietly, the way a word stops being allegedly and starts just being the word. She had filed seven since. She had not been tracking the increase against anything, because tracking accumulations against each other was not within her assignment.

The units turned at the loading-dock approach and moved toward the south utility wing, handlers holding their sidewalk positions through the turn. She watched until the last unit cleared the corner and the courtyard stood empty again except for the food-service cart and, at the far end, a Heloth courier idling in the shade with the stillness of a machine waiting rather than switched off.

She knew the south utility wing. She had been thirteen years old when the lock authorization for its staging hall came through the copy desk where she was doing her junior apprentice hours. A senior Scribe had handed it to her because it was a straightforward maintenance lock — an instrument that required documentation but not judgment, the appropriate kind for an apprentice still learning the judgment part. She had copied it in the hand she was still correcting then, the one that pressed too hard on the downstrokes because she had not yet learned to let the weight do the work, and had filed it and not thought about it again. The authorization specified no end date, which was unusual. She had not known then what unusual looked like. That had been eleven years ago.

The staging hall had not appeared in any instrument in six weeks.

It was not locked now.

She returned to her desk and made no annotation. The courtyard was not an instrument category she had been given to work from. She finished the maintenance authorization for the south utility wing — expanded storage calibration, lower west section, the fourth of the week, the fifth if she counted last Thursday's, which she did, because she counted everything — and added it to the stack.

She went to the registry room in the late afternoon, when the light in the main office had gone gray enough that she would need to angle the lamp again and she was tired of angling the lamp.

She had a legitimate purpose, or told herself so going in. Her sister's name had appeared in thirty-one instruments she had processed in six weeks. Each was individually within the scope of her assignment; together they mapped to an operational structure she had been copying without being given the shape of it. Understanding the structure was not intervention. Knowing what she was recording was what let her record it accurately. She was a Scribe. Accuracy was the virtue she had been certified to practice, and if that argument was doing more work than she'd asked of it, she chose, for the moment, not to notice the strain. She signed the access log with her name, the time, and cross-reference, canonical processing in the purpose field, and went to find the Ocipheus muster.

The Templar deployment registry was a bound volume, thick-spined, the pages dense with the specific notation of active service records — house of origin, posting date, current station, active assignment, any suspended authorities. Not House soldiers, not conscripts: the Liturgy's own field corps, the ones who had chosen the institutional mission over every other claim on them. Each entry carried a name and a rank. The Liturgy did not reduce its Templars to a number, whatever it did to its machines; it kept that distinction, formally, whatever else it failed to keep. She had filed volumes like this in her apprentice years without reading them, and the filing had taught her the structure well enough to move through it now. The room smelled of old ink and the cold that comes off stone kept deliberately cold. She found the Ocipheus section and turned the pages carefully, using the flat of her finger rather than the tip; the paper had the dryness of decades in an unheated room.

She had known the name the instant she saw it, six weeks ago, before the ink under her hand had finished drying. What she had not had — what she had come to this room to find — was the shape the name fit inside: the weight of thirty-one instruments in the same authorization chain, the structure with Ocipheus at one end and the Canon at the other and, in between, a suspended transit authority that none of the instruments she had copied troubled itself to explain. She had been carrying the name for six weeks the way a Scribe carries the shape of an instrument before she has finished reading it — present, felt, refusing to resolve into language — and she had allowed herself to keep processing accurately rather than follow the shape to wherever it led. That had been a choice. She was looking now at what the choice had produced.

She found the entry.

Dame Lyra, Ocipheus. Templar. Current status: deployment, active. Transit authority suspended, Canon's instrument — and the reference number she had copied and filed and cross-referenced and cited in three subsequent instruments. She had handled that number thirty-one times.

Posting date, three years prior — the same cycle in which she herself had been certified and taken her first desk. They had been in different cities since. They had written letters. Her sister had told her, every time she visited Ocipheus Station, that the station bread was not worth eating, and Vega had made not listening a habit in return.

She had been at this desk eating a roll of that same bread the morning the transit suspension arrived, six weeks ago. The runner had brought three instruments. She had read her sister's name before her hand had finished the second line of the copy, and she had put the roll down for a reason she had not let herself name at the time. The suspension was the second instrument. She processed it anyway, in under an hour, sealed the canonical original, filed it with her working copy, and moved to the third instrument, because that was the discipline.

She had known. She had copied it anyway.

She stood in the registry room a long time, long enough to notice how cold it was and how still she had gone in it, both hands gripping the spine of the volume, her right middle knuckle pressed against the binding without her having decided to press it. She released it. She did not find having known, and having copied it anyway, any more consoling than not-knowing would have been. She replaced the volume. She had made no annotations. Her name was in the access log. She returned to the office.

The instruments she had copied that day were dry. She aligned them and moved them to the transfer stack; the runner would carry them, in the morning, to the sealed archive, where they would become canonical and unalterable, the official record of what had been decided. She would not see most of them again.

She kept working copies. Every Scribe did — not canonical, not official, nothing the Liturgy needed to acknowledge, just her own hand, her own reference, in the box in the lower left drawer. It was heavier than it had been at the start of the posting.

The transit suspension from six weeks ago sat near the bottom. She found it by the texture of the page — she had pressed harder than usual that morning, the nib had been cold, the lines ran slightly heavier than her standard hand. She had not noticed at the time. She noticed now.

She set it on the desk and picked up her pen. She did not know why, exactly, except that her hands had been doing something all day — copying, measuring, folding, unsealing, resealing — and hands accustomed to doing something reach for what's nearest when the day runs out. She wrote her sister's name in the margin, in the larger hand she had used before the canonical script and the certification, the one she still used in letters: a little more room in the vowels, the first letter held a beat longer.

She wrote the name.

Then she drew a line through it.

This did not remove it. The name stayed under the line, the ink only slightly darker where the stroke crossed the letters, the letters still in the shape she had known since she was old enough to know her sister's name — because a line drawn through ink does not take away what is underneath. It only says that someone drew a line through it. She could see it. She would always be able to see it. It was, she thought, the most honest disposition notation she had entered in six weeks — the only one with no rubric standing over it.

She put the copy back in the box and closed the drawer.

Outside the window the courtyard was empty. The Heloth was gone. Whatever waited in the south utility wing's staging hall was in it now, unlocked for the first time in eleven years, delivered by handlers following correct procedure. In the morning the runner would come, and she would know something about the day's business by the weight of the stack before she read the lead sheet, because six weeks had trained the reflex, and the volume had gone on increasing at a rate she had not been tracking against anything.

The Carnyx maps were in the review sleeve, bound for a shelf between a supply reassessment and a water-usage report, filed until someone needed to prove the Liturgy had read them. In her own head the survey points stayed where she had put them: three positions along the eastern boundary, the margin of error at each, the line at both dates, the rate projection in the commander's careful field hand. High-uncertainty, extrapolated from 15-year sample. Forty years.

She was the record.

She had understood this in the abstract at the start of the posting, when a senior Scribe explained what canonical notarization meant: the instrument said what the instrument said, you said it again in the canonical hand, and it became true in the saying of it. That was the arrangement. That was the whole of the office.

She was understanding it differently now. There were things she had said in the canonical hand that she was carrying in her own head alongside the things she was authorized to carry, and neither set was separable from her body, and her body would go on existing after the transfer stack had been sealed into the archive and made official and unreachable. The Carnyx commander had put his projection in the record because the record was where things went to matter or be buried. She had done what she was authorized to do: copy it accurately and send it to be buried. The measurements lived in her head now, beside her sister's name under a line that had not erased it. Neither had a canonical form. The archive kept no instrument class for them, no disposition field, no rubric she'd been taught to render, no space to enter the four words she had been writing for three years.

The institution had built the arrangement with that in mind, she thought — a Scribe who would be furniture, who would render the sound of a thing without asking what it meant. It did not seem to have considered what happened when the furniture began keeping track of itself. Or it had considered this, and the arrangement was working exactly as designed — the not-knowing had always been the feature, not the flaw. A small, cold, patterned beauty, the kind she had loved the sound of for three years before she caught, this once, what it was for.

She did not know which was worse.

She angled the lamp for the last time and covered the ink.

Bressant was three things stacked on top of each other: the Veil River's low north bank, the road down from the eastern passes, and three hundred years of grain dust worked into the mud between them.

Timber and stone, raised to hold weight and shed rain and no further — the discipline of a working district, not the discipline of pride.

Cargo tags hung pole to pole between the rows, half gone soft and unreadable in the damp, and nobody had replaced them, because the carters already knew their own loads and a tag was a courtesy Bressant paid only when a patrol made it pay, and patrols came through on a schedule the district had long since memorized.

Thirty years of Verlaine mobilization had trained this ground out of asking who moved through it before dawn, and trained it well — she counted four buildings on the approach with doors that opened inward only, the mark of people who had learned that an outward-swinging door announces itself to anyone watching the street.

She held ground while Orim found his wall. North-facing, always, when he was scrying for something that had come south to north — he had stopped needing a reason for the orientation forty years before she'd met him, and she had stopped asking for one.

He took the tincture from his breast pocket, counted two fingers to three, and went still. Not more than three counts — she'd seen him pay for a fourth once, years back, a week of nausea logged against a single mistake he had never once repeated. She had stood this same watch behind him more times than she'd bothered to log, and the discipline of it — hold position, watch the street, say nothing until he surfaced — was one of the few things about him she had never needed teaching twice.

He opened his eyes with his report already assembled, which was the correct way to do it.

"The Relic passed through. Four days ago. The trace ends at the second dock — they took a commercial barge north."

He looked past her, at the tree line where the road ran out and the district's outer fields began.

"There's something else in that district. Old residue. Not the standing-army kind — those go quiet inside a year, because a mind trained to stop remembering leaves nothing behind to find. This one hasn't stopped."

She read the cluster the way she read any block she meant to enter: for the exits, for the sight-lines, for what a man hiding there would need.

"A person?"

"A mind."

He put the vial away.

"Whatever's wearing it forgot to let him forget."

She was already moving.

The tree line ran along the district's outer field, scrub oak and something like birch gone silver in the grey morning, and Orim had pointed her at it rather than at the warehouses. Whatever's wearing it forgot to let him forget. She read the ground with the same close attention she gave any approach she meant to make alone: the trunks wide enough to matter, the sight-lines a man with a long gun would want and the ones he wouldn't have thought to deny himself.

He had a sight-line on her from the first line of trees. She knew this the way she knew weather — not from anything she could point to, but from the specific stillness a wood holds when it isn't empty. She kept walking. Fast movement announces intention as clearly as a shout; a woman crossing open ground at a working pace announces nothing.

The shot did not come.

She reached the first trunk wide enough to matter and put it between them, and in the half-second that cost him a clean line, she read what the delay meant: not a man deciding whether he could hit her. A man who'd had the shot and had not taken it, and was now working out, too late, what that decision had cost him.

She closed the second trunk before he'd finished working it out.

He found her again through the scope at eleven yards, which was close enough that the scope was already the wrong tool and too far for him to know it yet. The shot went where the barrel was pointed a half-second before she was — she'd given him the angle on purpose, coming off the second trunk at a line no rifle could correct for fast enough, and the round went through the space her shoulder had occupied a stride back and did nothing but announce, uselessly, that the fight had started.

The bolt-action bought her the rest of the distance. A man works the bolt, chambers, resets his cheek weld, finds the sight picture again — four actions, each with its own small cost in time, and she had spent seventeen years being the reason those four actions never finished in time. She took the second half of his rebuilt sight picture on her shield, the round skating off the boss with a sound like a struck bell, and by the time the bolt cycled again the rifle was a club-length weapon in a fight that had already closed past clubbing range.

She put him on the ground with the shield's edge, not the sword — a Templar's first choice, when the first choice is available, because a shield can end a fight a blade can only decide the terms of. He went down against a root the size of a forearm, the rifle skittering free of both his hands, and she had her knee on the stock before he'd finished falling and her blade at the black jointed housing where a man's throat would be before he'd finished deciding whether falling was the end of it.

He did not reach for the rifle again. He went still the way something goes still when it has run out of options it is willing to spend, and the oval receptor where a face should have been held on her, unlit, giving nothing back to read, and under her knee and blade both, so did he.

She had killed things that fought less honestly than this and felt less before doing it. She held the blade where it was and gave him the three seconds a person gets before a Templar's mercy runs out on a contested field, because Orim's word had been mind, not weapon, and she had learned to leave room for the difference even when the room cost her something.

He used the three seconds to speak.

It came up out of the chassis's throat flat and metal-toned, without inflection, in no cadence she'd have called speech if she hadn't already caught the shape of it a hundred times in a hundred canonical documents — the Received Tongue, the fixed dead formula the Liturgy dressed its most sacred instruments in, the one she had learned to render without understanding a word of it:

"Ave Maria, gratia plena, Dominus tecum—"

Her blade did not move. Her attention did.

He was not reciting an instrument. No Scribe's hand had ever set this shape in an archive she'd copied — the meter was wrong for law, the repetitions wrong for doctrine, and under the flat delivery, for the first time since she'd found him, something that was not procedure at all: a man's voice, badly out of practice at being afraid out loud, doing the one thing a body does with no other options left to spend.

He got through it once. Then, quieter, the syllables changing shape, something shorter, something that landed with the particular weight of a door being pulled shut from the inside:

"In manus tuas, Domine, commendo spiritum meum."

Into your hands, Lord, I commend my spirit.

She did not have the words. She had the shape of them, close enough to the Received Tongue's own dead grammar that her hand — three years of copying that grammar into a canonical archive without once being told what it meant — understood the sentence a half-beat before the rest of her did: a man lying down for the night not knowing if he would rise, saying so, to whatever was listening, because it was the one sentence built to be said with a blade already at the throat.

He was not chanting a formula. He was praying. And the language he was praying in was not his own tongue rendered strange by a translation faculty — it was the Liturgy's own dead grammar, the one thing in this entire encounter that had no business belonging to a stolen mind off a Sphere none of them had ever named.

She held the blade a moment longer, because seventeen years of drill did not release its grip in the space of a single prayer. Then she took her knee off the rifle stock, and stepped back a half-pace, and did not sheath the sword — but she lowered it, and that was the whole of the mercy she had to give, and she gave it.

"Where," she said, "did you learn that."

He answered her in the same flat, metal-toned voice, in the plain vowels of a man from somewhere none of them had set foot, laying the words down deliberate, like he had all the time there was and none of it to waste proving so.

"My mother taught it to me. Before any of this." A pause, the receptor holding on her, unlit, giving nothing back to read. "I did not expect to need it again so soon."

She did not have an answer for that. She stood, and stepped back a further pace, and did not sheathe the sword, but she let the point drop toward the ground, which was as much of an answer as she had to give a man she had just put on his back under a tree with a blade at his throat.

The rifle lay where it had skittered, a stride off, in the roots. He looked at it. He looked at her. He did not move toward it.

"Take it," she said.

He read the whole of that the way a man reads an unfamiliar mechanism before he puts a hand to it — the same wary attention he'd given the hand and the sheathed blade a moment before combat made both of those things irrelevant, weighing what it cost her to say and what it would cost him to refuse. He rose, retrieved the rifle, and slung it over his shoulder rather than raising it, and she felt the old pull toward her own belt a half-beat before she'd chosen to feel it, seventeen years of drill outrunning her judgment — and he saw that, and stopped, hand on the sling, and held there: the rifle comes. Your call.

She breathed once. She nodded.

He moved when she moved, holding a body-width of distance between them — not submission, the working spacing of a man who wanted room to act if action became necessary, though something in the angle of him had changed since the trees, and she thought it was not trust, not yet, but a decision, the shape a decision takes once it is made and cannot be unmade. On the tree line's edge, where the warehouse district began, she raised one finger, pointed at the buildings ahead, raised it again. One person, ahead. His hand found the sling and stayed there. Ready. Not anticipating.

Orim stood in the passage, back to the entrance, watching the loading yard. He didn't turn.

"Eleven minutes before the manifest closes."

"Trace runs north from the second dock."

She glanced at the automaton beside her, watching him read Orim's stance with the same close attention he'd given hers a few minutes and one prayer ago — the turn coming at the sound of their footsteps and not the sound of a weapon.

"Whatever's in there. It's what you said."

Orim turned and gave him the attention he gave everything small: complete, unhurried, a scryer's habit of the specific over the general. He took in the rifle. The riveted plate. The worn patch at the left knuckle-joint where a hand had gripped it too many times in the dark. Stamped along the collar housing, in the flattened block lettering Verlaine used for anything it didn't intend to explain: M-101888.

"M-101," Orim said, reading it aloud, for the record.

Something moved through the automaton's stillness that her hand, her blade, and the open lid had not put there. He straightened, minutely — the way a man straightens at his own name in a stranger's mouth — and answered in a voice that came up out of the chassis's throat, flat, metal-toned, without inflection.

"M-101."

It was the first sound he'd made in front of anyone but her. Drilled, unhesitating, correct — offered the way a captured soldier gives rank and serial number and stops there, because it's the one truth he can't be made to regret.

Ardea came round the north corner — come the legal way, days longer, on a soldier's book nobody had thought to suspend — took in the rifle, took in Lyra.

"You're bringing it," she said.

Not a question — a professional logging new intelligence and rebuilding her plan around it in real time.

"Him," Lyra said. "Yes."

Ardea took it in without visible reaction and moved on.

"The courier carries four poorly."

"He walks."

"He walks well," Orim said, ahead of the obvious follow-up. "A Myrmidon shell doesn't tire. It dents, or it doesn't. It never sits down."

Ardea looked at him a beat longer than required.

"That's not a comfort."

"No," Orim agreed.

She had read the intake literature the way she read all literature assigned to her: a problem, categorized, set aside, held at professional distance. An M-prefix serial branded a Myrmidon — a mind Scried out of a living man on some other Sphere, hauled up through the warm gold between worlds, installed without ceremony in a shell built to be replaced, not repaired. She had read the specification sheet on the shells themselves once, in an intake briefing years back: no life-support architecture, no provision for feeding or rest, because a Myrmidon required neither and Verlaine had not paid for capacities it would never draw on. Not a captured man. A stolen one, wearing the only body Verlaine judged worth spending on him. She had read the phrase complete linguistic disjunction upon initial Sphere crossing in a footnote and set it aside as a curiosity — nothing more. She was standing in a passage in Bressant with the footnote's subject a meter off, having just had him hand her, unasked, the one word he owned outright, and she found she had no ready frame for what that actually was.

"Eight minutes," Ardea said.

"We'll call him that," Lyra said. "M-101. Until he tells us different."

Orim glanced at her — not disagreement, closer to recognizing she'd taken the harder, more honest option when a softer one had been sitting there for the taking. She could have handed him a name of her own choosing, the way a quartermaster tags an unclaimed effect, and it would have cost her nothing and given him nothing either. She'd used the one true thing stamped on him instead, which was uglier, and, she judged, more his.

He'd followed all of it — the naming, the reasoning under it, Ardea's clock-counting, the whole exchange three strangers had conducted a stride from him as though he were freight that happened to have opinions. He'd followed the warehouse before that. He'd followed the latch, Orim's read of the collar, every word since. What he did with any of it he kept to himself. He kept it a moment longer.

Then he didn't.

"It'll do."

The voice came out of the chassis's throat again — flat, metal-toned, the same instrument that had returned his number to Orim a minute before. But this was not the one drilled syllable, offered and nothing else, that rank and serial number had been. It was a full sentence, unhurried, in the plain vowels of a man from somewhere none of them had set foot, each word laid down deliberate, like he had all the time there was and none of it to waste proving so.

Ardea went still in a different register than before.

Lyra recognized, under the sentence, the exact shape of what Ardea had just set aside — complete linguistic disjunction — and watched it land on her: that whoever wrote the phrase had never stood in a room with the thing it claimed to describe. There was no disjunction. There never had been. Lyra had known that since the trees. He'd understood the warehouse. He'd understood Orim reading the stamp off his own collar. He'd understood Ardea calling him "it" twice in one breath and let both land without comment, the way he let everything else pass, and had simply not, until this exact moment, chosen to answer in front of anyone but her.

"You speak?" Ardea asked.

Not a question. An objection lodged a beat too late to do any good.

"I do."

The receptor was already on her before he finished saying it.

"Gave the number first. Seemed the truer thing to lead with."

Nobody spoke for a stretch that ran a half-second past comfortable. The flatness of the delivery did double work: the voice of a man choosing his words with care, and, to ears that hadn't learned better yet, indistinguishable from the voice of a slow mind reciting something taught to it. Ardea caught the difference before she trusted herself to speak again.

"Seven minutes," Ardea said, recovering the one piece of the moment still hers to hold, and was already moving.
