The cave mouth was barred — iron, old, salt-eaten but not weak, a gate meant to keep exactly this kind of visit from happening without a great deal more noise than any of them could afford to make.

"Cut lock," Doyle said, running his three-band scan across it. "Old work. Loud work, if we force it."

"I can be quiet," Wren said.

Nobody answered him for a moment.

"Cut his hands loose," Lyra said finally, to Doyle, not looking away from Wren while she said it. "One knife. Watched the whole time."

Orim did not say what he was thinking. He did not have to. He simply moved to where he could see both the gate and the boy at once, and stood there, and waited.

Wren flexed his freed hands twice, testing them the way a man tests an old injury, and put his palm flat to the iron.

It would have been easy, he thought, in the half-second before he went through it — easy to fold to the far side alone and simply keep going, into whatever this coastline had for a man with no crew, no case, and no further debts to anyone. Nobody was holding him. Not really. A knife and three people watching was not the same thing as a rope.

He went through the gate instead, and came back out through it a moment later from the inside, and lifted the bar, and stood back to let them through.

"Wasn't loud," he said, which was as close as he ever came to asking whether that had counted for anything.

Doyle looked at him for a second longer than the gate required. "Wasn't," he agreed, and went first down the stairs.

They went down. The south stairs were cut into the limestone, worn round at the edges by salt and boots. He took the third step light, matching what Lyra had shown him without meaning to. He set it once, correct, permanent — the same instinct that told a miner which timber in a low seam didn't want his full weight on it, learned once and never forgotten.

The dock was planking over water, the cave's version of the sea moving underneath it, not surf, not current, just water in a space that didn't go anywhere and knew it. The cold down here wasn't the cold above. Above was sea cold, which had range, which came from somewhere and went on to somewhere else. Down here was drift cold, the cold that had been sitting in the same dark since before anybody cut a shaft into it, uninterested in sharing any of that dark with something as temporary as a man passing through.

The Balthier looked big from the cave mouth, and kept being bigger the closer he got, until by thirty meters off it was just the size of the thing: deep in the belly, high in the shoulder, built for load and weather and not for looking at, the same blunt logic as a tipple. The envelope sat against the cave ceiling like a darker solid in the dark, the rigid frame running her whole length.

RANGE: four meters. TARGET: seated, stationary. NOISE FLOOR: acceptable.

The read came and went behind his eyes, quick and cold and none of his, same as always now, laid over whatever he actually felt like a grease sheet over a page. He went to the man from the water side, in the hull's shadow, where the running lights didn't reach. He didn't run. Running made a noise a man's ears caught before his mind did. He closed the last four meters weight-forward and quiet, feet finding the dry spots on the planking by something that wasn't memory of this dock, because he'd never walked it, but was memory all the same.

The soldier's chin came up a quarter turn, the start of a thought that was going to arrive half a second too late to matter to him. Then it was over. Hand to the jaw and the base of the skull, blade in at the throat, up and in — the same angle he'd found once already and hadn't needed shown to him twice, same as a man doesn't need reminding twice which way a pick swings easiest. There wasn't much sound. There was a lot of weight, all of a sudden, in his other arm, and he took it, and set the man down on the planking instead of letting him drop, catching the rifle with his boot before it could ring.

He held there three seconds, one hand still at the collar, feeling for the second heartbeat. It didn't come. He'd have been surprised if it had.

The second man had finished with the mooring lines and stood at the hatch coaming, still, in the specific way of a man who'd run out of tasks and was waiting on the clock instead of an officer to give him the next one. He never heard it coming. Same arm, same economical work at the throat, same setting-down instead of a drop. Declan stood over both of them afterward with his hands loose at his sides, and didn't feel much of anything he had words ready for.

"Clear," he told her, when she reached him. He looked at his hands, turned them once, and didn't say anything else about that.

They moved. The gangway held their weight. He found the cargo hatch release before she did — recessed, manual, a Liturgy design he'd never once laid eyes on in his life, but a handle was a handle regardless of the paint on the hull around it. Machinery kept a family resemblance to other machinery, whatever Sphere had built it, same as a tipple frame here would still look like a tipple frame to a man who'd worked one on the other side of everything. He had the hatch open before she'd finished crossing the gangway.

The hold smelled of fuel oil and dry warehouse dust, a smell that didn't change whether a man was standing in it at Ocipheus or the far end of the Wall. Orim came down last, carrying the small shell over one shoulder as though it weighed nothing like its size suggested it should. Black, jointed, the oval eye dark and flat, arms loose in the way of a thing built to move on its own say-so and currently not saying anything.

That was him. Or had been him, twenty minutes back, walking itself down off the shelf under its own power before folding still at the mouth of the path — left there like a coat a man means to come back for.

Orim set the chassis against the cargo crates, upright, propped, the eye facing the room like something not yet opened. "Guard the shell," he said, mostly to himself now, the instruction long past needing to be new.

"I have it," Lyra said, putting herself between the shell and the hatch, because that was the door, and the door was where the taking-away happened.

The small shell stayed exactly where Orim had propped it, dark, unneeded for what came next. What went forward was only the part of him that had never needed legs to begin with — Declan, alone, to the bulkhead between the hold and the control car. Dark instrument cluster on standby, needles resting at zero, still as a sleeping animal's legs. He put his hands flat against the metal, testing for heat before he opened it.

There was something on the other side.

He'd known there would be. Orim had felt it from the shelf and hadn't wanted to push through it, and Declan didn't blame him - some men could read the shape of a thing and knew better than to walk into it. Declan didn't have that choice. He'd never had that choice, not since Sphere 31, not since whatever had been done to him made him a mind that could be poured out of one vessel and into another like water changing which cup held it.

She's not empty, he thought, and the thought carried no surprise at all, only the flat certainty of a man checking a gauge and finding the reading he'd already known he'd find. A ship like this - Liturgy iron, built to bring herself home even with her crew dead at the wheel - wasn't given standby current and left to sit dumb in the dark. She was given a mind to keep her warm. Something had been living in Balthier the whole while Atrament called her captured inventory. Sleeping, maybe. Sleeping wasn't the same as gone, and a mind left alone that long, in the dark, with no hand ever coming to the wheel, wasn't sleeping so much as waiting to be asked for something - anything - again.

He remembered Turret #34, though he tried not to, most days. Remembered being one gun among a bank of guns, all of them crewed the same way he was crewing this - a mind fitted into an emplacement built for something without one, made to answer for cannon fire as lungs answered for breath, without being asked first whether it wanted to. He remembered what refusing to fire that one gun at the Forge had cost the operation, and what it had felt like after, small and single, in a mount built for a hundred men's decisions and given only his.

He had been the resident mind, once. Now he was the hand reaching in to take one out.

There wasn't a version of this that let both of them keep the room.

He went in anyway. Not the way a man walks through a door — there wasn't a door for what he was doing, just intention laid against intention, his whole back thrown into a jammed valve wheel that hadn't turned in ten years and wasn't going to turn easy. He found the other mind in the dark behind the bulkhead as a man finds a gas pocket in a low seam: not by seeing it, by the wrongness of the air right before it kills him if he isn't careful. It didn't fight him with words. It didn't have words, maybe never had, or maybe had lost them — a lamp guttering out in bad air, not from an attack but from something taking up all the room where the burning used to happen.

He put it out.

It took less time than the two men on the dock had taken, and less sound, and he felt every second of it in a way he hadn't let himself feel the two men, because the two men had wanted their next breath in a way he understood in his body, mule-simple, and this — whatever it had been, however long it had sat dark in a Liturgy hull answering to nobody's name — hadn't fought like a thing that wanted anything at all by the end. That was worse, somehow.

He counted it. One more, quiet, alongside the two on the dock. Three now.

He didn't tell her. He wasn't going to.

Then the ship was his the way the small shell had never once been big enough to let him forget it was a shell.

The oval eye behind him in the hold went dark — he felt it go, the flat nothing of a hand already asleep before he thought to look down and check — and for four seconds there was nothing. Then the port outboard engine caught, one decisive turn instead of the cough of an ordinary cold start, the whole huge fact of the thing remembering all at once what it was built for. It ran through him like voltage through a rail he'd have known better than to touch back home. The running lights came up down the gangway rail one after another, west to east, and each one was a nerve he hadn't had an hour ago, waking in an order he hadn't set and couldn't have stopped.

He had been a shell a man could carry over one shoulder. Now he had a keel, four engines, a rudder aft answering questions before he'd finished asking them, and a hull forty meters long holding water and air and two people trusting all of it to a man who, three days ago, hadn't known this vessel existed. It was the loneliest kind of big he'd ever been. Bigger than Turret #34. Bigger than anything they'd built him for at Sphere 31. Bigger, even, than the monster corridor had felt with the bolt jammed and no time. There was room in him now for things to happen he wouldn't feel arrive until they already had — a hatch forty meters aft, opening while his attention sat forty meters forward — the same blind spot that let a round take a man in the back while his eyes stayed fixed on the thing in front of him.

He didn't love it. He didn't have a better way home, so he'd allow it.

The ballast found its own level without his announcing it to himself, same as breathing didn't wait on being told. The hull backed off the dock, the planking groaning against his own flank now, and that was a strange one to sit with — feeling wood complain against a body he had no skin on.

Through the whole backing-out he held the small shell motionless against the crates, dark, empty, a woman standing guard between it and the door, and he was aware of that guarding only distantly — present in his mind, absent from his hands.

Near the middle of the dock the envelope's shoulder found the cave ceiling and dragged. A long sound, metal on stone, dry and sustained — the kind of sound that put everybody in earshot dead still, working out whether they were hearing a scrape or a failure. He felt it up through the whole envelope — the same low-body certainty as a truck axle grinding, he knew whether it was going to hold before he could have put it into words. It held. It stopped. He kept moving.

Then there was no ceiling. There was the cave mouth, and past it the flat gray promise of open water, and past that nothing but the shape of a sky that hadn't decided yet whether it meant to be visible this morning.

He backed her clear of the cliff, felt the swell take the hull's weight the way ground takes the weight off a man's boots when he steps from pavement onto softer footing, then ran her up to speed across open water, bow spray going up at the hatch seam, the skip at the edge between dragging and lifting, and then the lifting itself — the whole ship coming up off the sea at once, the water-sound falling away, wind and engine taking its place. He felt the particular nothing of a hull that had quit touching anything at all.

He climbed her east along the cliff line, banked once, leveled her into open sky, and somewhere in the middle of that climb he understood something his own body had never been asked before: that flying a thing this size wasn't like wearing a coat that fit. It was like being handed a whole crew's worth of decisions to make alone, and finding, to his own private surprise, that he could.

For a while — call it twenty minutes, he wasn't counting that one — he let her fly herself, trimmed and level, needles where they belonged, heading held, and came back down into the small shell in the hold, because there was a woman on the other side of that bulkhead who'd earned the right to see him do it.

The oval eye came alight as the engines had caught — not building toward brightness, just arriving, whole. The chassis's head came up off its chest by degrees, joints finding their places with the hesitance of a man standing after sitting too long in cold weather.

"M-101," it said. The voice was thin, tin, no tone in it at all — the shell had never once given him a way to sound like anything, and tonight, for the length of one held breath, he was almost glad of it. A voice with no color couldn't accidentally tell her what the last twenty minutes had cost.

"You're back?" she said. Not a question. She'd learned not to make it one.

He didn't answer that. He looked at his hands instead, turning them just as he had over the two men on the dock, except this time the checking meant something else — a man confirming that the hands in front of him were the ones he currently owned. They were. He held the position a beat longer than the confirming needed.

"Ship," he said. He tapped two fingers against the bulkhead, once. "Big."

There wasn't a word in Entos, or in whatever was left of his old tongue, for what the interval between casting out and casting back had felt like — going somewhere that size and finding the way back to somewhere this small. He didn't reach for one. She'd watched his hands. That would do for tonight.

Then he went forward again, back through the bulkhead gap, and the small shell's hands went still against the frame again, and the oval eye went dark, and the ship — steady, level, already committed to her heading — kept flying, because she had never once actually stopped being him.

He surfaced once more before the channel, without needing to check his hands first this time.

"Two," he said.

He held up two fingers, in case tin and no-tone hadn't carried the number on its own.

There was a word for what he was — murderer, or soldier, or some third thing the language hadn't built a slot for yet — and he had it, as much as he'd ever had anything in his own mouth, and he didn't reach for that one either. He gave her the number instead. Stated it. Made her the one who received it, the way a man hands over a tool he's done with rather than asking someone to take it off him.

Two. Not three.

Three sat behind his teeth where the number for the men on the dock didn't reach it — the truth held back, only one number let out into daylight tonight. He wasn't going to give her the other one. It wasn't hers to carry, and it wasn't a debt that paid any easier for a second person holding half of it. A man learned, working underground long enough, which loads he could rope a second pair of hands onto and which ones a body carried alone or not at all.

"It was the operation," she said.

He heard the frame close around it when she said it — the kind of language built to take a fact and set it down someplace that made it carriable. He knew what she was doing. He let her do it, and didn't correct the number, and didn't explain what the number would have to be if he told her the whole of it.

"I know," he said, quiet, in Entos that didn't stop short of any of it this time.

Then the eye went dark again, and the ship — which had never once stopped holding her own heading — kept holding it, because she hadn't needed him visibly present to be flying, and hadn't needed him present, either, for the rest of it to already be settled.

Orim, from the crate he'd claimed as the one in the hold likely to hold his weight, said, without opening his eyes: "That is a very large room to be lost in."

"He isn't lost," Lyra said, ahead of him, before he'd even decided whether he needed to answer for himself.

"No," Orim agreed. "Not yet."

Declan — the shell in the hold, dark, propped, saying nothing — heard both of those from two places at once, same as everything else now, and corrected neither one, because both were true enough for tonight, and the part that wasn't true belonged to the one he hadn't told her about.

Later, when the ship took her name — Doyle's own words carried over into Entos, the ground that undid, a thing that had been solid and had stopped being solid and never told anyone it intended to — Declan, from wherever in the hull he was keeping himself just then, thought that whoever had chosen it had never asked the thing living inside her whether that was the word she'd have picked. Nobody had asked her that. He hadn't asked her that either, in the end. There hadn't been time to ask, and he wasn't sure, even now, that asking would have changed what he'd had to do to her.

That was the one he counted and didn't tell.

The horizon went from black to the first gray suggestion of morning, the sky doing what it did every day without needing anybody's attention to accomplish it. The cave was thirty kilometers behind them. The channel was hours ahead, and somewhere in those hours he was going to have to tell Lyra something else he hadn't told her yet — not this, not tonight — and there was no getting ready for it, just hours of flying left to spend finding out whether getting ready was even possible.

The ship flew east, and Declan Doyle flew with her, in two places, one of them empty and propped against a crate, one of them everywhere at once, and only one of them keeping count.

The channel at altitude gave off no smell worth logging, which was the first fact about it that didn't square with what nine years underground and fourteen months in France had taught him to expect of open water. Water announces itself. Salt, or river mud, or the flat mineral nothing of a lake at night — a man learns to read a place by what's on the air before he trusts what his eyes tell him about it. The Balthier's hull sealed all of that out. What was left, inside the envelope, was the ship's own history instead of the sea's: machine oil, old rope, the flat institutional smell of Liturgy supply crates stacked and stenciled for a use nobody aboard remembered anymore. Iron handholds bolted along the bulkhead at intervals, for a crew that had once expected the air to turn rough under them and had, evidently, been right about that.

He had taken her out past the Sal Vento roofline with his own attention doing the flying and the shell sitting empty against a crate, like a coat left on a chairback while he did the actual work in his shirtsleeves. He'd learned something out over open water he hadn't fully reckoned with before that night: a man — even a man wearing a ship — cannot be two places. Coming back into the shell was worse than going out of it. Half a beat behind himself every time, an echo answering a shout the shout had already stopped meaning.

- trim held, heading true, envelope pressure nominal — the report arrived from nowhere, same as always, and he let it pass through him and didn't answer it. He never did.

He'd told Lyra once the ship would hold her heading on her own trim after he was out of her, and that it would hold it until something changed. She'd chosen to believe that instead of watching the wheel all night herself, and the wheel had not moved in three hours, and it no longer put the fear in her that it once had.

She had the notebook open on her knee, and had had it open, blank, for two hours. He knew what she was trying to write without being told outright — Ardea's service record, the instrument that let the Ocipheus garrison notify a family properly — and she'd been chasing a first sentence around that page since before they cleared the roofline. She had the facts. He'd watched her have the facts for two hours: intake year, posting history, tincture certifications, the action in the field that had ended her. He didn't know a service record's proper shape any more than she knew a drift's, but he knew what stuck looked like on a person — the same recognition as spotting a man pinned under a beam that won't come loose. It looked like her.

She closed the notebook. She didn't put it away.

Orim was at the forward porthole, fingertips flat against the glass, the passive attunement position he used when he wanted to know if the air ahead had anything in it worth knowing about. He'd been tired since the gorge. A different tired than the road kind. It hadn't resolved.

Doyle had been at the starboard porthole himself, half an hour, looking down at water the color of something that had been cold a long time and didn't expect to change. He knew what he was thinking. He didn't know yet if it was ready to be said. He'd been circling the decision same as he used to circle a bad prop before he trusted himself to pull it — walking the same ground twice, three times, until the ground stopped changing its mind about him.

He came away from the porthole.

He sat down across from her, against the bulkhead, at the distance he kept now from habit in enclosed spaces — room enough to do something different if something different became necessary. Hands to his knees. Rifle slung same as always. He knew what expression he was wearing without needing a mirror for it. He'd worn it before, alone, saying this exact sentence into the dark over a crate wall until it wore the groove he stepped into now without slipping.

She put the notebook down behind her, against the bulkhead, and didn't open it again.

He said, in Entos: "I want to tell you something."

The grammar came out correct, as it always did — the shell gave him that for free, complete sentences from the first hour in this world whether he wanted them or not. What wasn't free was the willingness. That part he'd built himself, the long way, saying it into the dark until it wore the groove it came out in now: flat, tin-flat, the chassis's same exact affect on every word regardless of what the word cost him. He'd been building to this since Schmalburg. He knew she'd noticed — the questions he'd started asking her afterward that weren't about danger, or food, or direction, but about sequence. About then. About things already happened that no better language could change.

She said:

"I'm listening."

He started with the mine.

Carter County had more coal camps than most maps bothered naming, and his wasn't the biggest of them — he'd worked bigger ones, further up the line, before the seam at the home drift took him on for good. He gave her the camp's name in his own language first, watching to see if she needed Entos for it.

"It doesn't need one," she said.

He nodded. Six years at that drift, longer all told once you counted the season on a different seam two counties over, the surface work before that. He knew the cutting machines, the pumps, the fan gear the company had put in his second year — the ordinary knowledge of any man who'd spent years underground.

"By sound," he said. "Different sound, different problem. You learn it, or the mountain teaches you the hard way."

There was weight in that last part, the weight of a fact that's also its own grief.

He stopped. He looked at his hands.

"I was good at my work," he said. "I want you to know that. I am not telling you I was nobody." He said the word plainly, flat and exact, and let it sit before he went on. "I had a name. I had a place. People knew me."

"I know," she said.

"I don't think you do," he said, without offense. "You know I was Scried out of somewhere. You don't know I was someone there, first."

She held still with that. She hadn't thought to separate the two facts before — he could see it land on her, the specific stillness of a person absorbing a correction against something she'd assumed was settled.

"Tell me," she said.

A Thursday in autumn. He knew the day because the day shift overlapped the fireboss's inspection for forty minutes on Thursdays, and the overlap changed the noise underground into something else, two crews occupying the same iron for slightly different purposes. He knew the hour from the sound the drift-mouth bell made past the half-hour. He knew the season from the angle of light through the tipple framing when he came up. He had always tracked the conditions of the room he was in. It wasn't a special skill. It was simply how he'd been made to notice things.

"Then," he said.

He stopped.

"Then," he said again, and let the one word carry the weight of the three hundred that would follow it — everything between half past the hour, Thursday morning and the floor was not the floor. He had all the words. What he didn't have, yet, was the nerve to spend them in order. His jaw worked once, the specific motion of a man arriving at the edge of what he was willing to say aloud and finding it a genuine edge, with a drop on the other side.

"Take it in pieces," she said.

He looked at her. He nodded.

The floor changed first. Not gone — wrong, a different quality under his boots, the kind of wrongness a man who works a coal face all day is built to notice before he understands it. He checked himself: was he unwell?

Had something failed in the rock over his head?

He wasn't unwell. The floor wasn't broken.

Then the stone room.

He told it evenly — the specific economy of a man who has learned what feeling costs and has decided, for the length of this telling, not to spend it. Dressed stone. Cold — not the drift's winter cold, which was just weather doing what weather does, but the cold of stone that had been cold for centuries and intended to stay that way. Three figures in robes. He gave her the colors and she recognized them before he said the word: Ocipheus white, canonical gold at the hem.

"Ocipheus," he said, flat as he said anything true. Not an accusation. A fact laid on the table between them. The word came out exactly like every other word he'd given her tonight — flat, level, uninflected — and that was what made it land as hard as it did. A fully articulate voice, saying the truth outright, in a register built to carry ammunition counts.

"Yes," she said.

"One of them held a book."

He was quiet a moment — not the quiet of having nothing left, the quiet of choosing which piece carried the most per word.

"I was not afraid," he said. "I want you to understand that. I have been afraid. I was in France fourteen months. I know what afraid does to a body." He paused. "This was a different thing. This was the feeling of a room whose rules no one had given me. Three people. Robes. No context for any of it. I did what a man does in that room. I made myself as legible as I could. Cooperative. I thought — if I am cooperative, someone will explain."

He looked at his hands.

"No one explained," he said.

"I know the intake protocol," she said, after a while. Flat, same as she always did with hard things — he'd noticed the family resemblance in how they each spent a bad sentence. "I read it my first year at the Station. I read it again three years in, when the documentation project needed hands. I wrote a line for the literature myself, once. I don't remember thinking it was cruelty when I wrote it." She paused. "Oath administered prior to language acquisition minimizes resistive response in the conscript cohort. That's the sentence. I meant it as an observation about efficiency."

He said nothing to that for a while. Long enough that she looked up from her hands to check he'd heard her.

"I know," he said, finally. "That's why I'm telling it in order. So you hear the room before you hear the sentence that runs it."

"The words had a rhythm to them," he said. "Official. I knew the shape of it — some of it, not all — the instant I heard it, the same as you'd know a hymn even in a language you'd never studied. I've never told anyone that until right now. I'm not telling you the rest of it yet either. I'm telling this in order, same as I said I would."

He looked at his hands.

She recognized it at once.

"The acknowledgment position," she said. "From the Compact Oath. Formal assent to canonical authority."

He paused.

"I never decided," he said. "I never understood fast enough."

Orim had not moved from the porthole. His fingertips stayed on the glass. He said nothing.

The barracks were stone-vaulted, high-windowed, old — a category he recognized without being able to place it, and had stopped trying to explain once the recognizable and the wholly foreign had gotten too tangled to separate. The rifles were on racks along the wall, and he had known rifles; he'd carried one in France.

"I knew the action," he said, and mimed the bolt, the cycling motion. "Not exactly. Later. Further along — like a later model of a thing you already know."

He looked at her.

"In my world there were men whose whole careers were tracing how a mechanism developed — who built the first one, who improved it, who licensed the design. You can follow forty years of small changes back to an origin. The rifles here don't belong to that history. They're past the end of it. But the lineage's there, if you know how to read a receiver housing." He held her eyes. "Someone had my world's designs, and time to take them further than my world has had."

"Someone had been watching," he said. "Long before they brought me here."

He waited.

"Yes," she said.

He nodded, and went on.

The barracks held conscripts from at least a dozen points of origin, by his count, and most of them shared no language. The House ran intake on a set of demonstrated commands that required no shared vocabulary at all. Stand. Walk. Carry. Wait. Those were the first words said to him in Entos, and the shell rendered them the instant they landed — understanding arrived whether he wanted it or not. The collar markers were administrative — a stamped disc, a sphere number, punched in during processing. He had seen his own: Sphere 31. He had seen Sphere 22. Sphere 14. A man from Sphere 8 worked the maintenance crews by then, competent in Entos, missing a fingertip to some machine that had closed too fast, and had looked at Doyle in his first week with an expression that said:

I know what this is doing to you, and it gets better in some ways and worse in others, and I can't tell you which way you're headed.

Orim's fingertips pressed a fraction harder against the glass.

The program had run, in its documented form, since Year 1064 — three hundred years — and the practice before the documentation, the part the early canonical scholars wrote around rather than through, went back further than anyone had troubled to count, which was itself the point of never counting it.

"How many people know what the stone room looks like?" he asked.

"Because the oath is given before the language."

She waited.

"On purpose," he said.

"Yes," she said. "On purpose."

He told her about the man from Sphere 22 next, because the numbers had opened a door he could walk the rest of the way through.

Two months further into his own time in Verlaine than Doyle was, mid-forties, the particular stillness of a man who has done physical work for twenty years and learned to conserve his motion. A missing fingertip of his own, on the other hand — a different machine, evidently, but the same category of loss. They had tried, some evenings, to find overlap between their own languages — not Entos, which the shells gave each of them freely and separately, but the tongues they'd each been taken out of, which no shell had ever been built to carry between two stolen men. The kind of trying two men do when there's nothing else to fill an evening. You pointed. You said your word. Mostly there was nothing.

"We found one," Doyle said.

He paused.

"The word was mother," he said.

Orim's tincture bottle made a small sound against the porthole housing, barely audible under the envelope's argument with the wind.

"In both languages, nearly the same word," Doyle said. "Close enough that you said it, and heard his version, and both of you knew what had been said." He looked at his hands. "I don't know what that means. Whether it says something about language, or about what's the same underneath every world it's spoken in." He was quiet. "I thought about it for months afterward. What that word was doing there, in both of us, without either of us having put it there."

There was another man, from Sphere 8, the one with the maintenance work and the missing fingertip and enough Entos to be dangerous to himself in a careful way. In his second year he had shown Doyle the inside of his boot — the lining worked thin and soft, and words scratched into the leather in a hand that wasn't his own, in a shape he couldn't read and had never learned to read, memorized instead as a picture is memorized. He had gotten someone to write it for him, or copied the marks from someone who could, and kept them against his foot because a body that has nowhere else to keep a thing will keep it there.

He had recited it to Doyle, slowly, over several weeks — the halting instruction of a man teaching something he didn't fully understand himself. Doyle had kept it just as carefully since — not as scholarship, as ballast.

"He was built to serve," Doyle said now, and she could hear the quotation in it, the particular careful cadence of words being reproduced rather than made, exact and level — the same flatness the chassis gave to everything. "He chose to refuse. That word made him more a person than the-" He paused there, not for the word but for what came before it. "Entos doesn't have one. Not for a group of men who decide things about a drift without ever going down it. Not the miners. Above them." He let the gap stand instead of filling it. "It doesn't matter. The line was about refusing."

Orim's face, at the porthole, did something small and quickly put away — something more than recognition - the look of a man who has heard a chord struck before in a different room and cannot place which room.

"Who wrote it?" Lyra asked.

"I don't know," Doyle said. "He didn't know either. It came to him from someone else, the way most things came to us." He looked at his hands. "I've carried it the better part of a year without a name on it. I don't think it needs one to be true."

Later, once he trusted himself to ask, he had put the stone room's actual question to a Verlaine intake administrator: what had the words meant. The man had told him it was the oath of service, binding him to canon law for the term of his conscription.

"I asked him if it was valid," Doyle said, "if I hadn't understood it?"

She was already waiting for the answer.

"He said that was a theological question, and he didn't have the training to address it."

"The notation," Doyle said. "On the rifle."

"Yes," she said.

"I've seen the pattern before. In my world. Men who studied where designs came from could see the gaps — pieces that weren't native to a lineage, things adapted in from somewhere else, a long time back. We had theories about it. A lost civilization. Older than us. We thought: they built the first things, and disappeared, and we found what they'd left and carried it forward." He paused. "We were proud of that. The search for the origin. The respect for the debt."

He looked at his hands.

"We were right that the knowledge wasn't originally ours," he said. "We were wrong about the civilization being lost. You didn't disappear. You just kept taking."

She held very still.

"I'm not angry," he said, in the even way he said accurate things — not flattened by distance, simply stated without adding weight to a thing that was already heavy enough to carry on its own. "I don't have the strength for it today. I'm telling you because you have the most of the language, and because your institution wrote the forms I put my mark on, and processed the files that had me in them before I had a name for Entos. I think you should know that one of the people in those files is telling you this himself."

"I know," she said.

"I have known, and not known, longer than I'd like to admit."

He looked at her, and didn't recognize what came back at him — the first time in six weeks her face hadn't been legible to him inside the two seconds he usually needed for it.

He sat with that a while before he told her there was one more thing, and that he'd put it last on purpose.

"There's one part of it I've been sitting on since before I had a name for anything else in this world," he said. "I want to say it now, while I've got the rest of it said."

She waited.

"You have a name for the Canons' rite-tongue," he said. "The one no scribe gets taught whole. The one that comes down over a rite, or a writ, or the dead, and nobody but a Canon reads the whole of it."

"The Received Tongue," she said.

"That's the name for it, then." He turned that over once, set it down. "I didn't have a name for it, the first time I heard it. I didn't have anything else yet either. Not Entos. Not you, or Orim, or a shell that answered when I told it to move. It came at me raw that first time, unlaundered — the one thing in that whole stone room that didn't arrive already translated for me. And I knew it anyway."

Her stillness had a different quality to it now than it had all night. He'd learned six weeks of her stillnesses without meaning to, the way a man tracks a room's noise without meaning to, and this one didn't match any he already knew.

"Say it," she said. "If you're going to."

He said it.

Level, unhurried, the same flat tin delivery the shell gave every sentence he'd spoken tonight, sacred and profane alike — the shell had never once made a distinction between the two.

"Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine."

Nothing in the hold answered it. The envelope kept its argument with the wind. Orim's fingertips didn't move on the glass, though something in his shoulders did.

"It says: grant them rest, forever, Lord," Doyle said. "That's the whole of it. One of those words is a name for God I don't think you use for anything else, and none of it is stranger than a stone somebody paid a mason to cut." He looked at his hands. "That's the same shape of word that's cut over the door of the church back home, and into fieldstone in the yard behind it, on markers going back further than anybody living remembers to visit. A schoolteacher in a one-room house with a stove that never drew right made every boy in the county say it until we could say it without thinking, whether or not the meaning ever stuck — the same as we said our times tables. That's where I know it from. Not from anything holy. From a stove-warmed room and a graveyard full of my neighbors."

He looked up.

"It's not a mystery," he said. "It's not the voice of anything. It's a language nobody living where I'm from speaks outside that stove-warmed room or a Sunday service, and it hasn't been spoken like that in about ninety years. It's old. It's dead. And it is not yours."

She didn't say anything for a long moment. He let her have the moment. He'd learned, from her, that a testimony wasn't done being given just because the mouth had stopped moving.

"The first word," he said, when she still hadn't spoken, "the very first sound made over me in that stone room, before the tally, before the numbers, before I had a shell or a name for a shell — was that same word. Requiem." He said it again, plain, the schoolboy shape of it instead of the holy one. "It means rest. That's all it ever meant. They took my name and my hands and nine years underground and the whole of what happened to me in France, and the first word they did it with was the word for rest." He paused, and the pause carried what the sentence before it hadn't. "I have carried that since the stone room without telling a living soul, because there wasn't one soul in this whole gold-lit place who I thought would believe the man with no country that your God's own voice was a dead schoolboy tongue off a Tennessee hillside. I'm telling you because you asked me to say it, and because you've heard it said over your own dead more times than I've heard it said over anything. I thought you should know what you've been hearing."

She sat with the notebook shut in her lap and didn't reach for it. Her hand found the strap of it instead — the specific white-knuckle grip he'd seen on new recruits handling a rifle for the first time, not from lack of respect, from too much of it arriving all at once.

It was Orim who broke first, not her.

He turned from the porthole. It was a small movement — he turned, and stood with his back against the hull, looking at neither of them in particular. His face had the quality Doyle had seen once on a foreman reading a tally that had come up short two shifts running: the look of a man confronting something he'd spent years managing not to look at straight on, and could no longer keep at the distance of the abstract.

"I have said that word," Orim said. "Over field-dead. Over departures. Over you, Doyle, at your own commissioning oath, though you'll have no memory of it being said a second time by a different mouth. I have said it more times than I have fingers and toes to count on, in the tone we are taught denotes reverence, and I did not know, until a minute ago, that I was saying rest the way a stonecutter says it."

His voice was even — not flat, even, the way an instrument reads even when it is accurate on both sides of a scale. "I have read intake reports for forty years. Verlaine commissioning requests. No names. Sphere designation, skill classification, intake number." He looked at the hold floor. "I knew what I was reading. I knew what M-cohort, Sphere thirty-one, intake ninety-seven meant, the way a surgeon knows what a wound classification means without needing to see the patient." He paused. "I told myself I was being accurate. That someone else would do the work if I didn't. That knowing the cost made the cost somehow -" He stopped. "I don't have the word either. Not in Entos. Probably not in any language."

Doyle looked at him.

"I can no longer say I did not hear it," Orim said. "I'm telling you that so you know that I know."

He didn't say more. He turned back to the porthole, fingertips returning to the glass, the passive attunement settling over him like a posture he had held for fifty years because it was the shape that went with hearing things accurately, and accuracy was the only thing he had left to offer anyone.

Doyle looked at the back of Orim's head a moment. Then at Lyra.

She didn't reach for the notebook. She didn't reach for anything. He'd learned, by now, that this was what her particular kind of nothing-to-say looked like from the outside — not empty, just working through something too large to hand back in words yet.

"I'm not asking you to fix it," he said, after a while.

"I know," she said.

"I'm asking you to know it. There's a difference."

"Yes," she said. "There is."

The silence that came after it was a different silence than the one before — not comfortable, not meant to be, but the silence of a thing said and no longer capable of being unsaid, which sat instead of pressing forward. He watched her sit with it. The notebook stayed shut behind her the whole time, through the whole of what he'd given her — the first testimony he'd have bet she'd ever taken down in nothing but memory, nothing a vigil rotation would ever get to close out as no action indicated.

"What do you call this?" Orim asked, from the porthole, without turning. "Not the ship. This — what just happened. In your own tongue."

The question dropped into the quiet like a stone into still water. Nobody answered at once. The envelope kept its argument with the wind. The gray at the portholes had gone from night-gray to the paler gray that meant the sea had decided to be seen.

Doyle said a short phrase in his own language. She caught the shape of it more than the whole of it, and offered the nearest Entos she had.

"The ground undone."

He looked at her. The vowel was wrong; he adjusted it.

"The ground that undid," he said. The construction wasn't quite the shape his own language would have given it, but it was the shape Entos grammar allowed, and it carried the weight regardless — a thing that had been solid, and had stopped being solid, and never told anyone it intended to.

Orim didn't answer that, still facing the water, in the manner of a man who'd asked a small question and gotten back something too large to hand back a reply to.

Lyra found the notebook and opened it. Not in the shape of a research entry — something she didn't yet have an institutional box for. She wrote the ground that undid, Doyle's words, and beneath it: Doyle. Sphere 31. Thursday.

She closed it.

The Calyx uplands came up on the eastern horizon three hours later — first a darkening in the gray that was more solid than sky, the specific dark of a thing that had stood in the same place a long time and did not intend to move for the wind's convenience. Then the particular black of high karst country, the gray limestone she'd told him weeks back she'd only ever studied on cartographic plates, and was now, abruptly, simply seeing.

The wheel by the aft bulkhead began, without a hand on it, to answer to a hand somewhere else — Balthier gathering herself for the descent to come, a held breath about to let go. He'd have to go back into her soon, down into the river gorge by feel, same as he'd lifted her clear of Sal Vento; the small shell that carried the rest of him would go still again on the floor, and somebody would need to stand near it, because a shell with nothing in it was a shell anyone could claim. He'd stopped finding that strange around week five. It was just a fact of travel now, like weather.

Lyra looked at the coast.

She'd told him once, some evening weeks back when neither of them could sleep, about a Relic she'd recovered years before this one — a tangled Verlaine provenance dispute, the better part of a season's work. She'd been thirty-two, entirely certain of herself, wrong about two of three key facts and right about the outcome anyway, and had set the whole business aside as a lesson in professional humility before moving to the next assignment.

She didn't have that certainty in her now. He recognized the look anyway — the bearing of somebody handed a fact that restructures everything standing behind it, walking forward into ground she hadn't had time to survey. He didn't know if she'd trained for uncertainty or just gotten used to other people's. Tonight looked like the first time it had been entirely her own.

Doyle came to stand beside her at the forward porthole.

He didn't speak at first. He looked at the coast. She looked at the coast.

After a while he said, in Entos: "What will you do with it? When you have it?"

He meant the Relic, and she knew it without him needing to spell out which them. Not a weapon, whatever Sanctorin actually wanted from it — not by her own account, given to him weeks back over a different fire. The founding canon called it the Eye That Remembers and never explained further, and she'd told him once, half-laughing at herself, that she'd served the Vigil seventeen years without ever once asking why a thing built only to remember needed three locks, a vigil rotation, and a Canon's countersignature standing between it and anyone who might want to look.

"I don't know yet," she said.

"But not what they want you to do with it."

She looked at him.

"You would have given it to them, before Bressant," he said. "I don't know who them is. But you would have. You were the kind of person who gave things to the right authority."

"And now?" she asked.

"Now you know I am in the authority's files."

She looked at Calyx, closer now, more specific — the scarps resolving into individual faces of old-bone rock in the coming light, the moor somewhere above them she couldn't yet see but knew was there.

"Yes," she said.

He nodded. He turned from the porthole and went to find a place to settle before the ship began her descent — a place near enough to his shell that when it went still, someone guarding it would only have to look, not search. He felt her watch him go.

Caer Glas was ahead, close enough now that the moor above the scarps had come clear in the gray, green going black going green again as cloud crossed it. He stood a while longer near the place he'd chosen, not because there was anything more to see there than a minute before, but because a man who has just spent the whole of what he was carrying needs somewhere to stand while his hands remember what to do with themselves.

He'd told it in order, the way he'd promised himself he would — the mine, the France of it, the stone room, the number that ate his name, and the last thing, carried longest, said last on purpose. It didn't feel like relief. It felt like a weight handed off to somebody with two working hands to carry it, instead of one man alone in a gold-lit hold with nobody who'd believe him.

Behind him, he heard the notebook open, and then, after a while, close again without the pen ever having moved on it.

Somewhere below decks a lamp had gone out and been relit with a different fuel — yggdra kindling, the ship's own dry stock of it, and the smoke of it came up through the grate at his feet before the wind took it. He went still over it for exactly as long as the earlier stillness had taken him, out at the trees, before the Rod had failed. Not the same stillness. A softer one, the kind a man doesn't fight.

Lyra didn't ask.

She'd learned enough of his stillnesses tonight to know this one wasn't hers to have — not because he'd said so, he hadn't said a word about it, but because a man who spends an entire night handing over everything a stone room and a war took from him does not owe the one thing he chose, on his own, to keep. She had a name for what he was doing, from her own life, though she'd never once needed the exact fit of it before tonight: some doors you don't knock on twice, because the first knock already told you there was somebody standing on the other side of it, holding still, hoping you'd walk on.

She let him have it. He never told her the smoke reminded him of anything, and she never asked what it was he wasn't saying. It was, she thought, the only part of the whole testimony that hadn't cost him something to give her — because he hadn't given it to her at all, and they had both, without a word passing between them on the subject, agreed that was exactly as it should stay.

Caer Glas was ahead. He left Lyra with what he'd given her, because that was hers to carry now, the same as the rest of it.
