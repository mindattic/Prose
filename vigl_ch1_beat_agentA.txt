He had already spent one fold - the trick of stepping clean through a membrane and out the other side of it - on the crossing before Esvane's boots found the archive floor, and the second one left him thinner just standing there. The membrane didn't care what a man owed or to whom. It asked the same fee off everyone - a heartbeat of warm gold with no up and no edges, and then stone underfoot again, colder for having kept him waiting in it.

He came through four seconds after her, sideways - not badly wrong, but wrong enough to catch the shelving with his shoulder, a codex sliding two finger-widths before he caught it flat-palmed. Esvane looked at the codex, then at him.

He straightened and said nothing; nothing he could say would cost him less than the shelf already had.

He'd made a promise once, to someone he did not let himself think about, that he would not become a man who stopped noticing what a thing cost him. The fold always left the same residue: gold cooling too slow on the skin, a half-breath of nothing that took longer to shake off each time.

The inner archive smelled of seven hundred years of Catalyst seepage - the same warm-metal tang that clung to a man after every fold he'd ever spent - and oiled iron, old and wet at the edges. The stone felt watched, patient the way only something seven centuries old can afford to be.

The box sat on the display table. Oiled oak gone black with handling, cornered in cold steel, locked to a standard no ordinary key would flatter. Built to be kept, not admired. A man who didn't know what was in it would walk past and never feel the debt it was owed.

Esvane crossed the room in four steps and put her hands on the box.

The Sentinel opened its eyes.

He'd read the founding deposition same as she had. The Liturgy's first Scriveners had written the Sentinel's construction with the care of people who knew exactly what they were building. Six arms, jointed three times each. Iron over consecrated stone, cut all over with doctrine instead of left smooth, so the whole of it read less like a lock than an altar built to worship the thing it guarded.

Forty-one years in that room, facing the lens, doing nothing — a debt fully discharged, never asked to run again. He'd envied that line once, reading it. He envied it again now, watching it move, and hated the envy a little, because nothing woken like this had ever finished paying anything off.

Its first motion was the head — the slow turn of a thing so long still that motion itself was an admission. The first pair of arms unfolded from a shape held since Year 1289, joints cracking loose forty-one years of pressure at once.

"Fold," Esvane said.

He folded — gone, then standing across the chamber, pulling the Sentinel's face off the table with him. She kept her hands on the box. Two folds left in what his body could still give the job, and he needed to leave with both unused.

The Sentinel crossed the chamber at the pace of a tide that had already decided it would arrive.

The signal came through the side passage — one knock, then two, unhurried. On schedule. The outer watch stayed bought; the rest of the crew held the main corridor.

The Sentinel's second arm swept the display table on its way past, and the table went into the far wall hard enough to split stone. Esvane was already three steps clear of it, the box against her chest, into the gap between the shelving units. The Sentinel didn't hurry after her. It didn't need to.

He kept its attention where it belonged — a blade out for show, since no blade had done a thing to consecrated stone in forty-one years and wasn't about to start for him; then a fold the instant it turned, gold still wet at his hairline from the fold before. It cost something he could see in how he landed — a half-beat slower, a fleck of shine not yet blinked clear — and something he couldn't see yet, that would show up later, the way it always did.

One fold left, now. His legs told him before his head caught up.

The Sentinel turned from him, because he had never been the problem. She was the problem. She had the box.

The door at the corridor's end opened. Someone had been told, plainly, more than once, to hold the corridor and nothing else, and came through it anyway, because there was a door and a woman in trouble on the other side of it, and he'd decided that made him needed. The Sentinel's lowest arm swung in a flat arc he had read about in the deposition and, until that second, only understood as a sentence in someone else's handwriting.

It was fast. Not the rest of the thing. Just that arm.

Fifteen years of discipline behind the man in the doorway, good instincts, a door in front of him, and a woman on the other side of it he'd stood two watches with the month before and had decided, without much ceremony, he liked. He had every reason to care about her and no way to get to her in time.

The arc took four seconds and spent the whole of him. The sound of it was smaller than Wren had braced for — not a crash, just a short, wet, final sound, over almost before his ear had finished registering the start of it — and something in his chest tried to close around that sound and couldn't find purchase, the way a hand closes on water.

He didn't look away from it. Looking away didn't unspend anything, and he'd learned that lesson somewhere he still owed for. His jaw had locked in those four seconds without his permission, and stayed locked after, his mouth gone dry and metal-tasting, the particular dryness that came before a fold and after one both, so that for a moment he couldn't have said which side of it he was standing on.

Then he folded, and the going-through gave him what it always gave him — no floor, no room, no arm still finishing its arc — before the world came back and made him carry the rest anyway.

He came out in the south passage, the case handed off to no one, because the plan had never called for two hands on the box at once and Esvane still had both of hers on it. His knees took his weight a half-beat late, the passage floor arriving harder than a floor should, and he let the wall take what his legs wouldn't. She arrived a breath before him, breathing controlled, the fixer already closing from the gate end.

"The third?" Esvane asked.

"Staying," the fixer said.

The answer they'd expected and hadn't let themselves hope past. Wren said nothing to it. He was still hearing the sound the arm had made, small and wet and final, underneath every other sound in the passage, and he understood, with the flat certainty of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want the answer to, that he would go on hearing it well past tonight.

Esvane did not stop walking. She did not slow, either. She had left men behind before, and had promised herself, a long time ago, that she would do her crying on the far side of the border and nowhere before it. She said nothing else about it. She would, later, when there was somewhere safe enough to put it down.

The postern would have cost them a countersigned warrant and forty minutes nobody in that passage had; the outer watch could be bought, but the postern's ward answered to canon law and nothing softer, and canon law wasn't taking calls that night.

So it was he who took the box — the one thing still needing carrying, and the only thing worth the price of it — and went into the wall the way he'd gone through open air all night, except stone fought him for every foot of it, and he felt the difference the whole eight feet — not the clean nothing of open air but a grinding resistance at the back of his teeth, like biting down on gravel that wasn't there, the wall's whole weight of years pressing back against him at once — worse than any fold he'd made that year.

He was through and out before the ward finished deciding whether it had felt anything touch it. Four seconds. He'd never carried more than one hand's worth in a single crossing. Tonight the box was the hand's worth, and it sat wrong the whole way, like something that would come looking for him later.

Alone on the yard side of the wall while the others crossed to the ordinary door, he let himself look. The catch had sprung a hairsbreadth on the crossing. Through the gap the lens showed itself, no bigger than his palm, weeping something slow and amber, warm as bread from an oven he hadn't owned in some years. He closed the case before he could want anything from the look, and sealed it.

His hands didn't shake, not yet — that always came later, on its own schedule, whether he asked it to or not — but the grey came up around his mouth, the same warning it always gave when he'd spent more of himself than he had left to spend, and his knees knew it before the rest of him caught up, told him plainly there wasn't much more in him if any of this went wrong from here.

Esvane and the fixer came out through the ordinary door two months of bought patience had opened for them, past a watchman looking at the wrong wall entirely.

Three Heloth couriers stood saddled behind the stable wall, heavy through the shoulder, patient with the stillness bred into anything built for distance. A fourth stood saddled from habit, a little apart, and nobody moved yet to strip its tack.

The fixer took the case from him without a word about the sprung catch, set it into the carry-pack, sealed it, checked the straps twice. Her hands didn't shake. She'd trained them not to since she was twenty-three.

The box was warmer against the pack's lining than oiled oak and cold steel had any right to be. He felt it and didn't discuss it. Six hundred years of remembering everything that crossed the membrane, without rest, since Year 771. It would go on remembering now, from somewhere else, and he didn't let himself decide whether that was mercy or something worse starting.

Esvane mounted without a glance back at the gate. He mounted last, and didn't look back either.

"Move," Esvane said.

Three riders took the coast road south while the light was still making up its mind. The fourth Heloth stood a while longer in the yard, and then a stablehand who'd been paid well not to ask what he was paid for led it back into the dark.

The argument had started over bread.

By her second cup, the common room gone quiet for the night, Lyra could not have said what it was actually about. Not why it mattered.

The bread was bad. She maintained this. Something was wrong with the batch the kitchen had been turning out for three weeks running — the grain stores, maybe, or the yeast gone off in the damp. It left a flatness behind her sternum. She knew that flatness. She'd had it before, at four other field postings, every time from bad grain. The pattern held. She had said this on the first morning. She had said it again three days later when the kitchen presented the same loaves in a different shape. She had said it this morning when Vega ate two pieces and looked fine.

Vega looked fine because Vega's stomach had never worked like a standard-issue stomach.

"You're describing a ghost," Vega said. She was looking at the table instead of at Lyra — they had already said everything they were going to say on the subject. "A symptom only you experience, from a cause only you've identified, that by nature cannot be tested."

"I've had this exact symptom at Bressant in Year 1357 and at the eastern posting in Year 1364," Lyra said. "Both times the grain stores were the problem."

"Both times you were right."

"Yes."

"About bread you don't remember eating?"

"I remember the flatness."

Vega went quiet. Lyra knew this silence. It wasn't empty — it was a held position, thought stacked up and not fired yet. She reached for her cup.

The station kept a grain spirit for long-watch nights — distilled from whatever grew on that coast, amber, not unpleasant, not something you saved for occasions. You drank it because it was there, the night was cold, and your sister was in the room. They'd been at it an hour. The lower common room belonged to off-duty soldiers — not comfortable, not formal either. The soldiers had gone to quarters two hours back. The fire had burned down to coals. The lamp was down to its last quarter. At the other end of the table sat Vega's scribing kit: ink vials stoppered, the good pen sheathed, the vellum stack face-down and squared off. Precise tools, precisely kept. Lyra approved of this. She didn't say so. Vega would make it complicated.

"The bread issue," Vega said, "is something you care about because you're leaving tomorrow morning and you haven't said when you're coming back, and you've decided that the bread is the correct object for that feeling."

Lyra considered this.

"The bread is also bad," she said.

Vega laughed — the specific short sound that meant you're right and I'm not going to say so — and leaned back with her shoulders against the wall, the posture of someone who had stopped arguing and started simply being in the same room.

The scribing contract was finished. Two days' work — the vigil archive's annual certification instruments — and it had given Vega reason to ride up from the Liturgy's central office and sit in this room, in this company. Lyra was glad of it. She did not examine that gladness too closely. Vega had made the trip four times now. Three or four days each time. Each time the same: an argument over nothing that stopped mattering by the second cup, the grain spirit, Vega doing something that let Lyra be stupid in front of her and not mind it. There was a rank of person you did not have to perform for. Vega held that rank with Lyra. Lyra suspected she held it the other way too, though Vega would have written the sentence better.

"The Descent request," Vega said.

"Emergency dispatch at the eastern sector. Seismic variation, below threshold, but the protocol says senior practitioners respond and I'm the only one currently stationed at full rank." Lyra set her cup down. "Three days, possibly four. The road up is straightforward."

"I know the road."

"You walked it once."

"I walked it in winter. It was fine." Vega looked at her. "What's the actual concern?"

"I don't have an actual concern."

"The eastern sector hasn't produced a genuine Descent event in-"

"Seventeen years," Lyra said. "The Keeper said twelve. I looked it up last week. Seventeen. There have been minor seismic events. None have required protocol response."

"And this one is below threshold and requiring protocol response?"

"Yes."

Vega went quiet again. Not a held position this time — she understood the shape of the information and saw no use in saying it out loud.

"I'll be back before your contract ends," Lyra said.

"My contract ends tomorrow."

"Then I'll be back before three days are out and you'll have already gone, and we'll see each other at Feast Day if the Keeper approves my travel request."

"Which he will."

"Which he will," Lyra agreed.

"Do you want me to do the Canon again?" Vega asked.

"No."

"Because I can do the Canon again."

"I don't need the Canon again."

Vega straightened on the bench. Her face went still — not dishonest stillness, the stillness of an accurate observation about to be delivered with care. She set down her cup. She folded her hands on the table the way the Canon Immaculate Anslem Sanctorin, by all available evidence, folded his hands when composing a formal response.

"It is the understanding of this Office," she said, "and I must stress that the matters presently under consideration are to be read in conjunction with the relevant canonical instrument issued in Year 1339, and I would further note, pending the outcome of the review currently in progress which may well indicate that further qualification is warranted, that the position of this Office on the substance of this inquiry is as follows, subject to such adjustment as the ongoing review may require and without prejudice to the standing memorandum of Year 1354 which I would invite the relevant parties to consult in order to properly understand the context within which the following position should be understood-"

Lyra had not meant to laugh. She'd known the impression was coming — she knew it exactly, because three years of copying the Canon's instruments had given Vega a catalogue of his verbal habits precise enough to serve as a reference document and, in the right conditions with the right amount of grain spirit in her, precise enough to make Lyra choke on what was in her mouth.

She managed.

Mostly.

Vega did not break register. She ran past the point where it was funny into the territory where it was simply what the Canon's correspondence sounded like — its own kind of horror. The accuracy was funnier than any exaggeration would have been.

"-and provided that the question is understood as arising within the framework I have described, which I would again invite the relevant parties to consult before drawing conclusions, the position of this Office may provisionally be stated as follows, subject to the foregoing-"

"All right," Lyra said.

"-without prejudice-"

"All right."

"-as may be indicated by subsequent findings-"

"I am going to put you in the storage archive."

Vega settled back and reclaimed her cup, smiling the small economical smile of someone who had achieved exactly what she meant to achieve. The lamp caught her face sideways. In that light she looked younger than twenty-four, and like their mother — same angle to the jaw, same patience in the eyes. Their mother's patience had come easy — the ease of someone whose world moved at her own pace. Vega was only starting to learn that trick. Lyra had given it up entirely at eleven.

"Tell me the one about Schmalburg," Vega said.

"You've heard the Schmalburg posting."

"I know I've heard the Schmalburg posting. Tell me the one about Schmalburg."

Lyra drank. The grain spirit had, at this hour and in this company, improved. It had not improved in any objective sense.

"The apparatus at the eastern Gate had drifted," she said. "The observation platform rotates on a mount that requires calibration every seven years. The calibration record showed it had been done on schedule — Year 1335, Year 1328, Year 1321. All correct, all signed by the relevant practitioner, all within margin." She set her cup down. "Twenty years of careful vigil records. Seventeen hundred and forty-odd observation entries, each one certified, each one logging the specific section of the membrane boundary that the platform was set to observe."

"Six meters," Vega said.

"Six meters south of where the platform was aimed."

"Because-"

"Because the mount base had settled. The stone platform it sat on had developed a three-degree tilt. Gradual. Not detectable in any individual calibration because you're checking the axis of the mount, not the angle of the base, and if the base shifts by a degree every seven years nobody in the room notices it — they just correct for what the mount shows, and the base keeps settling." She paused. "Twenty years of records. Every piece of membrane data from that installation. Every Descent assessment, every notation in the archive logs. All of it for the section six meters to the south of where anyone was actually looking."

Vega had heard this before. She listened the way she always did — the attention of someone who finds a thing funnier each time, not because the facts change but because the picture sharpens with repeating. The archivist whose life's work was those seventeen hundred entries had been standing two paces off when Lyra made the finding. He'd been there for the calibration check that showed the settlement. He'd read the corrected coordinates himself.

"He never said a word," Vega said.

"He offered me tea."

"Was the work actually less useful?"

"That's the part that kept me up," Lyra said. "The section we'd been observing was entirely unremarkable for twenty years. Which meant either the membrane in that section was genuinely unremarkable, or the section we were supposed to be observing was also unremarkable, or they were both unremarkable, or the drift was the most interesting thing to happen at that installation in twenty years and the interesting thing was the fact that nobody had known where they were looking."

"What did you put in the report?"

"'Calibration corrected per findings. Historical records amended to reflect actual observation coordinates. No operational impact assessed.'"

Vega was quiet for a moment. "That's the correct thing to write."

"I know."

"You wanted to write something else."

"I wanted to write: for twenty years this installation has been observing the wrong section of the membrane boundary, and nobody asked whether the interesting thing about twenty years of unremarkable records was the unremarkability or the twenty years."

"That would have been longer."

"Yes."

"You'd have had to cite something."

"Yes."

Vega finished her cup, looked at the decanter, decided against a third — the decision of someone who'd need a steady hand by morning. She took her pen from its case and held it a moment, not to use it, just to hold it — the habit of someone who thought better with something in hand — then set it back.

"You should sleep," she said.

"I know."

"You're going to leave before I'm up."

"I am."

Neither said anything for a moment. The fire had gone to almost nothing. The lamp was near the end of its burn. Two hours in this room: the bread argument, the grain spirit, the Canon again, the Schmalburg story again. Neither of them had said anything that mattered. That was the point. The lower common room was a soldier's space, not comfortable, the fire wanted wood, and they were both tired. These were the conditions under which Lyra was most certain of what she had.

"Don't eat the station bread," Lyra said.

Vega stood and gathered her scribing kit with the automatic care of someone who'd carried good instruments long enough that the care was bodily, not thought. "I eat what I like," she said.

"Your stomach is wrong."

"My stomach is fine."

Lyra stood. She had a full head on her sister, had since Vega was twelve, and it had never stopped being worth noticing. Vega moved at a different scale — lighter, compressed, a desk-worker's body that spent its rest hours in motion instead of stillness, as though the two balanced each other out. She gathered her kit and looked at her sister with the look of someone deciding whether to say something.

She didn't say it. She went to bed.

Lyra put the cups away. She banked the fire as far down as it would go. The lamp guttered; she trimmed the wick and left it low on the table, for the night warden's rounds. Then she went to her own quarters, lay down without fully undressing, looked at the ceiling, thought about the eastern sector for a while, and stopped — there was nothing more to think tonight — and slept.

She was gone before the first light.

Vega's door was closed. On the scribing table, the certification instruments sat in their careful stack, the inked pen sheathed in its case. Lyra set the note on top, where Vega would see it before she touched anything else.

Back in three days. Don't eat the station bread.

The Heloth waited in the stable yard — a heavier model, built for distance. It had made this run before and would make it again with the same indifference it always had. She checked the headgear. Checked the supply mount. Checked the road kit. Mounted. The predawn air was cold enough to ache in her nose with each breath. The south passage was dark.

She rode.
