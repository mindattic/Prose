# The Log She Didn't File

The arcology had no clocks, which Amara had learned to read as a kind of clock.

Daud Mtembe's building sat in Zone 5, one of the Orison-affiliated residential towers where the air was managed to a flat, conditioned neutrality — no diesel, no lake, no season. The lobby news feed ran a curated loop of good infrastructure announcements and weather that was always about to improve. She had been in a dozen towers like it. They all smelled faintly of nothing, which was a smell, and they all withheld the time, because a person who could not check the hour was a person who stayed.

She had been in buildings like this her whole professional life. The Tribune headquarters had managed air. Her own building had managed air. The gradient between conditioned air and real air was something she had learned to notice only recently — by being in the Seam for source meetings, by standing at the zone boundary on the way back, by the way her face recognized the transition before her mind did. She had been reporting on the built environment of this city for seven years and had not once filed a piece about the air.

Daud met her at the door with both hands out. Forty-three, broad through the shoulders, second-generation neuretics — you could tell from the older mount scar behind the ear, the seam slightly raised where the first-gen housing had been swapped. A freight scheduler. He remembered her. He remembered the segment.

"The coffee correspondent," he said, warm, and she let him, because warmth was data too.

She set her recorder on the table between them and ran the standard follow-up — the polite architecture of beat journalism, checking in on a series subject two years out. How's the routing work. How's the implant holding. He answered easily. The implant was fine. The work was fine.

Then she asked about the workers' association.

In 2223, over coffee after the cameras were off, Daud had talked for forty minutes. The Z5 freight corridor was being re-routed without community input. He'd been organizing — quietly, carefully — with the transit workers' association. He had used the word *theft* about the corridor. He had used the word *survival*. She had not been able to record it and had written it down afterward in the back of the cab on the way home, the way you preserve a thing you might need.

"That all worked out fine," Daud said now.

She kept her face in its broadcast neutral. "Worked out how?"

"The routing went through. It's fine. The association looked at it and we were all" — he paused, found the word, and she watched him find it — "fine with how it landed."

"You had concerns. About the corridor."

"I remember that." He said it without defensiveness, which was the thing. A man hiding a reversal got defensive. Daud was reporting weather from a country he no longer lived in. "I had a lot of concerns. I don't really feel that way about it now. It's fine."

Four times in seven minutes. She counted, because counting was what she had instead of a name for it. *Fine.* Not resigned. Resignation had friction in it — the sandpaper of a man who'd lost and knew it. This was smoother than that. He remembered the opinion the way you remember a city you visited once. He could describe the building. He could not tell you it had ever been cold.

She thanked him, professionally. She took the elevator down through the managed air and out the lobby doors, and the Seam hit her in the face — the zone boundary where the conditioning ended and the real city began, concrete and diesel and, under it, the cold mineral edge of the lake. She stood in it a moment and breathed like a person surfacing.

She had done this before, this brief pause at the zone boundary — the thirty seconds she allowed herself before walking back to the transit platform. She had always understood it as a body-level preference, as proof that she liked cities with weather in them. She stood in it now and understood for the first time that it was not preference. Every building she worked in, lived in, took meetings in, was conditioned. The Seam was where people went who could not afford the conditioning. She did not live in the Seam. She visited it, recorder on, notebook open, and then she went home to managed air and called the visit fieldwork. Daud had lived three floors above it for fifteen years.

She walked to the platform.

---

On the platform she made her notes.

The professional ones first, into the recorder transcript that synced to the Tribune content management system: *Mtembe, follow-up, 2226. Implant performing to spec. Subject reports satisfaction with Z5 corridor outcome.* Clean. Filable. True in the narrow way that things were true when you only said the part that could be verified.

Then the other notes. Not the Tribune stack — those left a record, and the record went places. She kept the real log on her own credentials, a personal partition the CMS didn't index, a private taxonomy eight months in the building.

Thirty-one entries. People she'd interviewed after their calibration visits who had drifted in a specific direction. They used to have objections. Now they were fine. She had sorted them by severity. Type A was tonal — a softening. Type B was deflection. Type C was the one that kept her up: a complete reversal of a previously held strong position, with no life event to explain it, and the source remembering the position while feeling nothing toward it.

Daud was Type C. He made four.

Four in eight months. She wrote his entry beside the other three and sat with the number on the cold platform, and the train came, and she did not pitch it.

Because she knew what filing was. Filing meant carrying it to her editor or pushing a formal pattern query through the Tribune analytics stack, and both of those things named the sources, and a named source was no longer a question she got to ask. Four data points. She didn't understand the mechanism. She didn't understand who would care that she'd found it. *What she can verify is what's true* — that was the spine she'd built a career on, and right now she could verify a count and nothing else, so the count stayed private until she could verify a cause.

She told herself this was rigor. She had said it in the third piece of the series: *a journalist who publishes before she understands the mechanism is a journalist who damages the story.* She had believed it when she wrote it. She believed it now, sitting on the platform with the count in her private log and Daud's address still in her recent history. She also understood, in a way that was newer and less comfortable, that rigor and self-protection used the same hands, and she had not yet worked out how to tell them apart from the inside.

The train took her north along the Zone 6 industrial corridor, the windows giving her the Seam in long horizontal strokes — stacked freight, the lake going pewter, the towers of the managed zones lit so evenly they looked drawn. She'd covered all of it. She had three years of being the face that said the new thing was safe. She'd worn the upgrade on air. She'd reported her own ninety days as evidence.

She got off at her stop and walked home into a neighborhood with weather in it.

---

The apartment smelled of egusi — she'd made a pot the night before, grandmother's recipe transcribed in the back of a notebook in a hand that wasn't quite her grandmother's because her grandmother couldn't write and a cousin had taken it down. The smell was the best thing about coming home. At the baseboard, the lumen mouse was leaking again: a small phosphorescent seam where the wall met the floor, blue-green, some old bioluminescent sealant breaking down into a slow glow. She'd stopped meaning to fix it. It was company.

The maps were on the wall, official zone surveys overlaid with her own annotations — source geographies, the places people lived behind the places the city said they lived. She put the kettle on and opened the content management log out of habit, the end-of-day ledger of what her account had touched.

There was an entry she hadn't meant to generate.

*Reader analytics: Source pattern J-44C. Tribune Wellness desk — active query: source wellness status check run against Batch 44-C cohort. Result: reviewed by Orison Communications content partnership desk, 21:44.*

She read it three times. Then she remembered: the standing search. She'd built it eight months ago, in the same week she opened the private log — a scheduled background check that re-ran every source ID she touched against the partnership portal's own wellness index, a housekeeping habit from the early days of the beat, back when she'd told herself it was due diligence and not surveillance of her own sources. She had forgotten it was still live. She had forgotten, because forgetting was easy when a thing ran itself, that the query didn't stay on her side of the glass. The portal was Orison's. Everything that touched it synced both ways, in real time, to whoever held the partnership desk. She had built herself a window and never once asked who else could see through it.

She had not filed anything. But she had looked, on her own machine, with her own tired hands, at the one name she'd promised herself she'd sit on until she understood it — and the portal had carried the shape of that looking to Orison's desk before she'd finished her commute home. They had the cohort. They had Daud's name, because Daud's name was the thread she'd pulled today, through a search box she'd forgotten was theirs as much as hers.

The tea went cold while she sat with it.

---

At 23:07, her hands closed around air.

She was at the table when it happened — not building from outside but arriving all at once, interior and total. Her hands were flat on the table. Then they are not doing that anymore. They are gripping a handlebar.

The specific pressure of standing weight over a bicycle's front fork: palms wrapped around a flat bar at shoulder width, the vibration of road surface traveling up through aluminum into the heel of each hand. Cold air on bare forearms at velocity — not the ambient cold of a room but the cold of movement, air made wind by passage through it. A city from a meter and a half above the pavement, no glass between her and it, just the road and the lean into a corner and the bicycle telegraphing every seam and patch in the asphalt up through the forks in a language she did not speak but, for eleven seconds, was fluent in. A district she had never entered — Zone 5 industrial, south of the Seam grid — rendered in smell and road texture and the sound of mag-lev passing overhead, the 139.2 beacon frequencies strobing through the frame metal, the way a courier would feel them and not notice.

She was at her kitchen table. She was also, entirely and at the same time, somewhere else, inside a body that knew things her body did not.

Then she was only at the table.

She looked at her hands. Still curved — fingers slightly bent, palms angled around a grip that wasn't there. She watched them return to flat over the span of three breaths.

The memory was in her tendons: the micro-tension of a grip held for distance, the compensatory pressure in her left wrist that corrected for a slight pull to the right in the front wheel. She had never held that tension. She had never noticed a pull in a front wheel. The knowledge sat in her hands with the specificity of repetition — a task done so many times it required no attention — and it had no origin she could account for.

She did not name it. Naming was filing of a different kind.

She opened the private log instead and added a column the taxonomy didn't have — not *Anomaly entry 32*, because this was not the same animal as the others, and she would not pretend a category before she had one. She dated it. 2226, 23:07. *Sensory, somatic, non-originating. Zone 5 industrial. Bicycle. Beacon freq 139.2.* She placed it on the page beside the four Type C entries and drew no line between them, asserted no connection she could not demonstrate, because the line was exactly the thing she didn't have yet.

She checked the content management log one more time.

She had not filed the anomaly. The query that had gone to Orison's desk at 21:44 was hers — built by her own hand eight months ago, running on rails she had never once asked who else was standing on. And that was the part she sat with as the lumen mouse glowed its slow blue-green at the baseboard and the egusi smell went thin and the tea finished going cold: not whether they had her search. They had always had her search. She had simply never had a reason to check who else was reading it until tonight.

Whether there were three other access records she hadn't seen. Each timestamped to a different day. Each pointed at a different source she'd gone back to check on. Each one carrying a name she had been trying, very carefully, to protect.

She was not the only person in the city sitting in the dark with a list of names and nowhere safe to put them.
