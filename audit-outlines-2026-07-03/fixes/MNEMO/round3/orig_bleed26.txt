# The Procedure

The corridor on the fourth floor of C-9's service block was cleared at 2200. Seto knew the schedule because he had delivered to this building forty times. Maintenance ran the south stack until ten, then moved to the east tower. After that the corridor belonged to no one for two hours. No camera covered the dead section past the freight door. He had checked it on three separate routes, the way he checked everything, by walking it and noting what watched him and what did not.

Nuru's unit was eleven floors up and 200 yards east. Within range. He could not go to her. The moment his courier tag crossed the residential threshold, the building logged it, and a logged visit to a Batch 44-C resident generated a flag, and a flag generated a person. So he stood in the dead section with his back against the cold ferrocement and did the only thing the distance allowed.

He had carried her for six weeks. Not the idea of her. The actual contents — the memories the protocol had cut loose from their weight and left floating in her like furniture in a flooded room. They had come to him through the bleed, arriving whole, arriving heavy, because the channel did not pass through the suppression layer that had lightened them. He knew the smell of the kitchen in the Eastleigh flat their mother kept. He knew the specific shame of the day Nuru was nine and broke the radio.

The bleed was read-only. Orison's documentation was explicit: passive, one-directional, receive only. Nothing moved back up the channel. The channel did not have an up.

He did not believe that anymore. He had nothing to base the disbelief on. He simply did not believe it.

He closed his eyes and took the weight in both hands — not literally, there was nothing to hold, but the act felt like holding, like cupping water that wanted to run. He found the texture of her memories where he kept them and he did not wait for the channel to pull. He pushed. He concentrated on the mass of each thing and aimed it the way it had come, back along the line, toward her, and he held the intent steady the way you hold a heavy door open with your shoulder while your hands are full.

For a moment nothing.

Then the line did not narrow toward her. It widened.

eleven floors up, the radio, the shame, nine years old, the whole weight of it landing at once where it had been hollow

847 of them at the same second, each one full, each one heavy, each one home in the wrong body or the right one, no one able to tell which—

—

In a transit car somewhere west of the Spine, Amara doubled forward in her seat as a memory she had never owned arrived with the weight of a memory she had buried for years.

—

Then the recalibration fired.

Seto felt it as nothing. There was no sensation to it, no closing door, no severance he could name. There was simply, after eight seconds, the absence of the thing that had been in his hands. He opened his eyes. The corridor was the same. The cold of the wall was the same against his back.

The weight was gone.

He checked himself for it the way you check a pocket. The texture of Nuru's memories — the kitchen, the radio, the broken thing she was nine and ashamed of — was not there. Not lightened. Not suppressed. Simply no longer carried. He had been holding it for six weeks and now his hands, the ones that were not hands, were empty.

He could not tell where it had gone. Either it had reached her and landed with the weight he had pushed into it, or the recalibration had swept the channel an instant after he opened it and the whole transfer had dissipated into the sweep, dispersed across 847 devices and then erased. There was no readout. There was no acknowledgment. The bleed did not confirm. It had never confirmed anything; it only arrived, and now it had stopped arriving.

He could not reach her. Eleven floors up, 200 yards east, and there was no version of crossing that distance that did not produce a flag and a person and a record of a courier who had no business in the residential stack at 2200. If it had worked, she would not know it was him. She would have eight seconds of her own life landing in her with its real mass and no source for it, no name attached, the same way the others had received pieces of strangers. If it had not worked, nothing had changed for her at all, and she was still furniture in a flooded room.

He would not know. He had built the entire act on the assumption that he would not know, and the assumption held.

Through the freight door's grimed window he could see the relay beacon on the corner of the next block. It had dropped from its standard eight-second pulse to a fast three-second flicker — the recalibration cycle running on every neuretic in the batch, drawing its power up through the local relay infrastructure. He watched it. Down the avenue the other beacons were doing the same, the whole six-block grid stuttering in a rhythm that was not its rhythm, ninety seconds of a borrowed pulse while the protocol finished its sweep across 847 heads.

He timed it without meaning to. Around ninety seconds the corner beacon stretched, hesitated, and returned to eight. One by one the others followed, the grid settling back into its standard interval, the city resuming the breath it had been holding.

Seto pushed off the wall.

He had another delivery. A sealed case in the satchel for a buyer in the east tower's ground retail, a man named Okonkwo who paid on time and never spoke. The drop was scheduled for 2230 and the corridor would not stay empty past 2200 much longer. He shifted the satchel's strap to his other shoulder, the loaded one now, the one that carried something with documented weight, and he walked back toward the freight door and the stairs.

The corridor logged nothing. He had not crossed any threshold that watched.

At the landing he checked the route in his head — east tower, ground retail, then the Pulse station before the last northbound slug at 2310 — and found it intact, every leg of it where it should be. He went down.

In an apartment six zones north, a woman was standing at a counter with a glass of water she had not drunk, and the afternoon was 2:11.
