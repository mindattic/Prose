# Something Borrowed

She was at her desk by 05:40, before the Tribune's newsroom came alive, when it was only her and the floor's overnight security feed and the specific quiet of a building that was thinking about beginning. The segment clock on her secondary screen read 47:22 until air. She turned it off.

Seto had sent the map at 04:50. The second message came while she was still pulling her calendar onto the main screen.

*Ignore the first crescent coordinate — transposed a digit. Corrected file attached.*

Brief. They were always brief, and they always came in pairs.

She laid her own source calendar beside his crescent overlay and began the cross-check by hand, because there was no query she could run through the Tribune's analytics stack that wouldn't leave a query in the system log. Source by source, by date of contact, by the Zone coordinates she'd built into her coverage map three years ago when she started the neuretics beat. She turned on the desk lamp — just hers, nothing else — and worked through the list in the dark outside the cone of it.

The vent above her station clicked through its cycle while she worked, the small metal complaint of a damper that had needed replacing since she moved into this desk slot. It clicked maybe forty times before she was done. Twenty-two minutes.

Inside the crescent: thirty-eight of her forty-seven neuretics sources.

She had made a coordinate error the first time through and so she ran it again, slower, her pen touching each entry as she counted. Thirty-eight. Nine fell outside the arc entirely, scattered through zones the crescent never reached.

She set the pen down.

She had built this source list over three years. She had been careful, cross-checked, maintained the kind of contact hygiene that distinguished a beat journalist from someone playing at coverage. Thirty-eight of those sources lived inside a geographic boundary Seto had drawn from courier route data and IA scheduling logs.

She stood and walked to the back-office water station and stood at it for a moment without filling a cup. The newsroom floor was dark at both ends; her desk lamp was the only active light on the floor. She came back.

For three months she had been logging sources who noticed something wrong — the textile artist's hands, the transit dispatcher's inexplicable vote, the community planner who'd approved a land transfer she couldn't explain. She had been treating it as a beat story: a pattern in the data that a careful piece of reporting would eventually explain. Now the crescent put a different frame around it.

She opened her personal health records application on her tablet and ran a search she had been avoiding: her neuretics maintenance history, full date range, all facilities.

March 2224. Zone 5, Clinic 9. She had it: she could picture the anteroom with the warmed-plastic smell, the forty minutes in the chair, the tech who had explained the calibration in more detail than she'd asked for. Standard visit.

October 2224. Zone 8. Facility C-17.

She tapped the entry.

Something went cold and wrong low in her stomach, the way it did when she heard her own recorded voice say a thing she did not remember writing.

She had no weight for Zone 8 in October. She had the ordinary shape of the month — the Tuesday lunches at the canteen downstairs, the segment she'd run on the third about neuretic response times in high-stress environments — and somewhere inside that shape, unmarked, was apparently an afternoon she had spent in a calibration chair in a zone she could not place on a map without checking.

She pulled her transit log. She almost didn't look, because looking meant she expected to find nothing, and the expecting was its own small fear. The nineteenth showed a transfer she'd logged under a corridor code, not a zone name — a name she'd have had to decode to know it meant Zone 8, and she had never bothered to decode it, because a transfer code wasn't a destination, it was a means of getting somewhere else. She had been in Zone 8. The record didn't lie. She simply had not carried anything out of it worth keeping.

The visit record was four lines: date, facility, duration, outcome. *Routine calibration. Outcome: nominal.* The reviewing clinician was listed by employee number in the sign-off field, and the employee number was hers — her own Tribune press certification, repurposed, because Orison's wellness program auto-enrolled press-credentialed subjects as their own file's internal reviewer, a paperwork shortcut that meant no second person's name ever had to sit next to a segment subject's. She looked at it twice.

Her own certification number, signing off on her own file.

She sat back. Her desk lamp held its cone. Outside it, the newsroom floor was its own variety of dark.

She had a Zone 8 visit she'd physically made and could not recall making, filed under her own authority because the paperwork had been built to need no other witness. Daud had this. Four people on her list had this — the visit remembered as a fact and felt as nothing, the record intact and the weight gone. She had spent eight months writing that sentence about other people.

The health records app had an edit history. She tapped into it.

The Zone 8 entry had been created on the date it listed: October 19, 2224. Created and never modified. It sat in her health record exactly the way a legitimate appointment sat, because it was one. That was the horror of it. Nothing about the file was fabricated. She had gone. She had sat in the chair. She had come out fine, and said so, and meant it, and the meaning was the thing that had been taken.

She pressed two fingers flat against the back of her neck, the way she'd seen sources do in waiting rooms before calibration visits, and felt the ridge of the implant housing under her hair. It was warm from her skin and smaller than she expected, a seam she had carried for years without once thinking of it as a door. She made herself stop.


At 06:15 her editor's overnight message surfaced on the Tribune platform: *Quarterly partnership review is 10AM Thursday at Orison. I put you on the list since you're primary on the neuretics beat. Formal but brief — they want to go over Q3 coverage metrics.*

She read it twice. She looked at the crescent on her secondary screen.

Thursday. Two days. Mandatory in the sense that declining would require an explanation she hadn't worked out how to give yet.

She opened the batch documentation — the one from the restricted portal tier that Ciro had suspended her access to a week ago. She had transferred copies before the suspension. She pulled the Batch 44-C recipient list and ran her name.

Row 312. Osei, Amara. Facility: C-6, Zone 5. Certification date: October 2222.

She looked at the certification date.

She was standing before she knew she had stood. She put the tablet down.

October 2222 was four months before the Tribune assigned her the neuretics series. She had received the series assignment in February 2223 — the call from her editor at the time, the pitch meeting a week later, the institutional arrangements with Orison Communications for press access that her editor had already started negotiating when she came in. The piece had felt like hers because she had written it and it had run under her name and it had been accurate. Every sentence was accurate.

She had received Batch 44-C in October 2222. She had written about it as an independent journalist subject starting in February 2223. She had reported her own managed experience as the evidence.

She sat with that.

She could still hear the February call — her own voice saying yes, glad, certain it was a thing she had won — and now the yes belonged to a room she had never been told about, a decision that had finished its work four months before anyone thought to let her feel like she was making it. She had brought her own credibility to a story that had been built around it. The credibility was real. It had been real, the whole time, and that was not the comfort it had always been.

The accuracy was the point.


At 07:30 her editor appeared at the newsroom door, scanning for early arrivals.

"You're in early," he said.

"Working ahead of the Thursday prep," she said. She had three source notes open on her tablet, the batch documentation closed underneath them. "Is the Thursday attendance list finalized?"

"You and Guzman. Maybe Yewande if she's free." He looked at her desk. "You eating anything? There's pastry downstairs."

"I'll come down in a bit."

He looked at her for one more beat — the editor's look, half attention and half schedule — and went back toward the elevator bank.

She waited until the elevator chimed. Then she turned back to the batch record.

She had thirty-eight sources inside the crescent. She had an October appointment with no memory under it. She had a series that had run under her name and been cited twenty-seven times as independent evidence by the people whose protocol she'd been inside while she was writing it.

She opened the channel and built the message to Seto by hand. The source cross-check. The October facility. The certification date. Her transit log and the visit she'd found buried in a corridor code. She wrote it the careful way — each fact apart from what it meant, no reach, nothing she couldn't demonstrate. She attached the batch record entry and the transit log page.

She stopped. Read it back. It was the kind of message a journalist wrote when they wanted a clear record of what they knew to exist outside the system that held them.

She sent it. Then she pulled up the Thursday partnership review calendar entry and marked herself confirmed.

She sat with the quiet for a moment. Outside the lamp's cone the newsroom was still dark. She had been at this desk for three years. She had built the source list at this desk. She had filed the series from this desk, entry by entry, and been proud of each one. She was proud of the work — the work was still hers. She had been careful and it showed. But careful inside a frame she hadn't known about was a different kind of careful than she had believed herself to be doing.

She had been accurate. And accuracy had been the mechanism.

His reply came while she was still holding that. Brief, as always — but she had learned to read brevity the way she read silence in a source: not for what it said but for what it didn't try to fill.

*The October facility is Orison-adjacent. I have the operator in my route logs. I'll pull it.*

That was all. He hadn't said: *that must be frightening.* He hadn't said: *I'm sorry.* He had gone directly to what he could do. It was the most useful possible response and it was also — she recognized this the way you recognized a thing you hadn't been able to name — a way of staying beside her without making her carry his presence as well. He was doing the work her side of the wall couldn't reach, in the register she would have chosen herself if she'd had access to it. Something in her sternum settled, not relief exactly, but the specific weight of being understood by someone who hadn't needed to be told what understanding looked like.

She wrote back: *Thank you.*

She didn't say what for. She didn't have to.

She had two days until the quarterly review. She had the source list, the crescent, and a certification date that put her inside the batch before the series existed. The series had borrowed her voice for something she hadn't agreed to. She would go to the Thursday meeting as the journalist who had written the series, because that was what her calendar said she was, because that was what anyone reading her files would confirm she was.

She knew what she had.

On her way to the elevator at 07:55, she passed through the dark end of the newsroom and put her hand to the wall briefly — cold plaster, the hum of the ventilation faint under her palm — not to brace herself, only to feel something that was the exact weight of what it was, before she stepped into the managed air of the elevator and went down to where the pastry was.
