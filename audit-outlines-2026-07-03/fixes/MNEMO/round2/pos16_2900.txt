# Everything She Carries

The pod ran express from the Loop to Zone 5, and Amara felt the air change at the boundary.

It was a thing she had learned to notice on the wellness beat, back when she did standups inside calibration suites and corpo lobbies and the climate-sealed atriums where Orison liked to host its product reviews. Outside air carried the city in it — particulate, weather, the smell of whatever the river was doing that week. Managed air carried nothing. It was conditioned to a specification. When the pod crossed into the Zone 5 corporate residential cordon and the cabin recyclers cut over to the building's central handling, she felt the difference land on her face before the indicator changed: a flatness, a stillness, a temperature that had been chosen by someone who would never have to live in it.

She had ridden into the system from the outside a thousand times for work. She had narrated it on camera as comfort. *Climate-stable, secure, designed around you.* She had believed every word, because every word was verifiable, and she had spent seven years treating what she could verify as the same as what was true.

Theodora Eze lived on the forty-first floor of a residential tower at the cordon's edge, and she had contacted the Tribune six weeks ago.

Amara had the message in her notes, because she kept everything. *Something is wrong with my hands.* Precise. A textile artist, thirty years at the loom, who had written three careful paragraphs about the diagnostics that came back clean and the work that did not. *My work is showing it and the machines aren't.* She had attached photographs. Amara had built the relationship slowly — two meetings, names confirmed, dates confirmed, the specific quality of the thing Theodora was afraid of nailed down to a sentence Amara could put in a script and stand behind. It was good sourcing. It was the kind of sourcing that took weeks and could not be faked.

The door opened before Amara knocked.

Theodora was warm. That was the first thing, and it was not wrong, exactly — she had been warm in the meetings too. But this was a different warmth, the warmth of someone with nothing held back behind it. She took both of Amara's hands in both of hers and her hands moved easily, no hesitation, no guarding, and she said it was so good to finally have her over, and would she have tea.

There was tea. There was a low table, and on the low table there was the piece.

Amara recognized it from the photographs. She had studied the photographs. It was a wide panel, indigo ground, a figure emerging in resist-dyed thread, and in the photographs from five weeks ago the figure had reached the shoulder. It had reached the shoulder now. The work had not moved. The relay token sat in the courier slot by the door and the corridor outside smelled of diesel and wet stone and Theodora poured the tea and did not look at the panel at all.

"I have to apologize," Theodora said, settling. "I don't know why I sent that message, honestly. It was a difficult month."

Amara said something. She did not remember what. The broadcaster in her supplied it, the way it always did — the bridging phrase that kept a subject talking. Her hands found her notebook.

"I'd hate to waste your time," Theodora went on. "You came all this way. I really am doing well. I had a calibration last Friday and everything's just — settled. You know how it is. Sometimes you don't realize you've been carrying something until it's gone."

She smiled when she said it. She meant it.

Amara looked at the panel. The figure at the shoulder. The thread that had not moved in five weeks, on the loom of a woman who had spent thirty years unable to stop her hands. Theodora did not see it. She was not avoiding it. It was simply not there for her — the thing that had made her write three careful paragraphs at midnight, the fear that her own work was telling her something, the weight of it. The weight was gone and she did not have a word for what was missing, because what was missing was the part of her that could have noticed.

She was still herself. That was the part Amara would carry out of the building. Her voice was hers. Her hands were hers, moving easily over the teapot. She was glad — really glad, no performance in it — that Amara's series had been so reassuring. She was not pretending to be at peace. She had no objection left to pretend over.

Amara wrote one word in her notebook.

*fine.*

She underlined it.

---

The café was at the margin where Zone 5 gave way to Zone 6, where the corporate cordon thinned out and the street took over, and the air came back. Real air, river-smelling, with the day's heat in it. The coffee was bad. She had ordered it because she needed to sit somewhere that wasn't conditioned, somewhere with weather, and she put her notebook on the table and opened the secure channel and started going down her list.

She had not gone to Theodora cold. Theodora was one of four. There had been three others — three people, cultivated the same careful way, two meetings each, names and dates and the specific texture of what each of them had been willing, finally, to put on the record. A community organizer in Zone 4 who had watched a land transfer she'd fought go through and could tell Amara, precisely, the moment she'd stopped fighting it. A facilities tech with maintenance logs. A woman who had signed a form and could explain exactly why she shouldn't have.

She messaged the organizer first. The reply came faster than it should have, and friendly. *So good to hear from you! I think I overstated things before — it really worked out fine in the end. Don't think there's a story there.*

The tech. *Honestly I was stressed when we talked. All squared away now. Thanks for checking in though.*

The woman with the form. A longer message, warm, apologetic for the trouble, certain she was doing well. The word was in there. She used it herself.

The same week. The same shape. Each of them remembered talking to her. Each of them remembered, in outline, what they had said. None of them could feel that any of it mattered, and each of them was apologizing for having bothered her with it.

She had watched this happen before. That was the thing she kept arriving at and could not get past. On the beat, over three years, she had watched other reporters lose sources this way — a contact who cooled, a witness who recanted, a story that softened until it dissolved. She had filed it, every time, under the ordinary attrition of the work. People got scared. People reconsidered. It was verifiable, the cooling; the reasons never were. She had moved on.

Watching it happen to people she had not built carefully, over months, with photographs and dates and a confirmed sentence each — that had been a category of fact she could hold at arm's length. Watching it happen to hers was a different kind of knowledge, and it did not fit anywhere she could file it.

She typed a note to herself. *Three sources. One week. Recalibration?* She could not source it. There was no document, no log she had standing to pull, nothing that would survive a transcript read. She had four people who were fine and a notebook that said so. It was evidence of nothing, which is to say it was evidence of exactly the thing, presented as nothing.

Seto's message came through on the other channel.

*Six of mine went quiet since Tuesday. All the ones who were going to talk. Same week.*

She sat with that. She put both palms flat on the table.

She did this when she needed the world to hold still — flattened her hands on a surface and let them tell her it was solid. The table was warm where the sun had been on it. The satchel sat against her hip and it was heavier than it should be, the strap cutting a diagonal line of weight across the shoulder and down, the particular drag of a full load in rain, water in the canvas adding pounds the manifest didn't list. She let it pass. The coffee was bad. She drank some.

She opened her work mail because she still, out of three years of reflex, checked it before she did anything else, and her editor's message was at the top.

*Hi Amara — the neuretics long-form has to come out of the next cycle. Not cancelled, scheduling conflict. We'll find a slot. Let me know if you have questions.*

She read it twice.

A scheduling conflict did not happen to an approved treatment three weeks from air. She knew exactly how the cycle worked because she had been inside it for three years; she had defended slots, traded them, lost them, and every loss had come with a conversation, because that was the courtesy and the cost of pulling something already cleared. You did not pull a finished long-form by message. You called. You explained. You let the reporter argue, because the reporter would argue, and the argument was part of how the schedule actually got made.

She knew what a pull looked like from the outside. She had reported on outlets that buried things; she had named the tells. This had every tell and none of the friction. The message was kind. It invited her questions. It was the most ordinary sentence in the world, and it had been written by someone who had no idea he was writing it.

There was nothing to argue against. That was the whole architecture of it. Theodora was fine. The organizer was fine. The schedule had a conflict. No one had raised a voice. No one had said a word she could quote.

Seto again.

*Found something. Ciro filed a batch wellness report Monday. Routine. It auto-triggered recalibration for the whole sub-batch your sources are in. He didn't touch them. He filed a form.*

She wrote back: *How many sources is one form?*

*All of them in the sub-batch. No limit specified in the protocol.*

She held the cup.

And then, a moment later, the one that took the floor out:

*Your editor's in the Orison comms database now. Ciro entered him last week. Media liaison contact. New entry. It's not in your editor's system — it's in theirs. By their definition he's their contact inside your chain now. Anything you take to him about this gets logged on their side.*

She set the cup down.

She looked at the message. She read it a second time, because she was not the kind of person who acted on a first reading. The second reading was the same.

She had worked with her editor for three years. She had brought him complicated, contested material before — the desalination story, the healthcare access series — and he had always been the person she went to when she needed the institutional weight of the Tribune behind her. He had backed her twice when sources pushed back. He had approved the long-form treatment when other editors at the table weren't sure. He was not a man who was on anyone's side except, straightforwardly, the story's.

He didn't know he was in a database. He had done nothing to be in a database. He had been placed there by a man who understood that the most useful asset was one who didn't know they were being used, who would behave with complete integrity and whose integrity would function, from the outside, as containment.

She sat at the café table and understood: she had no chain of communication left. Every path she would normally take this material through — the editor, the legal desk, the Tribune's institutional processes — now ran through a man who had been entered into Orison's own record of her problem.

She messaged Seto: *He doesn't know.*

The response came back in the time it took her to look up and look back down. *No. And telling him makes it worse for him. He becomes someone who received the information.*

She had thought of that already. She had thought of it in the time it took to read his first message a second time. But seeing it written by someone else — confirmed, from the outside, by the person who had been watching the shape of this thing from the courier's angle — landed differently than thinking it herself. Not comfort. The specific weight of not being alone in an accounting she had already done.

*I know,* she sent.

*Are you still clear?* he wrote.

The question was practical — routes, credentials, exposure. But she knew by now that when he asked it, he meant it as all of those things at once, and the all of it was the point.

She looked at her hands on the table. Both palms still flat. Both of them hers. *For now,* she sent.

---

She made herself do the accounting, because the accounting was the only thing she still had that was hers, and she had learned long ago not to close a thing until she knew its shape.

Six weeks of source development on the four. Months across the whole file. Three relationships built one careful meeting at a time, confirmed, photographed, made solid enough to broadcast — and they were not destroyed, nothing so loud as destroyed. They were fine. The people who had given her the evidence were still alive, still warm, still themselves, and could no longer feel that the evidence mattered, and so could no longer give it. The evidence was still in her notes. The notes were now the only place it existed.

The segment, three weeks from air, in a slot that no longer had her name on it. Not cancelled. There would always be a slot, somewhere, later, never.

And the editor. A man who had done nothing wrong, who would call her back if she called, who would mean every word of it — turned, without his knowledge, into a name in a database he could not see, on the wrong side of a line he didn't know had been drawn. She could not warn him without telling him things that would only document her as the unstable one. Any complaint she made through him would land, neatly logged, in Orison's own record of how unwell she had become.

There was no one to be angry at in the room. That was the design. Ciro had filed a form. The form had run. A man had been entered as a contact. A schedule had a conflict. Three people had gotten better. Nothing in any of it would survive a transcript read, and she had spent her whole career believing that the things that survived the transcript were the things that were true.

She still had the archive. The thought arrived steady, in her own voice, and she trusted it because it was checkable. The archive was live. The permissions gap she had found — the one no one had closed because no one knew she'd seen it — was still open. That was a fact. She could verify it. It would survive.

She had eleven days before her next calibration visit. She knew the date. She had always known the date; the visits were on her calendar like a dentist's. Eleven days, and after the eleventh she would walk into a room with managed air and flat light and walk out fine, and she would mean it.

Ten of those days were going to matter. The eleventh was going to take everything she'd done in the other ten and make her glad it had worked out the way it did.

She closed her hands around the coffee cup. It was cold now. The café was loud and the air was real and the strap of her satchel was the right weight again, just canvas and a notebook and the ordinary day.

Her hands were hers. The weight had never been hers. She set the cup down.

She picked up her notebook and started to write down the date, and the next date, and the eleven days between them, in the careful hand she'd kept since she was twenty-two, because the work was the work whether or not anyone would ever run it.

She kept going.
