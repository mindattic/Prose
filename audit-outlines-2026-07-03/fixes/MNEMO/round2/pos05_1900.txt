# Account Liaison

The thirty-second note began the way the others had, with a timestamp and a refusal to round.

Amara worked it standing, tablet flat against the press-room workstation, the local partition open and the cursor blinking inside a file that lived nowhere but here. Not the Tribune's CMS. Not the partnership portal. A notes-to-self file on a partition she'd carved out herself, six weeks deep into a thing she could describe but not yet name: behavioral drift in sources from the wellness series. People she'd interviewed two years ago, on camera, fine. People who were still fine. People whose fineness had developed a grain to it, a flatness in the places a person usually has texture.

Thirty-one notes. Not consistent enough to pitch. Specific enough to track.

Around her the newsroom ran its late shift the way it always did — somebody arguing a headline two desks over, the soft collision of a dozen audio feeds bleeding past their earpieces, the building's air handling pushing recycled cool across the open floor. She'd learned to write inside the noise. The noise was how she knew she was at work and not somewhere worse.

She'd typed four words of the thirty-second note when she stopped, because a man had come through the press-room doors and she could already tell he was the wrong shape.

She had time to read him. He crossed the floor at a pace that didn't apologize for being there and didn't announce itself either, and in the distance between the doors and her desk she catalogued what he wasn't. Not a source — sources hesitated in doorways, did the small chart-pull of someone deciding whether they were really going to do this. Not a colleague; she knew the colleagues. Not a delivery. He had the slight interior quiet of a man raised in spaces built to meet a body correctly at every surface — rooms that had never once been the wrong temperature for him, doors that had never stuck.

The suit was cut to be forgotten. That was the expensive part. Cheaper money bought a suit cut to impress; his had been cut to leave no afterimage, which cost more and said more.

His hair sat within a millimeter of Orison's grooming maximum. She knew the figure because she'd covered the compliance brief two years back — three-quarter inch above the collar, published, enforced — and the number had stuck the way numbers did with her. His was just under it. A man does not land that close to a published maximum by accident. He had measured himself against a standard and chosen to live one millimeter inside it, every day, on purpose.

He reached her desk and did not stand across from her, and did not come around beside her. He took the chair at an angle — the position that makes a conversation possible without anyone having to call it an interview.

"Ciro Fonseca, Orison Neuretics," he said. "Account liaison for the Tribune media partnership."

In four years on the neuretics beat, no one from Orison's internal side had ever come here in person. They moved everything through the content partnership portal — approvals, embargoes, the occasional firm note about framing. The portal was the relationship. The portal had a face precisely so no one would have to.

"I didn't know the partnership had a liaison," she said.

"It hasn't needed one." He let that sit without weight. "I wanted to understand the beat. Not the published work — I've read that. The work before the work."

"The work before the work."

"How you handle a lead that doesn't meet a publishing threshold. How you decide what to track before you decide whether to pitch it."

She answered him. It was an easy thing to talk about and a hard thing to talk about honestly, and she found herself doing the honest version, because he was listening with his whole attention and that was rare enough to be disarming. Partnership contacts heard you until they'd heard enough to check the box, then moved to the next item with the box still warm. He wasn't doing that. He was letting her finish sentences she'd have let herself trail off.

"How do you handle variance that falls below your publishing threshold?" he asked.

"I log it."

"Tribune's system?"

"And my own."

He nodded as if she'd confirmed something already settled. "I saw that in the account review. A personal notes file. Off-system." He said it the way a person says a thing he has read and found unremarkable — not the contents, she understood, but the access metadata, the shape of a file existing where the system could see that it existed and not what it held. He didn't lean into it the way oversight people did, hunting for the flinch. "That space between tracking and filing — most journalists file or they don't. Working the middle ground takes a kind of judgment you can't write into an editorial brief."

He had handed her own method back to her as a virtue, polished, and she felt the shape of it land: that was a sophisticated way to open a door. She kept her face the face she used on camera.

"You're tracking something now," he said. Not a question.

"I'm tracking a few things."

"Anything in the batch data?"

She decided how much to give him and gave it carefully. "Behavioral drift. Sources from the wellness series. Not consistent enough to pitch. Specific enough that I haven't stopped looking."

"Drift in the production, or drift in the subjects."

It was the right distinction, made too fast. Most people heard *drift* and reached for the cheap version — bad batch, manufacturing variance, a thing you recall and replace. He'd gone straight past that to the other thing, the subjects themselves, and he understood — she could see him understand it, in the half-second before he smoothed his expression — which of the two would be the more expensive thing for Orison to have to be true.

"That's the question," she said.

"It's a good one to be careful with."

Then he reached into his jacket.

What came out was a straight razor, bone handle, folded shut. He opened it with one hand and drew the underside of the blade along the line of his jaw — a checking motion, the way you'd test whether a surface had held since morning. His other hand stayed flat on his knee. Four seconds, maybe. Then he folded it closed and slid it back into the pocket, and the whole of it happened while he was still looking at her, which meant he had done it enough times that the gesture asked nothing of his eyes. He didn't break the sentence for it.

She did not read it as a threat. She'd interviewed men who performed their edges; this wasn't that. This was a man with a precise relationship to his own surfaces who did not particularly mind who watched him keep them right. She'd seen the same stillness in surgeons and in people who'd spent time learning to be very still for someone else's benefit.

She made a note in her head. The note was careful.

He stood. He produced a card — a physical one, pressed stock with his name on it, which in 2226 was not a convenience but a statement, because anyone could push a contact token to her tablet in the time it took to think of it and he had chosen instead to carry an object and hand it to her.

"If you find something in that data that concerns you," he said, "I'd rather hear it from you than find it in a published piece."

He said it the way a person says a thing he has thought through beforehand and still means.

She took the card. Holding it, she understood she'd just been handed a trap with the manners of a gift. The published piece was the floor — the worst outcome, from where he sat. He had named the floor out loud and then offered, generously, to stand between her and it, and the price of the offer was that everything she was tracking would now travel through him first. The kindness and the catch were the same motion. You couldn't take one without the other.

"Thank you for coming in," she said, because that was true and cost nothing.

"Thank you for the time." And he crossed the floor and was gone, leaving no afterimage, exactly as the suit had promised.

She finished the thirty-second note standing up.

She wrote him into it as a catalogue. The suit, cut to be forgotten. The millimeter under the maximum. The angle of the chair. The full attention, which she now distrusted precisely because it had worked. The razor got two lines — the bone handle, the four seconds, the eyes that never left her, the conclusion that it was maintenance and not menace. The card, physical, on the corner of the desk.

The last line she wrote and did not soften.

*He understands the work.*

The press room ran late. She'd sent Seto the batch documentation that afternoon — the whole private file, through the secure channel — and his answer came in a way that wasn't an answer.

The bleed arrived before his message did.

It came up through her the way it always came, body before mind, never a voice and never a picture. This time it was a stillness. The specific stillness of someone who has been reading for a long time and arrived at a number, and the number is sitting there on the page, and the mind has already accepted what it means while the body hasn't finished catching up. Not the decision. The moment before the decision, when there's nothing left to do but feel the figure be true.

She set both palms flat on the desk until it passed. The newsroom noise came back in around the edges.

Then she sent: *Someone from Orison came to see me today.*

The ambient paused.

*Tell me what he wanted.*

She noted how fast that had come back — faster than typing, the speed of a hand that was already on the screen.

She looked at the card on the corner of the desk. Pressed stock. A name and nothing she'd asked for.

*He wanted to seem helpful,* she sent.
