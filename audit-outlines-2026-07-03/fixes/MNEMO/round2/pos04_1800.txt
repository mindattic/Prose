# The Other Side of the Noise

At 0545 the rear hub bearing on Seto's bike had a tick in it he could feel through the frame before he could hear it. He'd noticed it the night before on the last run home, a half-beat hesitation in the spin-down, and he had not slept right knowing it was there. So he was out in the access alley behind his unit while the sky over Zone 9 was still the color of cold solder, the bike upside down on its saddle and bars, the rear wheel off, the bearing race in his palm.

He cleaned it with a rag and then with his thumb. Pitting on two of the balls, fine as salt. Not failure. Forty miles of warning, maybe sixty. He had a spare cartridge in the parts drawer he'd pulled from a dead courier's frame two winters back, and he pressed it in with the old socket he used as a drift, seated it, spun the wheel. The tick was gone. The spin-down ran clean and long and died without a stutter.

This was the part of the work nobody paid for and the only part he fully owned. The bike was his. The routes were his — five years of them, built relay by relay, a private map of the gray channels that didn't show on any corpo grid because he'd cut them himself, around the edges of campuses and checkpoints and dead zones, through the seams where one corpo's coverage ran out before the next one's began. He ran nothing that ran him. He answered to the parcel and the clock and otherwise to no system at all. He bolted the wheel back on, torqued the axle by feel, and rolled out into the gray with the count already starting in his head: first relay at the Mercer cut, four minutes, then the long straight down the old freight grade.

The signal arrived at 0612, between the second checkpoint and the third, on a stretch where there was nothing to do but pedal and watch the road.

It came in wrong. He knew the geography of his own stack the way he knew the routes — the intercept buffer where ambient traffic landed, the throwaway space he let fill and dump, the primary stack he kept clean for the work. Things came in through the buffer. That was the door. This came in through the floor. It seated itself in the primary stack like it had always lived there, no handshake, no source flag, a thermal shift at the base of his skull as if something had been switched on behind the bone.

It was not a message. There were no words in it. It was a weight, and it arrived in both his hands at once, and his hands were on the bars so he knew it was not his hands. It was the weight of holding something you had read so many times the reading had worn through — a page held open out of habit, the eyes moving, nothing arriving. The shape of feeling something, with the feeling itself scraped out. He coasted. He put a foot down at a corner he did not need to stop at.

His first read was hardware. A fault in the intercept stack, a buffer leaking into primary, some piece of his own gray-market kit finally throwing the error it had been promising for years. He ran the diagnostic he kept for exactly this and the diagnostic came back nominal. Everything nominal. Which was the second wrong thing, because the weight was still there, sitting in his hands while the readout told him his hands were empty.

He did not push it down. You did not push down a fault you hadn't sourced; you traced it to where it lived and you read what was actually broken. So he traced it. The signal had no origin flag but it had a shape, and a shape was a route, and a route could be walked backward. He pulled over into the lee of a stanchion under the freight grade and he walked it back.

It led to a credential. A live media registration on the open corpo directory, the kind anyone could pull: *Tessera Tribune, wellness-tech desk, accredited correspondent.* Carrier-grade neuretics, the good in-head hardware, the kind that didn't throw the errors his did. And a batch line, the manufacturing lot, the way a tire has a date stamped in the rubber. Batch 44-C.

He read the desk archive because it was attached and free. A series. Six pieces over a season on calibration culture — the wellness practice of going in for a tune, the way you'd take a car in, except the thing being tuned was the part of you that carried the weight of what you remembered. The series summary read like good copy. Balanced. Curious. One line near the end of the third piece described the calibration suite reception area.

He had been in that reception area.

Not that one specifically. A calibration suite — the same design language, he knew the design language. He'd waited in one on a parcel drop months back, sat fifteen minutes for a signature, and he had read the room the way he read every corpo space: by what it cost to maintain and what the cost was for. The chairs were moulded to reduce slump, the kind you specified when you wanted people to sit without shifting. Overhead lighting that was flat and even with no color temperature to it — no warm and no cool, a light that did not tell you what time it was, which was not an accident in a room where you wanted no one to check. He had worked in and out of enough Tier 3 and Tier 4 corpo spaces to know the difference between a room designed for the people in it and a room designed for the operation running on them. Both could look expensive. This one read as the second kind: comfortable, seamless, built to keep its purpose off the surface. And the people in the chairs — not unhappy, not performing patience, just sitting with nothing to say against anything. He had registered all of this and indexed it as: managed waiting area, high-tier. He had not asked what was being managed.

The weight in his hands was the inside of that room. It was what being well-managed felt like from the chair.

The journalist had gone in. Carrier-grade hardware, Batch 44-C, and a published series that named the calibration visits as evidence of quality — went in, got tuned, and wrote it up as a clean experience. And four months or six months or however long later the tuning had worn through to where the reading wears through, and the weight that should sit behind your own words had been scraped out, and it had leaked into his stack across a gray channel like water finding the one crack in a wall. He did not know yet how that was possible. He knew it was in his hands.

He had a run to finish. He finished it. He always finished the run.

---

The third run of the day took him through the Orison campus delivery lane at 1340, a standing parcel route he'd held for seven months. He knew this lane the way he knew the hub bearing. You came in off the service spur, you queued behind the freight intake, you waited for the lane controller to cycle you to the dock, and you did not get out of the queue early because the cameras logged the lane and an early move was a flag. So he waited, one foot down, the bike ticking with heat, and he did the thing he always did in a queue. He placed himself on the map.

He knew the Orison campus as a boundary. A market boundary — a place his routes ran around, not through, because the through was corpo-controlled and the around was his. He had the boundary in his head as a line, the way he had every boundary, the edge where their coverage started and his channels died. He'd routed against this line for months. It was geometry. It was a condition of the territory, the same as a flood barrier or a checkpoint, a thing you accounted for and stopped seeing.

He pulled up the boundary now to check the weight against it — to see how far the journalist's credential sat from where he was queued. And while the line was up in his head, with the campus rendered out to its full registered extent, the legal footprint and not just the buildings, he saw the south margin run down past the freight spur and across the old Zone 6 line and take in three blocks he had always counted as Zone 6 because that was where they were on every map that mattered to a courier.

Facility C-9 was inside the margin.

He had Nuru's clinic logged at C-9. Zone 6, community clinic, nutritionist, the building she walked into at 0700 and out of at 1900, the coordinate he'd had for seven months because a courier keeps the coordinates of the people he'd cross the city for. He had it as a point. He had never put the point inside this particular line. The line had been a market condition and the point had been his sister, and they had lived in different parts of his head, the way the route lives in your legs and the reason you're riding lives somewhere you don't look at while you ride.

He put them together now. It was not a thought arriving so much as two things that had always been true sliding into the same frame, and the frame holding, and not letting go.

The lane controller cycled him forward. He rolled to the dock. He scanned the parcel out, got the signature, logged the drop, and rode the lane back out to the service spur with his count running clean, four minutes to the next relay, and somewhere under the count the thing that had changed was sitting where the bearing tick used to sit, a half-beat hesitation he could feel through the frame.

He did not name it. He checked the rear hub bearing at the spur, off the bike, the wheel up, the spin-down long and clean and dying without a stutter. The bearing was fine. He'd seated it himself at dawn. He spun it again anyway, and watched it, and then he got back on and rode.

---

He reached the journalist that night through the cleanest gray channel he had, a relay he trusted because he'd built it. He did not message the credential. He routed in under the credential, the way the weight had routed into him, and he opened on what he knew.

*My name is Seto Banda. Your hardware is leaking into mine. Batch 44-C. You went in for calibration. I can pull the dates if you can't.*

The answer came back faster than he expected and cleaner than he expected. *I don't know what that means and I'd like you to say it as a sequence I can verify. Start with what you received and when.* No alarm in it. No demand for who he was. A practiced economy, every word load-bearing, the way a person talks when talking is the work they do. He'd recognize later that it was the cadence of someone who spoke for a living, who calibrated a sentence before she let it go. He recognized now only that it was the same precision he used on a manifest, and that he trusted it for the same reason.

*I received it at 0612. A weight, no words. It came in through a channel that shouldn't carry it. I traced it to your registration. The series you wrote — you described the room. I've been in that room.*

A pause that was not the network. *How long since your last calibration visit,* he sent, while the pause held.

*Four months.*

He did not respond right away. Four months was the number. Four months was the wear-through, the page worn thin, the weight scraping loose and finding the crack in the wall. He sat with the four months and let it tell him what it told him, which was that this was not going to stop, and that it had a clock on it, and that the clock had already been running for four months before it reached him.

---

He did not contact Nuru that night. He thought it through the way he'd think through a route with a checkpoint on it. Her registered line was inside the margin now — had been inside it for seven months, he just hadn't drawn the line through it — and a message in on her registered line was a move the system would log. He did not know what a flag looked like from inside the system. He knew exactly what it looked like from outside, because he'd just spent a day reading it: nothing. No windows. Flat light. People in the chairs with no complaint to perform. From outside, a flag looked like everything nominal.

She texted *Clear. -N.* every night, and every night a typo went into the second message and a corrected one came after it, and he had teased her about it for years and she had never once stopped doing it. He waited for it. It came late. He did not send anything in on the line that would log.

He sent four words through the cleanest channel he had, the one he'd built, the one that didn't touch the margin. *Call me when you're clear.*

Her sign-off came late, later than the typo, late enough that he stood up from the bench and sat back down.

*Catch you tomorrow,* he wrote.

He did not tell her about the margin. He had one day to work out whether knowing would put a wall around her or paint a mark on her, and from where he sat he could not yet see which, and a courier who guessed at a route he hadn't walked got people hurt. He turned the bike up on its bars instead. He pulled the rear wheel and spun it and watched the spin-down die clean, no tick, no stutter, the bearing he'd seated at dawn still holding true, and he sat in the alley in the cold-solder dark with the wheel turning down to nothing in his hand, and he did not sleep well, and at 0545 he would be out here again with a count already starting in his head.
