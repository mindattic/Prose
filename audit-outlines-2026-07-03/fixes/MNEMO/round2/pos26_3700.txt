# The Last Route

The job was sealed cargo. A drop at a receiving bay off the canal road, credential scan, no signature. Standard. He took the canal road because it was the last time he could.

He pulsed the first beacon at the zone edge. Eight seconds. Clean. The next one, eight. He counted them the way he always counted them, not because he expected eleven but because the count was the road.

The Veil started where the ferrocement went pale.

He knew this stretch. He had ridden it hundreds of times. The geography had not moved. The same low buildings. The same clean gutters. The same trees the municipal crews kept at the same height. He came around the bend by the old rail spur and the corridor opened the way it always opened.

He passed the brick walk-up on the corner. Four floors. Twelve units. Seven of the names were in the building. He knew that now. He had read them one at a time, with full attention, and seven of them lived behind those windows. The windows were clean. The planters were watered. A man on the second floor was wiping down a sill, and he did it slowly, and he did not look up.

The building read like a building.

He kept his speed even. In here you kept your speed even. He passed the corner store and the laundromat and the bakery with the green awning.

The bakery owner's name was Tomas Okeke. He knew that now too.

Last spring Okeke had a permit dispute with the zone office. A loading-zone line repainted six feet short of his door. Okeke had filed twice. Then the filings stopped. The line stayed short. The bread still came out at five. Okeke waved at the crews who painted over his complaint, and the wave was real, and that was the part.

Seto rode past the green awning and did not slow.

His hands were on the bars. Then for a half-second his right hand was reaching down and to the left, thumb finding the foam grip of a recorder mic, pulling it to mouth-height, the small drag of the cable catching on a chair arm — and then it was the bars again, and the canal road, and the pale wall going by.

He let it pass. He always let it pass. He knew whose it was. He knew what it had weighed when she still carried it, because he carried it now, in his hands, a thing she was no longer allowed to set down because she no longer remembered picking it up.

Nuru's row was Zone 5. This was not Nuru's row. But the reach was the same reach. They had calibrated the same protocol into eight hundred forty-seven people and the reach came out of all of them the same way, body before mind, and his body answered every one it could find.

The corridor narrowed toward the water.

He pulsed another beacon. Eight. The relay net on this block would be dead by tomorrow. The monitoring node two streets over had gone live four days ago, and once a node was on your block the block was finished, and you did not argue with a finished block. You re-routed. He had already started drawing the new approach in his head, the one that came in from the north culvert instead of the canal, longer, colder, no beacons for the first two miles.

This was the last clean line through the Veil.

He did not feel it as a goodbye. The Veil did not permit that register. The buildings stayed clean. The neighbors waved. A woman walked a small dog past the shuttered relay café and the dog stopped to sniff a post and she waited for it, patient, untroubled, and the whole street was untroubled, and the trees were the same height.

He came to the Facility 4 bay.

The checkpoint had a new arch. Orison hardware, matte, no badging, the housing still bright at the seams where it had been bolted in this month. The old reader had been a credential scan. This one was a reader. He slowed to the line on the ground and let it take him.

The drives were not on him. He had run the cargo clean — sealed, dumb, a destination and nothing else. He had nothing to find. He held the bars and breathed at the rate he breathed and let the arch sweep him front to back.

Through the glass the bay guard sat at a primary terminal. To the guard's right a secondary terminal woke up.

A line resolved on it. Seto read it at an angle, reversed, the way he read everything, by motion and shape before letters.

A name.

His name. His real one. The one he had not been in a database under for five years.

It sat there on the secondary terminal in pale type, matched and logged, and the guard did not look at it because the guard had no reason to look at it. A courier cleared an upgraded reader. The system noted who. That was all the system did. The bar lifted.

He rode under it.

The drop was 130 feet in, a roller door, a credential ping, a chute. The cargo went down the chute. The door confirmed receipt. No one came out. No one had to. He turned the bike and rode back under the arch and the secondary terminal had already gone dark and the guard was looking at something else.

The Veil released him where the ferrocement went gray again.

The friction came back into the world in small pieces. A vendor arguing price two blocks east. A car horn. A line of three people outside a clinic, one of them shifting weight, impatient, alive with the small ugly impatience of a person who still minded waiting. He rode into it. He had never been glad of a car horn before.

He went east along the gray wall.

He would not ride this route again. The canal road was finished. The block was finished. By tomorrow the beacons would pulse eleven or they would not pulse at all, and the green awning and the brick walk-up with its seven names would be on the far side of a line he could no longer cross from this direction.

He did not carry it as loss.

He mapped it as data. The Orison reader had his name now. That was the new boundary condition. Facility 4 knew Seto Banda existed and the canal approach was closed and the north culvert was two miles of dead air. The route had changed. He had changed it before, when the world changed under him, and he had stayed a courier through every version of it.

He logged the beacon count. He logged the reader. He logged the building, seven names, brick, four floors, clean windows, a man wiping a sill.

He rode east and started drawing the next line.
