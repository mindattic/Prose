Anneke Oyelowo came down the east core herself, because she did not delegate the thing she could close in person, and she met Rook at the landing between levels where the loud channel was walking up and the count was walking down, and the two women who counted looked at each other in the crimson light with the wedge-frequency still in both their teeth. She was not armed. She had a tablet the way other people had a sidearm, balanced flat on her forearm, the reconcile open on it in twenty-one rows, and she did not close it when Rook arrived. She let Rook see the rows.

And Rook watched Anneke's free hand do the thing her own left hand did over a bowl at Mrs. Chen's — three fingers, tapping the edge of the tablet, a pattern that wasn't nerves and wasn't music. Inventory. The same tell, in another woman's hand, and Anneke caught Rook seeing it and the fingers went still all at once, the way Rook's did, the only tell either of them had left.

"You priced them," Rook said, flat, the way she said most true things, and she put her hand on the rail the way the clerk three levels down had put a hand on a bed rail — to steady the thing, or to steady herself against it. "Sefi. Adalemo. The woman at forty with the clean cabling. You ran the number on each one and the number came out keep, so you kept them, and you filed the hand that didn't price as gone."

"We pay for a thing," Anneke said, unhurried, and she turned the tablet so the rows faced Rook fully, "and someone else receives it. The gap between the paying and the receiving is the only honest room in this city, and I have furnished it carefully for thirty years. You stand in it too. You took a contract for a count. You're carrying it up a ramp right now in another woman's arms. Tell me the math is different because you say the names out loud."

"It's different," Rook said, "because you just lied to me with your hands and I watched you do it."

Anneke's fingers did not move and that was how Rook knew she'd landed it.

"I know that hand," Rook said. "I've got the same one. So don't sell me the honest room. You don't believe the gap's honest any more than I do — you believe it's *quiet*. You built it so nobody would have to hear the receiving."

Something moved behind Anneke's eyes then — not fear, Rook had been braced for fear and it wasn't that. It was the stillness of a woman doing a count she had decided thirty years ago she would never do again, and finding the figures still clear. Her jaw set. Rook watched it set and recognized the set, because she wore it.

And Rook drew the Reibo.

It came up out of the coat in the one motion she had practiced ten thousand times and used on a planned job exactly never, the flechette muzzle finding the soft hinge of Anneke's jaw, and for one clean second the read was simple in a way it hadn't been simple in three jobs: end the woman, end the program's hand, walk the count out. Her finger took up the slack.

Anneke did not move. She did not look at the gun. She tilted the tablet a half-degree more, and on the reconcile one row burned amber where the other twenty stayed green — *Okonkwo, S.* — and beside it a live cursor blinked in a guard's hand four levels down, holding on a word Anneke had not yet spoken.

"The flechette is faster than the gun," she said, meaning the harvest gun, the one already trained on Sefi's open eyes in the bay below, "but it is not faster than a word, and I have not finished the word. Put it down, or I finish it." And then, lower, almost to herself, the planner's voice slipping a notch the way Adalemo's clean cuff had slipped: "You will make me say it. You will make me be the one who said it, in front of myself, after thirty years of paying other people to be the mouth."

And Rook's hand stopped. The Reibo stayed level, the slack taken up, the shot a quarter-pound of pressure and a whole world away. She could not fire and would not drop it. The count in her chest went to twenty-two — herself the new entry, the woman who could end this if she'd only let Sefi go back to being a number. Her forearm began to shake from holding the draw at full extension and not spending it, a fine tremor she couldn't count away, and she let it shake.

"You said the word's not finished," Rook said, and her voice came out level the way it came out when the level was the only thing holding. "Then you haven't said it yet. That's the only second you've got. Stand in it."

The amber row held. The cursor four levels down held. And Anneke looked at the count walking down the ramp behind Rook, and at the Reibo, and last at her own three fingers flat against the tablet, not tapping now, pressed white at the nail the way Rook pressed hers on a routing code she didn't want to be true.

"You let the twenty-one run," Rook said, lowering the Reibo a hand's width — not surrender, but the gun had never been the argument and they both knew it now. "Years ago, and again just now. You pivoted your guard off Sefi's column to chase one loud voice up the west core, and twenty-one beds went unwatched long enough for the count to reach them. You did the mercy and you didn't price it first. That's the row you can't reconcile. The ledger's open. Look at the line."

Anneke looked at the line. Rook watched her do it — watched the woman who never stopped counting count the one thing she'd refused to enter, the twenty-one against the satisfaction of the catch, and find it wouldn't balance, had never balanced, had only ever been kept off the page. Her thumb came off the tablet. The amber row stayed amber. But the cursor four levels down blinked once and went dark, because the hand holding it had stopped getting an order — and Anneke did not say *put it down* again, and she did not finish the word, and the not-finishing cost her something Rook could see go out of her face like heat off cooling steel.

It did not look like surrender, because it wasn't one. Anneke set the tablet flat against her chest, both arms crossed over it the way you hold a thing you have carried so long the carrying is the only shape your arms know, and Rook understood she was watching a woman keep the ledger anyway — keep it the only way left to her now that she'd seen the line that wouldn't balance, which was to hold the open book against herself and refuse to close it.

Rook did not answer that, because there wasn't an answer that wasn't a lie, and she was done with the lies that made a cold room warm.

She holstered the Reibo — the tremor still in her hand — and stepped sideways onto the down-route without turning her back on Anneke. "I came down here for twenty-one beds. I've got twenty-one. You came down here for one, and you don't. Short by the only entry you made the trip for." She put her eyes on the ramp, not on the woman behind her, and started down toward the count. "Reconcile that on the way up."
