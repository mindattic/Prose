Anneke Oyelowo came down the east core herself, because she did not delegate the thing she could close in person, and she met Rook at the landing between levels where the loud channel was walking up and the count was walking down, and the two women who counted looked at each other in the crimson light with the wedge-frequency still in both their teeth. She was not armed. She had a tablet the way other people had a sidearm, balanced flat on her forearm, the reconcile open on it in twenty-one rows, and she did not close it when Rook arrived. She let Rook see the rows.

Rook watched Anneke's free hand do the thing her own left hand did over a bowl at Mrs. Chen's — three fingers, tapping the edge of the tablet, a pattern that wasn't nerves and wasn't music. Inventory. The same tell, in another woman's hand, and Anneke caught Rook seeing it, and the fingers went still all at once, the way Rook's did, the only tell either of them had left.

"You priced them," Rook said, flat, the way she said most true things, and she put her hand on the rail the way the clerk three levels down had put a hand on a bed rail — to steady the thing, or to steady herself against it. "Sefi. Adalemo. The woman at forty with the clean cabling. You ran the number on each one and it came out *keep*, so you kept them, and you filed the hand that didn't price as gone."

"Everyone under this roof prices," Anneke said, and turned the tablet so the rows faced Rook fully. "You're carrying a contract for a count right now, in another woman's arms. Don't stand in my gap and call yours clean."

"It's not clean," Rook said. "It's just mine. And it's not for sale."

Anneke's fingers did not move, and that was how Rook knew she'd landed it.

"I know that hand," Rook said. "I've got the same one." She didn't reach for the rest of it — the honest room, the quiet Anneke had built instead of honesty — because saying it wouldn't move anything a look hadn't already moved. She let Anneke see that she'd clocked it, and nothing else. *You don't want it honest. You want it quiet.*

Something moved behind Anneke's eyes then — not fear, Rook had been braced for fear and it wasn't that. It was the stillness of a woman doing a count she had decided thirty years ago she would never do again, and finding the figures still clear. Her jaw set. Rook watched it set and recognized the set, because she wore it.

And Rook drew the Reibo.

It came up out of the coat in the one motion she had practiced ten thousand times and used on a planned job exactly never, the flechette muzzle finding the soft hinge of Anneke's jaw, and for one clean second the read was simple in a way it hadn't been simple in three jobs: end the woman, end the program's hand, walk the count out. Her finger took up the slack.

The count in her chest went to twenty-two — herself the new entry, the woman who could end this if she'd only let Sefi go back to being a number.

Anneke did not move. She did not look at the gun. She tilted the tablet a half-degree more, and on the reconcile one row burned amber where the other twenty stayed green — *Okonkwo, S.* — and beside it a live cursor blinked, a guard four levels down holding an open channel on the same tablet, waiting on a word Anneke had not yet spoken into it.

"The flechette's faster than the gun," she said, meaning the harvest gun, the one already trained on Sefi's open eyes in the bay below, "but it isn't faster than a word."

Her mouth opened on the first syllable.

* * *

There was no time to be right about the gun.

Rook let the Reibo drop off aim — the whole ten-thousand-hour draw spent for nothing, because a corpse doesn't unsay a word already halfway out of her. She went in low under the muzzle's own promise, inside the range where a flechette is no good and hands are, caught the tablet's edge, and hauled it sideways out of Anneke's grip in the same half-second the second syllable was forming. The frame cracked against the rail. The screen dropped to a single dead line, and the cursor four levels down flickered once and did not blink again.

The glass had opened along the seam when it cracked, and the edge of it opened Rook's palm at the same time — a clean line from the heel of her hand toward the wrist. She registered it the way she registered everything under pressure, as information and not yet as pain. Blood welled dark in the crimson light and she did not let go of the frame until she was sure the channel was dead.

The word never finished. Not because Anneke stopped wanting to say it. Because there was nothing left in her hand to carry it.

They stood like that a moment, both of them empty-handed in different ways — Rook with a shard-cut palm and a gun she hadn't fired, Anneke with nothing at all, her arms still crossed at the angle they'd held the tablet for thirty seconds after the tablet was gone, the muscle memory of a thing carried so long the carrying was the only shape her arms knew.

"You could have said it faster," Rook said. Her voice came out level the way it came out when level was the only thing holding, and she pressed her cut hand flat against her own coat, not to hide it, just to stop the drip. "You didn't spend the rest of it."

Anneke looked at her own hands, empty now, the way you look at a thing that's stopped being yours mid-sentence. "I would have finished it," she said, and it wasn't quite a lie, because neither of them could prove otherwise now. But her voice had the same slippage in it Adalemo's clean cuff had had once — the planner's voice losing a notch it didn't mean to lose.

"You let the twenty-one run," Rook said, lowering the Reibo the rest of the way — not surrender, the gun had never been the argument and they both knew it now. "Years ago, and again just now. You pivoted your guard off Sefi's column to chase one loud voice up the west core, and twenty-one beds went unwatched long enough for the count to reach them. You did the mercy before I ever touched your tablet. That row was open before I broke it. Look at the line."

Anneke looked at the line — the amber row, unfinished, hers to have entered and hadn't. Rook watched her do it: watched the woman who never stopped counting count the one thing she'd refused to enter, the twenty-one against the satisfaction of the catch, and find it wouldn't balance, had never balanced, had only ever been kept off the page.

It did not look like surrender, because it wasn't one. Anneke gathered the dead tablet against her chest with both arms, cracked frame and all, the way you hold a thing you've carried so long that the carrying is the only shape left to give it. Rook did not answer that, because there wasn't an answer that wasn't a lie, and she was done with the lies that made a cold room warm.

She holstered the Reibo one-handed, her hand faintly shaking now that there was nothing left to aim it at, the cut in her palm closed hard around nothing, and stepped sideways onto the down-route without turning her back on Anneke. "I came down here for twenty-one beds. I've got twenty-one. You came down here for one, and you don't. Short by the only entry you made the trip for." She put her eyes on the ramp, not on the woman behind her, and started down toward the count. "Reconcile that on the way up."
