Boiler had spent his whole working life listening to load — the complaint a stair made under more than it was rated for, the way a wall told you what it was carrying before the carrying killed it — and now he stood in the sub-basement with both hands flat on the harvest manifold, the cold one, the one Cinderblock had built into the spine of the building to draw what it priced down through forty levels of bed and clerk and cabling, and he felt it the way he felt everything, structurally. *It's a stair. It's just a stair carrying more than it's rated for. Except the load is people, and the load has names, and I read the names same as I read a beam.* He stopped whistling. He had been whistling the whole job, low, tuneless, the thing the crew teased him for, and he heard himself stop and did not start again.

He cut the manifold the only way a man on foot cuts a thing rated for machinery: shoulder under the conduit junction where the draw concentrated, boots set wide, and lift. He drove up with his legs first, the way you take a fallen beam off a person — controlled, not fast — and the junction broke its seat with a sound like a hinge shearing, cold metal giving up the contact it had held for thirty years in one clean pop, and then the full load of the draw came down onto him.

The conduit was colder than the room, colder than metal had any business being, the chill of a thing that had spent thirty years drinking heat out of warm rooms forty floors up and never once giving it back, and the cold went straight through his jacket and his shoulder and into the joint before the weight did. He found the seat of the junction with the meat over his collarbone and held, and the load came onto him all at once — not a number, a *pressure*, the entire downward want of the manifold finding the one place in the city that had agreed to stand under it. *Twenty-one. Boiler, you can hold for twenty-one. You held the boiler seam for Scout, you can hold this.* The first surge went through his left arm like a cable taken past its rating, and he heard the sound his own joint made — not a crack, lower than that, a wet structural give, the exact note a fitting makes the instant before it stops being a fitting — and his body did the thing bodies do, which was tell him in one white flood to set the weight down and step off the line. He kept lifting. He counted instead of listening to it. He had spent a life trusting what load told him, and this once he made himself the wall that lies, the wall that carries the thing it was never rated for and says nothing, and he held the give in his arm shut by main force the way you hold a door, and somewhere four levels up the crimson light hitched, dimmed, and the draw on the working floor went slack — and the count Gerald was breathing reached the rooms it was meant to reach, and the rooms held.

Adalemo's crossing finished on the bay-C feed, which Rook had left open on purpose so the building would have to log its own witness. He was three levels up at the compliance junction, still dressed in the gray Helix hadn't yet thought to take off him, his credential doing the last piece of work it had left in it. Scout saw him find the count in the frame — his eyes going to where she stood with twenty-one and not twenty in her arms — and hold there. On the feed it was the smallest motion a body could make and still mean something: a half-turn of the head, slow, against the pull of the loud channel that had run this floor for four years. Helix's voice was still routing through every speaker in the building, still asking for the count back. Adalemo did not answer it. He turned instead to the panel in front of him and finished rerouting the alert his own hand had built the door for, and that was the whole of the crossing — not a rescue, not a redemption speech, just a man who had spent four years deferring people finally putting his weight on the other side of the ledger before anyone could revoke the credential that let him do it. It did not look like mercy and it did not look like rescue. It looked like a man putting down a weight he had been made to carry, and the weight was the other people in the room.

Helix did not go dark. Helix's voice came back up the open channel thinned, the clerk-softness gone ragged at the edge where the audit had failed under it — "Manifold's down. We have a structural casualty in the sub-basement and an audit that is now, regrettably, public" — and that was the corp's own address system saying *public* into every ear on every level, the building putting on record the thing the building had spent thirty years built not to file. Boiler heard it from under the dead junction with his left arm wrong — not numb, he knew numb, this was the arm reporting in from a long way off, the way a far span reports the wind — his cheer burned out of him and twenty-one beds out of reach behind a door he'd just shouldered shut from the wrong side. He did not whistle. He counted, once, under his breath, careful, the way you read a gauge you do not trust your eyes on, to make sure the number that had cost him the arm was the number it was supposed to be — and it was, twenty-one and not twenty, and that was the only clean thing in the room.

Lace came through the sub-basement door thirty seconds later at a dead walk, not running because running down a ramp with a casualty was how you became two casualties. She took one look at the arm and did not say anything, which was how Boiler knew it was bad. She crouched, got her shoulder under his good side, and pulled him up. He let her. The calibration wheel was still in his right hand, closed in his fist, and she did not ask him to open it. Some things you hold until the hold becomes a choice and not a reflex. She understood that. She had been reading people under load her whole working life and she understood exactly what a man looked like when the thing he'd carried had finally settled into him, and she walked him up toward the light without saying a word about it.
