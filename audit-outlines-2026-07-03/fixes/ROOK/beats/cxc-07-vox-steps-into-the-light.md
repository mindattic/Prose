The PEREGRINE in front did not draw. It tilted its head the way a thing tilts its head when something behind its eyes is doing the asking, and Gerald's breath came through the open channel just before the voice did — Helix's voice, routed up through the working floor's own address system so it landed in every ear at once, soft as a clerk's.

"Channel four, we have a face. Vox. Late of Cinderblock Ai, retail-grade, sold the second tier in the Gray Zone. You went dark four years ago and a building remembers what a building bought." The bay's sodium lights held their pitch. "We are not addressing the crew. We are addressing the woman who has been a hole in our audit since intake. Come up the rack assignments and we close the door on the rest. One name for twenty-one beds. Helix is offering you the math."

In a basement under a building that filed her hand away as gone, Vox sat where Boiler had set her — a fold-down clerk's stool bolted to the substrate wall, the kind the day shift perched on to scan rails, cold steel through the back of her thighs and the lip of it cutting a line she'd stopped feeling an hour ago. Her hands were where her hands always were on a job, flat on her knees, palms down, the channels parked behind her eyes idling at the low hum she ran them at so nobody downstream could feel her listening. That was the whole of her trade. A Channeler kept a stable of unlicensed proxy minds and rented them out as deniability — voices the corps couldn't subpoena, faces that didn't appear on any retinal log — and the deniability worked because Vox herself was never on the line. Only the channel was, only the borrowed voice, never the woman who held it. She had built her entire life on the proposition that the woman could not be found. And here was a building reading her retail SKU back to her like a receipt.

She ran the numbers the building had handed her, and found that for once in four years they came out clean. *The thing I sold so I'd never have to be it again, and they walk it back to me like a debt with my own face on the coin.* For four years she had been the channel and never the body on it — the one who spoke through other throats so her own would never have to stand in a lit room and be counted. Every job before tonight, Scout had been the one who rode in absence — Husk left slumped somewhere safe, herself gone into Gerald's frame, nothing in the room a scan could tie to a name. Vox had done it differently and arrived at the same place by the other door. She had simply never let there be a body to leave. The whole architecture of her had been engineered so that no scan, no manifest, no ledger could put her anywhere, could make her stand somewhere as a person who could be carried twice.

She felt the decision before she made it, the way you feel a wedge in your teeth before you hear it in the room. It came up out of the cold steel under her thighs and the flat of her own palms on her knees and the idling hum behind her eyes that she had kept low her whole working life so no one would know she was there. Her pulse went into her throat. She turned her palms over on her knees — open, face-up, the gesture of a person with nothing left to keep hidden — and she was already on her feet before her legs had the full order, the channels behind her eyes ramping up off their idle in a single hard pull, the way you throw a breaker: click, and then the hum was gone and the roar was there, every proxy line she owned slamming open at once, her own voice flooding all of them simultaneously so that for the first time the woman and the channel were the same thing in the same room. The stool's hinge cracked against the wall behind her. She let it.

The fear stayed — it sat in her throat the whole time — but underneath it was the other thing, the thing she'd buried so deep she'd forgotten it had a floor, which was that she had always known how to be loud and had only ever chosen not to be.

"You want a hole in your audit to stand up where you can see it," Vox said, and she said it into the loud channel, the one Helix had opened, the one the whole working floor could hear in their teeth. "Fine. Here. Vox, Late of Cinderblock Ai, retail-grade, the face you bought. I'm walking up the east fire core and I'm going to keep talking, and every clerk on every level is going to hear exactly which beds you deferred and exactly whose hand you filed as gone, and you can spend your whole reconcile chasing the one voice that wants to be found instead of the twenty-one that don't." She was moving now, away from the down-route, away from Scout, a body deliberately wrong in the dark, every channel she owned thrown open behind her and pushing her own voice out through all of them at once. "Scout. Boiler's seam holds. Go. I've got their attention. It's the only thing I ever had worth giving them."

Lace came in over the channel a half-second later, low and precise, the voice of someone who had been reading the room from the top of the east ramp the whole time and had already decided. "I've got her back. East core, second landing." Then, quieter, for Vox alone: "I see you. Walk straight."

Stave's line opened last — no words, just the static of a comm left on a table for four years, live again, the sound of a man who had said he'd never be the call and had picked it up anyway. It closed after two seconds. It was enough.

In bay C the PEREGRINE turned — the whole frame of it pivoting off Sefi's open eyes toward the east core where the loud channel was walking itself into the light — and Scout got her arms under the count she'd come for and heard, under the choir waking in their teeth, the whole crew close around the absent one made loud: Boiler dropping a second wedge to muddy the floor for the woman walking it, Rook's flat voice in the channel saying *east core, all of you, she's not walking it alone*, and Gerald — the boy named, the deal that held even here — breathing out the only thing he had, which was the count, twenty-one, steady, so the floor that was about to remember Vox would have to hear the children first.
