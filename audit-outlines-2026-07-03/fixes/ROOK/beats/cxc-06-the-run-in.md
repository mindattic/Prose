The down-route was a stairwell that had stopped being a stairwell. Helix had poured a service ramp through the old fire core sometime in the dark middle of the building's life — a working slope wide enough to roll beds up and down two abreast, the tread surface scuffed pale in two parallel lanes by years of caster wheels going one way full and the other way empty. The lights along it were the kind they never turned off. Sodium-warm, recessed behind wire cages every four meters, humming at a pitch Boiler had named for them on the way in the way he named everything, under his breath, so the thing would feel less like it was watching you.

Scout took the ramp on foot with Reibo slung across her back where the grip rode the notch of her spine, and Gerald a half-step off her right shoulder, close enough that she could feel the small displacement of him in the air without turning her head. That was the deal. He was in because she was in, and the whole crew heard the boy before they heard her, and she had walked into the concrete agreeing to that and she would walk out of it the same.

She was aware, on the ramp, of the specific wrongness of being present like this. An Exo's whole tradecraft was the absence — ejected from her own Husk and injected into a drone or a crawler, leaving the Husk parked somewhere safe, moving through a space as a thing the room couldn't touch or ID or carry back to a ledger. She had walked into rooms as weather. She had run surveillance as a draft under a door. She had spent eight years making herself the part of an operation that couldn't be hurt because it wasn't there. The ramp was different. She was here, in her body, in her boots, making weight on the tread surface the way the caster wheels had made weight for years, and the building would log her if it bothered to look. Gerald paced her. She could feel him in the air without turning her head. She had argued herself into this in the planning — *the perimeter doesn't work on a building you go down into* — and she had been right, and being right hadn't prepared her for the way it felt to be a person walking somewhere instead of a ghost passing through it. Her throat was tight with it. She breathed through her nose and counted turns and let the tightness be there without trying to put it away. This once, she was the thing in the room the room could feel.

The ramp dropped in a long lazy spiral, one full turn per level, the far wall always just out of reach of her eyes so the working floor came at her in pieces. She counted the turns the way Rook counted heads, because down here a count was the only map that didn't lie. One turn: the stencil on the inner wall read SUBSTRATE -2, the paint old and the arrow under it pointing further down. The smell changed at the second turn before anything else did — warm bodies and saline and the sweet machine reek of coolant cycling through a hundred cradles at once, banking up the core the way heat banks up a stairwell. Third turn, and the sound came in under the sodium hum: breathing, four hundred sets of lungs out of phase, a tide that never came all the way in or all the way out. The ramp leveled into a landing twenty meters on a side, and the first deferral bed sat at the mouth of it, out of the curve before she had a frame to hold it in.

A person. Intact, breathing, eyes taped, a rack assignment stenciled on the bed rail in the older gray of a manifest nobody had bothered to revoke. Not a drive in a rack. A woman, maybe forty, the cabling at her temple dressed clean and looped and tied off the way you dress a thing you intend to keep — not the slapdash spider-work you'd run into a body you meant to strip and recycle, but neat, almost tender, the work of a clerk who came back to the same bed every shift and had stopped being able to tell themselves a story about what it was. The woman's chest rose. Fell. The clean cabling caught the warm light and held it.

*Four hundred warm deferrals in the concrete. Rook said it like a number. It's not a number. It's a hallway, and the hallway has faces.*

Scout locked the woman the way you lock a wound you can't dress yet — bed at the landing mouth, condition stable, owed — and kept moving, and that was the hardest thing she did on the whole descent, harder than what came after, because what came after she could fight. From the landing the floor ran flat and long under a low ceiling, racks in rows of beds receding into the sodium haze, the lit door she'd come for a pale rectangle at the far end maybe sixty meters off. Bay C. She put it on her right and started down the row toward it, counting beds as she went the way she'd counted turns.

Boiler's wedge went into the floor two levels above her — she had the layout in her teeth now, knew it landed up at SUBSTRATE -2 where the conduit junction concentrated the draw — and she felt it arrive before the channel said a word. A pressure-drop in the soles of her feet first, the concrete flinching under her boots, and then the return came up through the slab the way he'd promised. Wrong. Not the clean ring of a wall coming back at her. A choir. It arrived in her own jaw a half-beat before the bodies on the beds felt it in theirs, a fine vibration in the back of the skull, and she had the sick clear thought that this was what it had always felt like to be one of them and never know it.

Then the floor woke.

It moved like wind crossing a field of something that should not be able to feel wind — up the row, bed by bed, a ripple running ahead of the wedge from the landing toward bay C. Heads that had been taped still turning on the pillows. Tendons standing up in throats as faces came around toward a sound their owners couldn't have named. Eight beds down a man made a sound through taped eyes, low, the sound you make surfacing from a dream into a worse room. A clerk three stations along stopped scanning and put her hand flat on a bed rail — not to brace the bed, Scout saw, but to brace herself against it, or to steady the body on it, the gesture didn't decide which, and maybe the clerk didn't either. The whole bay was a held breath letting go in the wrong direction.

She reached the threshold of bay C and stopped one step inside it to read the room the way Rook would want it read. Twenty-one beds, two rows, near row of eleven and far row of ten, the aisle between them running straight to the lit door at the back wall. The near row, ninth bed from where she stood. A name on the rail Rook had said out loud across a partition wall in the planning — Sefi, no QUANTA mark, intake they never sold, the one the building had no honest reason to be holding. Scout had been ready for taped eyes. She hadn't been ready for the rest.

Sefi's eyes were open. They were tracking the wedge — following the vibration the way you follow a fly you can't see, drift and fix, drift and fix — and then they fixed, and they were tracking it straight up to Scout's face, and they held there. Awake. Afraid. Counting on her without knowing yet that there was anyone to count on.

"Gerald, eyes up, they're waking," Scout said into the open channel, low, and heard him pull a breath before he answered, and heard the whole crew go still around the sound of the boy breathing — the deal she'd made holding even here, two levels under the concrete, with a floor full of people coming awake at her back. She crossed the nine beds to Sefi's rail counting them off, and got a forearm under the woman's shoulders, and felt how light four years in a cradle had made her.

That was when she understood the shape of the room she was standing in. The vault was a person, and the lock was a person, and somewhere in the planning that had sounded like the cruelest count Helix could file. Standing in bay C with the choir turning toward her and Sefi's open eyes on her face, it stopped being a count. *I came down here to carry the count up. I'm going back up holding it in my arms, and there's a choir behind me that's awake and afraid, and the soft knock was going to land before the building reconciled itself.*

The soft knock did not get to land.

The lit door at the end of bay C — ten meters up the aisle, dead ahead — opened from the inside.

It opened on a credential that wasn't Adalemo's. She knew it the instant it swung, because Adalemo's credential opened doors slow and apologetic, the door of a man putting his own hand away as gone, and this door came open clean and fast and unbothered. The figures that came through it moved the way PEREGRINE-grade security moved — the private contractor force Helix ran on wetwork contracts, ex-military chassis and no serial number you could pull — economical, unhurried, certain. Helix's now, and they wore it. The seam at their wrists caught the crimson spill of the door same as the cabling at the forty-year-old woman's temple had caught the warm light, same as Sefi's open eyes — same unlogged date stamped into all of it, the building's whole inventory wearing one reconciled mark. Three of them, fanning into the aisle to put a body between Scout and the door, and Scout got her arms tight around the count and her boots set before the first one fully cleared the frame, and in that half-second she understood the thing Rook had built the whole clock around, and gotten wrong only in the timing.

She read the room the way she read a route — fast, on her feet, no schematic. The aisle ran ten meters from her to the door, and the three of them owned the door end, fanned shoulder to shoulder so there was no lane down the middle. Behind her, the threshold of bay C she'd come in by, and past that sixty meters of woken floor and the spiral ramp up. The far row of ten beds on her left, the near row of eleven on her right, and Sefi half-up in her arms making her one body wide where she needed to be none. No high ground in a room with a low ceiling. No outside to fall back to in a building you went *down* into. She couldn't run the count out the way she'd come — that meant turning her back on three PEREGRINE inside knife-range, with both arms full. She couldn't set Sefi down without leaving the one bed in the room that had no honest reason to be occupied undefended in front of the people who'd occupied it. Every lane she'd have taken alone closed the second the count was in her arms, and that, she understood with the same sick clarity the wedge had given her teeth, was the whole point of building a vault out of a person: the thing you came to save was the thing that pinned you.

She backed one step toward the threshold anyway, slow, Sefi tight against her chest, and Gerald slid to her right hip without being told — the small displacement of him in the air, the boy on the channel breathing the count steady so the crew upstairs would hear the floor wasn't lost yet. The lead PEREGRINE matched her step. Did not close. Held the aisle. Waiting on something that wasn't a guard's order.

The building hadn't waited for shift change to reconcile itself.

It had reconciled while she was still on the ramp. The soft knock had gone hard before her hand ever touched the door, and the choir at her back was awake to hear it.
