The door knock came in three days later, but it wasn't Stave who opened it. It was the manifest.

Soraya laid it on the booth table folded to the wrong page — the page nobody at the registry desk was meant to read in sequence, the one that only made sense if you turned the order inside out and read it backward against itself. She set it down without a word, three fingers flat on the paper, and slid it under the crimson light where the steam from Mrs. Chen's kitchen kept fogging the laminate and clearing again.

Rook spread the manifest flat and read it the way Boiler reads a column: not the words, the load. She let her eyes go past the letterhead and the Voluntary Relocation header and the stamped department seals, all the apparatus a thing wears when it wants to be believed, and she found the shape of the weight underneath. Twenty-one names. A single barge out of Old Harbor, departure logged to the minute, arrival never entered. And the names didn't disperse into twenty-one destinations the way relocated people are supposed to disperse. They funneled. They ran down the page into one column, under one repeated word, the word stamped beside every name like a checkmark — *processed.*

Below that column, smaller, was a routing code — a chain-of-custody tag, the kind a corp stamps on freight it expects to be questioned about.

She put her finger on it and did not lift it.

She'd seen that code before. Four years ago, burned into the side of a tank, and she'd read it then as a serial number, the kind of thing you don't think about because the thing it's printed on is already trying to kill you. She'd carried it out of Axiom in the part of her memory that keeps receipts whether you ask it to or not. Now it sat under her fingertip beside the word *processed*, beside twenty-one names that had walked onto a boat, and the two facts found each other in the dark and clicked.

*That's not a transfer order.* Her finger stayed where it was, pressing the paper flat against the table as if holding it down would keep it from being true. *That's a bill of lading. They didn't lose seven the way you lose a name in a flood. They wrote it like freight because freight gets a routing code and a count and a destination, and people get a story, and somebody figured out you could pay for one and file it as the other.*

"The Reclamation ran the barge," Soraya said, and let it sit a moment before she went on, the way she always did with the bad ones. "Registered Reads, all of them — neuretics-capable people the corps log and covet, the ones any facility would pay to have live and cooperative. They walked aboard at Old Harbor under their own names, in daylight, because the order said sunrise. Said sky. Said a place the processors can't redirect you to and back. They believed it because it was the one promise this city keeps — that if a relocation says open water and morning, it means open water and morning." She turned the page back the right way then, slow, smoothing the fold flat with two fingers, gentle, the way you'd draw a sheet up over a face. "One of them came back. She's the reason I have the count at all."

The woman came in behind the steam.

Mrs. Chen put a bowl down for her without being asked — set it at the empty place at the end of the booth with the small economy of a hand that has done it many nights running — and that, more than anything Soraya could have said, told Rook how long this woman had been folded into the back of this room, fed and waiting. She had a left hand that didn't fully close. At the wrist the chrome and the flesh argued, a seam where something had been done and then undone, gone in and come back out wrong, and she carried the hand the way you carry a thing that used to be yours.

She set a paper photograph on the table, face-up.

Twenty-one people on a barge deck, squinting into a morning.

Rook looked at it and did not pick it up. They were standing the way people stand when somebody's told them to gather for a picture and they half-believe it's good news — shoulders not yet braced, one of them caught mid-laugh, a man at the rail with his hand up against the low sun, a kid turned the wrong way to see the water. Morning light coming in flat and clean off the lake, the kind of light that doesn't lie about anything, that just shows you what's there. They didn't know yet. You could see in every face that they didn't know yet.

Something turned over low in Rook's chest, under the ribs, before she had a thought to attach to it — the involuntary lurch of a body recognizing the weight of a thing before the mind catches up. She let it. Then she set her jaw against it and put it where she put the rest, because the photograph wasn't asking her to feel it. It was asking her to do something about it.

"Sefi Okonkwo," the woman said. "There were twenty-one of us when we walked on. I can give you nine." She didn't look at the photo. She'd had four years not to look at it. "The other twelve I watched go through a door. I can tell you the door. I can tell you it locked behind them. And I can tell you that nobody on that registry desk wrote down the sound it made — because the sound it made wasn't on the manifest, and if it's not on the manifest it didn't happen."

She slid the photo across the table to Rook's right hand and left it there.

"You've carried a count like this before," she said. "Fourteen for twenty-one, off Tidewell — Soraya told me there's a name at the bottom of it you don't say out loud. So don't sit there and tell me the number's too big. Tell me when we leave."

The booth went quiet. In that quiet Rook looked at Soraya — not to ask anything, not to confirm. Just to look, the way you look at the person who was standing next to you when the floor went. Soraya was looking at the photograph, at the morning light on twenty-one faces that didn't know yet, and her hands were very still in her lap. She felt Rook's eyes and did not look up, which was its own answer: *I know. I've known for four years. I'm sorry I waited this long to put it in a room where something could be done about it.*

Rook picked up the photograph. She turned it face-down.

"Get me the full barge manifest," she said. "All forty names. I want to know who isn't on it."
