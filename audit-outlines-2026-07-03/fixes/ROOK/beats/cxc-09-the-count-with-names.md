The Sojourn sat where Cinderblock's tier met the Gray Zone seam, a long counter and a long window, and the window gave the lake the way only the cheap places did — nobody on this tier paid for a view of water that predated their lease. Rook took the stool at the far end where the glass met the wall, set the receipt-paper roll on the counter beside a coffee she would not finish, and looked at the lake while it did the thing it always did, which was nothing, gray and flat and useless to the audit Helix had read into every ear four levels underground. The window gave her back to herself over the water — olive and tired, dark hair come loose from the tie, the pale eyes, the scar high on the right cheekbone she had carried longer than any name she was about to write. For once she did not take inventory of the face. She let it sit there over the lake, a person and not an operator.

She unrolled the paper. She had a pen, the analog kind, the kind that did not log to anything and could not be edited after the fact, and she wrote *Sefi* and under it *Adalemo* and under that *the woman at forty with the clean cabling — name pending, get the name* and she kept going, down the rows, not the rows that priced but the rows that received.

She wrote until the names ran out at twenty-one, and then she counted them, once, the way Boiler had counted under the dead junction to make sure the arm had bought the right number — and it was twenty-one, and not twenty, and the one who would have been the gone hand had a name on the line with the rest of them.

She left a gap after the last name, enough space for the seven who had come out of the secondary block and who she did not have names for yet, and below the gap she wrote *31 confirmed*. Then she did not close the roll. She left it open on the counter facing the window.

Outside, the lake held the color of old concrete — the gray of a city that had been raining for weeks and was thinking about stopping, but hadn't decided yet. She had grown up without water this size. The first time she'd seen the lake she had been twenty-two and running a courier job through Z11, and she had come out of the drainage corridor at the wrong exit and found herself at the lakeshore with the water going further than she could estimate and a seam-runner's mentality that hadn't been built for horizons. She had looked at it for approximately four seconds and then she had gone back into the corridor and finished the job, because she didn't have time for horizons.

That had been before the counting. Before the Rook that had a method and a crew and a gun with a registered identity and a stool at the end of a counter where the water was visible without needing her to stand at the edge of it.

The Reibo was in the holster, chrome that swallowed the dawn light and gave nothing back. She put her hand on the counter, not the gun. She wasn't taking inventory. She was sitting.

The stool beside her had been empty since she sat down. It stopped being empty when Soraya took it — no announcement, no asking, the quiet arrival of a woman who had spent four years not saying the thing out loud and had nothing left to announce. She had her own coffee, also bad, and she set it on the counter and looked at the lake too. The roll of paper sat between them, twenty-one names written in analog ink below the gap and *31 confirmed* at the bottom, the kind you couldn't edit after the fact.

Soraya read the list. She read it the way she read data — completely, from the top, every entry. When she got to *the woman at forty with the clean cabling — name pending, get the name* she stopped on it a moment, and something moved in her jaw, not speech, and then she kept reading. She finished the list and set her finger at the bottom, below the last name, the way you indicate where a signature should go.

"I know her name," she said. "The woman at forty. She was in the Axiom lab three floors below mine. Emeka. She was teaching herself Mandarin from a program she'd borrowed off a colleague, and she kept the earpiece in one ear the whole shift, even on breaks." She lifted her finger off the paper. "She had good recall. The corps would have noticed that on the intake scan."

Rook picked up the pen. She crossed out *name pending, get the name* and wrote *Emeka* and below that, smaller, *Mandarin, one earpiece*. She wasn't sure why she wrote the second part. It seemed like the right kind of fact to have on the paper. The kind that didn't price.

"There was one more," Soraya said. "In the secondary block. The one Lace found — the seven. I knew one of them from the lab. A kid named Bram, early twenties. He'd been an apprentice surveyor before the intake. He'd been mapping elevation data for a dam project in Z8." She was looking at the lake, not the paper. "He was good at estimating distances from reference points. Better than the engineers who supervised him."

"What block?"

"Secondary substrate staging. They moved the lab residuals into that block when the primary facility filled." She picked up her coffee. "He was lucky. Or unlucky. Depending on when you start counting."

"We start counting when someone's on the list," Rook said.

"Yes." Soraya drank her coffee, bad as it was. "I know."

Rook wrote *Bram — apprentice surveyor, Z8 dam project, secondary block* in the gap after the twenty-one. The gap was filling.

They sat with the lake for a while. The coffee went cold between them at the same rate.

"It came out whole," Soraya said. She wasn't asking.

"Thirty-one," Rook said. "More than it came in with."

Soraya almost smiled at this. The almost was as far as it went, because Soraya was not a person who finished smiles when the work wasn't finished, and the work wasn't finished. But the almost was there, at the corner of her mouth, for three seconds before the lake took it back.

"I need to tell you something," Soraya said. She set her coffee down and turned to face Rook directly, which was unusual for Soraya at this kind of table. She did not usually look at things that cost her to look at. "The Marrow was not the only one. The program ran at three facilities — the Marrow was the one that was closest to the delivery cycle. The others are further along." She paused. "I didn't tell you before the run because I needed you focused on the twenty-one, and because the other two are not problems this table solves tonight." She held Rook's eyes steady. "But I'm telling you now, because you should hear it from me and not from a registry desk."

Rook looked at the list. Twenty-one names plus the gap, filling. *31 confirmed.*

She set the pen down without writing anything else.

"It won't be tonight," she said. "And it won't be this list. Whatever's running at the other two gets its own count, its own crew, its own morning — not this one, and not this list, and not this coffee going cold on this counter." She looked at Soraya, and at the lake, gray and cold and indifferent, and did not reach for the roll again. "The GLMZ doesn't stop being what it is because one building came down. I know that. I've known it since the bridge in Z5. But the number I'm keeping tonight is thirty-one, and I'm not going to let a number I haven't earned yet crowd the one I have."

Outside, the morning was the same morning it always was over the lake, which was gray and cold and indifferent. A barge was crossing in the middle distance, slow and heavy, the kind of barge that carried substrate without caring what it carried or where it went when the transit was done. She watched it for a moment, and then she didn't, because there was a list on the counter that was actually hers to finish, and she turned back to it instead.

Soraya was watching the barge a moment longer. Then she reached over and rolled the paper closed herself — gently, the way she'd smoothed the original manifest, except now the smoothing wasn't covering anything. The paper said what it meant, all the way to the last name, and she was done adding to it tonight.

"Thirty-one," Rook said again, quieter, the way you say a thing you intend to keep saying. "That's the one I'm keeping."

They sat with the lake a while longer, and did not talk about the other two facilities again that morning, and the coffee finished going cold, and the lake did the thing it always did, which was nothing, and for once that was enough.
