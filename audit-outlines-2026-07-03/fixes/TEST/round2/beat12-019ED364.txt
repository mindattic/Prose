The window estimate had been running in the back of his mind for three years, and he trusted it the way he trusted the Harvest Frame's charge reading: not because he had verified it, but because he had built it carefully enough to stop second-guessing it.

Forty-eight hours after the committee sat, Halcyon's authorization suspension protocol would run. That was the public architecture, the part Brandt had handed him as a courtesy on the phone this morning. The private architecture was his own: six to eight hours between the handshake and the moment their network resolved the transponder lag and knew exactly where he stood. That window was the one he'd been building toward. Everything else - the identities, the service passage, the lumen mouse and its wall - was the logistics of fitting himself through that window before it closed.

He had the plan. He had always had the plan, more or less. What he was sitting with now was something smaller than the plan and harder than strategy.

He was sitting with the girl.

The girl from account eleven had bent her share toward a bursary four years ago, on her own. He didn't know her name. He had chosen not to know it, which was a different thing from not knowing it, and the difference had been keeping him decent for eight years. He had paid and looked away. That was the architecture of the obligation: his eyes out, his hands open.

Testimony changed that architecture.

A dead colonel proved nothing. A dead colonel was one more body in an institution that had been filing bodies for a decade and calling them categories. But testimony - three sheets into an open civil accountability record, signed, dated, placed in a proceeding designed to outlast the man who filed it - that was a different instrument. It didn't close the question. It held the question open, in writing, in a place where the girl from account eleven could, if she wanted to, in five years or fifteen, when she was studying whatever she was studying and needed to understand what happened at a compound in the Cortland sector the year she was four - pull the record and find a count that was true and a man who had held it.

He hadn't thought about it in exactly those terms before. He was thinking about it now.

She had paid the money forward because someone had given it to her and she had decided it wasn't hers to keep. That was a moral accounting she had done alone, at thirteen, without a template. He had been the template - the unseen man who sent the transfer on the same date every month, who signed his real name onto trust paperwork a smarter fugitive would have refused to sign. She had taken the shape of that and made her own decision inside it, and the decision was: *I am not the end of this. I am part of the chain.*

He sat with that for a while, his forearms on the table, the warmth at his sternum low and steady. Then he took his hand away from the back of his neck - he hadn't decided to put it there - and laid it flat.

He wasn't going to kill Brandt. He had been honest with himself about that long enough to know it was settled. Not sentiment: the numbers. A dead colonel was an entry the institution already knew how to file. A live record was a problem it couldn't unwrite. And the only thing that made the record worth a damn - the thing that stood the count up on its own legs where a girl in Meridian could find it someday and not have to trust that the man who made it was telling the truth - was that it entered the civil accountability system clean, uncompromised, not the testimony of a man who had then gone and put a hand through someone's chest. Evidence left by a soldier who told the truth and walked. Not a grievance. An account.

He had an estimate. He had carried it long enough to trust it.

He got up. He started writing the three sheets.
