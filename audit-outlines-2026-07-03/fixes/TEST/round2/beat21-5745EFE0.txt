Declan Iyengar was standing outside the IRB building entrance at 1247.

Bear saw him before he was close enough to read the face - a man at the lobby threshold who wasn't waiting for transit and wasn't smoking and wasn't looking at his comm, which made him a man waiting for a specific person. The suit told the rest of it. It was a good suit, the kind that had been measured rather than bought, charcoal with no shine to it, and Declan stood inside it the way men stand inside clothes that cost more than a day of their work: very still, so the lines stayed clean. He had no coat against the cold off the lake. That was a choice too. A coat made you look like you'd come a long way and might leave soon. No coat said *I belong to this building.*

Bear let his gaze travel down once, the old habit, the scan he didn't bother suppressing because it cost nothing. Hands loose at the sides. Jacket buttoned. No break in the line of the cloth at the hip or the small of the back where a frame would print. No bulk at the ankle. As far as Bear could determine at distance, Declan didn't have a weapon. Declan had never once needed one with him, and they both knew it, and Declan had come anyway in his good suit to stand in the cold without a coat, which Bear respected. It was a kind of effort.

Then he was close, and Declan smiled.

It was the smile. Bear had seen it three times across eight years and he knew it the way you know weather - the smile that meant Declan had prepared something careful and was about to deliver it with the warmth of a man handing you a gift he had wrapped himself. There was real warmth in it. That was what made it work. Declan wasn't a liar. Declan was something more expensive than a liar.

"Boris," he said.

"Declan."

"I want you to know that the division takes no position on the substance of what you're going in to say." He said it cleanly, no rush, the sentence already smooth from the night he had spent sanding it. "That's not our role. You understand that. Whatever's in the record is in the record." He let a small space open, the way a man does when he wants you to feel the next part arrive on its own. "What I want to flag - and I'm flagging this as a professional courtesy - is that your license renewal comes before committee in six weeks. Separate track. Different personnel entirely. I won't be in that room. But those processes" - and here the warmth didn't waver, not by a degree - "tend to be sensitive to the atmosphere at the time."

He let that sit.

The lake wind came up the avenue and moved nothing about him. Bear stood in it and felt the cold find the seams of his own jacket, the honest cold - and under the jacket, the low banked heat of the frame idling through its cycle, the one warmth in the morning that was his and not the building's.

"Whatever you decide today," Declan said, "I'd welcome ten minutes after. Just us. No record." The smile again, smaller now, almost rueful, as though he too regretted the necessity of standing here. "Boris."

Bear had always respected how Declan did this. He meant it as a compliment, privately, the way you respect a craftsman. Everything above-board. Everything true. The license renewal was real. The separate track was real. The different personnel were real. And every true thing, laid edge to edge in that careful architecture, made one shape, and the shape was *you are going to lose your work authorizations, and I am the man telling you so kindly that you will thank me for it.* Declan had probably spent the night on it. He had spent the night, and worn the good suit, and come out into the cold without a coat, all of it for a man he couldn't threaten, because the institution had told him to and Declan did good work.

So Bear didn't give him hostility. There was nothing in him to give it from. He wasn't afraid of Declan and didn't need to show that he wasn't - the not-needing was the whole thing, the easy size of a man standing in front of someone who had brought him a careful gift that wouldn't do what it was built to do. He felt, instead and against his own intention, a small involuntary fondness for the effort of it, the night of sanding the sentence smooth. He let that be on his face for a moment, because it was true, and then he set it back where it belonged.

"Thank you, Declan," Bear said. He meant the thank-you the way he meant most things, completely. "Take care of yourself out here. It's cold."

He put a hand briefly on Declan's shoulder - not a grip, just the flat warm weight of it, there and gone, the way you steady something you have no intention of moving - and he walked past him into the building.

The panel was at 1300. He was nine minutes early.
