He did not think of what he owed as guilt. He had turned that one over enough times to be sure of it. Guilt was about the person who felt it - a thing that lived inside the man carrying it, that wanted to be witnessed, soothed, set down. Guilt asked for something back. What Bear owed asked for nothing back, because the people it belonged to couldn't hear him, and a debt you pay to the dead and the bereaved isn't a debt that comforts you. It was about the forty-three. He had a word for it, and the word was obligation, and he said it to himself the way other men said grace - plainly, without flourish, because it was true.

He was standing at the window, one big hand flat on the cold glass. Down in the street a vendor was cranking a shutter up its track, the metal complaining the whole way. Bear watched it until the shutter settled, then turned back into the room.

Sixteen names. He could have recited them in his sleep and some nights did, not as penance but the way a man checks that his keys are still in his pocket. Sixteen account numbers, and behind the numbers, sixteen households that had once had someone in them who did not anymore. Three of the recipients had been too young to hold funds when he started - children, in the legal sense and every other one - so he had opened trust accounts, sat across a desk from a fiduciary in a beige office and signed his real name onto paperwork that a smarter fugitive would have refused to sign. The accounts had since passed to the children directly, who weren't children now. He had let them take the keys. That was the point of giving them.

The amount was fixed. He had calculated the rate in the third month after he walked off the base, sitting alone with a cheap slate and the kind of reckoning that doesn't improve with feeling: what a sustained, non-interest-bearing obligation looked like spread over twenty years, set against what he could actually move every month without drawing a flag. He had landed on a number that was honest in both directions - enough to matter, low enough to last. He hadn't adjusted it since. The temptation, in flush months, was to round up; to let a good job translate into a heavier transfer, as though the families were a gauge he could watch tip toward filled. He never did. The number was the number. Inflating it would have made the gesture about him, about how he felt that month, and the whole architecture depended on it not being about him.

One account had no living recipient. He had looked - paid people who were good at looking - and there was an estate and no family found, the line gone quiet the way lines went quiet in the gray zones, no forwarding, no next of kin, just a name and a closed door. He could have let that one lapse. No one would have noticed; no one was waiting on it. He sent the transfer instead to a civil victims' endowment on the twenty-first, the same as the others, the same amount, because the obligation didn't evaporate just because there was no longer a hand to receive it. He had done this for eight years. He didn't know her name. He knew the child's jacket had been yellow.

The twenty-first had become a thing his body knew before his calendar did. He would wake somewhere - a rented room, a Pulse berth, once a freight container with the door cracked for air - and feel the date sitting on his chest the way the Harvest Frame sat there, present and faintly warm, a weight he had stopped resenting and started living inside. He did the transfers in the morning, before coffee, before anything, sixteen confirmations one after another, and there was no ceremony to it because ceremony would have been for him. He read each name as it cleared. He watched the small green acknowledgment, the only reply he was ever going to get, and he moved to the next. It took eleven minutes most months. It was the most certain eleven minutes he had.

Account eleven ran a little differently now - a standing redirect the recipient had set herself, sending part of each transfer somewhere he'd never intended. He let it run and didn't examine it. That one had its own story, and it wasn't his to tell.

The other two trust accounts belonged to people who were now seventeen and fourteen. He didn't know their given names. He had decided, early, that it was cleaner not to - that the obligation was strongest when it was owed to the fact of them and not to a face he could grow attached to, a story he could tell himself about how they were doing. He sent the money to numbers. The numbers stood for people he would never sit across from, and that was correct. A man could fool himself into thinking he had made something right if he got to watch the good land. Bear had decided not to watch. He paid, and he looked away, and the looking-away was part of the paying.

Eight years of the twenty-first of every month. Ninety-six transfers a year if you counted the dead account, and he counted the dead account. He had never missed one. Not when he was hurt, not when he was hunted, not the month he had four hours of warning and a city to cross. There was no version of him that missed one, and he knew this about himself the way he knew his own reach - not as virtue, just as fact, the floor he stood on. He had decided what he owed forty-three people who couldn't hear it, and then he had paid it, on time, without being asked, for eight years. It was the one thing in his life that was finished and not finished at the same time. He was at peace with that. A promise you keep on the twenty-first is a promise you get to keep again on the twenty-first. He intended to.
