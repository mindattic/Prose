They called it in at 1159.

For nineteen days they had watched a man go to work. Bear had given them that on purpose, the way you give a dog the slow hand before the fast one. He woke at the same hour, took the same transit, ate at the same noodle counter where the woman behind the steam knew he liked the broth darker than the menu allowed and charged him for it without being asked. He stood his rotations on the Ironbend dock. He paid his sixteen transfers on the twenty-first, because the twenty-first doesn't move for surveillance, and a man who breaks his obligation to hide it is a man who has already lost the argument he is trying to win. He let them log all of it. He wanted the pattern fat and well-fed, so that when he stepped out of it the step would carry weight.

It wasn't difficult. Bear had spent a long career being looked at, and somewhere in the middle of it he had stopped minding. The trick people never believed was that there was no trick - you simply lived your life and let them write it down, and the writing-down became the cage they trusted. Brandt's teams had pulled back on the fourteenth, thinned to a watch rotation, which told Bear they had decided the pattern was the man. That was the mistake. The pattern was the gift.

So the nineteen days had a texture he had come to like, the way you come to like a long approach march once you stop fighting the boredom of it. Climate-control hum in the freight halls. The dock manager who deferred the seam in the corrugated metal through six fiscal quarters and would defer it through six more. The lumen mouse that had shown him a wall that wasn't a wall. He carried the decision the whole time the way he carried the Manowar's frame - six times organic output, manageable, not comfortable, always there. He had made the decision long before the first watcher took up position. The nineteen days weren't deliberation. They were patience, which is a different muscle, and Bear had the larger one.

At 1158 he turned away from Ironbend.

Wrong direction for a freight shift. Wrong transit for any errand he had ever run in front of them. He felt it land before any alert could - the small clean click of a thing he had planned arriving exactly where he had set it down. Not nerves. The opposite of nerves. The settled weight of a man executing a decision he had finished making weeks ago, so that the body had nothing left to argue about and could simply walk. He didn't look for the watchers. Looking would have been a courtesy he didn't owe them and a tell he couldn't afford.

Brandt's surveillance team would have had the deviation logged in under a minute, and they did: 1159. Bear had given them everything they needed in that single turn - a clean break from nineteen days of habit, heading toward the transit lines that ran past the IRB building. He wasn't hiding the destination. He wanted Brandt to read it correctly and early, because a Brandt who guessed right would commit his people to the obvious choke points, and the obvious choke points were the ones Bear had already walked.

They had thirty-eight minutes before he reached the freight bay. Brandt was good at using thirty-eight minutes. Bear knew this the way he knew the man's posture and the inch-and-a-half scar - knew it as a fact he had built the morning around, not a fear he had to talk himself past. Thirty-eight minutes was enough to move a stack of operators into a corridor. It wasn't enough to find a service passage that an eight-pound animal had used and Halcyon's route analysis had never indexed. Bear had spent the difference between those two facts the way another man spent savings: carefully, and only once.

The gap was eighteen inches. Bear was not eighteen inches. He had given this discrepancy careful thought over three days and arrived at the same answer each time: sideways, committed, don't stop. There was no clever version of it. There was only the going.

He went through the freight bay at 1237. He didn't get stuck, which had been his primary objective. The laminate scraped, the frame complained through the myomimetic mesh, and then he was through and breathing in a stairwell that smelled of cold concrete. Three flights of service stairs. The fire door on the third level that the building's system flagged as alarmed hadn't been tested recently enough to know it wasn't - Bear had read the maintenance ledger the same way he read everything, looking for the deferral, the quarter where someone had decided it could wait. He put his shoulder to it. It opened on nothing. No bell. The system believed in an alarm that no longer existed, which was the most honest thing the building had told him all day.

He was inside at 1247. He had thirteen minutes.

He took the charge reading the way he always did before a thing - not by hand, just the number surfacing through the neuretics: accumulation nominal, two discharges still in reserve from a morning of movement, the capacitor lattice fed by every stair and every scrape. He checked it once, the way you check a weapon you already know is loaded, and let it go.

He straightened his jacket and walked toward the main corridor. The maintenance side of the building smelled like climate control and nothing else - the particular blankness of a space that had been air-managed into neutrality, scrubbed of anyone who had ever stood in it. His footsteps were the loudest thing in it, big and unhurried, a man arriving on time to a thing he had decided on long ago. He noted that.
