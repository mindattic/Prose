The maintenance VTOL came down wrong — Boiler feathered the throttle too late, caught a gust broadside, and the touchdown was a controlled stumble that left the left skid kissing the deck at thirty degrees before correcting. Not a crash. Adequate.

Rook was out before the rotors stopped.

The wind off the lake hit her like a shove — forty-five km/h, gusting, driving rain horizontally across deck 1's landing area. The deck lights on the starboard side cut out in the same second she stepped onto the wet grating. Scout was already working.

Boiler's voice on comms, cheerful: "Contact."

The close-quarters specialist was fast. He came out of the maintenance bay access at a run, low, something short-barreled in his right hand. Boiler met him at the base of the crane arm. What followed lasted eleven seconds and involved Boiler using the weight of his own body as a weapon in a way that shouldn't have worked against someone that trained. It worked. The specialist went down hard against the crane base, out, and Boiler was already at the control panel, rain running off his shoulders, reading the override with the focused calm of a man who has operated worse equipment in worse conditions and considers this Tuesday.

"Crane override. Manual. Old system — they didn't convert it. Beautiful."

Rook was already moving toward the corridor access to deck 2.

Lace came on: "Sending now."

On PEREGRINE's operational frequency — Rook had it patched in — a voice said: "Alpha, this is command. Pull back to deck two, corridor. Support network op. Deck one is clear. Priority shift." It was Seun Adalemo's voice. The cadence, the specific flatness he used issuing low-urgency orders, the faint clipping on the *p* sounds. Lace had built it from nothing and it was very good.

A pause. Then: "Copy, command."

Ninety seconds. Maybe. Rook moved.

---

The deck 2 corridor was dark — Scout had cut that circuit too. Rook got through the access with Ohara two meters behind her, running quiet, and she could feel the woman's controlled breathing, the deliberate steadiness of it. Not trained. Practiced. There was a difference. Ohara had prepared for this the way a person prepares for a surgery they're performing on themselves — by reviewing the steps until the steps are automatic enough that the fear has nowhere to land.

The network operator was somewhere in the corridor. Rook heard him before she saw him: a boot scuff, a held breath. She put Ohara into the stairwell alcove with one gesture — flat hand, stay — and moved toward the sound.

The engagement was brief and unkind. The corridor was narrow enough that his weapon was a liability. Hers wasn't. He went down in the dark, breathing, and Rook stepped over him and got back to Ohara.

"Stairwell," Rook said. They went up.

---

Vox on comms: "Layer two is down. Working layer three. I need Ohara's auth window in approximately —" A pause, recalculation, not hesitation. "— four minutes from now. I'll tell you when."

"Understood," Ohara said, the way she said everything in the field — precise, flat, dictating a result she'd already confirmed.

Deck 3 was open observation on the lakeward side and enclosed on the platform side, the broadcast array shelter a squat rectangular structure that looked like something a utility company would put on a roof and never look at again. Rain hit every surface. The safety railing ran the observation perimeter — two-meter sections, bolted, rated for storms exactly like this one. Except the port-forward corner, which had torn partially free from one anchor in the last hour and now swung loose by thirty degrees, chain rattling in the wind.

Rook clocked the loose rail and kept moving. A thing to use or a thing to die on, she'd know which when she got there.

Seun Adalemo was standing at the array shelter's near corner, and he saw her come up the stairwell at the same instant she saw him. Soaked through, coat dark with rain, his position exactly where she'd have put herself in his place — clear line to the stair access, cover behind the shelter angle, wind at his back.

Ohara wasn't where she was supposed to be. Rook hadn't told her to move laterally behind the structural columns. Ohara had looked at the deck layout for four seconds when they crested the stairs and decided, on her own, that a support column beat open sightlines whatever came next — the same read she'd made in the corridor an hour before this one, the same instinct that had put her two meters sideways in the Bloom Quarter before anyone told her to move. Rook clocked the empty space where Ohara should have been, clocked the shape behind the column instead, and let it go. It cost her nothing. It was the correct decision, made by the person best positioned to make it.

Adalemo came at her from the shelter corner, and they closed the distance in a way that made talking briefly impossible.

---

He was good. Not faster than her — no one operating at the level he'd been operating for four hours was faster than anyone — but adapted, reading her weight shifts the way you read them when you've done this enough times that the reading is below thought. They ended up on the loose section of railing, the one that had failed, and the railing gave another centimeter under the combined weight and screamed on its remaining anchor, and the lake was forty meters down and very dark and very cold.

Adalemo held the railing with his left hand. His right was on her forearm. Her hand was on his shoulder. This was collaborative in a way neither of them had planned, two people balancing each other over the water while they decided which one was going into it.

"The formula," Adalemo said. Not winded. She respected that. "You understand what happens when it's public?"

"Axiom loses control of the Class V application. Someone else weaponizes it in three years instead of five."

"That's the correct answer." He shifted his weight. She countered. The railing moved. "So why?"

"Payment."

"No." He said it almost gently. "I pulled your file on the flight out. Inkeri Saarinen. Meridian PD, intelligence, dissolved 2208. You've turned down four wet contracts in three years that would've paid better than this. You don't do the kind of work I do, and you could. I'd like to understand the line, because I'm standing on the other side of it and I used to think it was the same side."

There it was — the thing under the man. Not a corporate voice reciting a contract. A professional who had once drawn the same line she drew and had moved it, one acceptable number at a time, until he was the hand that priced a woman in a corridor and didn't blink, and some part of him knew the migration had happened and could no longer find the day it started.

"You had a line," Rook said. The railing groaned. "You moved it for terms. I keep mine because the people who walk through the doors I open have to be able to trust the door. That's the whole line. It's not moral. It's structural. The day I move it for a better number, the door's worth nothing, and so am I."

"That's a luxury for a small operation."

"It's the only reason a small operation gets to stay small and alive." She got her weight to the inside. "You sent a round into a crowd today. Or your man did, on terms you wrote. Fifties. A waiver argument. She's the line, Adalemo. You can't see her from where you moved to. I'm not going to be able to stop seeing her. That's the difference between us, and it's the only one that matters tonight."

Something crossed his face that wasn't tactical — quick, and gone, and real. "Maybe," he said. "It's a cold way to be right."

"Yes." The railing gave another centimeter. "Adalemo. You're going into the lake."

He looked at the water. "I know."

"Survival float?"

A pause that answered the question.

"Then we're done," Rook said, and meant it without pleasure and without hesitation, and the railing gave all the way and Seun Adalemo went off the observation deck in a controlled fall that told her he had done exactly one thing right: kept his arms at his sides.

She heard the float inflate before she heard him hit the water.

---

Boiler's voice: "Rook. I have a firing position. Relay junction behind the shelter glass — it's feeding the broadcast node's authentication trunk. If I open it up, whatever's racing Ohara's revocation down that line has to find a new road."

"Do it," Rook said.

"Copy. You might want to look away from the glass."

She didn't look away. Through the rain she could see the crane arm — Boiler had swung it to maximum extension, out over the deck 3 edge, and he was at the end of it, hanging off the superstructure in the middle of a November storm, rain running off him in sheets, sighting through the viewport glass at exactly the angle she'd have wanted him at if she'd been able to ask. He fired once. The reinforced pane starred and blew inward in a sheet of glittering fragments, and behind it something in the relay housing threw a fat blue spark and went dark, and half the deck's running lights stuttered and came back on a different circuit. He was grinning — she could tell from twenty meters in the dark, rain running down a face that had just done something to a Lake Platform relay panel the maintenance logs would spend years failing to explain.

Vox: "Whatever that was, thank it. Axiom's revocation just lost its fastest path to this terminal. Layer three. Ohara. Now."

---

Ohara was already at the terminal.

She'd gotten around the broadcast array shelter's far side while Rook was on the railing. She had the access panel open — she'd cleared the shelter door with a maintenance code she'd memorized from the platform's published Cooperative documentation six months ago — and her hands were on the input surface, rain-wet, steady.

She entered her credentials. Her Axiom ID, her research tier, her neuretics handshake, all of it. She hadn't revoked them before leaving the compound, because she'd known she would need them for this, and the revocation would have flagged the exit early.

The terminal accepted her.

Vox: "Axiom's revocation is still routing around the junction Boiler just took out. You have the window, and it's wider than it was. Not large. Wider."

"I know," Ohara said.

She opened the neuretics upload protocol and held the interface point against the terminal's optical reader. The formula — four years, three failed approaches, one classification she had identified herself and reported to no one because she knew what would happen if she had — moved from the encoded partition in her own neural storage into the broadcast queue.

Four minutes and twelve seconds after authentication began: published. Global. Irrevocable. The most utility-minded piece of equipment on deck 3 had just done the most consequential thing any civic broadcast node in the GLMZ had done in three years, and it had done it without drama, because it was built to transmit and this was what it did.

Ohara stepped back. Her hands were still steady. Her expression wasn't quite anything that had a word for it — the face of a person who has watched a count she ran for two years come out even, and is discovering that even isn't the same as relieved.

---

PEREGRINE's VTOL had been holding off deck 1 — the driver steady, reading altitude, waiting for Adalemo's extraction call. When the broadcast acknowledgment propagated and no call came and Adalemo's position read null, the pilot made the correct decision and began climbing. Pulling out was correct. Rook would have done the same.

She was on deck 3. The stairwell to deck 1 was on the far side of the array shelter from where she stood. The maintenance VTOL — their planned extraction — was on deck 1 with Boiler. She could take the stairs. She could take the maintenance VTOL. That was the plan.

The PEREGRINE VTOL rose past the deck 3 level, twelve meters off the edge and moving, landing skid swinging in the gust.

She looked at the skid. She looked at the deck edge. She ran.

Not a considered run. The railing was under her hands and the rule was behind her, because Adalemo was on that VTOL and Adalemo was the only person left on this platform who could reconstitute what remained of PEREGRINE, and the skid was twelve meters out and swinging back. She went over.

Her hands caught the skid, both wrists taking the full shock of her body weight plus forward velocity, and the cold of the aluminum through wet gloves was irrelevant because she was holding on and the VTOL was still climbing.

The pilot banked hard starboard. Clean instinct — weight on the skid, unannounced, bank to shake it. Rook swung, held, swung back. Caught her grip. The pilot banked again, harder. Rain and altitude and the lake below and the deck 3 lights getting smaller.

She held.

She got her left arm over the skid bar. Then her right knee. The pilot was good, and she was better, and she pulled herself up the skid frame and found the cockpit hatch — side-mounted, unlocked, rated for emergency egress — and got it open on the fourth try because the wind kept taking it and her gloves were wet and there was more altitude than she was comfortable with, but she got it open and pulled herself through.

The pilot was reaching for something. Rook put her hand on his forearm.

"Deck one. We are landing on deck one. This is not a discussion."

He looked at her. She looked at him. The altitude question had been settled by her presence in the cockpit.

He brought it down.

---

The landing was bad. One skid hit hard before the other, the deck was wet, and the VTOL yawed fifteen degrees before the pilot got it corrected and killed the lift. Both of them sat in the sudden stillness a moment. Rain on the cockpit glass.

"Okay," the pilot said.

"Okay," Rook said. She climbed out.

---

Deck 1 in the rain. Boiler was down from the crane, shaking water off his shoulders, apparently unbothered by the forty minutes he'd spent hanging over the lake in a storm. Ohara came down the stairwell forty seconds later, coat soaked, carrying nothing. Vox was in the maintenance VTOL — agents disconnecting, channels closing one by one, three becoming two becoming one becoming silence.

Scout sat up in the back seat. Her eyes came back in stages — the narrow return, the human field of view replacing whatever the crawlers had shown her. She blinked. Looked at Rook through the rain-streaked window. Her expression said she had been entirely elsewhere for all of it, which was true, and that she didn't find this a deficiency, which was also true. She had cut the lights. Her crawlers had done everything she needed them to do. She hadn't been present for any of it, and she had been essential to all of it.

From deck 2, the Cooperative maintenance crew began unlocking their own lab doors. They'd been in there the whole time. They came out cautious, found an empty corridor and a downed man and the storm still running, and Rook supposed they'd have questions. The questions would take a while to arrive.

"We're taking their VTOL," Rook said. "Boiler flies."

"Obviously," Boiler said, already moving to it.

It was a better aircraft than the maintenance VTOL, and for the ninety seconds it took Boiler to lift it off the deck and climb into the rain, nobody in the cabin said anything — and the nothing wasn't the operational quiet Rook was used to. It was the quiet of five people who had gone into a storm to keep a wet squad from containing a woman, and had, and were all of them still breathing to feel the heater come up. Vox had her bad arm in her lap. Lace had her eyes shut. Ohara sat with the empty case between her boots, its contents already indexed across seventeen repositories before Rotterdam woke, and she was watching the back of Rook's seat with something Rook caught in the canopy glass and had no clean word for — not gratitude, which Ohara would have found imprecise; nearer to the stillness of a person who had run a count for two years and watched it come out with everyone still in the column marked living.

Rook looked at the four of them and the fifth she hadn't planned to keep, and the count her hands did out of habit came up five. And then, because she was tired and the old discipline ran slow when she was tired, it came up five and a woman she had never met — fifties, a liability waiver, the Bloom Quarter — who had been on no one's manifest and in no one's column and had been part of the price anyway. PEREGRINE's count, not hers. She knew the difference. Tonight it had been a near thing, and she didn't pretend otherwise, and she didn't put the woman anywhere — there was nowhere clean to put her, and Rook had stopped looking for one somewhere back in a station bar.

Forty meters below the observation deck, Seun Adalemo's float was keeping him warm enough. He'd be recovered. He would file a report that was largely accurate and changed nothing, because the data was already out — transmitting behind them from a broadcast array indistinguishable from any other unit on a civic science platform, doing exactly what it had been built to do. *It's a cold way to be right,* he'd said. She turned that over and decided she could live with cold. Cold and alive and with everyone she'd brought still breathing in the cabin behind her.

The crew was already half-gone, in the way that counted: the job had made them and the end of it was unmaking them, one closed channel at a time. Rook watched the lake go black beneath the aircraft and let herself feel, for the length of one breath, that she would miss this — not the storm, the people. Then the breath was done and she was a professional again, and that was fine, and that was the job.
