"Where are they," Yemina said.

"I don't know."

The answer came without hesitation, which Yemina noted the way she noted everything: a person who had decided what she didn't know was as deliberate as a person who had decided what she did. Selvamani had stopped pretending to a certainty the instruments couldn't underwrite. That was rarer than competence. Most people, handed two years and a question this size, manufactured an answer to keep the silence from costing them anything.

"I know they went through the schism - the burnout pattern is consistent with weeks of synchronization and then one crossing, not a slow exposure that just wore them out. They were tuned, and then they walked, and then they went through. But every schism I've mapped shows threshold instability - you can't hold one open long enough to follow, and even if you could, there's no guarantee you'd arrive where they did."

"Threshold instability." Yemina turned the phrase over the way she turned over a clause in a contract, looking for the place it had been written to hide. "Meaning the door doesn't stay where the door was."

"Meaning the door isn't a door. I use the word because there isn't a better one. It opens onto a place that doesn't keep its address." Selvamani crossed to the kitchen table and pulled a tablet from under a stack of printouts, the stack settling with the slump of paper that had been lifted and replaced ten thousand times. She turned the tablet to face Yemina.

A GLMZ map. Not the transit-overlay version Yemina pulled forty times a day, the one the districts kept clean for liability - this one had the anomaly layer switched on, the layer the permit offices generated and then suppressed because a flagged schism within a school's catchment radius was a disclosure nobody wanted to file. Across it, markers. Most of them small: the usual scatter of minor anomalies, dozens of them, the kind that turned up in building permits as a variance and in transit displays as a rerouted line and in nobody's investigation as anything at all. Each one logged once, to one authority, and never read beside the others. That was the architecture, Yemina thought. Not a conspiracy. A filing system doing exactly what it was built to do, which was keep each fact in the place where it couldn't testify against the fact in the next place.

She read the map the way she read a ledger - not for the entry that screamed but for the pattern the entries made when you stopped trusting that any of them was an accident. She started counting. The minor markers clustered near schools. Not all schools; she discarded the ones that didn't. She wanted the arrangement to be coincidence, because coincidence closed a case faster than pattern did, and she made herself try to break it before she let herself believe it. She picked three districts that shared no corp contract, no transit corridor, no catchment boundary. The markers held their relationship to the schools anyway. Three districts that had no reason to agree, agreeing.

"How many sites," she said.

"Twenty-two. Across twenty-two months." Selvamani didn't have to look it up. "One to three incidents each. I checked the thresholds first thing - before I let myself believe any of it. Every site's tally sits one short of its own corp contract's alert trigger. Not two short. One." She let that land. "If a single site had lost a fourth child, an automated flag would have generated and a corp would have been obligated to escalate. None of them did. Twenty-two operators, each watching its own number climb to the edge of the line and stop."

Yemina understood the shape of it before Selvamani finished, the way she understood the shape of a misrouted invoice - not as malice, as procedure. To see twenty-two tallies as one number you needed cross-corp clearance, and cross-corp clearance existed at exactly one kind of node: a cross-district contractor whose whole purpose was to look across the lines nobody else was allowed to look across. She was the node. The only one from which the sum was visible at all, and she'd been handed the posting as a demotion, a place to file nothing important until she retired into it.

Something went cold along her forearms, the small involuntary chill of a number resolving into a body. She let it pass. She had a method for what to do with a feeling she couldn't file: you wrote it down, and you kept reading.

At 35th and Halsted, a different marker. Larger. Red.

"Except that one," Selvamani said. "That one has been holding stable at Class-3 for twenty-two months. Most schisms flicker - they open a hair, close, drift a band. You can watch one wander a whole frequency over a week and never sit still long enough to measure twice. This one stopped drifting almost two years ago and has been broadcasting on the same frequency ever since." She didn't touch the red marker. She had the manner of someone who had stopped touching it a long time ago. "Twenty-two months ago is when-"

"When the first child disappeared," Yemina said.

"When the first child disappeared."
