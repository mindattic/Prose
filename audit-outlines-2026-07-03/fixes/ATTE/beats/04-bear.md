She should have left. The transfer was complete, the folder was out of her hands and into a
chain of custody she had no further standing in, and every procedure she had ever followed told
her that a completed handoff was the point at which an investigator withdrew. She stood by the
gray car instead, on the wrong side of a parking restriction, and watched.

Nadia crossed the yard toward a cluster of men working a pallet stack near the second bay, and
Yemina picked him out before Nadia reached him — not hard to pick out, the biggest man on the
floor by a margin, built the way men got built doing twenty years of freight work instead of a
gym membership, a dock coat straining at the shoulders in a way that looked less like the coat
was too small and more like the shoulders had simply outgrown every coat the district issued.
He had a hand-truck tipped back on its wheels and was saying something to the man beside him
that made the other man laugh outright, a real laugh, head back, and the big man grinned at
having caused it, pleased with himself in the uncomplicated way of someone who liked making
people laugh and didn't need credit for it beyond the laugh itself. Someone two pallets over
called something Yemina didn't catch, and he called something back without breaking stride, and
a third voice said, clear across the yard, "You're an idiot, Bear," with real affection in it,
and he took a small mocking bow with the hand-truck still in his grip.

That was the name. Bear. Nobody used another one, or if they did, Yemina didn't hear it in the
sixty seconds she watched him before Nadia reached him.

Nadia didn't wait for a lull. She walked up beside the hand-truck and held the folder out, and
Yemina watched the change happen from thirty feet away, in real time, the way you watch a
temperature drop on an instrument rather than feel it on your skin.

He stopped laughing. Not slowly — between one breath and the next, the way a door closes when
someone on the other side decides it should. He looked at the folder before he looked at
Nadia's face, the barcode catching the gray morning light the same way it had in Yemina's car,
and something crossed his features that Yemina, who had spent fifteen years reading what faces
did when people were told things, recognized immediately and could not have named. Not
surprise. He had been braced for exactly this and unbraced anyway, the specific look of a
man who has been expecting a debt to come due and had let himself hope, in the meantime, that
it wouldn't.

He took the folder in both hands.

The coworker who'd been laughing with him a moment earlier didn't stop moving the pallet. The
man who'd called him an idiot was already three loads down the line, unaware anything had
changed. The yard kept working around the two of them at full volume — a forklift's warning
chime, a compressor cycling, someone shouting a load number twice because the first shout
hadn't carried — and none of it slowed, none of it noticed, because to everyone else in the
yard this was a man taking a piece of paperwork from a courier, the least remarkable transaction
a freight dock produced in a given hour.

Nadia said something to him Yemina was too far away to hear. He nodded, once, without looking
up from the folder in his hands.

Then Nadia turned and walked back across the yard, past the gate, past Yemina, without a
glance in her direction, the canvas bag still held against her chest with both arms, lighter by
one folder and carried exactly the same as before. She went out through a gap in the perimeter
fence Yemina hadn't noticed on the way in, and was gone before Yemina thought to look for where
she'd parked, if she'd driven here at all.

Yemina stayed by the gray car.

She had assumed, standing in this yard forty minutes ago reading a strange notice, that a
records classification requiring physical courier handling meant the folder was going somewhere
more secure than a server — a vault, a sealed archive, some cross-corp facility built for
information too sensitive to risk in the wire. She understood now, watching a big man in a dock
coat stand very still with her report in his hands while pallets moved past him on either side,
that she had built the wrong picture. Something went cold in her then, low and specific, the
cold of a fact arriving in the wrong order. This was not a more secure version of the system.
This was outside the system entirely, wearing the system's clothes long enough to get a case
number through a gate.

The case had left the official channel. She had watched it happen. She had, in fact, handed it
across the threshold herself.
