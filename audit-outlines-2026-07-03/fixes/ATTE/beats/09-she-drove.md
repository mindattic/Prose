The report went into the queue the way every report went into the queue: assigned a docket
position by a system that ranked incoming filings on a schedule nobody had ever shown her,
somewhere behind fourteen other cross-district submissions logged that same morning, ahead of
whatever came in after hers, reordering the stack the way a stack always reorders itself when
one more thing is added to it. She checked, twice over the following week, whether the docket
position had moved. It had, both times — up a little, then back, the ordinary churn of a
system doing what systems did to things that weren't urgent by any measure it recognized. She
found no note attached to it that suggested anyone had read her findings and understood them as
anything other than a truancy report with an unusually thorough chronology. She found no note
suggesting they hadn't.

That was the whole of it. That was as far in as her clearance went, and she had learned, this
month if not before it, exactly where that clearance ended and what came after it — not a wall,
not a locked door, just a locker with no name on it in a freight yard she had no further reason
to visit, and a name, Nadia, that returned nothing when she ran it, quietly, against every
district personnel roster her access allowed, on a night when she told herself she was only
confirming a detail for the file and knew, filling out the search field, that she was doing
something closer to grief.

She did not run the search again after that.

The chalk letters on the sidewalk outside the AAMA building washed away in a rain two days
later, or someone hosed the walk down, or a child came back and dragged a shoe through them on
the way past — she never found out which, because she had no further reason to be on those
steps, and she had stopped, this month, checking on things she had no further reason to check
on. The pattern of twenty-two sites and twenty-two months remained, as far as she knew,
unaggregated anywhere but in her own closed file, a fact that existed and mattered and had
nowhere higher to go, the way most of the true things she'd found in fifteen years had nowhere
higher to go.

She kept driving the seam. The car stayed gray. The cold kept coming off the lake sideways in
March, the way it always had, arriving from a direction the map said it shouldn't, and she felt
it find the gap at the base of the windshield every morning and let her shoulders come up and
put them back down, because it wasn't information she could file and never had been.

Somewhere in a locker in a freight yard, or somewhere past that locker, in a chain of hands she
would never be shown the length of, one folder sat closed, filed by a system that had never once
needed a name for itself, doing whatever it did with the things it decided to keep.

She didn't know where it went.

She had a caseload. She still had one — new names now, new flags, more of them every week than
anyone above her seemed inclined to read together.

She drove.
