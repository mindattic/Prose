The Halsted Freight Yard sat two blocks south of the seam, close enough that the cold off the
lake found it the same way it found everything else in the Veil — sideways, sliding up from the
south where the buildings opened, arriving from the direction the map said it shouldn't.
Yemina had driven this stretch of Halsted a hundred times without once looking at the loading
gates, because loading gates were not, ordinarily, a category of thing her clearance had
business with. This morning Gate 4 had a number stenciled on rolled steel and nothing else to
distinguish it from Gates 1 through 9 — no sign, no directory, no window with a bell to ring.

She parked the gray car across the street, in a loading-restricted zone she had no permit for,
and decided that a folder marked *no substitutions* probably outranked a parking citation, and
got out.

The yard was already working. Half-past five was an hour the freight side of the city treated as
mid-shift, not morning — pallets moving on manual jacks because the automated ones ran the
overnight loop and handed off at seven, two men arguing without heat over whether a crate had
been logged twice, a compressor somewhere cycling on the same rhythm she'd heard from her car a
hundred mornings without listening to it. Nobody looked at her the way people look at someone
who doesn't belong. Nobody looked at her at all. She understood, standing there with a sealed
folder under her arm, that whatever process had routed her here expected exactly this: a person
arriving among people already working, indistinguishable in the gray dawn from anyone else with
a reason to be carrying something.

The woman was standing apart from the loading activity, near the gate's inner edge, in a
district-gray coat with the collar up against the cold. She held a canvas bag against her chest
with both arms, not the way you carry a bag that's heavy, but the way you hold something you're
protecting from a wind — and there was no wind in the yard this morning, the walls broke it,
Yemina had felt the difference the moment she came through the gate. The woman held the bag
that way anyway.

A clipboard hung from a nail beside the gate, the shift manifest, names and load numbers in a
dockhand's shorthand. Yemina read it the way she read every surface with writing on it, a
reflex that had outlived every posting she'd ever held, and found, three lines down, a name
next to a time she recognized: *N. — 0540 — special handling, per instruction.* No last name.
No department code she could place. The woman hadn't clocked anything in, hadn't clocked
anything out. The line existed to account for her presence and told Yemina almost nothing else.

"You're the courier," Yemina said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Nadia." The woman said it the way you hand over the one credential you've been authorized to
hand over, flat, complete, nothing behind it offered voluntarily. Her eyes went to the folder
under Yemina's arm and stayed there.

Yemina held it out. "Sealed. Case classification's on the barcode."

Nadia took it with one hand, freeing the other from the bag just long enough, and for a moment
both objects — the folder and whatever was in the canvas bag — were pressed against her at
once, the folder joining the thing she was already keeping from the wind that wasn't there.

"That's it?" Yemina asked. She had prepared herself, on the drive over, for a form to sign, a
transfer number, some artifact of the exchange that would let her verify later that it had
happened the way it was supposed to happen. There was none of that. There was a name off a
clipboard and a folder changing hands in a freight yard at 5:41 in the morning.

"That's it," Nadia said.

She didn't explain why the notice had sent Yemina here instead of an intake window six blocks
north. She didn't explain what the canvas bag was, or why it mattered to hold both things the
same way. Yemina had interviewed enough reluctant witnesses to know the difference between a
person withholding something and a person who had simply never been given the words for the
thing she was doing — Nadia had the second quality, not the first. Whatever she was, she wasn't
hiding anything from Yemina. She had nothing to hide it with.

"Thank you," Yemina said, because it was the correct thing to say and she had nothing else
that would have been more correct.

Nadia didn't answer that. She turned, and Yemina expected her to walk toward the small office
structure at the yard's far end, the only building with a door that looked like it led to
anything resembling a records room.

She walked the other way.
