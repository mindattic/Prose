The lockers ran along the back wall of the second bay, a dozen of them, gray steel gone the
same accrued color as everything in this part of the city that had stopped being maintained on
purpose and started being maintained by inertia. Half of them had names taped to the front in a
dockhand's marker scrawl, half didn't. He stopped in front of one without a name on it — she
noted that, the way she noted everything, and set it aside without deciding what it meant — and
opened it with a code punched fast enough that she couldn't have read the sequence from thirty
feet even if she'd been trying to.

He set the folder inside. Not shoved, not hidden behind a jacket or a lunch bag the way a man
conceals a thing he's ashamed of having — set flat, the way she set a case file into a drawer
at the end of a day, in a position that suggested he expected to come back to it, or that
someone else did, or both. There was a practiced quality to the motion that told her more than
anything he'd said or hadn't said in the last five minutes: he had done a version of this before.
Not this exact thing, maybe not this exact folder, but the general shape of it — receive, hold,
place, close. His body knew the sequence the way her own body knew the sequence of a form.

He closed the locker. No key turned in it that she could hear. No additional gesture, no
pause, no beat of hesitation before he shut the door — the four minutes had apparently used up
whatever hesitation the moment required, and what remained was administrative, a task completed
in the ordinary tempo of a man with a shift to get back to.

He picked the hand-truck back up. Someone called his name — the real cadence of it this time,
not the nickname, something with more syllables that the yard noise ate before it reached her
clearly enough to keep — and he answered, and his voice had the same warmth in it that it had
carried before Nadia crossed the floor, unbothered, easy, a man who had set something down and
picked his morning back up without apparent difficulty. He didn't look at the locker again. He
didn't look toward the gate again either. Within a minute he was fifteen feet away, laughing at
something the same coworker said, exactly as he had been laughing before any of this happened,
and Yemina understood that whatever this had cost him, it had already been paid, in the four
minutes, and did not carry forward into the rest of his shift.

She looked at the bank of lockers. Twelve doors, gray on gray, one of them unlabeled, and she
had no way, standing where she was standing, to determine which one, and no clearance that
would let her walk across a working freight yard and check, and — she made herself hold this
part steadily, because it was the part that mattered — no plan for what she would do with the
information if she had it. She was not going to break into a locker in a stranger's workplace.
She was not going to interview a man who had, by every visible measure, done nothing but accept
a piece of paperwork from a courier and put it away. There was no protocol violation here she
could document. There was no crime. There was a folder, in a locker, in a freight yard, and a
big man who would deny, correctly and without lying, that anything unusual had happened this
morning, because from where he stood, nothing had.

The case had gone somewhere. She had watched every step of the somewhere with her own eyes and
could not have described, to anyone who asked her to file it, where the somewhere actually was.

She got back in the gray car.
