He didn't open it.

Yemina watched him carry the folder to a spot against the wall of the second bay, out of the
main path of the hand-trucks, and set the hand-truck he'd been using down against the same
wall, and stand there. He held the folder in both hands at chest height, the way you hold a
thing you've been handed at a funeral, and he looked at the barcode on its face, and he did not
break the seal.

Her hand went to her wrist out of a habit she hadn't consciously started, and she was timing
him before she noticed she'd begun. It was the same reflex that had run underneath the entire
case — the flag against the departure, the query latency, the interval between a call placed
and a call answered — the part of her that could not stand in the presence of a duration
without measuring it. She watched the second hand of her own attention move the way she'd
watched every clock in this investigation, and some old, tired part of her recognized what she
was doing and did it anyway, because it was the only instrument she had left that still worked
in a freight yard before six in the morning.

One minute. He hadn't moved. A forklift passed behind him close enough that its mirror nearly
caught his shoulder and he didn't flinch, didn't seem to register it at all, the way a man
doesn't register a fly landing on a hand he's already decided not to move.

Two minutes. His grip hadn't tightened and hadn't loosened. He wasn't gripping it the way a
person grips something they're afraid of dropping. He was holding it the way you hold something
whose weight you have already accepted and are simply carrying for as long as carrying it is
required.

She had expected — she realized this only now, watching him not do it — that a man handed an
unopened case file at a dock gate would want to know what was in it. Curiosity was the ordinary
human response to a sealed thing arriving without explanation. He showed none of it. He wasn't
fighting the urge to look. There was no visible urge. Whatever was being
asked of him, he seemed to have understood it before the folder reached his hands, and the not-
opening wasn't restraint. It was already the whole answer.

Three minutes. A coworker called something to him — a question about a load, ordinary, work
continuing around him with no awareness that anything had stopped — and he answered without
lowering the folder, a short reply, something Yemina couldn't hear, delivered in a voice that
carried none of the strain his stillness suggested. He could still do the job with one hand,
apparently, while the other part of him held this. That was its own kind of information. This
wasn't a man being tested past what he could bear. This was a man doing a familiar thing,
competently, the way she did her own job — the surface unbothered, the cost paid somewhere
underneath it where it didn't show.

She thought, watching him, of the word she'd have used for herself in his position, and the
word was *guilt* — the anticipatory guilt of being handed something you hadn't earned the right
to hold, the itch to justify yourself to it before you'd even opened it. She didn't see that in
him. What she saw instead, and could not have said how she knew it except that she had spent
fifteen years reading what people's bodies did when they thought no one important was watching,
was closer to duty discharged without complaint — a man who had been asked to carry a thing to
a place and had agreed to carry it, and was carrying it, and the four minutes of stillness
wasn't him deciding whether to look. It was him finishing the part of the job that came before
the part where you set it down.

Four minutes, near enough. She didn't check the second hand for the fifth. She let it go.

For the first time in three weeks of gaps — four minutes here, eleven there, every interval in
this case a fact she had logged, cross-referenced, entered into a field with a header and a
column — she did not write the number down. She had no form it belonged on, and for once that
felt less like a limitation of the form and more like the correct shape of the moment: a thing
that happened and was allowed to have happened without becoming evidence of anything.

He straightened. He looked, for just a moment, toward the gate — not at her, she was certain of
that, there was no recognition in it, nothing that suggested he knew a woman by a gray car had
been counting his stillness in seconds — and then he turned and carried the folder toward the
lockers at the back of the bay.
