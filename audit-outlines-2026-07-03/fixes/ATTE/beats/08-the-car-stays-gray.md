The district office at this hour was three people and a bank of terminals nobody had gotten
around to replacing since the last hardware refresh, and nobody looked up when she came in,
which suited her. She sat at the terminal she always used, the one with the sticky shift key
that had annoyed her for two years and that she had stopped requesting a replacement for around
the time she'd stopped believing a facilities ticket did anything but generate a confirmation
number of its own.

She closed Ren's line first. *Vasquez, R. — witness interview, third-row classroom incident —
disposition: cooperative, no further contact required.* True, as far as it went. It said
nothing about a phone call at two in the morning, or a tremor that hadn't stopped in three
weeks, or the word she'd written under his name nine days before any of this and never crossed
out. The form had a field for cooperative. It did not have a field for what it had cost him to
be cooperative, and she had learned a long time ago not to try to force a field to hold more
than it was built for. She entered the disposition and moved to the next line.

Selvamani's consultation closed the same way — *external consult, mechanism inquiry, logged
and closed* — three words standing in for a woman who had spent two years trying to get anyone
with clearance to read a paper nobody had cross-referenced, and who had, for one morning, been
the only person in the district who understood what Yemina was looking at before Yemina fully
understood it herself. The form didn't ask what Selvamani had risked or hoped or given up on.
It asked for a status. Yemina gave it one.

The AAMA voicemail's confirmation number went into the permanent file exactly the way she'd
rehearsed it in the queue, days ago, standing with her back to a building whose lobby wouldn't
take a clean signal. Case numbers. Address. Count. Forty-seven children. She had dictated it
flat and without inflection because a flat voice reading verified numbers was the one kind of
statement the system couldn't file under *distressed caller,* and she pasted the same numbers
into the record now, for the same reason, because a written record was the only kind of
evidence that outlived the person who'd made the call.

She came, last, to the line for Kito Bramley.

She had the cursor in the disposition field for longer than the field usually took her. There
was a temptation, sitting there, to write something that resolved the sentence — *transferred,*
*pending recovery,* even *deceased,* any word with an ending built into it, because a field
that ended was a field a supervisor could close without asking her a question she had no answer
for. She did not have evidence for any of those words. She had a chair in Room 214 that had
stayed empty for three weeks, and a grandmother who had asked her one question in a doorway that
morning and received the truth instead of a kindness, and a dock-yard locker with no name on
the door.

She wrote: *Open. Investigator's clearance exhausted. No further movement available without
escalation this office cannot authorize.*

She read it back once. It was the least satisfying entry she had ever filed and the most
accurate one, and she had learned, over fifteen years, to prefer accuracy in exactly the
proportion that it cost her something to give it.

She logged out. The gray car was where she'd left it in the lot, and it looked, from the
office door, exactly the way it had looked every morning for six years — not painted gray, not
designed gray, the color of a thing that had stopped being argued with, no different this
morning for having spent an hour parked half a block from a freight dock while its owner
watched something she wasn't going to be able to explain to anyone who asked her to. It hadn't
become anything. It was still just the car.

She got in and drove it home, the long way, past the seam, because the seam was hers and driving
it had never once required a reason beyond that.
