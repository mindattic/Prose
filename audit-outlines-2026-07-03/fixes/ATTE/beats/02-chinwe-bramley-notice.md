Chinwe Bramley opened the door before Yemina had finished knocking, the same as the first
time, and Yemina understood, standing on the mat with the gray folder under her arm and a
single-page notice in her hand, that the old woman had probably done that every day since the
first visit — heard a car, heard a step on the stair, opened the door before the person on the
other side of it had decided how to say what they'd come to say.

"You have something," Chinwe said. Not a greeting.

"A status notice. I wanted to bring it myself."

She had thought, driving over, about what she would say if asked what the notice said, and she
had not arrived at anything better than the truth, which was that it said almost nothing. She
held it out. Chinwe didn't take it right away. She looked at the shape of the page instead —
one sheet, official letterhead, a case number in the header — the way you look at an envelope
before you decide whether you have the strength to open it this week or next.

"He's not found," Chinwe said. Not a question.

"No."

"But the case isn't closed."

"No. It stays open." Yemina heard the sentence leave her mouth and heard, underneath it, how
little it did. An open case was not a door. It was a status. It meant a clerk somewhere, some
year, might look at the file again if a new fact arrived to look at it with. It did not mean
anyone was looking now. She had built her whole career on the difference between those two
things and had never before had to say the sentence to someone for whom that difference was the
rest of her life.

Chinwe took the page then. She held it the way she'd held the folder in her lap the first
time, at the edges, like a thing she didn't want to leave a print on.

"I made tea," Chinwe said. "The first time you came. You didn't drink it."

"I remember."

"I won't make it today." It wasn't unkind. It was a fact being filed, the same way Yemina
filed facts, in the order she'd learned them. "I know what a notice like this means. I raised
four children before him and buried one and I know the paper that means something and the
paper that means the county remembered you exist." She folded the page once, along a crease
that wasn't printed on it, the way you fold a thing you intend to keep rather than a thing
you intend to act on. "This is the second kind."

"Yes." Yemina did not soften it. Softening it would have been its own kind of lie, and she had
told this woman exactly one thing in three weeks that was entirely true, which was that she
would come back if she learned anything, and she had come back, and she had nothing.

"Is he suffering." Chinwe asked it the way you ask a question you've already decided you can't
survive a full answer to, in a voice pitched to receive only as much as could be handed over
standing in a doorway.

Something moved in her throat that she did not let reach her face. Yemina thought of a boy who
was not on this page in any way that would help either of them, and of everything she had
learned in three weeks about how a child like him walked out of a room like his — none of which
she was ever going to say in this doorway, not because she'd been ordered not to, but because
there was no version of it that returned him to his grandmother's kitchen a day sooner. She
thought of what her training told her to say, which was that nothing conclusive could be
determined at this time, and of what would be true if she said it plainly, which was that she
did not know, and that not knowing was the most honest thing she had left to give this woman
after three weeks of paperwork that had produced exactly one page.

"I don't know," she said. "I looked as far as I could look, and I don't know."

Chinwe nodded slowly, the way you nod at a scale reading you already suspected. She didn't
thank her. She didn't need to, and Yemina didn't need her to. There was a version of this
where the investigator said something that made the not-knowing survivable. She had never been
able to perform it, and had decided years ago that a real silence was a better gift than a false
comfort dressed as one.

"You'll come back," Chinwe said. "If it changes."

"If it changes."

The door closed the way it had opened — without a sound she could have described afterward,
efficient, a hinge that had been oiled by someone who took care of what was theirs. Yemina
stood on the step a moment longer than the moment required, then went back to the gray car,
where the folder was waiting on the passenger seat exactly where she'd left it, sealed,
unremarkable, the barcode catching the early light at an angle that made it, for a second,
look like the only object in the car that had anywhere left to go.

She checked the clock. 5:11. She had twenty-nine minutes to reach a freight dock she had never
had a reason to visit before this morning.

She drove.
