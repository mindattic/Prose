She didn't go back into the yard. She didn't wait to see whether Nadia reappeared, or whether
the man they called Bear opened the locker again before his shift ended, or whether anyone in a
district-gray coat came to collect what had been left there. She had spent three weeks learning
that the system she worked inside answered to a logic she could document without needing to
understand, and the correct response to that logic, every time she had encountered it in
fifteen years, was not to force it open. Forcing it open was how you lost the thing you were
trying to protect. She put the gray car in gear and pulled out of the loading-restricted zone
before anyone came to ticket it, and drove.

She ran the morning back the way she ran every sequence, in the order it had happened, without
letting herself skip to what it might mean. The notice at 6:02. The dock at 5:40 — she noted,
distantly, that the notice itself had arrived after the appointment it named, a small
administrative impossibility she filed next to the 0.4-second confirmation and the 0.3-second
query, three data points that didn't fit anywhere but fit together, the way three points on a
map that shouldn't share anything in common turn out to share a bearing. Nadia, a first name off
a clipboard, no last name, no department code. A canvas bag held the same way before the folder
joined it as after. A big man, well-liked, laughing before the transfer and laughing after it,
carrying whatever the four minutes had cost him somewhere she couldn't see and wouldn't be shown.
A locker with no name on the door.

She had built her entire career on a single proposition: that careful documentation was the
only lever she controlled. She had used that lever on everything in this case that would hold
still long enough to be levered — the timestamps, the flags, the twenty-two sites nobody but her
had cross-referenced. She had used it, this morning, on a scene she had watched from thirty feet
away with nothing to show for the watching but an accurate memory of what she'd seen and not one
usable fact about what any of it meant.

Her private log — the running file she kept, off the record, cross-referenced to nothing,
existing because some part of her had learned early that a mind needed somewhere to put the
things a report wouldn't take — had headings for almost everything by now. *Noted, not acted
on.* *Protect.* She had used both, over fifteen years, more times than she could count without
looking. She opened a new one this morning, sitting in the gray car half a block from the yard,
because none of the existing ones were honest about what this was.

She wrote: *Unconnected. No further action available at this clearance.*

She closed the file.

The dashboard clock read 6:52. She had a chapter of paperwork left in the day that had nothing
to do with a dock gate or a locker or a woman named Nadia — Ren's interview needed a final
disposition, cooperative, no further contact required; Selvamani's consultation needed to be
logged as external and closed; the AAMA voicemail's confirmation number needed to go into the
permanent file so that if anyone ever asked whether she'd notified the authority of record, the
answer would be documented and true. She had built her whole method on finishing what she
started. She was not going to stop finishing things this morning because one part of the case
had ended somewhere she couldn't follow.

She drove the seam back toward the district office, and the cold came off the lake sideways the
same as it always did, and found the gap at the base of the windshield the same as it always
did, and she let her shoulders come up a half-inch and then put them back down, because the cold
wasn't information she could file, and the heater would catch up at the next light or it
wouldn't.
