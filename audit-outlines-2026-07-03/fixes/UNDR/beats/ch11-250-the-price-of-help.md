## Synopsis

Glim and Knuckle go up to the Skin-border meeting point to collect the fever medicine the Mission
promised. The field administrator will not release it without first scanning Slip into the
Mission's intake system — a genuine procedural requirement on her end, not a trick, not cruelty —
and holds firm even once she understands a child is actively febrile three levels down. Knuckle and
Glim have no way to satisfy the requirement without bringing Slip to the Skin, which they refuse to
do. The exchange costs real minutes before Noor, arriving late, forces a resolution by falsifying
the intake herself. This is the Mission's rung of the antagonist ladder finally doing damage: not
malice, but an unbudging kindness that nearly kills the person it means to save.

## Full draft prose

The meeting point was a service grating two levels below the Skin, a place the Underclan and the surface had apparently agreed on without either side quite admitting they'd agreed on anything. Knuckle chose it. Glim didn't ask how Knuckle knew where the Mission would be waiting; there were things Knuckle had arranged in the three days since the first Lamplighter raid that he hadn't explained to anyone, and this was evidently one of them.

The woman at the grating had a case in one hand and a flat surface-device in the other, and she introduced herself with a title Glim didn't retain and a name he did: Adaeze. She had the particular calm of someone whose calm was a professional tool, deployed the same way regardless of what was in front of her.

"You're here for the pediatric fever kit," she said. "I need the patient's intake first. It's a five-minute process. Retinal capture, basic vitals if you can get them to me, a name if he has one he'll give."

"He's three levels down," Knuckle said. "He can't come up. He can't be moved."

"I understand that's difficult." Adaeze's voice didn't change. "But I can't release medication to an unregistered minor without documented need. It's not policy I have discretion over — it protects the child. If something goes wrong with the dosage, if there's a reaction, we need to know who we treated and be able to follow up. I've seen what happens when well-meaning people administer medicine without a record. It doesn't end well for the child."

"He is dying while you talk about records," Glim said. He was aware, saying it, that his surface words were coming out flatter and harder than he meant them, the vocabulary too small for what he was trying to carry in it.

"I hear you," Adaeze said, and the terrible thing was that she did — her face did the thing surface faces did when they meant something, the crease at the eyes, the forward lean. "I'm not trying to make this harder than it has to be. I need five minutes of information. That's the whole of what stands between you and this case." She held it up, slightly, the way you'd show a child a treat to prove it was real. "I can't lower that bar. If I do it for you, I have to do it for everyone, and then the whole safety framework means nothing, and next time it's a child who needed the framework and didn't have it."

Knuckle's hands had gone very still at his sides, the stillness Glim had learned to read as the loudest thing about him.

"Bring the case down," Knuckle said. "Register him where he's lying. Your five minutes, in his tunnel."

"I'm not authorized to descend past the Skin-border without an escort clearance, and that takes —"

"How long."

"Days, typically. I can request an expedite."

Glim felt something behind his sternum pull tight, like a valve seized shut. He counted it the way he counted everything that mattered: Slip did not have days. Slip might not have the hour this conversation was already costing.

It was Noor who broke it, arriving at a run with her chest heaving, having been told by Sorrel where they'd gone. She didn't argue with Adaeze. She took the device out of the woman's hand — not roughly, but with the specific speed of someone who has decided the cost of being wrong is smaller than the cost of waiting — and thumbed through three screens with the fluency of someone who had spent a career inside exactly this kind of system, and entered a false intake under her own authorization code, backdated, her name standing surety for a boy she had met twice.

"That's a violation," Adaeze said, quietly, and did not take the device back.

"File it," Noor said. "File it against me. Give him the case."

Adaeze looked at her for a long moment — not hostile, Glim understood, watching her face the way he watched everything: something closer to grief, the specific grief of a person who believes completely in a system that has just failed in front of her and cannot see any way the system was wrong to fail. She handed over the case.

They ran the whole way back down. It was not enough time. It had never been going to be enough time; Glim understood that even as he ran, even before he knew it for certain. But he carried the case himself, and he did not let himself think about the minutes at the grating until much later, when there was nothing left to do with the thought except hold it.
