## Synopsis

Unchanged in structure and function — Glim's morning air-check, candle-count, the routine of the
Hollow. The only change: one of the two unnamed elders at the chemical-heat stone is now named
Leaf, with one small, specific, load-bearing detail of her own, so that when she dies in the Ch10
raid the reader has met her.

## Full draft prose

Glim knew the hour by the feel of the air on his forearm.

Cool from the right. No smoke, no sharpness. He lay still on the fungus-hide shelf and let the air tell him what it knew. Right-hand tunnel: the Vent Cog's morning work, same as every fourth sleep without fail — the same wheel, Glim imagined, the same rusted quarter-turn. He had never been late. The air from his direction arrived like a promise renewed.

The Underclan had a word for that quality of predictable return. It was the same word they used for the Deep One's voice.

He sat up slowly, reaching for the candle before he reached for anything else. The glow-rat was already awake — it had heard him move, or felt him, and it came to his hand with the purposeful nuzzle of a small animal that had its own schedule. He held it up and read its breathing in the light it gave off: steady, unhurried, the bioluminescence clean and consistent. The air was good. It was morning.

Across the Hollow, two of the elders were already at the chemical-heat stone, pressing dried fungus-meat against it in the methodical way of people who have done the same work for thirty years and see no reason to alter the approach. One of them was Leaf, who had tended that stone longer than Glim had been alive and who pressed the meat in a rhythm nobody had taught her and nobody else could match — three presses, a turn, three presses — so that Braves coming off a long harvest could find the heat-stone in the dark by the sound of her hands alone, before they ever saw the glow. She hummed while she worked, low enough that it was closer to breath than to music, a habit the Hollow had stopped noticing the way it stopped noticing its own hum. The smell of the meat reached him: familiar, faintly sweet, a smell that meant home the way the Current's voice meant depth.

He set the candle in its dish and went to check the others.

Seven candles in the common space. The girl called Lark, who had taken the night watch, had fallen asleep against the far wall with her candle balanced on her knees. He crouched beside it. Steady breathing, clean light. He didn't wake her. He counted: all seven, plus his own, plus the two sentinel candles at the tunnel mouths. Ten candles breathing. The Hollow was alive.

He passed the heat-stone on his way out and Leaf caught his sleeve without looking up from the meat.

"You're thinking about the Surfacing already," she said. Not a question. She had raised half the Braves currently walking the Hollow, in the sense that mattered — fed them, warmed them, corrected their harvest-grip when they were too young to hold a shelf-tool properly — and she had opinions about all of them that she rarely spent on speech.

"Twelve cycles," he said.

"I know how to count." She turned the meat. Three presses, a turn. "You'll do it the way you do everything. Carefully, and then not carefully enough, and then it'll turn out you were fine the whole time." She said it without looking at him, the way she said most true things, and held out a strip of the meat, still too hot to eat, so that he had to juggle it between his palms on the way out, which was — he understood only later, laughing about it with Sorrel — exactly the point.

He made the three-finger sign to no one in particular, which was also to everyone, which was also to the habit of making it — a daily accounting, given and received. The air is well. I checked and came back. The Current breathes today.

He had work to do. He went.
