## Full draft prose

On the fourth day the surface learned the legend was real.

He understood this because the small flat surface-device that Noor carried and communicated through — he had watched her use it; it carried voice and image and appeared to be some form of tap-speech made elaborate and visual — began showing images of his own face.

Not accurately. The image was a surface drawing, an approximation, which told him the surface had seen him and had tried to reproduce what it had seen for distribution. He recognized the approximation as himself only because he had seen his own reflection in the cistern's still water at the right angle and the right light, which was rarely, but enough times. The drawing was not exact but it was close enough.

The legend is true.

He watched Noor's reaction as she looked at the device, and at the drawing, and at him, and at the device again. Her face was doing many things at once — too many to follow at the same moment. What he caught was this: the quality of a person who has been handed a problem they did not make and did not want.

He understood that quality. He felt it often.

The device showed a second image: a place. Not Homewater — he would have known Homewater by smell even in a flat image, and this was different. But underground. The image had the quality of the deep: the arched ceilings, the brick-and-tile of the old infrastructure, the low light. Someone had been down there taking images, and now the surface was looking at them.

He looked at the image for a long time. The angle was wrong for a person — too low, too steady, a viewpoint with no human body behind it. He didn't know the word for what had done this. Noor, later, would tell him: an Exo's crawler, sent ahead of the hunt, inhabiting the machine while the Exo's own body waited somewhere above. The crawler had moved through the tunnels in the dark and the Exo had looked through its eyes and the surface had written down the way down.

Someone had written down the way down.

He held this.

He couldn't tell the clan. He was on the surface. He was in Noor's space with Noor's locked door between him and the street, and the street above the Works, and the Works above Homewater. In the time it would take him to return and report, the surface would have the recordings and they would contain something that could be followed. The clan didn't know this.

He kept this separate, stored apart from the rest: the surface had machines that could see in the dark.

He breathed. He watched Noor, who was still watching the device, and then watching him, and doing the thing she did when she was calculating something she didn't have enough information to resolve.

He wanted to go home.

He had not let himself think this plainly until now. He had been sorting and assessing and staying because the clan needed what he was learning and leaving would close that information channel permanently. But the information had now changed. The question was no longer what he could learn by staying. The question was what would reach Homewater first — him, or the people who had now written down the way down.

He did not yet know that Noor, looking at the same device, was doing the same reckoning, and arriving at the same conclusion, and that her conclusion would complicate his exit in ways he couldn't yet account for.

He stayed still. He looked at the image of the tunnels on the surface device — his tunnels, his architecture, his dark — and he held it the way you held a stone lifted too soon from the heat-stone: briefly, with your whole attention, until you had what you needed from it.

Then he put it away in the place where the body kept things that needed a response.

He was already thinking about the route back.
