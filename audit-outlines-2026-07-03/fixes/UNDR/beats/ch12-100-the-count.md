## Full draft prose

The Tartarian Empire was beautiful.

This was the first thing and the wrong thing — not that beauty was wrong, but that he hadn't expected it, and the wrongness of expectation in a place of genuine danger was itself a danger. The ceilings vaulted high above them, and the walls were tiled to the waterline with glazed work: faces and shapes and patterns in colours the candle-light kept changing. The bioluminescence moved through the space and the tiles threw it back from a hundred angles and the Tartar glowed.

He checked the candle. Fine. The air was cold and still and very old but it was good air.

He needed everyone to know the air was good. He made the three-finger sign — flat palm, three fingers extended toward the ceiling, the signal for the air is well, breathe normally — and watched it pass backward through the chain of people behind him: each person receiving it, registering it, passing it to the next.

He counted everyone. It was the thing he did after movement, the thing he had done every arrival since before he could remember doing it on purpose: confirm what you have.

Forty-one people breathing, plus the candles. He counted again, the way you counted a thing twice when the first count gave you a number the body didn't want.

Forty-one. He knew, before he let himself know it in the part of the mind that assembled words, whose absence made the number what it was. He did not say her name into the vaulted hall. He made himself set the count down the way he set down anything too heavy to carry while moving: in the place below the mind, for later, where the fragments of his own beginning had always waited.

He sat down on the tile and pressed his palms flat against it and tried to feel the hum.

It was not there.

He pressed harder. The tile was cold under his palms — colder than the tile in the Old Deep, colder than the ferrocrete of the tunnels above. Cold the way stone was cold when it had been sealed from the Warm for a very long time, when the thermocline's reach had never arrived here, when the tokamak's breath had dispersed in the levels above and left nothing to warm what was below. The cold went through his palms and up his wrists and he pressed harder and there was still nothing.

The Current was silent here. Had always been silent here.

He had understood the taboo as superstition. Not with contempt — the tribe's taboos had practical foundations, always: the air that killed, the routes that flooded, the old deep that held things the tribe couldn't afford to learn at cost. He had thought the Tartar-fear was the same: a practical danger translated into sacred language because practical language was harder to enforce. He had understood this as the tribe's method, and he had respected it as their method, without quite believing the ghosts. He believed something closer to it now, sitting a stone's throw from the line Sorrel would not cross, though he could not yet have said what, precisely, he had come to believe.

The tribe was watching him. He rose from the tile. He made the sign for *breathe*, then *rest*, then *wait*. They sat.

He walked to the far wall and pressed his palm flat against the tile — a face in glazed ceramic, ancient beyond counting, looking out from the wall with the unconcerned gaze of something that had been looked at long enough that it had stopped noticing. The tile was cold under his palm. The face looked through him.

He had brought the surface down. The Tartar, and what it had already cost to reach, was the consequence. He would not number that cost while people were watching him decide.

He turned back to the clan.

"The air is good," he said, quietly, in the voice that carried just far enough. "The cold is bad but manageable. We will rest here." He looked at each face — tired, frightened, watching him the way candles watched bad air. He did not look for the one face that would not be among them. "We will find what we need and we will decide what comes next. But the air is good. We are breathing."

He sat down on the tile again and put his palms back on the cold and waited, with all forty-one people around him, for the Current to confirm or deny that this was survivable.

It confirmed nothing.

He breathed anyway.
