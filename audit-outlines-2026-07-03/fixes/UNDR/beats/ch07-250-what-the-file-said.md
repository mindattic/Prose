doorway). Supplies the causal chain the audit found missing: a caseworker who has kept Noor's
twelve-year-old missing-persons file open notices two physical markers from intake photographs of

allowed near him. This is complementary to, not a duplicate of, Glim's smell-based recognition in

## Full draft prose

[Surface POV — Noor]

The woman who called herself Odalys had kept Noor's file active for eleven years past the point where the department stopped requiring it. Noor knew this because Odalys had told her once, years ago, over a cup of something neither of them finished: that most files closed themselves, administratively, when the algorithms decided the probability curve had gone flat enough to stop paying a person to watch it. Noor's hadn't closed. Odalys had kept renewing it by hand, a small act of stubbornness that Noor had never known how to thank her for properly, because thanking her for it made it real in a way that was easier to leave unsaid.

The call came at eleven at night. Odalys didn't apologize for the hour. She said, "I need you to look at three images. Don't hope yet. Just look."

The first was a static frame pulled from a hunting outfit's own recovery footage — grainy, lit wrong, a boy on his knees with his hands zip-fastened and his face turned three-quarters from the lens. Noor's stomach did the thing it always did when she was shown a possible boy: a lurch that arrived and then corrected itself, because she had been shown boys before, a dozen times in twelve years, and none of them had been hers, and the body had learned distrust faster than the heart could manage it.

The second image was cropped tight on his left eyebrow. A thin scar cut through the outer third of it, pale against the skin, the kind of mark a child got from a hard corner met at speed. Noor's own boy had earned an identical one at two and a half, falling into the edge of a low table, and she had held a cold cloth to it while he screamed and she had photographed it healing, badly, on the terrible little device she'd carried back then, because some instinct in her had wanted the scar recorded even before she understood why a mother records anything.

The third image was the crown of his head, where a fall of hair refused to lie the way the rest of it lay — a cowlick that swirled counter to the grain, stubborn since infancy, the one thing about him that no comb had ever won against.

"The eyebrow could be coincidence," Odalys said. "Scars like that aren't rare. But the cowlick's growth pattern — I pulled your intake description from twelve years ago, word for word, before I looked at this footage. You wrote 'grows backward, top left, won't sit flat no matter what.' That's not a common enough phrase for me to believe you're describing two different heads."

Noor didn't answer for a long moment. She was looking at a photograph of a stranger's scalp and trying to decide whether she was allowed to feel what she was already feeling.

"I'm not promising you anything," Odalys said. "There'll be a formal match process. Records, if they still exist for him, which they may not, given where he's apparently been. But I've done this a long time, and I've learned when to make the call at eleven at night instead of waiting for the paperwork to catch up to what I already believe. I believe this is him."

"Where is he."

"Custody transfer's in progress. He'll be brought to a secondary facility, not the first one — the recovery team's methods aren't something we advertise." A pause, careful. "He hasn't spoken. Not one word, in either direction. They don't know if he can't or won't."

Noor sat with the phone against her ear and the three images open on the table in front of her — a stranger's kneeling shape, a stranger's eyebrow, a stranger's stubborn hair — and let herself, for the first time in eleven years of files that closed themselves and hers that hadn't, believe a caseworker's eleven o'clock certainty over her own body's practiced distrust.

She was at the facility before dawn. She did not touch him. She stood in a doorway and looked at a boy who did not look back, and before she understood anything else about him, she understood the eyebrow and the crown of his head, because she had looked for exactly those two things, specifically, for twelve years, in every child's face the surface had ever shown her by mistake.

This time they were correct.
