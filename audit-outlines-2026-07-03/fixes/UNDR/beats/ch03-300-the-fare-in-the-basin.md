the Oarsman's Fare-basin. Removes the audit-flagged contradiction (4781 explicitly states there is

pull toward the object as bodily and inexplicable — consistent with the register's "body precedes

## Full draft prose

There was a crossing — he had been fifteen or sixteen, old enough to go alone — when the candle in the vessel burned at a different angle than usual, the flame longer and straighter in the stillness of the black water, and the basin lit in a way he hadn't seen before.

He had perhaps ten seconds.

The vessel was in the middle of the crossing. The Oarsman was moving. He'd learned not to watch it move — the look it had in motion worked on something behind the eyes, something that wasn't fear but traveled in fear's company. He looked at the basin instead, and used the ten seconds.

He knew what the basin held. Every crossing taught you: the corroded metal of the sealed-off city at the bottom, the layered record of everything the Underclan and their predecessors had given the crossing since before anyone could remember. What he hadn't understood until Knuckle explained it to him once, in passing, the way Knuckle explained the mechanics of things he'd stopped finding strange: the visible layer didn't simply grow forever. Something in the Oarsman's working — a shift, a settling, a mechanism no one alive had ever seen operate — took the older offerings down, sooner or later, into a deeper hold the crossing never showed you twice. What sat on top at any given moment was recent. What sat beneath it was gone from sight, not gone from the world. You didn't touch the basin's contents. The law on this was absolute and unwritten, which meant it held the way only unwritten laws held: completely and without the possibility of arguing about the wording.

He wasn't looking at any of that tonight. Tonight the visible layer had been disturbed somehow — a listing of the hull, a settling further down that had pushed something old back up near the top. Partly hidden behind a dried fungus-cap three offerings newer lay a flat object on a cord, one he'd caught the shape of before in less light, from worse angles. This time the angle was right and the flame was right and he had ten seconds of clear view.

There was writing on it he couldn't read, and above the writing, a face.

It was small and pale, with large dark eyes — the kind of dark that pulled light in rather than reflecting it.

His hand was on the vessel's rail. He wasn't reaching for the basin — the law was the law — but his weight had shifted without his permission, the body leaning toward the light the way it leaned toward warmth. He didn't know the face. He was certain, in the specific way the body is certain of things the mind hasn't caught up to, that it wasn't nothing to him — that the pull was real even without a name to hang it on.

Then the vessel moved, and the angle was gone, and the candle was the candle again.

He sat back. He hadn't touched the basin. That was the important thing. He hadn't touched the basin.

He told no one. You did not speak of what you saw in the basin. The Oarsman's fares were the crossing's property; observation was permitted, speech was not. He had looked, and he had seen, and the seeing had done something to him he wasn't yet done accounting for.

He didn't try to make the face into the hand and the noise and the smell that lived in him from before Homewater. He had learned, from the fragments themselves, that reaching for them scattered them, and this was the same kind of reaching. There was no face in what he carried from before — he knew that plainly, had known it since the telling stirred the fragments loose and left only the hand's grip and the smell with no name where a face should have been. This was a different thing: an object, a stranger's face, a pull in the body with no story attached to it yet. He let it be what it was, unexplained, rather than force it into the shape of what he already carried.

He kept it the way the Underclan kept all things with no immediate use: stored, not abandoned; present, not handled; in the dark where things waited until the time was right.

He had been carrying the memory of that object for four years by the time Knuckle called the Surfacing. And he had never once told anyone, because telling would make it a question, and he did not know if he was ready for a question with no answer waiting behind it.
