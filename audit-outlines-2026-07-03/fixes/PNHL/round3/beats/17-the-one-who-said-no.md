## Synopsis

Placed between Arcturus Civil Security (1075) and What Staying Requires (1100) — the last beat of
her investigative research before the decision-and-preparation beats begin. She goes back to the
one thread from West Town she hasn't pulled: the market kid's story about a fellow tech, "Cotter,"
who said no to the Assessor and "left." Expecting to confirm ordinary weather, she instead finds
three checkable administrative facts that don't add up to a voluntary departure: a calibration
bench sold at a loss through the Assessor's own holding-company chain, a transit manifest paid for
by a stranger, and reputation-destroying incompletes filed against Cotter's handle after he was
already gone. No violence, no body — the horror is that the erasure is invisible, indistinguishable
from the truth unless someone checks it the way she just did. She ends the beat checking her own
exposure in the same systems. Feeds directly into the Confrontation patch (1200), where this
becomes leverage.

## Text

The ACS ticket had closed nothing, but it reminded her what she was actually good at: checking
things everyone else had already decided didn't need checking.

She went back to the one thread from West Town she hadn't pulled. The market kid's story, tossed
off between customers — a guy he used to trade parts with, who got the assessment and said no.
Booked out east inside the week. Somebody saw the manifest, his name right there on it. Weather,
the kid had called it without calling it that, and weather was exactly the kind of information
nobody thought to check. She checked it anyway, the way she checked everything she touched — a
habit that had never once cost her anything for being thorough.

Finding the handle took most of an evening: cross the parts-trading boards against six weeks of
gray-zone job postings, narrow by specialty, narrow again by the shape of silence that follows a
name that's stopped posting. The handle was Cotter. Calibration and coupling work, mostly — the
kind of trade she'd have swapped parts with herself if she'd been here eight months longer instead
of two.

Cotter had owned a bench. Registered gray-zone equipment carried liens the same as anything
financed, and Cotter's — a real asset, new enough to still be worth something — sold at a loss of
2,200Φ against its own registered valuation, four days after the assessment date, routed through a
holding-company chain she already recognized. She'd traced its far end once before, to a
restaurant reservation and a fifth-floor office with no name on the directory. A person leaving
for a new city sells a bench he can't carry. That much made sense on its own. She kept going
because one data point lining up with a chain she already knew the shape of wasn't a coincidence
she trusted.

The transit manifest was the second point, and it was the one that made her reread the line twice
before she believed it. The ticket east had been paid for — not from any account of Cotter's, which
showed no unusual withdrawal anywhere near the departure date, but by a third-party credential
that had never, in two years of visible transaction history, touched anything connected to Cotter
at all. No shared job. No payment either direction, ever. A stranger had bought a man a one-way
ticket out of a district he'd worked for two years, and the manifest wore Cotter's name like a
label addressed by somebody else's hand.

The third point was the quietest and did the most work. In the five weeks after the departure
date, four incompletes landed on Cotter's gray-zone account — jobs marked started-and-abandoned,
each one timestamped for a date after the manifest, each one worded in the flat procedural
language of a platform that never asked who had actually typed it. Nobody audited a departed
specialist's abandoned jobs. That was the whole design. The incompletes weren't punishment for
leaving. They were insurance against ever coming back — small, boring, permanently attached facts
that would make any future employer pass on Cotter without a second thought, forever, with no one
having laid a hand on him to make it true.

The cold reached her before the words did. It settled low in her stomach, the way it had the few
times a rig was seconds from failing and her hands knew it a half-step ahead of the readout — the
body arriving at the conclusion before the part of her that would have to prove it caught up.

Then she made herself put it into words, because words were the only thing she could hold up to
anyone else. Nothing here was violence, not in any sense a report could take hold of. Nobody had
hurt Cotter, as far as any record showed. Cotter had simply left — sold his equipment, bought his
ticket, stopped showing up — exactly the way the kid had described it, the way weather describes
itself. What she kept coming back to wasn't a cover story built to survive a glance. It was one
built to survive her kind of look, from the visible to the inference, and it had very nearly held.

The kid hadn't lied to her. The kid had repeated, faithfully and without malice, exactly what the
record was built to make him believe. Three separate systems, three separate administrative facts,
none of them false enough on its own to flag, all of them true enough together to hold up a
departure that had never happened the way it read. "The ones who say no mostly just leave" wasn't
street wisdom passed down from something that happened once. It was product, manufactured with the
same care as the ticket and the bench and the four incompletes, circulating exactly as designed,
mouth to mouth, becoming folklore because the paperwork underneath it never gave anyone a reason
to doubt it.

She logged Cotter's name and the three points under it, dated and sourced, the way she logged
anything she meant to be able to prove later. She was slow over the entry, and it wasn't fear of
the Assessor that made her slow — she'd priced him in weeks ago. It was the plain new fact of what
one man could do to another with nothing but paperwork, and how nearly it had worked without a
single thing about it looking wrong.

Without quite deciding to, she pulled up her own account next — the job history, the reputation
ledger, the two incompletes she'd genuinely earned and the clean record around them — and checked,
line by line, how much of it lived in systems she didn't personally hold the keys to.

Most of it did.
