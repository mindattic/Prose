The building had been empty long enough that it had developed a sound. Not the sound of settling — buildings that settle do it in the first decade, working out the disagreement between the load they were built for and the load they were given. This one had finished settling sometime around 2218 and had arrived at a steady state of accommodation: the floors sagging precisely where they had decided to sag, the stairwell junction doing its small perpetual work of load transfer around the damaged timber, the west room on the second floor holding still in the specific stillness of something that hadn't been asked to carry anything since the last thing was removed. It was the sound of a place that had been left alone for exactly the right amount of time.

Lace had been in first, as arranged. Rook had watched her go into the building two hours before the rest of them, the cover story already running, Lace carrying a utility transit bag that looked right for the cover and contained nothing useful for it and everything useful for a social engineer managing a building for a night. She didn't emerge. She sent one ping on the channel — not words, just presence, the signal that meant arrived and clear — and then went quiet. That was the last Rook heard from her until the crew arrived and found the west room on the second floor prepared: the compromised patches in the floor marked with tape she'd sourced from somewhere in the building's old maintenance supplies, the two flat surfaces Stave had specified leveled and shimmed with wedges cut from the door frame of a closet that hadn't been in use since the building was condemned. The work was clean. She had found the materials in the building itself, which meant she had spent her two hours learning the building's inventory rather than bringing her own.

Stave went through the room twice without speaking, running calibrations on the rig and its interface, checking the shimmed surfaces with a small level Rook hadn't seen them produce until it appeared in their hand, checking again. The calibration took thirty-two minutes. When they finished they said nothing, but they stopped moving, which was the difference. The rig was ready when Stave was still.

Ohara arrived with Vox, who had walked the approach route three times in two days and had it memorized at the level of body knowledge — she moved through the building without looking at the stairs, knew the bad step on the second floor landing by the sound it made and stepped past it before it had time to make it. Ohara moved through behind her with the careful attention of someone who was counting everything they stepped on, which was the correct approach for a building you'd never been in. She arrived at the door of the west room and stopped at the threshold. Not hesitation — inventory. She looked at the rig, then at Stave, then at the two flat surfaces, then at Stave again, and Rook watched her run the position and arrive at the same answer Stave had given in the transformer room: survivable, probably.

"Sit down," Stave said. They said it the way they said everything — flat, without modulation, with the specific quality of someone who had decided sentiment was a variable they couldn't account for in the procedure. "Not in the chair. On the board." They indicated the second flat surface, the one that wasn't the rig's interface. "I need your head level with the interface during the approach. If you're in a chair I'll be compensating for angle drift every ninety seconds and I can't do that and read the wall at the same time."

Ohara sat on the board the way she did everything in the careful room — with precise economy, weight shifted forward slightly, back straight, knees together. The posture of someone who had spent four years learning to take up exactly as much space as required and no more.

"Rook." Stave didn't look up from the rig's interface. "I need everyone not directly involved in the procedure outside the room. The rig is sensitive to electrical interference, which means phones, comms, and anything with a battery that isn't mine needs to be outside the wall."

"Scout's on the stairwell," Rook said. "Boiler's on the ground floor."

"Vox?"

Vox was already in the hall, leaning against the corridor wall with her eyes closed and all four channels pointed outward, building a live map of the block's signal environment one packet at a time. She didn't open her eyes. "I'm not in the room," she said.

Rook moved to the doorway. Not in the room, not yet out of it — in the threshold, which was the position she kept on jobs: close enough to be called back, far enough to have the full picture. She looked at Ohara on the board, at Stave setting the rig's interface with both hands, at the shimmed surfaces and the marked floor and the work Lace had done in two hours inside an abandoned building with materials it had kept for exactly this use without knowing it.

"If it goes wrong," she said. Not a question.

Stave's hands didn't stop moving. "If it goes wrong, it will go wrong in one of three ways. The first is recoverable. The second is recoverable if I know within the first ninety seconds that it's going wrong. The third I'll know immediately because Ohara will go still, and if Ohara goes still we stop and I spend the next hour on the recovery protocol and you do not interrupt me while I run it." They set the last calibration and stepped back from the interface. "I know all three ways. I've thought about the third one continuously for two years. I know what to do."

Rook looked at Ohara. Ohara looked at Rook.

"I know what to do too," Ohara said. "I've had four years to think about being very still."

Stave made an adjustment to the interface that wasn't on the calibration list — a personal habit, the kind of small modification a person made to equipment they'd been running for years to correct for a drift they'd stopped trying to explain because explaining it would mean explaining when they'd learned it. They made the adjustment the way Boiler ran a thumb along a load path: the touch of someone who was checking something only they had the skill to feel.

Outside the door, Rook moved into the hall. She put her back against the wall beside Vox and found her own still.

"Any contact on the approach vector?" she said.

"Not yet." Vox's eyes stayed closed. "There will be."

"How long?"

"Not long." She didn't open her eyes. "But long enough."

Rook stood in the hall of the condemned building and counted the sounds the building made — the stairwell joint, the sag in the floor above, the silence of the west room where nothing was moving except the rig and the two people who were very deliberately not moving. She had done this before, the counting at a threshold. The count was the only instrument she trusted in a moment that didn't yet have a number.
