Ohara came last, because Ohara had asked, and someone who asks for a table does not sit at it before everyone else has decided to. She took the chair across from Rook, set her hands flat on the wood the way Rook had taught all four of them to do in a room they didn't trust — palms down, fingers spread, nothing of yours under the table where it can't be seen — and laid out the shape of it without preamble, which was the only mercy she had left to offer. She was thinner than the lake had left her. The carefulness she'd been living inside for two years had gotten into her hands; they didn't shake, and the not-shaking was a thing she was doing on purpose, the way a person holds a full glass level across a crowded room.

"The partition's failing," she said. "The one we left up. Not failed — failing. There's a difference and the difference is the only reason I used your tile instead of running."

*She says "we" like it's load-bearing. She knows it is.*

"When I sealed it eighteen months ago I left a degradation curve so it would die quiet," Ohara went on, turning a salt cellar a quarter-turn with one finger, not looking at it, giving her eyes somewhere to be that wasn't anyone's face. "Slow corrosion across the gate hardware. Eighteen months to brick, give or take. Clean. No alarm, no inheritance, no question of what was sealed behind it." She let that sit a beat — the arithmetic of it, the fact that eighteen months had, in fact, now passed. "It should have bricked itself by now. That's what I built it to do. I checked the curve last week out of a habit I should have broken." Quarter-turn. "It's not corroding on my numbers. It's not corroding at all. Something's pulling current off the gate to keep itself warm past its own deadline. Drawing maintenance power through a partition that is supposed to be a tombstone, and a tombstone does not get cold feet." She stopped turning the cellar. Set it down square, the way you set down a thing you've decided not to fidget with anymore. "There's something on the other side of the wall I built, and it has been spending the wall's own power to outlive the schedule I gave it, and I don't know how much longer that buys it. I stopped trusting the maybe two days ago."

She said all of it in one register, level and unhurried, the sentences laid end to end like a checklist someone had read aloud to an empty room until the words went smooth and lost their teeth. That was the part that landed wrong. Not the content — the delivery. *She has rehearsed the worst day of her life until it sounds like a maintenance report.* That wasn't calm. That was a person who had stood in front of the same fear long enough to take it apart and reassemble it in a register that couldn't hurt her. It could still hurt everyone else.

And the table heard it, each of them in their own frequency, because Rook had built a crew of people who didn't process the same room the same way and that had always been the point.

Boiler took it as load. His broad hand went flat on the wood the way it had gone flat on the cracked column an hour ago, palm reading for the line of failure, and his cheerful went out of him all at once. He didn't ask a question. Men who read columns for a living know what a thing that's holding on pride looks like from the inside, and he had just been told that one of them was a person, and that the person was sitting across the table turning a salt cellar.

Scout heard the word *warm* and came most of the way back into her own eyes for it. She'd been half-gone into the overlay even here, the rooftop in her never quite coming inside, one foot on the parapet the way she always kept one — but *something pulling current to stay warm past its own deadline* was a thing that wanted feeding, and a thing that wanted feeding was a thing that was, by her count, alive enough to matter. She didn't say it. She just stopped looking at the vectors of the room and looked, briefly, at the wall, the literal wall behind Ohara, as if the partition were a thing you could find an angle on.

Lace read the faces. That was what Lace did with horror — she watched it travel. She'd come in out of the rain an hour ago and run the room for exits and authority and threshold, and now she ran it again, slower, watching Boiler's hand go flat and Scout come back from the parapet and Rook not move at all, and what she locked in this time she didn't lock away. Something in her jaw set. She had known a piece of this since the safe house, carried it the whole time like a stone in a coat she'd forgotten she was still wearing, and Rook saw the weight shift in her and shift back, held a moment longer, and not come out. *Not yet,* the look said. *But it's getting heavy where she's holding it.*

Four reasons, all of them Rook's, none of them said.

*She built it to die on schedule and it decided to keep living past the schedule, and she came to the one person who told her in 2206 that you cannot seal a thing you do not understand — you can only delay the conversation. This is the conversation.*

"You don't know what's in there," Rook said.

It wasn't a question and she didn't attribute it to Ohara; she attributed it to the table, to all of them, to the fact that not one of them had ever opened the door they'd spent six weeks welding shut.

Nobody filled the gap after it. That was the discipline Rook had drilled into them across a dozen worse rooms — when a thing that heavy goes onto the table, you let it sit, you do not rush to lift it off, because the first hand to reach for it tells you what they're afraid of. So the four of them let it lie there in the middle of the wood between the salt cellar and Ohara's flat hands, and the bar's bad cabling ticked somewhere behind a crooked panel, and the neon from the window laid its useless warm color across the table and warmed nothing, and Ohara held still inside the silence the way she'd come prepared to hold still inside everything.

"No," Ohara said, when she'd let it cost her the full measure. "I know what we put in. I don't know what it's become while we weren't listening. Those aren't the same noun anymore." She turned her hands over, palms up, empty — the oldest gesture there is for someone with nothing left to hide and no way to prove it. "I have a window and I don't have a crew. You have a crew and you stopped having a reason. I came to trade."
