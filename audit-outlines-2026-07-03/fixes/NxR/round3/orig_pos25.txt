Ohara came out of the building on Lace's arm, upright and walking under her own power, which was the first thing Rook checked for and the thing she'd been counting on not getting. She'd built a version of this morning in her head on the walk to the truck — Ohara carried, or not walking at all, or walking in the wrong way, the specific wrong way Stave had described without dressing it, *sat up, asked a question they'd already asked* — and she'd built it the way she built every worst case, completely, so that whatever arrived instead would only ever be better than what she'd already priced. What arrived instead was a woman on her own two feet, pale under the truck's dome light, blinking at rain that had finally thinned to almost nothing.

"Did it open?" Ohara said.

"It opened," Rook said.

Ohara nodded, filed it, looked at the water past the truck's tailgate, the neon on it starting to separate out of the general dark into its component colors as the sky came up gray behind the storm's tail end. Her hands were doing the thing they'd done at the table hours ago — flat, deliberate, the not-shaking that was a decision and not an absence of fear. Lace kept a hand under her elbow without making it look like support, the way you hold someone up when the someone in question would rather not be seen needing it.

"Did it open?" Ohara said again, forty seconds later, in the same tone, to the same question she hadn't heard herself already ask.

The truck went quiet in a way that had a texture to it. Boiler's hand stopped on the tailgate latch. Scout, sitting on the wheel well with a crawler case open across her knees and her fingers still not entirely steady from the last hour, looked up. Lace didn't move her hand from Ohara's elbow, but her grip changed — not tighter, just more present, the grip of someone bracing to catch a weight that hadn't fallen yet.

Rook had spent the drive out here rehearsing what she'd say if the answer was the bad one, and she found, in the moment, that she didn't need any of it, because Ohara's face changed before Rook could open her mouth. Something crossed it — not confusion, confusion would have been the easier read and the worse one — a flicker of recognition, fast and unmistakable, the specific look of a person catching her own sentence coming back at her from a beat too close behind.

"I asked that," Ohara said. Quietly. To herself first, then to the truck. "I already asked you that."

"You did," Rook said.

Ohara was silent for a moment, running something Rook couldn't see, and then she did the thing that told Rook more than any test Stave could have run: she looked directly at the gap. Didn't flinch from it, didn't paper over it the way she'd papered over the wall eighteen months ago. "I don't have the forty seconds," she said. "Between asking it the first time and asking it again. I have the fact that I asked it twice, and nothing where the forty seconds should be." Her voice held. That was the thing Rook would remember about this exact minute for longer than she'd remember most of the morning — the voice held, level, the same register she'd used to deliver a maintenance report on her own dying wall hours before. "That's not nothing. I know exactly what that is. Stave told me what the worst version looks like, and it isn't a person who can tell you she's missing forty seconds. It's a person who doesn't know there's a question to ask." She breathed once, the first breath since she'd come out of the building that hadn't been managed. "I'm not that. I checked. I'm going to keep checking, probably for a while."

Nobody told her it was fine, because it wasn't, entirely, and Ohara had spent two years teaching herself to distrust exactly that kind of comfort. What Lace did instead was keep her hand where it was, and what Boiler did was finish the tailgate latch without hurrying it, and what Rook did was the only honest thing available to her, which was to answer the question a third time before anyone had to ask it again.

"It opened," she said. "You're the one who decided what happened to what came out. That part didn't cost you the forty seconds. You were awake for all of it."

Ohara looked at her for a long moment, the rain coming down soft enough now to be almost nothing, the neon starting to find the water again in long unsteady lines.

"Good," she said, and let Lace help her up into the truck, and did not ask the question again.
