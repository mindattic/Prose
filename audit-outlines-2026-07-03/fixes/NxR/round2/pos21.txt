She walked the failure backward without the reference, which meant she walked it against the one wall she had left — the loud room's eighteen months of complaint, the patch's own held breath, the cold's steady rate of pull. She used the lie as the measure for the lie. The wall had been telling its near face it was live for a year and a half; if she could find where the lie ran thinnest, where the patch had to work hardest to keep saying live, that was where the real seam was widest. So she stopped reading for the silent room and started reading for the strain in the loud one, and the order came up out of it inverted — not the thin places first but the place that lied loudest, because that was the place most afraid of being read.

It was slower this way, and slower was a thing she could feel in her own throat down on the floor, the dry catch of breathing she'd stopped managing the second she went past the loud room. The hum was gone. The hum stayed gone. She measured against the only honest thing in the room, which was the wall's exhaustion — because a wall that has lied for eighteen months isn't a wall that is hiding something well. It is a wall that is tired, and tired leaves a grain, and grain is a thing a hand can follow.

Two stories of structure away, where her body knelt, Ohara was the quietest thing in the building. Vox couldn't see her — Vox couldn't see anything; she was a hand-span of cold and a dead bus and the inside of a partition — but the stillness reached her anyway, the way a room tells you someone in it is holding their breath even when your back is turned. Ohara wasn't holding her breath. That was the tell. She had gone past holding, into the slow even draw of a person who had walked the steps of her own undoing so many times in the dark that there was nowhere left for the fear to land when it finally arrived; she had spent the terror in advance so that none of it would be left to spend now, at the moment it counted, when a stranger's hand was inside the door she'd built and the only job left to her body was to keep that hand a steady target. So she kept her mouth where Lace had told her to keep it. She kept her hand flat on the cold steel of the cabinet, over the partition that was over the patch that was over the thing, and she didn't move, because moving was the one variable she could still take off the table and she took it off.

The open came when she took the loud place first instead of last. It didn't come the way she'd braced for — no give, no breach, no flood of room-light into the dark she'd been navigating by feel. It came as an inversion. The wall stopped pushing back at exactly the point she'd expected its hardest resistance, and the missing push was its own kind of vertigo, a stair-not-there in reverse: she'd leaned her whole weight into the loud place expecting it to be the keystone, the brick the whole lie was built to protect — and the loud place folded, not because she'd beaten it but because it had been waiting eighteen months for someone to lean on it from this exact angle. It didn't fail forward into the room she'd been positioned under, the live cache PEREGRINE had bought a clock to stand beneath. It failed backward. Into itself. The seam unsealed along the line of the original patch, peeling the way a label peels when you finally catch the corner that was always meant to lift, and the thing behind it came up cold.

Cold-stored. Not the live data, not warm with use, not the kind of thing a person keeps because they're still deciding what to do with it. Time-stamped — and the stamp was the part that stopped her, a date the operational log had never once carried, a date that sat before the partition itself, before the lie, before the eighteen months. Older than the wall that hid it. And under a credential the wall surrendered the instant she leaned on the loud place — not a credential she had to break, not one Stave had priced, but one the wall handed over the way a tired person hands over a thing they have been clutching too long: Ohara's own credential. Ohara's own hand. The lock she'd come to crack wasn't a lock against the world. It was a door Ohara had shut on something and then built eighteen months of false-live over the shutting, layer on layer of a wall pretending to be empty, until even she would read the place as already gone.

That was the thing Vox hadn't seen coming, and it arrived in her body before it arrived as a sentence — a drop, low and physical, the particular cold of finding a second floor under the floor you thought was the bottom. She'd cracked a hundred walls. She had never once cracked one and found the architect on the inside of it, locked in with the secret, locked in *by* the secret, the maker and the made-against the same person.

*She didn't pay for a door she forgot she had. She paid for one she forgot she'd locked. And I'm the only one inside it now, reading her reason in the cold.*

On the floor Rook's lamps held their meters, the readouts steady in their cradles, still counting a distance that had nothing to do with what the wall had just turned out to be. Ohara stood where Lace had put her against the cabinet, mouth kept where she'd been told to keep it, hand flat on the steel of a thing she'd sealed and paid Stave to open and stood inside the door of — not knowing, couldn't have known, that the open was already past the data she'd come for and into the data she'd hidden it behind. A version of herself eighteen months gone had stood somewhere with her hand on a different surface and decided that the safest place to put this was inside herself, behind a wall she would teach herself to forget. That version had left the cold like a letter, and the present one was standing on top of it with her palm flat against the steel, waiting for a stranger's hand on the loud place to remember it was there — and the stranger's hand had just remembered.
