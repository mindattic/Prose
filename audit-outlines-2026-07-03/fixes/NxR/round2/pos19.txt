Boiler had been in this building for four months before Rook ever climbed his stairs, and the thing he hadn't told her — hadn't told anyone, because it wasn't the kind of thing you led with — was that he'd wired the stairwell junction the second week. Not to bring the building down. To *choose* when a piece of it came down, on a schedule that was his and nobody else's, in a structure the demolition order had already condemned and the city had already forgotten to finish killing. He called it maintenance. It was the only kind he'd ever been good at.

He heard the stack on the stairs before Scout's ping confirmed it — four sets of boots taking the compromised flight with the confidence of people who'd been told the building was empty and had no reason yet to doubt it. He put two fingers on the detonator he'd carried in his coat pocket since the second week and did not throw it. Not yet. Structural work wasn't about the throw. It was about the load, and the load wasn't distributed the way he needed it yet — three of the four still bunched on the landing, the fourth already past the junction and moving fast toward the west room, moving toward Vox.

"Scout," he said, low, into the channel Vox had left them before she went under. "I need one more off the front before I can drop the middle without dropping the one who's already through."

Scout was two floors up and nowhere, her body folded into the dead space behind the stairwell's service panel, eyes rolled white, four crawlers running the ductwork above the advancing stack in a pattern that had nothing to do with efficiency and everything to do with sound. She peeled two of them off and sent them scraping metal against metal forty feet down a side vent — the small, specific noise of something alive moving where nothing should be — and the third operator on the landing broke formation to check it, exactly the way trained people check a noise they can't place, because ignoring it costs more than confirming it does. One off the front. Boiler had his gap.

He threw it.

The junction went the way he'd built it to go, which was mostly sideways and downward and not into anything that mattered — three meters of already-compromised timber and the landing above it folding into the stairwell's own footprint, a controlled failure dressed up as an uncontrolled one, the kind of collapse that reads to anyone standing in it as the building finally doing what it had been threatening to do for six years. For half a second it ran past him. A floor member he'd read as sound months ago let go early and the failure reached west, a bay further than he'd drawn it, toward the room, toward Vox — and Boiler stood flat-footed in the dust and could do nothing but watch the load travel, because once it was moving there was no hand you could put on it. Then the structure found the path he'd spent four months trusting and caught itself exactly where he'd known it would, and the reach stopped a full bay short of the wall. His breath went out of him. He hadn't marked that he'd been holding it. The two operators still on the landing went down with it, not far, not clean, into a drop that would cost them a slow climb back up through debris and nothing worse than that, which was the whole design: Boiler didn't build things to kill people who were simply doing a job for money. He built things to make a job cost more than it was worth continuing. The dust came up through the shaft in a gray column that ate the beam of every light in the stairwell at once.

That left the third — the one Scout had peeled loose with the noise in the vent — closing on her real position in the dark behind the panel, her body sitting there with its eyes rolled white and its breathing down to nothing, the single most defenseless thing in the building at the exact moment it was doing the most important work in it. A boot found the panel's seam. Fingers found the edge. Scout didn't move, couldn't move, was four floors and forty feet distributed across six crawlers and had nothing left over to spend on her own throat.

Lace was already in the channel. She'd taken the seed Vox left her — a live patch into PEREGRINE's short-range comm, stolen clean in the two hours before the procedure started, the kind of theft that only worked if nobody on the other end ever had a reason to check the signature — and she ran it now the way she'd run a commander's voice across a storm-lashed lake platform two years back, precise to the muscle, nothing wasted. "Fall back to the junction," she said, in a register that wasn't hers, wasn't anyone's, was simply *authority*, the forty-three muscles of an officer nobody could see giving an order nobody in the field would think to question. "Two down. I want eyes on them before anything else. Move."

The operator at the panel went still — the still of a man taking an order he hadn't expected, in a voice he only half-placed. Then his hand moved, not off the panel but to his own comm, thumbing it to send: the reflex of a professional confirming a strange instruction back up the line before he acted on it. And a confirmation was the one thing Lace couldn't survive. She'd built the voice off a single field capture, enough to give an order into and nowhere near enough to hold a conversation in — the second he keyed back, the ruse stopped being a voice and became a duet she didn't have the other half of. She heard his carrier open and spent her entire margin in one clause, cutting across the key before it could resolve into a question: "Do not make me say it twice." Flat. Bored. The specific impatience of an officer who has never once been asked to repeat himself and would remember the face of the man who made him. It was a bet that a soldier feared irritating his commander more than he needed the strange order confirmed, and it landed by the narrow margin that bets like that land by. His thumb came off the comm. He peeled off toward the junction to confirm his fallen partners, and Scout's crawlers went still and watched him go, and somewhere behind the panel her breathing found its floor again without her body ever having had to decide anything.

Three down, in the sense that mattered: delayed, disabled, or turned. Boiler stood in the drifting dust with blood coming off a cut along his forearm he hadn't priced yet and wouldn't until later, watching the stairwell hold the shape he'd given it. Scout came back into her own eyes by degrees behind the panel, the crawlers' feed thinning to background the way it always did on the way out, her hands finding her own knees before her mind fully caught up to where they were. Lace killed the channel before the impersonation could be tested twice — you never ran a voice a second time in the same conversation, that was the first rule, the trick only worked once per audience — and put her back to the wall, and for the length of one breath let herself feel what it had cost to hold another person's authority in her own throat while a friend's real body sat exposed forty feet away.

The fourth was already past them. He'd been past the junction before Boiler threw the charge, moving on his own read of the building instead of the stack's shared one, and no amount of collapsed timber or borrowed voice was going to call him back to a formation he'd already left behind. He was the one whose boots Vox would feel as pressure in her sternum two floors down. He was the one who'd already found the frequency on Rook's open line and closed it on a sentence that mattered. Three of four wasn't four of four. It was, on a morning that had never once offered them a clean number, the only number they were going to get, and it was enough to matter, and it wasn't enough to be safe.

"One's through," Boiler said into the channel, flat, the cheerful entirely gone out of his voice for the second time that morning. "We slowed the rest. Rook — one's through, and he's not stopping to confirm what he broke to get there."
