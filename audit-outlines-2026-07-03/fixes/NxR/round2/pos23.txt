What was inside was a manifest. That was the first thing, and it landed wrong, because Vox had built the whole reading around the shape of a data-cache — a slab of formula-adjacent research, dense and dead and exactly the size of PEREGRINE's purchase order — and a manifest had a different texture under the Read entirely. Not a slab. A list. Rows. Something written to be counted off by a person standing at the foot of a ramp, and a list left a feel in the cold the way a column left a feel under Boiler's hand: load distributed in entries, not in mass.

Not corporate. Not a data-trade ledger, nothing PEREGRINE had a name for on their purchase order. A personnel manifest, cold-stamped two years before the job that had sent her into this wall ever existed. She read the header first, the way you always read a header first, and it was a transit code — six characters, the kind of routing string the city stamped on anything that moved through a berth — and she read it once and parsed it as freight, because that was what transit codes were, and started down into the rows.

Then she stopped, and went back up to the header, and read it again.

She made herself take it in order, because out of order it was just dread, and in order it was evidence, and the only thing she had left to stand on in here was the order of things. The transit code was a barge; she had crawled enough port traffic to read a hull out of six characters. The barge had a date, cold-stamped in the second field, machine-flat, and it took her a full second to place it because it was an ordinary night two years back with nothing on the public record to mark it — and then she placed it, and the second went long.

The date was the night Old Harbor lost a whole tenement of registered Reads to a relocation order nobody had signed.

Everyone who worked the channels remembered the gap — a building's worth of registered sensitives current in the rolls one week and flagged *reassigned* the next, no destination filed, no receiving district, the kind of hole in a ledger that you learned to read past because reading into it cost more than you had. She knew that gap. She'd had six channels then, and she'd felt the absence like a frequency she'd been calibrated to and then lost overnight, a band of the signal just gone, no explanation, no body, only the *reassigned* in the column and the silence where sixty-one people's broadcasts had been. She'd never said it aloud, not even to the crew, because saying it out loud meant sitting inside it, and the column was cleaner.

Sixty-one names. She counted them the way she counted everything in here, not by reading each but by feeling the length of the row-stack, and it came back sixty-one — sixty-one registered Reads loaded onto a hull on a night the city had agreed not to remember. And then she felt the second column, and the second column was worse than the first, because the first was a crime and the second was a count of it.

Forty of the names carried a second mark. A small flat notation set beside the entry, the kind of glyph that meant the same thing in every ledger language Vox had ever crawled through a wall to find — receipt, reconciliation, the line item closed against its delivery. *Delivered.* Forty names with the books squared beside them. Forty people moved off a barge and onto whatever the relocation order had actually meant, and a clerk somewhere had ticked the column and the column had balanced.

The other twenty-one had no second mark at all.

She read that column three times, and the three reads weren't the same read done over. The first was a tally — forty marked, twenty-one bare — looking for the error that would let her put the number down. There was no error. The second was a read for a reason: she went down the bare entries one by one looking for a reconciliation tucked small, set faint, where a tired clerk might have under-stamped it. The cold gave her nothing to find. Clean white space where forty of its neighbors carried a glyph, and clean white space does not get less clean for being read.

It was the third read that did it, because some facts will not go into the body on two passes. The tally was right and the searching was done and the column was still empty, and on the third pass there was nothing left to do with it but believe it — and the believing arrived the way it always arrived for her, not as a thought but as a place going wrong in the meat of her: a tightening low under the sternum, the cold of the wall reaching past the hand-span of partition and into her own held breath two floors down, where her real throat caught on a real intake of air she hadn't told it to take. Twenty-one entries that went onto the barge and never reconciled against a delivery. The absence stopped being a number missing a mark and became the size of twenty-one people.

And outside the wall, a hand-span from where she knelt, Lace's two fingers were still on the cold. Still steady. Still not knowing that the silence they were keeping watch over contained sixty-one names on a barge and twenty-one columns of empty space. The steadiness felt unbearable from the inside, not because Lace was failing her but because Lace couldn't know what she was steady for, and Vox was the only person in the room who understood the full weight of what those two fingers were holding place for. Lace had sat on eleven days and called it nine; here was the inverse — Vox sitting inside the full accounting, the only witness, with Lace's warmth fading into the patch a hand-span away.

*Twenty-one she walked off the manifest before it could square. Twenty-one she papered over with eighteen months of a wall pretending it was empty. That's not a secret she sat on to protect herself. That's the only good thing she ever did, and she built a year and a half of lying just so nobody could thank her for it and nobody could hang her for it — and she built it so well she forgot she'd done it.*

And PEREGRINE was forty meters out and closing — Rook's knock still in the bones of her hand, the patient vector folded into the last of the floor — having bought a clock to stand under a wall they thought held a data-cache, and that actually held the one document that put forty delivered names and a barge and a date in the same buried hand. The instant the seam finished unsealing, the manifest stopped being Ohara's secret and became the thing in the room, readable off the far face by anyone with a hand on it the second the wall stopped lying.

The decision was a verb and the sentence had been waiting all morning for it. She could finish the open — let the seam fail the way she'd half-built it to fail, hand PEREGRINE the live data and the buried data both, the data-cache and the manifest in one clean break — and let the change clear the way she'd priced it back in Stave's transformer room. Or she could do to the wall what Ohara had done. Reseal the loud place behind her on the way out. Leave the twenty-one in the cold where Ohara had left them. Walk out of the seam carrying a thing nobody on the floor knew she was carrying, including the woman whose two fingers were still on the steel a hand-span from hers, steady and waiting and unable to know what they were holding watch over.

*I priced this against a data-cache. I never once priced it against this. And the change still clears in eight days either way — except now I'm the part of the wall that decides whether twenty-one people stayed lost on purpose, and the meters don't stop for that, and Lace's two fingers are still on the cold a hand-span from mine, and I have to decide before PEREGRINE puts the forty-first mark up in a column that doesn't have a glyph for what they'd be writing.*
