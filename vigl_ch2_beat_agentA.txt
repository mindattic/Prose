Vega walked back into Averlyne with Lyra's note still folded in her coat pocket, and because her own contract did not resume until the following morning, she let herself be talked into standing witness at the petitioners' court instead of going straight to bed — a favor to old Registrar Kessendine, who liked having a scribe present whose handwriting the archive could actually read.

The petitioners' court was not canonical business. No instrument opened it, no Received Tongue invocation, no Canon Immaculate presiding — only King Cedric Averlyne, in a plain chair on a dais one step too low to properly be called a throne, hearing whatever the city brought him on the second Wexday of the month. She had stood witness twice before. She liked it more than she let herself say out loud in the registry, because it was the one room in the city where the law spoke in the common tongue and meant, mostly, what it said.

A miller's widow was petitioning for relief on a grain-tithe assessed against a mill that hadn't turned in eight months, her husband dead of a fall and no son old enough yet to work the wheel. The King heard the whole of it without once looking down at his own clerk's summary. Sanctorin did that too, but Sanctorin's attention was an instrument's accuracy; the King's was something plainer, closer to the thing it was imitating.

"The tithe is forgiven for the year," he said, "and the year after, until the boy's old enough to turn the wheel himself or you've found someone to turn it for you." He was already reaching for the next petition when the Liturgy clerk at his elbow — a thin young man in Vigil grey, there only to record what was said for the canonical registry, no more standing than the ink he carried — cleared his throat and murmured something short.

The King went still. Vega watched him hear it, watched the sentence land somewhere behind his face and cost him something on the way through.

"The tithe," he said, "is forgiven for this year." A pause long enough that the widow's shoulders had already begun to fall before he finished it. "The matter of subsequent years will be reviewed under the standing agricultural instrument and a determination rendered by the appropriate office." He did not look at the clerk when he said it. He did not need to; everyone in the room had heard where the sentence turned.

He found the widow afterward, Vega noticed, on his way out through the side passage he was not required to use — said something to her privately that made her nod, made her not-quite-smile, made her leave the hall walking a little less like a woman carrying the whole eight months on her back. Whatever he'd promised her would come out of his own household accounts rather than the crown's. It was the kindest thing available to him. It was also the smallest.

She thought about it on the walk back to the station. He had wanted, plainly, visibly, to give that woman her two years and not merely her one. He had wanted it enough that the wanting showed on his face where any clerk could read it.

She had watched him fold in under a boy barely old enough to grow the mustache he was cultivating so carefully. Walking home in the last of the light, she could not put a name to what she felt. Fondness and grief sat too close together to pull apart.

A kinder man than Anslem Sanctorin was going to spend his whole reign losing to him, one small mercy at a time. She was not ready to call that a fact.

It stayed with her anyway.

The Vigil checkpoint sat at the narrows, where the coast road squeezed between the cliff face and the drop with no way around it in any weather. The hut was old — stone, low-roofed, built in the first century of the Patrol's tenure, reinforced twice since. The officer on duty was a man she'd spoken to six times in seventeen years without either of them learning the other's name — the nature of checkpoint work. He had the departure log open on the desk, the stamp ready, her field authority already in hand. The dispatch had gone out on the standard chain. He'd been expecting her.

He stamped the log. Noted the date. Gave her the standard brief on the eastern sector event — minor, below threshold, no further escalation since the initial report, two days estimated for a full perimeter assessment. She rode into the zone.

This time of year the eastern sector was a long coastal traverse — scrubby moorgrass, loose shale, the flat grey sky of a headland that never quite dried out. She had made this assessment eleven times. Every time the membrane boundary read the same: stable, unremarkable, the specific quiet of a boundary post with five hundred years of vigil attention on it, familiar enough by now to stop surprising anyone. The seismic event was minor — a shelf shift, three hundred meters southeast, the rock settling its own weight. Within parameters. She rode back.

Four days total, out and back. The Heloth was worse on the return — she'd pushed it hard outbound, and it had spent its efficiency giving her the speed, so the way home ran longer and rougher. Her hips ached by the second day, the specific ache that meant the last two days would keep reminding her she was thirty-five. She ate rations in the saddle. She slept badly at the overnight way-stops. She spent the final hours watching the coast track unreel ahead of her in the thin pre-dawn grey that meant she was close.

She wasn't thinking about anything in particular when she came down off the road and saw the Station entrance.

The doors stood open.

She pulled back on the control line. The Heloth settled, weight dropping onto both legs with its usual brief check. She sat the mountain shoulder and looked at the entrance, then at the high archive window — which should have shown the orange cast of the lamp and the night warden at his reading table.

The window showed darkness.

She came down off the handgrips. Her legs worked badly the first three steps — four days in the saddle, nothing more — and she walked toward the entrance, reading what it told her from a distance.

The hinges were sound. The frame was intact. The door had been opened by someone with a key, or by someone who had persuaded someone with a key to open it, or by the night warden himself under circumstances that had not left him a choice. None of these was better than the others.

She pressed her palm to the archive lamp housing. Metal, room temperature. Not guttered — cold the way stone goes cold when it's been ignored for hours, not minutes. The cold that means something was here and isn't anymore.

She stood with her palm against the cold metal and thought about this.

Then she went inside.

The night warden lay beside the reading table.

His tea mug was on the floor three feet away, on its side, still wet inside.

He lay the way people lay when they don't get time to argue about it — arms dropped, one cheek against the stone, a body stopped mid-step with nothing left to do about it. His service blade was sheathed. In seventeen years of watching this man — eleven days from the retirement he'd been narrating to anyone who'd stand still for it long enough — finish the night catalogue, replace the lamp wick, and sit with his tea with the unconscious ease of a man who'd done it so many times he'd stopped noticing he was doing it, she had never once seen him draw that blade. He had not drawn it now.

She crouched beside him and read what he offered. The angle of the fall. The feet, still oriented toward the table. He'd been standing with his back partly to the entrance, working — the catalogue lay open beside him to a page dated four days before the Descent dispatch, which meant he'd been reviewing the backlog — and whoever came in had come in quiet enough that he never turned.

No blood.

She looked closer. No blood. The mark at the base of his skull was consistent with impact — specific, deliberate, the placement of someone who knew where to strike and how hard. Not a guess. Knowledge. Or practice enough to become knowledge.

She stood.

The two field soldiers were in the corridor beyond the archive entrance. The first was eight paces from the threshold. The second was four steps further. They had heard something — not enough to know what it was, but enough to come to the corridor. Neither had drawn a weapon.

She took it in. She caught herself doing it — the flat quality observation takes after four days of poor sleep, facts registering without the weight of feeling attached. She caught that too, and moved on.

Professional work. Practiced, targeted, nothing in it that had been done in anger or haste. The kind of work that does not get done by people for whom it is still interesting.

She returned to the archive and read the floor.

Boot-prints. The distinctive heel width of Loera coast tannery — narrower than Atrament service issue, wider than Verlaine civilian standard, the non-standard toe-cap Loera service had used for thirty years. She'd seen this pattern twice before, in cross-House reviews during her research tenure. Both times the print belonged to soldiers, not contractors. Contractors did not get issued Loera military kit.

Three sets. One overlapped the others at the central table — the leader, by the weight distribution: heel-deep at the table, lighter by the door. The leader had picked something up, added its weight to their own, and walked to the door without pausing. No hesitation in the turn from standing to moving. They'd known what they were coming for. They'd known where it was.

She stood at the table.

In the far corner, seated against the stone with its hands folded and its eyes closed, sat the old figure the Station kept in the vigil room — carved or grown or something older than either word covered, robed, still, exactly as it had been since the Oculus came to this installation in Year 1330. Whatever had passed through this room in the last few hours had not moved it from that posture. Or it had moved, and settled back before she arrived, and no instrument in her kit could tell her which. She looked at it two seconds longer than she needed to, then made herself stop.

She knew before she opened the case. Not intuition — every piece of evidence pointed at it, and the only fact that would have said otherwise was someone putting it back, and no one who'd gone to this much trouble to take it would put it back. But you do not substitute certainty for evidence. She opened the case.

The velvet held the shape of a lens — a shallow oval worn smooth by forty-one years of careful placement and careful removal, the same hands performing the same motion year after year, then the next practitioner's hands, and the next. She pressed the back of her hand into the impression.

Room temperature. And the room at this altitude in high autumn was cold. Cold meant the lens had been absent for hours.

She stood with her hand in the empty case for longer than she needed to, which she felt in herself and set aside.

The Oculus Lacrimans Vigilantiae was Sinter work, Year 630 — the founding documents were plain about that much, plainer than they were about anything that came after. Seven hundred and forty-one years of unbroken sight, if you counted from the day Sinter's own Scriveners had bonded it to its paired Orb and opened the membrane between the two. Ocipheus had held the lens itself for a good deal less than that: forty-one years, since the Nine Days at Caer Glas Moor took it out of Calyx hands and put it into the Order's, the transfer dated plainly to Year 1330 and never once dressed up as anything older. The gold fittings on the case were newer than the case pretended. She'd read the founding documents in her first year of tenure, again in her third, again in her sixth, and each time the two figures weighed differently against each other — the sight's great age, the Order's much smaller claim on it — more years behind her each reading, she supposed, before she'd learned to hold both numbers in her head at once instead of collapsing them into the one everyone found easier to say. Seven hundred and forty-one years watching the membrane boundary, wherever the paired Orb happened to sit — first from inside Sinter itself, now from a crater that used to be Sinter, the sight running on regardless of which building's roof the lens itself slept beneath: through the vigil apparatus, through the practitioner's attention, through whatever mode of observation a Relic accumulates across that span. Every scry she had logged in seventeen years of vigil work was a look and then a loss — the wound closed, the image went with it, and what stayed in the record afterward was her own handwriting describing something already gone. The Oculus lost nothing. That, more than its age or the gold fittings on the case, was what made it a Relic and not merely an instrument: it kept what it saw. The literature called it instrumental. She had always read that as a careful word for not yet characterized.

Someone had put it in a pack and walked out with it four hours ago.

The lamp's temperature gave her the window: four to five hours. The Descent emergency dispatch had gone out at about the same hour — a seismic event at the eastern sector, below threshold, requiring senior practitioners, no exceptions, no consultation. She had followed protocol. Everyone of sufficient rank had followed protocol.

She stood in the middle of the archive and thought about protocol, for the first time, as a thing that could be read from the outside. Who had been in the room when response requirements were set? Who understood what a senior-practitioners dispatch cleared out of this archive? Who had known, the moment the message went out, exactly which rooms would sit empty, and for how long?

The prints didn't stop at the table.

She followed them out through the passage door and into the yard, where four days of hard frost had done what hard frost always did to a print left in it — held the shape and sharpened the edges, better evidence than fresh mud ever gave you. Three sets, holding the same spacing they'd kept inside, moving without hesitation toward the south wall and the narrow service gate the day staff used for deliveries rather than the main gate any patrol would have logged.

The gate stood unbarred. Not forced — unbarred, the bar itself set down against the wall rather than dropped where a struggle would have left it. Someone had opened it from the inside, calmly, the way you open a door you already know isn't going to be watched.

Past the gate the frost thinned to bare ground and the prints thinned with it, three sets narrowing to two clear tracks and a scuffed third before the ground gave out onto the cart road entirely — south, away from the Station, away from anywhere a Descent dispatch would have thought to look. She stood at the point where the last print disappeared into gravel that held nothing, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the cold metal under her palm at the lamp housing: two dead soldiers, a warden who never drew his blade, and three people who had walked out through a gate they knew wouldn't be watched, calm enough the whole way not to leave a single print out of step.

She had a direction. South, then wherever south connected to. She did not yet have a destination, and a direction without a destination was worth exactly as much as it took to keep walking before it became one.

She heard Vega before she saw her — someone trying to be quiet in an old stone corridor and not quite managing it, a small scrape, a caught breath. She turned.

Her sister stood in the doorway in the clothes she'd slept in, holding a candle losing its case to the dawn light through the passage window behind her. She should have been three days gone — back at the Liturgy's central office, certification instruments filed and countersigned — but the Keeper had asked her to stay one more day for the quarterly notary work, and she'd agreed without much reluctance, and the world had come apart on the one morning she hadn't meant to still be here for. She was looking at the third body.

"Lyra," Vega said.

"Yes."

"Is it-" She stopped. She was twenty-four years old. She had been copying canonical authorizations for three years and she had never been in a room with a dead person before. "Whatever they were doing — is it finished?"

"They took the Oculus." There was no other way to say it. "The case is gone."

Vega was quiet for a moment. She looked at the seated figure in the corner, motionless. Then at the empty case. Then at her sister.

"You've been awake for two days?" she asked.

"Don't."

"You have a cut on your face that you haven't addressed. From the eastern sector."

"Vega."

"I'm not saying anything about the rest of it." She said it the way she said everything — with the particular care of a woman who has been writing other people's words for three years and is now choosing her own. "I'm saying you have a cut on your face."

Lyra touched her cheek. Found it. Yes.

"There's a kit in my pack," she said.

"I know where your kit is," Vega said, and went to get it.

They sat in the corridor outside the archive while the station woke up around them, and Vega cleaned the cut with the precision of someone who'd done this before — not training, just years of being Lyra's sister and addressing things on Lyra's face since she was old enough to reach. The candle was unnecessary now. The morning was handling it.

"Do you know who took it?" Vega asked.

"Not yet."

"But you have a direction?"

She did. The boot-prints, the frost still holding its line on the outer sill, three sets moving like they'd rehearsed the room before they'd ever stood in it. Loera work, south passage, gone before the fourth hour. It wasn't certainty. It was enough to walk on.

"Yes," she said.

Vega tied off the bandage. Didn't lean back. She was looking at Lyra the way she'd looked at her since she was old enough to assess anything — eleven years younger, shorter, their mother's coloring where Lyra had their father's, the same habit of waiting for the whole sentence before answering.

"Three days," Vega said.

"The note was optimistic."

"The note was wrong." Just the fact, without weight. Then: "Come back with it."

"That's the assignment."

"I didn't say come back for the Liturgy." She picked up the candle and stood. "Come back with it. Then come back here. The Liturgy can file the appropriate recovery instruments."

Lyra looked at her.

Vega was already walking back toward the archive, toward her scribing table and the certification instruments she'd been hired to finish. At the door she paused.

"The bread is fine," she said. "I don't know where you get these ideas."

She went back to the empty case and made herself think past the anger of it.

Three people, Loera tradecraft, gone before the fourth hour, south passage. That told her the who and the exit. It didn't tell her the destination, and a Relic didn't travel far on foot before somebody needed a document to keep moving with it.

She knew the Oculus's history the way she knew her own service record — Sinter work, Year 630, out of Calyx hands and into the Order's only forty-one years back, the transfer dated plainly to the Nine Days at Caer Glas Moor and never dressed up as older. House Calyx had never stopped filing the old property-claim on it, every year, on schedule, the way a family keeps paying tribute at a grave nobody visits anymore — a formality nobody in the archive had ever expected anyone to act on, least of all Calyx itself, five generations removed from the boy who'd handed the thing over.

A formality was still a document. A document still opened a harbor gate.

If whoever had hired the Loera crew wanted the case to cross customs without an inspector's hands on it, the fastest paper in Entos for exactly that purpose was sitting in a filing cabinet three floors below her own desk, available to anyone with the standing to request a certified copy — and House Calyx's claim would honor itself at any harbor customs post that had ever processed Liturgy freight, whether or not the hands carrying the case had any right to invoke it.

South passage ran to one road worth taking at that hour. The road ran to one harbor — the Verlaine crossing, where the coast road came down to the water.

She had a direction now, and something worse than a direction — a shape to the theft that made it look less like opportunism and more like a plan drawn up by someone who'd read the same founding documents she had, and known exactly which forty-one-year-old formality would still open a gate.
