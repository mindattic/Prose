# The Weight of a Receipt

> A child rides up to Mrs. Chen's stall with a contract written on a Carrion receipt. The contract names Ayushmaan Horváth.

Mrs. Chen Wei-Lin's stall smelled like star anise and honest work.

Kyle sat on the third stool from the left, the one with the wobble. He ate slow. The array behind his sternum was quiet. The street outside Tier 7's public promenade moved the way it always moved after shift-change: shoulders down, eyes on the grating, everyone calculating what the next twelve hours would cost.

The broth was correct.

He heard the bike before he saw it — a pedal-assist Carrion Logistics LLC courier frame, the kind they sold to gig workers on a lease that never ended. The frame was too big for the rider. The rider was maybe eleven.

The kid threaded between two Pinnacle Civil maintenance drones and braked hard on the grease-stained tiles outside the stall's awning.

"Kyle Corbin-Vister?"

Kyle set his chopsticks across the bowl.

"Yeah."

The kid reached into a canvas bag strapped across her chest and produced a folded square of paper. Carrion receipt paper, the pink-and-grey thermal stock they used for delivery confirmations. On the back, handwriting in blue ink. Real ink. Kyle hadn't seen real ink in two weeks.

He took the paper.

The handwriting was careful. Someone had taken time with it.

*To whoever Mr. Corbin-Vister is — my name is Miriam. I was told you are fair. We have a problem with a company called Tessera and I do not know if I am being helped or eaten. The congregation at the container complex on Tier 4 North, berth 9-through-14. Tonight if you can. — Ayushmaan Horváth sends his regards and says you will understand.*

Kyle read it twice.

The array warmed a degree. Not threat-heat. Something else.

He looked at the kid.

"You know Horváth?"

"He's my uncle's neighbor," the kid said. "He paid me thirty chips to deliver that."

Kyle folded the receipt and put it in his jacket's inside pocket.

"Tell him I'm coming."

The kid was already back on the bike.

Mrs. Chen refilled his broth without being asked.

"Eat," she said. "Tier 4 North is cold."

He ate.

Tier 4 North was the kind of place Pinnacle Civil put in its heritage reports as *organically developed residential infrastructure* — which meant they had stopped maintaining it in 2198 and started calling the deterioration a feature.

The container complex at berths 9-through-14 was six rows of stacked shipping containers, the old Carrion Logistics intermodal type, forty-footers in sun-faded red and green and one oxidized yellow that had been there long enough to become a landmark. Someone had cut doors and windows into them with plasma torches, decades ago, and then someone else had filled those windows with actual glass — salvaged, mismatched, some of it colored. The containers were bolted and welded into a structure six stories tall that leaned slightly south, the way the whole tier leaned south, the way everyone on the tier leaned south after years of walking the same slanted floors.

There were window-box gardens on every level.

Herbs, mostly. Some small peppers. One box on the fourth container up had a single marigold in it, orange in the cold vapor light.

It was the only unbranded object Kyle had seen since leaving Mrs. Chen's.

He stood on the grating below and looked up at the complex for ten seconds. The array read the structure: load-bearing welds sound, roof-level has a new antenna cluster — not Carrion, not Pinnacle Civil. Community-built. Someone had strung fiber between the containers and run it down to a junction box that had been painted over so many times the brand name underneath was illegible.

Kyle walked to the front entrance.

The front entrance was a container door left hinged and decorated with a painted mural: the GLMZ skyline upside down, so the towers hung below and the roots of things reached upward. A child's handprints formed the border. The paint was not corporate paint. It had the slight texture of something mixed from available components.

A woman stood in the doorway.

She was in her late fifties, small, with grey-streaked hair pulled back. She wore a wool-blend coat — Helix Thermal, the budget line, the one they advertised with the slogan *Warmth Is a Right* while lobbying to restrict residential heat subsidies. The coat had been repaired at the elbow with a different-colored thread.

"Mr. Corbin-Vister," she said.

"Miriam."

"Come in."

The interior of the ground-level container was set up as a common room. Low tables, mismatched chairs, a gas burner in the corner with a kettle. A Hyacinth ClearBreath air filter — the industrial model, the one Hyacinth leased to clinics — sat in the opposite corner running on what looked like a spliced Arcturus power line. The filter's display read *AIR QUALITY: ACCEPTABLE* in green. The air outside had been amber.

Eight people sat in the chairs and on the floor.

Miriam sat across from Kyle and put a Tessera Group brochure on the table between them.

The brochure was expensive. Matte finish, embossed logo, the kind of print job that cost more than the monthly maintenance budget for an entire tier. The Tessera Group called themselves *a heritage and cultural continuity enterprise*. Their tagline: *We Preserve What Matters.*

Kyle picked up the brochure and read the relevant page.

Tessera was offering a Heritage Stewardship Agreement. In exchange for filming rights and the right to use the complex's image in *educational and promotional materials in perpetuity*, Tessera would register the complex as a Cultural Heritage Site under GLMZ Corporate Preservation Code Section 14-C. A Cultural Heritage Site could not be demolished for development without a forty-eight-month review process.

The current demolition order on the complex was from Arcturus Development Group and was dated six weeks ago.

Kyle set the brochure down.

He looked at Miriam.

"You read the sub-clauses?"

"I read what I could," she said. "There are parts I don't understand."

"Do you have the actual contract?"

A man in the back — young, maybe twenty-five, NeoCortex-badged jacket with the badge torn off — produced a tablet and walked it forward.

Kyle took it.

The contract was forty-one pages. He read fast. The array didn't help with legal text but it helped him hold structure, track clause dependencies, notice when a later paragraph quietly negated an earlier one.

He found the first problem on page seven.

He found the second on page nineteen.

He found the third on page thirty-one and sat with it for a moment.

"Okay," he said.

The room waited.

"Page seven," Kyle said. "Clause 4.2. Tessera gets 'exclusive narrative rights to the history and stories of the complex and its residents.' That means anything anyone here has ever done, any story you've ever told about this place — they own the version they film. You can't tell your own story after this without their approval."

Miriam's face did not change.

"Page nineteen," Kyle said. "Clause 12.1. The Heritage Site designation protects the *physical structure* from demolition. It does not protect residency rights. Arcturus can still remove the residents. They just have to keep the containers standing."

Someone in the back said something sharp in a language Kyle didn't catch.

"Page thirty-one," Kyle said. "Clause 19.4. If the congregation fails to 'maintain the cultural character of the site as determined by Tessera's heritage committee,' Tessera can withdraw the designation. They define *cultural character*. They define the committee. They can revoke protection any time the complex stops being useful for their content."

He set the tablet on the table.

"It's not preservation," he said. "It's a lease. You pay with your stories. They keep the right to evict."

Miriam was quiet for a long moment.

"The Tessera representative," she said, "told us we had until Friday to sign."

"It's Wednesday."

"Yes."

Kyle looked at the brochure again. Then at the room. A woman near the wall was holding a sleeping infant. The man with the torn-off NeoCortex badge had his jaw tight. An older man in the back had his eyes closed, either praying or calculating.

Kyle thought about the marigold in the window box.

"Who's the rep," he said. "Name."

"She said her name was Cass Inoue. She works out of Tessera's Tier 9 office."

"You have her contact?"

Miriam nodded.

"Don't sign anything," Kyle said. "And don't tell her you've talked to me."

Tier 9 was the kind of tier where the ambient light was better because the corporations that owned it had decided good light reflected well on them.

Tessera's office was in a Hyacinth-managed mixed-use building, which meant Hyacinth collected the rent and Tessera paid it and both of them wrote the transaction off as a *community investment*. They were different corps. The building's lobby had a mural of a GLMZ sunrise. The mural had been painted by an artist Hyacinth had contracted for a flat fee and then retained the copyright of. The artist's name was not on the mural.

Kyle arrived at 2200 and found Cass Inoue still at her desk.

She was in her thirties, polished, the kind of tired that got concealed by good skincare. Her jacket was Arcturus executive-line. Not Tessera. He noted that.

The array warmed. She had augmentation — low-grade Helix social-calibration implant, the kind that read microexpressions and fed you prompts. Sales tool. Negotiation tool. She would know he was reading her and she wouldn't know what he was reading with.

"Mr. Corbin-Vister," she said, without looking surprised. Someone had told her to expect him or she was good at not being surprised. He filed both.

"Inoue."

"Sit down."

He sat.

"The congregation sent you," she said.

"I came to ask a question."

"Ask."

"Arcturus filed the demolition order," Kyle said. "Tessera is Arcturus's heritage division."

He watched her face.

The Helix implant would be telling her: *maintain neutral expression, rephrase toward benefit framing.* He watched her decide whether to follow it.

She followed it.

"Tessera operates independently from—"

"They're the same corp," Kyle said.

She stopped.

The array gave him her tells: a 0.4-second lag before the next breath, a micro-contraction around the left eye. Not anger. Calculation. She was deciding how much he knew and whether it mattered.

"The designation is real," she said. "The protection is real."

"For the containers," Kyle said. "Not the people."

"Residency rights are a separate—"

"Separate department," Kyle said. "Same corp."

She was quiet.

"What do you want?" she said.

"Amended contract," Kyle said. "Three changes. One: narrative rights stay with the congregation. Tessera gets filming rights, non-exclusive, for seven years. Two: Heritage designation protects residency, not just structure. You add a clause, you get it co-signed by a GLMZ civil registrar, not just your own committee. Three: revocation requires independent review, not Tessera's panel."

"Those terms would require—"

"I know what they'd require."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"And if we don't amend?"

Kyle stood.

"Then the congregation doesn't sign," he said. "And I spend the next week making sure every community org on Tiers 3 through 6 knows what the contract actually says."

He put the original brochure on her desk.

"You have until Friday," he said. "Same deadline you gave them."

He left.

He was back at the container complex by 2300.

Miriam met him at the painted door.

He told her what he'd said and what he'd asked for.

She listened without interrupting.

When he finished she was quiet for a moment, her hands folded in front of her. Behind her, through the open door, he could hear voices, the kettle, the sound of the Hyacinth filter doing its small work.

"Will she do it?" Miriam asked.

"Don't know," Kyle said. "If she does, have a civil attorney look at the language before you sign. Doesn't have to be expensive. There are legal aid orgs on Tier 5."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then you've got two days to decide if you're going to fight the demolition order directly," Kyle said. "That's a different conversation."

Miriam nodded slowly.

"What do I owe you?"

Kyle looked at the mural on the door. The upside-down skyline. The handprint border.

"Nothing," he said.

She started to speak.

"Ayushmaan sent his regards," Kyle said. "That's enough."

He turned and walked back toward the tier ramp.

Behind him the container complex stood the way it had stood for thirty years: unlevel, repaired, lit from inside, leaning south the way everything here leaned south.

The marigold in the fourth-level window box was still there.

It was the only thing on Tier 4 North that didn't belong to anyone.

He took the ramp up.

The receipt was still in his pocket.

The broth had been correct.
