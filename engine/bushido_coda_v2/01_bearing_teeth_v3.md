# Bearing Teeth

*Protagonist: Kyle Ellen Corbin*

## Part I — Teeth

The freight elevator was the only thing in the building that worked. The resident elevator had been there once; there was a shaft and there were doors on every floor and a piece of paper taped to one of them that said OUT OF SERVICE in three languages, none of them recent. Kyle rode the freight up. The cage had a fine carpet of pulverized concrete on the grating from the last load — somebody had moved a refrigerator, or somebody had moved a man, or somebody had moved both and the man had complained less.

The building was a welded conversion bid out in 2189, slotted twelve stories above ground between two towers, contractor paid, contractor moved on. The CorpoNation registry had erased the conversion from the map two years later to dodge a tax penalty and ever since then the building had not officially existed and had also continued to be where eighty-six families lived. Carrion Logistics LLC owned the freight elevator on paper. Carrion Logistics LLC owned a lot of things on paper. Carrion Logistics LLC ran the body-pickup federation, the unofficial street-license registry, and the freight elevator in this building, which were three businesses that turned out to share a customer base if you squinted.

The airshaft brought the whole building's news up the side of the elevator like a wet radio. Camphor from the second floor — somebody's grandmother. Burnt rice from the fifth, the same burnt rice every Thursday. And tonight, from somewhere between four and seven, the smell of Chen Wei-Lin's deep fryer: pork bone, scallion, oil that had not been changed since the lunar new year because the oil was the seasoning.

Kyle was on the eighth floor before he understood that the smell of the stall he was about to be hired by was already in the apartment he was about to enter, coming up the flue six floors ahead of him.

It would later strike him as a clean little parable. The work was always already there before you got to it. The smell was the contract. He stepped off the elevator and the building knew his answer before he did.

---

Unit 802 was at the end of the hall behind a door that had been painted four different times at four different attempts at the color white. Mrs. Chen answered before the second knock.

"Come in, Mr. Corbin."

She had been working on his name for the duration of the phone call and had decided, somewhere between *Corbin-Vasik* and *Corbinster,* to fall back on *Mr. Corbin,* which was correct in the way that a man's middle name with *Mr.* in front of it was correct. He let it go. The array catalogued her face whether he wanted it to or not: *83% Cantonese, 11% Hakka, 6% noise in the orbital geometry, scar on the left thumb-base consistent with a slip with a cleaver at age nine or ten in a kitchen with poor task lighting.* The read was just there. He had stopped saying the count out loud at nineteen. He had not stopped registering it.

The apartment was three rooms and a curtain. The front room had two folding chairs, a low table, and a television last turned on the year before last and now serving as the surface on which the family kept clementines, envelopes, and a small carved wooden frog the size of a thumb. The husband was in the corner. A wicker chair with electrical tape over two breaks in the cane. He sat with his weight off the broken places like a man who had been sitting like this for twenty-nine days and had stopped expecting the rest of the chair back.

His jaw was wired. Field-clinic work, titanium four-point, the kind a Gray Zone doctor put in when there was no budget for plates and there was not going to be any budget for plates either. The skin over the lower mandible had healed inside-out in places — the wire had been pulled tight before the swelling went down. He breathed through his nose, slow, even, the way a man breathed who had been breathing through his nose since March.

"My husband's name is Liu." Mrs. Chen said it as if it might matter. It did. It mattered to her. "He was a cook. He was a very good cook."

Liu blinked once at Kyle. The blink was the bow. Kyle returned it, smaller, the way men returned bows in rooms where speech was not an even option for everybody present.

"The men who came," Mrs. Chen said.

"Yes."

"There were six. The one I want is named Tien." She gestured with one hand at her husband, small and economical, the gesture of a woman who did not want to enlarge the wound by pointing at it. "He hit my husband three times with the back of a wrench. The first time on the side of the head. The second on the jaw. The third on the jaw also. After the third he was satisfied."

"Why."

"The license."

She said it as if she had been certain Kyle would understand the word *license* in the specific sense it was used in this building on this floor. He did. The stall license was three thousand QUANTA a year. The license was issued through Carrion Logistics LLC. Mrs. Chen had probably never met an officer of Carrion Logistics LLC in her life. She had met Tien. Tien was the part of Carrion Logistics LLC her transaction surface touched. This was how the CorpoNation world arranged itself everywhere it had reached, which was everywhere — abstract on top, a man with a wrench at the bottom.

"You were short this month."

"Eight hundred."

"Why."

"The cold snap. We ran the fryer harder; the gas bill cleared on the third. The eight hundred would have been in the box on the third. Tien came on the second."

"On purpose."

"On purpose."

The second channel, on the band Kyle had stopped subscribing to several years ago, said: *the bill is the bill, the boy is the bill, the bill is the boy.* It was not Kyle's phrasing. He held *Vasik* down with the back of his molars and the line passed. Somewhere in the firmware substrate a dead test subject was rephrasing the world for him in their own diction. He had stopped trying to identify them by twenty-two. There were too many.

"He offered to forgive the eight hundred."

"On what terms."

"Terms I did not accept."

"All right."

"My husband — on the floor — he agreed with me. He could not speak. He agreed with his eyes. Then Tien did the third time with the wrench. The third time was because we had both said no. So that we would tell other women, and they would tell their husbands, and the next time Tien came everyone would already know what no cost."

"Pedagogy."

"Yes."

A figure moved behind the curtain into the second room. The breathing pattern had been there since the hallway. Kyle had filed it under *child, female, twelve to fourteen, has been doing this for some time.* The curtain shifted now and a face appeared at it. The jaw was her father's, before the wrench. The eyes were her mother's. The expression was not any one of the expressions a child should be having to make. She did not come in. She did not retreat. She watched.

Kyle did not look at her directly. Looking at her would put her in the conversation. The conversation was not for her.

Mrs. Chen had not turned. Mrs. Chen knew her daughter was there. Mrs. Chen had decided, possibly tonight, possibly six years ago, that *daughter sees what daughter sees* was a discipline of a kind she could not protect against and would not try to.

The damp credits came out of Mrs. Chen's apron in a thin stack, banded with a hair-elastic. She pressed them into Kyle's palm with both hands. Warm. The warmth was the warmth of the hand. She had been holding them all evening waiting for the knock. He felt the weight and ran the count. Six hundred and forty QUANTA. Half the standing rate.

"I do not have all of it, Mr. Corbin."

"It is enough."

"I do not have all of it because—"

"Mrs. Chen. It is enough."

She had the specific exhaustion of a woman who had practiced the sentence seven times and had now been told the sentence was not required. The relief did not arrive in her face. It arrived in her hands — they stopped trembling, second knuckle of the index finger first, the thumbs after, the rest of the hand on a half-second delay, the way circulation returned to a limb. The array filed the order of return because the array filed everything.

"You will be careful, Mr. Corbin."

"I will be efficient."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

She bowed. He bowed. The bows were the contract. She told him the rest of what he had come to know — the address at the bottom of the eastern strut, the side door that was never locked because nobody was foolish enough to walk in the side door, the front door that was never locked because nobody was foolish enough to walk in the front, the schedule of when Tien drank and when Tien slept and when Tien did the thing he did instead of work. All of which Kyle had already cross-referenced against Sable's last week's update on Carrion Logistics LLC roster movement in this strip of the Gray Zone.

He had known where Tien would be before he had climbed the stairs.

He had come anyway because Mrs. Chen needed to give him the address. The address was the gift she had to deliver in order to be the person who delivered it.

He left through the door he had come in. The freight elevator was where he had left it. On the ride down, the channel said *the bill is the bill, the boy is the bill,* and Kyle said, aloud to the empty cage, "no," and the channel went quiet for the rest of the descent. The boy from the Grind held. He always did. Eventually.

The bike was where he had left it, two blocks east, under a tier-six awning that did most of the work of keeping it dry. Matte black, unbranded. The plate currently displayed read like a plate issued to a Carrion Logistics LLC subcontractor in 2206, which was a useful fiction because the Gray Zone's surveillance grid was a forty-year-old CorpoNation cast-off that would let a Carrion plate go anywhere a Carrion plate had ever been. He had three plates magnetized to the underside of the rear cowl. He swapped them by feel.

He rode south. The route to the bottom of the eastern strut was twenty-two minutes of switchbacks down the freight ramps the CorpoNations had stopped maintaining around the second tier rezoning. Each ramp dropped him about eight stories. By the third ramp the architecture had stopped pretending to be architecture and had become what it actually was, which was welded industrial grief. By the fifth ramp the rain was warmer — upper-tier rain came straight from cloud, lower-tier rain had been through six tiers of HVAC exhaust on the way down and had been re-vaporized and re-condensed twice. He did not roll his visor up. The rain on the visor was rain.

A VTOL crossed the gap between two towers a long way overhead, running lights amber, climbing toward the CorpoNation roof traffic stratum. Some board director was late to dinner. Forty stories below the board director, in a stairwell that smelled like ammonia, a child whose grandmother had died because the building's stair lighting did not work was learning to read the dark. These two facts were the city. Kyle did not file the running lights. He had stopped filing the running lights at some point in his twenties. The rich travelled rich. He travelled the way he travelled.

The Pulse hub at the Lakefront emitted its low-frequency commute hum, a Mach-six slug bound for Rotterdam by the timetable. Rotterdam was a memory the Pulse called by name now without anybody remembering why. Kyle had been to the Pulse station twice in his life. He had not been on a slug. The slugs were not for him. He was not for the slugs. The arrangement was satisfactory to both parties.

His left forearm itched under the bracer. The corundum strop sat against the inner forearm — a thumb-thin plate of corundum on a carbon-fiber shell, two titanium-wire braid loops, designed by Seo, made by Seo, given to Kyle by Seo on a Tuesday in 2199 along with the sword. The strop had no electronics. It had a face of corundum and a piece of carbon-fiber behind it and that was all it had. The work it did was kinetic.

When Silence's flat passed across the corundum on the draw, the corundum produced a crisp high-frequency stress pulse that propagated down the blade length. The PZT shingane harvested the pulse and the blade came out of the saya at baseline charge. The gesture, to anyone watching, looked like the cleaning motion done at the wrong end of the cut — a sloppy chiburi performed on the draw instead of the sheath. The kind of mistake an under-trained street swordsman would make.

Seo had built it that way on purpose.

*They will think you are sloppy,* he had said, in the room behind his repair shop that they had used as a dojo, when Kyle was eighteen. *They will adjust for sloppy. They will not adjust for what you are doing.*

Seo had been correct about that. Seo had been correct about a great many things and had been killed by the things he had been correct about, which was the kind of math the city did to people who tried to live by precision.

Kyle parked the bike a block out and walked.

The loading dock sat at the bottom of the eastern strut where the strut met the tier-zero substrate and the substrate was just concrete without any further architectural pretense. Six bays. Two roller doors open. The light inside was the amber sodium of overheads installed sometime before LED was a word, which made everything look like a polaroid in a kitchen drawer.

Six men. Card game on a crate. They were playing pai gow with hand-marked tiles instead of cards because somebody had spilled something on the cards a week ago and the cards had not been replaced, and the tiles were what was on hand. Tien was winning. Tien was always winning, because Tien beat the others when Tien lost, and the others had figured this out and had agreed, quietly, to let Tien win. None of this was on the dock floor where Kyle could read it; the array offered him the dynamic anyway, on inference from posture, betting pattern, and the specific tension in the wrists of the two men Tien had cleaned out last round.

The two chrome-arm men were leaning against a stack of dollies. Hydraulic Solutions ARM-9 series by the wrist articulation pattern — corporate-grade arms, fifteen to eighteen thousand QUANTA a side, the kind of arms a man wore when he had earned them in service to a CorpoNation he no longer worked for and was reluctant to part with. Of the four seated, one had subdermal thoracic plating, visible as faint geometric ridges along the collar where the seam between dermal layers had not healed correctly — *low-end clinic, fast install, no aftercare.* One had chrome knuckles and a face the array put at nineteen, give or take, augmentation strictly cosmetic and very fresh. One had no visible work done, which meant whatever he had was internal, which meant expensive, which meant he had made decisions.

One had a face that had been rebuilt cheap and tight on the left side. The skin pulled the wrong way over the lower mandible. The parts of his face had run out somewhere along the line.

Kyle stepped across the threshold.

He came in through the front because surprise was free, and because there was a particular moment he had come for — that specific moment when six men who had spent six weeks feeling like weather looked up and saw something worse than weather standing in their doorway, and the moment was worth the trip.

The laughter died at the tempo laughter always died when the math changed in a room. It died last in the chrome-knuckle kid, who was nineteen and had not yet learned to read tempo.

The no-visible-work man set his tiles face down on the crate.

"You lost, friend?"

"Looking for Tien."

The boss looked at Tien. Tien looked at the boss. The boss looked back at Kyle. The boss had decided to make this take one more sentence than it strictly needed to, which was the decision of a man who had not yet been told that the math had finished resolving.

"Tien is occupied."

"He won't be."

The boss did the thing with his head that men did when they wanted the room to know something interesting was happening. The chrome-knuckle kid's hand began to move toward his waistband, the motion telegraphed at the shoulder a full half-second before the hand committed. The two chrome-arm men shifted their weight a quarter inch each, hydraulic shoulders waking the actuator buses. The subdermal-plated man set his tiles down. Tien — Tien sat very still and watched, which was Tien's correct response, because Tien had made it to thirty-four in this work by knowing exactly which fights were his and exactly which fights were not.

Kyle's chest warmed. Not the bracer. The array.

A coal blown to life behind the sternum. Thirty-two thousand electrodes coming up from rest cycle to operating cycle. The room rendered itself in the second channel. Six bodies. Six firing solutions. The micro-contraction of the kid's forearm flexor. The angle of the saya inside the boss's coat — visible to the array, invisible to anyone who did not know to look for the way the fabric was draping. The boss had a sword. The boss was carrying a katana inside his coat at a card game on a Tuesday night, which was the kind of choice a man made when a man was expecting somebody specific to come through the door.

The boss had been expecting somebody. The boss had been expecting *Kyle.*

It clicked into place the way these things clicked into place. Somebody had paid the boss to be here tonight, with a sword, prepared. Somebody had been watching Kyle long enough to know that the Chens were about to call him. Somebody had wanted to know what would happen if a man who had specifically trained against Silence stood in front of Silence.

The second channel filed it.

He would think about it later.

"Kyle from down the street," Kyle said, in answer to a question the boss had not asked yet.

The boss smiled. The smile was the smile of a man who had been waiting for this moment and now had it. The boss had read the literature. The boss had paid money to fight this fight. The boss was about to find out what the money had bought.

Chorus came out of the holster in Kyle's left hand.

Four shots in three seconds. Buckshot at the chrome-knuckle kid's right knee — the joint did not bend, it separated; he went down sideways and the sound he made was not screaming because screaming required composure. Slug at the subdermal man's left knee, because his entire fighting philosophy ended at his chest. Slug at the near chrome-arm man's right knee, because the hydraulics did not load-bear on the leg. Slug at Tien — left knee — because that was the face Kyle wanted to meet the floor at an angle that would loosen teeth.

The far chrome-arm man had begun his swing in the half-second between the first shot and the second.

Kyle had budgeted for that.

He stepped under the arc — the arm passed at the height where his collarbone had been — and brought Silence up. The blade cleared the saya, right hand over the right shoulder, the flat passing across the corundum strop on the inner left forearm at the bottom of the draw.

The strop did its work. The PZT shingane harvested the pulse. The blade came out at baseline charge.

To the boss, who was watching for the flare that the literature said Silence produced on the draw, the gesture read as a corrupted chiburi. A sloppy cleaning motion at the wrong end of the cut.

The boss's smile widened a fraction.

*Sloppy draw,* his face said. *I have got him.*

He had not got him.

Kyle put Silence's edge into the chrome-arm man's shoulder seam where chrome met flesh. Three and a half centimeters of unprotected actuator housing. The edge passed through the gap. The arm did not stop; the arm finished its swing into empty air without its owner. The motor bus was severed at the shoulder. The rest of the man's body did what bodies did when the load-bearing chrome stopped sending signals.

Three seconds. Four shots. One sword cut. Five men down. Four kneecaps still bleeding out their respective contracts with the floor.

Tien, face-down in the drainage channel, made a noise that was not really a noise.

Kyle turned.

The boss had stood up.

He had not drawn during the count. He had stood, unhurried, with the calm economy of a man who had watched his crew dismantle on schedule. The hand that went to his hip came back with a katana. The saya had been inside the coat. The blade came out at the right angle in the right tempo, point neither high nor low, weight settling into a guard that was not borrowed from any school Kyle could name. He had been trained. Somewhere expensive. Specifically against blades of the kind he had been told Silence was.

"Sloppy draw, kid."

The boss said it like a teacher correcting homework. The boss had paid a lot of money for the right to say that line in this room tonight. The boss was going to die before he found out what the line had actually cost him.

Kyle did not say anything.

He had four exchanges in him.

*First exchange — read the line.* A short cut at Kyle's right shoulder, no commitment in it, the kind of strike a swordsman used to read his opponent's reach and tempo. Silence rose. The blades touched edge to flat at a shallow angle. Steel rang in concrete. The boss read his information and stepped back without expression. He had also wanted to confirm something else — some signature he had been told to watch for, some flare or pulse or visible change in the blade — and what he had been told to watch for had not arrived. His face did not show this. His weight stayed off the front foot. But the guard moved a quarter inch in a way the array catalogued and filed.

He was already, in his head, writing his after-action report. Kyle could see it in him.

*Second exchange — Kyle pressed.* Two-step advance, the cut coming high and right. The boss met it with a deflection rather than a parry. The literature said you did not take edge-to-edge contact with Silence because edge-to-edge contact was when the bad thing happened. There was no bad thing. The boss did not know that and would not learn that in the time he had remaining. He slid Silence's edge along his blade's flat to redirect the force past his shoulder. Smart. Conservative. The boss had paid for the smart conservative answer. The smart conservative answer was correct against a sword that did the thing the literature said Silence did. Silence did not do the thing the literature said it did. Silence was a sword.

*Third exchange — the boss committed.* He cut high left, fast, clean. Kyle's parry was a fraction late because the array's micro-tremor read had been clean but the boss's body had been clean in return. The follow-through grazed Kyle's left forearm — three inches of skin opened above the strop, not deep, vein not artery, would impair grip on the shotgun side in approximately ninety seconds. The array tagged it without comment.

The boss saw the blood and read it as evidence that he was correct about everything. *I am inside his range. I landed first. The exotic countermeasure I trained against has not arrived. I have read the literature correctly.*

He chose this interpretation because the alternative was that he had spent money training against a weapon whose mythology had outpaced its mechanism by thirty years, and that interpretation was not the kind of interpretation a man chose mid-duel.

*Fourth exchange — the thrust.* Straight, committed, full extension, the line clean. Silence came down to meet it not at the edge and not at the flat but at the seppa — the small collar between the tsuba and the blade — and the thrust caught there, blade to blade, locked. Kyle drove forward. He kept the lock. He let Silence's edge ride along the flat of the boss's blade like a skate on ice, the edge tracking from tip toward middle toward tsuba, and where the boss's blade met the boss's hand, the edge of Silence met the boss's right wrist.

Bone. Tendon. The radial and ulnar arteries. The median nerve. The carpal architecture that had held a sword for whatever number of years the boss had been training for this duel.

The edge passed through. The angle was right and the line was committed.

The boss's right hand and the boss's katana fell together to the corrugated steel.

The boss looked at his wrist. The boss looked at his sword on the floor. The boss looked at Kyle.

"Oh," the boss said.

The brachial did most of the work. He bled out in nineteen seconds. The array timed it. The array timed everything.

Kyle's hands were steady. They would shake in four minutes. He had learned to use the window.

Twenty-three seconds for the duel.

Twenty-six on the dock floor, end to end.

He sheathed Silence. A thin line of red on the edge near the kissaki; he wiped it against the boss's coat before the blade seated. Chorus went back into the holster. Four chambers spent. One slug in the cylinder. He registered the count the way other people registered the time.

The boss's face, the array offered him on closing — for the file, because the array did not have a setting for *do not file* — was 71% Korean, 22% Han, 7% noise. The literature on Silence had been published in Seoul. The training had been bought in Seoul. The boss had been sourced in Seoul. Somebody, somewhere — and Kyle would think about this later, after the noodles, after the sleep he was going to fail to get — had spent money in Seoul to buy a Korean swordsman to come to Chicago to fight a samurai who lived above a noodle stall.

Whoever that somebody was, they had spent the money for the lesson.

The lesson was now ambient on the floor of a loading dock in the Gray Zone at 0157 in the morning, and the somebody, wherever they were, was about to learn what the money had bought them.

Tien was crying. Not screaming. Crying. He had figured out by the third kneecap shot that he was not going to die tonight, and the prospect of *not dying tonight* in his current condition was now striking him as worse than the alternative.

Kyle moved through the bodies, methodical. He was looking for the third tooth.

He had two of them in his left hand already — two loose ones, recovered with a thumb and forefinger from the steel where Tien's face had landed. The third was still seated. He found Tien's mouth and, with the careful neutrality of a man pulling a nail out of a board, gripped the third tooth and turned. It came free with a sound that was not really a sound. Tien sobbed once, briefly, and stopped, because what he was sobbing about was no longer at the top of the list of things he had to deal with.

Kyle stood up with the teeth in his hand.

He had decided what he was going to do with them on the ride down, twenty-two minutes, somewhere around the fifth switchback. The thinking had taken approximately one minute. The remaining twenty-one minutes of the ride down had been spent verifying that the thinking was correct and that he had not missed an option. He had not missed an option. The option was correct.

Across the dock, behind the ventilation unit on the far wall, the breathing pattern Kyle had tracked since the threshold shifted. Twelve, maybe thirteen. The breathing had been there in Unit 802 too. She had come down. She had taken the freight elevator, or she had taken the stairs and the elevator separately, or she had asked Carrion Logistics LLC nicely. He did not know how she had done it. He did not need to know.

She had come to see.

Kyle walked to the ventilation unit. He did not go behind it. He stood where she could see him — a tall, blood-misted shape in the dock's bad amber — and he waited.

The breathing stopped.

Then a slow movement. A face came around the corner of the unit. The jaw was her father's, before the wrench. The eyes were her mother's. The expression was a child's expression that had not been issued to a child before tonight.

Kyle crouched. He went down to her eye level the way he went down to a child's eye level whenever the situation gave him room to. The discipline that said *protect the vulnerable* had also said, in Seo's voice, *be at their height when you speak to them.* Kyle did not know whether Seo had originated this or had quoted it. Seo had quoted a lot of people and had not always cited.

Kyle opened his hand.

The three teeth lay in his palm. They were warm. Two were intact. The third had a hairline fracture from where he had gripped it. The smell of blood was the smell of blood.

The girl looked at his hand. Then at his face. Then back at his hand. She had not yet processed what he was offering her, because what he was offering her did not have a category.

"Present these to your father." His voice was even. He had practiced even for a long time. "I think he will appreciate the gesture."

The girl looked at the teeth. Then at him. Then at the teeth. Then at him.

Kyle was, for one brief second, on the edge of saying *eye for an eye* — the second channel was offering him the phrase, in the diction of a dead test subject who had clearly grown up in a Sunday school somewhere — and he did not say it, because the joke was funnier if you did not write it across the chalkboard.

She held out her hands. Cupped.

He poured the teeth into her cupped palms.

She did not drop them.

She shrieked. High, thin, a sound that bounced off every hard surface in the dock and came back changed. She did not drop them. Her fingers closed over them. She looked at her closed hands. She looked at him.

And then, slowly, deliberately, with a gravity that was not in any culture Kyle could name but which her body seemed to know on its own — she bowed.

A full bow. From the waist. Held for two seconds. Her hands stayed cupped at her chest the entire time.

The bow of a child who had just been handed an inheritance she had not asked for and was not yet sure what was going to be expected of her in the carrying of it.

Then she was gone, swallowed by the dark beyond the door, her feet making no sound on the steel because she had been raised in a building where you learned, by age five, not to make sound on steel.

Kyle stood for three seconds.

The second channel said, in a register that was not Kyle's, *that one will outlive most of us,* and Kyle did not recognize the diction at all this time — it was three subjects deep, possibly four, the syntax suggesting a vocabulary Kyle did not own and had never owned. He did not have the bio-battery to chase it. He let it pass.

Behind him, Tien was still crying.

Kyle cleaned the blade.

The tremor started in his right hand at the four-minute mark, moved to his left, became briefly bilateral — the array's invoice arriving, several thousand calories called in twenty-six seconds, the body presenting the bill. His stomach was a closed fist. He needed protein, carbohydrate, something hot in a bowl, something that required no decisions. He needed to sit somewhere that smelled like something other than what this smelled like.

He walked back to the bike. The Gray Zone's night-architecture closed around him — flickering overheads at the ramp intersection, the drip of recycled water from the tier above, the distant compressor whine of a HVAC stack two blocks over running its end-of-shift cycle.

A second VTOL crossed the gap forty stories up, running lights amber. Some board director was still late to dinner.

Kyle did not file the running lights.

The bike started on the first turn. He swapped the plate two blocks short of the Wicker boundary, where the surveillance grid changed character, and continued north on a different fiction.

## Part II — Standard Rate

Chen's stall was two blocks south of the freight tier and one alley off the main, and the warm orange of its hood lamp was visible through the rain from a block away the way a hearth was visible through the trees in the kind of children's book Kyle had not read but had heard described.

He walked the bike the last block. The bike rolled easier at walking pace than it rode at speed because the front wheel was true and the bearings were clean and he had spent two hours that afternoon repairing a chain tensioner that he could have replaced for forty QUANTA, and forty QUANTA was the price of two of Mrs. Chen's bowls. This was not a hierarchy Kyle had thought through. It was simply how the math came out.

She was at the counter when he arrived. The apron was clean — a service apron, not the kitchen apron she had been wearing in the apartment. The kitchen apron had bone broth fat on the front. The service apron had no fat on the front because the service apron did not deal with the stockpot, only with the bowls and the counter and the customers. The apron she had worn at home in front of her wired-jaw husband had been a third apron, and Kyle now retroactively confirmed its existence because Chen Wei-Lin was a woman who would not have offered service in a kitchen apron and would not have sat with her husband in a service apron either.

The small scar at the base of her left thumb caught the lamp light at an angle the array catalogued without making the catalogue mean anything yet. The daughter was somewhere he could not see. She had taken some route Kyle had not mapped from the loading dock back to here, faster than the bike, which meant she had either run through the airshafts or had been on a private route Carrion Logistics LLC ran for its hourly couriers and had been allowed to use without anybody asking. The daughter was a freight system unto herself. Kyle filed this for later.

She did not greet him. She set a bowl down on the counter — pork bone broth, the long-simmered kind, the kind that took fourteen hours to render properly and which Mrs. Chen made in a stockpot that had been in her family for two husbands and four children and three cities, simmering at the back of the stall on a propane burner she had bought used in 2218 and had maintained herself ever since. The chili oil was already in the bowl, the way she would remember he liked it for the next four years. She ladled with three quick dips of the ladle into the stockpot — once to skim the surface, once to stir to the bottom, once to draw — and the third dip was the one she poured. The first two dips were a ritual she had not been taught and which she had refined without thinking about it across thirty-one years of running stalls in three cities.

He sat on the stool.

The stool was the right height for him. She had not adjusted it. He had not adjusted it. The geometry simply was what it was the first time.

He sat. Kyle ate standing up everywhere else. At Mrs. Chen's, he sat. Not because she had offered him the stool. Because the stool had been offered, and the discipline of accepting what had been offered was the discipline he had begun the moment Mrs. Chen had brought the bowl out.

He ate. The broth was hot. He ate the whole bowl and did not speak. She did not refill it. She did not move. When he set the chopsticks down she was still standing exactly where she had been when she set the bowl. She was looking at him the way she had looked at the chips she had pressed into his hand six hours ago. She had more she wanted to give and no language for the giving, and she was now finding the language.

Cantonese first, low. *Mh̄gōi.* Two syllables doing the work of an entire ledger. Then, in the tongue she had chosen to run the stall in because she did not assume every customer spoke hers:

"Free. Always. For you. Forever."

Her hands were flat on the counter beside the bowl. Her scarred thumb was the closest thing to him on the wood. She had just offered him the only thing she owned.

The second channel said: *she means it. she is not negotiating. she does not understand that she is offering you a thing you cannot accept.*

Kyle reached into his coat and set a credit chip on the counter beside her hand. Standard rate. The kind of standard rate a man paid when he wanted the transaction to be a transaction and not the beginning of an obligation he had spent his life refusing to accumulate.

"I pay," he said. "Every bowl. Standard rate. Every time."

She did not pick up the chip. He did not pick up the bowl. The standoff lasted four seconds and was the most important four seconds of their relationship, and they both knew it without naming it.

She tried again, slower, the way you tried again with a man you had decided not to insult by giving up on the first refusal.

"You took the contract at half. You did the work whole. The bowl is nothing. Forever is nothing."

*Forever is everything,* said the second channel. *Forever is the largest thing she has and she is offering it because she does not yet know how large it is.*

Kyle did not move the chip. He looked at her in the way the discipline allowed him to look at a woman who had just tried to give him something he could not, in good conscience, accept. The blade was the discipline. The bowl was the discipline. The credit chip was the discipline.

"If I do not pay, I do not eat. If I do not eat here, I do not eat. I would rather pay you than not see you again. The chip stays."

The four seconds became five.

Her hand closed over the chip the way her daughter's hand had closed over the teeth — not refusing the gift, accepting that the gift was the form the man in front of her needed the gift to take.

She bowed. Not the convulsive bow of the apartment six hours ago. A smaller bow. The bow of a vendor accepting a customer's price. The kind of bow she would give him every night, roughly three hundred nights a year, for the next four years and longer.

He stood. He bowed back, smaller still — the bow of a man who had just successfully refused a kindness larger than he could carry.

"Tomorrow. Same bowl. Same price."

"Same price."

The agreement was the contract. The contract was the bond.

He turned and walked the bike out of the stall's lamp circle. The rain had not stopped. It was not going to stop tonight. The towers leaned in overhead the way they did this far down, and the gap between them was full of the city's wet patient light.

He laughed.

Not the laugh the action movies he had never watched had taught the audience to expect. A single tight exhalation. The laugh of a man who had just seen the joke and had decided that the joke was funny anyway, the kind of laugh you laughed in private walking a bike up a slope toward Wicker in the rain because the alternative was the alternative.

The joke was this:

He had walked into a room of six men who would have killed an ordinary person for trying what he had just tried, and he had walked out, and he was carrying a sword that the literature said did something it did not do, and he was about to refuse free noodles from a woman whose entire life he had just rearranged, and he was going to do it because honor was a discipline and the discipline did not bend for noodles, and he was going to do it knowing perfectly well that every reasonable observer would conclude he had lost his mind.

He had not lost his mind.

He was just the exception to the rule.

The rule said: a man performing samurai discipline in 2226 will be killed for it inside a year. The rule was correct. The rule was correct about almost everybody. It was not correct about Kyle, and the reason the rule was not correct about Kyle was that Kyle was very good at the discipline, and the city — for all its talk — turned out to have not yet built a counter for *very good at the discipline.* It had built a counter for the literature. It had not built a counter for the work.

He was going to die at some point. Probably soon. Probably for reasons that had nothing to do with whether he was good at it.

In the meantime, the noodles had been the right temperature, and the chip had stayed on the counter, and the rain was warm at this tier, and the bike was running clean, and he was, this evening, between contracts, having just refused a kindness so that he could afford to keep being offered the kindness, and the world was still the world but it was, this evening, the world at a tempo he could ride.

He laughed once more, smaller, almost to himself.

He rode home.

## Part III — The Watcher

Somebody had been watching the loading-dock work.

The camera was on the second floor of the building across the alley, in a window that had been boarded over with plywood for the last eleven years, and a panel of the plywood had been replaced with a panel that looked like the rest of the plywood but was not. The camera saw through it. The camera was running on a private feed that did not touch the Carrion Logistics LLC grid. Carrion Logistics LLC did not know about the camera. Nobody on the dock knew about the camera. The boy from the Grind, who had spent his childhood being measured by people whose names he was never told, knew about the camera the same way Kyle knew about a draft — by feel, with no source.

The watcher noted the pace. The watcher noted that Kyle's hand did not move toward the saya at any point during the walk back to the bike. The watcher noted that his shoulders did not square, and that the breathing telemetry the watcher had access to — and they had access; organizations like this had access — stayed at the rest cycle. The watcher noted, in particular, the moment Kyle had walked Silence's edge through the chrome-arm man's shoulder seam without any visible flare, glow, or arc, and the watcher's report would treat that as a calibration data point, because the literature on Silence had been wrong about a lot of things, and the watcher's organization had been paying for the literature, and the watcher's organization preferred their literature to be correct.

The watcher noted one further thing.

On a higher-grade microphone than anyone on the dock had known was present, the watcher caught Kyle muttering a phrase, three syllables, between the wrist cut and the sheathing of the blade. The phrase was not in any language Kyle spoke. The watcher's audio analyst would run it against eleven candidate corpora and would get back an eighty-one percent confidence match to an extinct dialect of Tocharian that had not been spoken in a thousand years.

The watcher would not include this in the first report. The watcher would not include it in the second report either. The watcher would file it separately, on the encrypted side of the personal log, because the watcher had been doing this work for fifteen years and had learned that the things you filed separately were the things that turned out, eventually, to be the answer.

The first report would be reviewed two months later by Hua, the south-arm cell captain of the Lotus Syndicate, who would close the file and pass it to her gang lieutenant Mira along with a second file labeled *Bucktown — first interview — fourteen-piece.* The second file would lead to the third, and the third to the fourth.

The Tocharian was not in the report Hua read.

It would not surface again for some time.

Kyle rode home. The noodles would be cold by morning. They always were.

He did not yet know that he had begun to be assembled into a shape that was no longer his own, by people who had been watching since before Mrs. Chen had called, in a longer pattern that ran underneath every contract he would take for the next eleven months and would not announce itself even when it finished. The city did things on long clocks. He was on one of them. He did not know it. He would, eventually, know parts of it. He would not, ever, know all of it.

But that was the work of years.

Tonight was the work of one night.

Tonight, the work was done.
