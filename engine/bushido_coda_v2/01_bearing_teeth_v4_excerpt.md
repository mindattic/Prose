# Bearing Teeth — Part I — Teeth (excerpt only)

The freight elevator was the only thing in the building that worked.

The resident elevator had been there once. There was a shaft, and there were doors on
every floor, and a piece of paper taped to one of them that said OUT OF SERVICE in three
languages, none of them recent. The CorpoNation that had built the resident elevator
had filed for bankruptcy in 2207 and the CorpoNation that had bought the bankrupt
CorpoNation had been bought by a third CorpoNation, which was, by 2226, the body-pickup
federation Carrion Logistics LLC. Carrion Logistics LLC did not maintain the resident
elevator. Carrion Logistics LLC also did not maintain the bodies. The naming conventions
of the late CorpoNation era are best left to the historians, but suffice it to say
that the people in charge of carrying you home alive turned out, fifteen years later, to
also be the people in charge of carrying you home dead, and the freight elevator was
the only thing in the building that worked.

(This will be more fully explored at a later time.)

Kyle rode the freight up. The cage was steel grating with a fine carpet of pulverized
concrete on the floor — somebody had moved a refrigerator, or somebody had moved a man,
or somebody had moved both and the man had complained less. The light on the elevator
ceiling was a flickering LED that had been the wrong color since 2218 and bathed
everything in the specific institutional green of a hospital cafeteria at 3 AM. The
elevator went up at a speed the elevator had decided on without consulting anybody. The
elevator was, in this respect, no different from anyone else in the building.

The airshaft brought the building's news up the side of the cage like a wet radio.
Camphor from the second floor — somebody's grandmother, somebody's joints, somebody had
been rubbing her with a salve since the cold snap. Burnt rice from the fifth, the same
burnt rice every Thursday — Mr. Yu, eighty-three, refused to use a timer because his
mother had not used a timer, and his rice had been burnt every Thursday since 2189,
which Mr. Yu considered a tradition and his neighbors considered an indictment.

And tonight, from somewhere between four and seven, the smell of Chen Wei-Lin's deep
fryer.

Pork bone. Scallion. Oil that had not been changed since the lunar new year because
the oil was the seasoning.

It would later strike Kyle as a clean little parable. The work was always already there
before you got to it. The smell was the contract.

He stepped off the elevator on the eighth floor and the building knew his answer before
he did.

Unit 802 was at the end of the hall behind a door that had been painted four different
times at four different attempts at the color white. Mrs. Chen answered before the
second knock.

"Come in, Mr. Corbin."

She had been working on his name for the duration of the phone call and had decided,
somewhere between *Corbin-Vasik* and *Corbinster,* to fall back on *Mr. Corbin,* which
was correct in the way that a man's middle name with *Mr.* in front of it was correct.
He let it go. The array catalogued her face whether he wanted it to or not: 83%
Cantonese, 11% Hakka, 6% noise in the orbital geometry, scar on the left thumb-base
consistent with a slip with a cleaver at nine or ten in a kitchen with poor task
lighting.

(He had stopped saying the count out loud at nineteen, after a particular evening at a
particular bar where a particular Vietnamese-Senegalese stevedore had taken offense to
being assigned percentages, which had been an instructive evening for everyone involved
and especially for Kyle's right eyebrow, which still did not sit quite level. He had
not, however, stopped doing the count. The hardware did not have a setting for *do not
count.*)

The apartment was three rooms and a curtain. The front room had two folding chairs, a
low table, and a television that had not turned on since the year before last and which
was now serving as the surface on which the family kept clementines, envelopes, and a
small carved wooden frog the size of a thumb. The frog had a chip out of its left back
leg. Kyle had no information about how the frog had lost the chip out of its left back
leg. He suspected the daughter.

The husband was in the corner. Wicker chair, two breaks in the cane, both wrapped in
electrical tape. He sat with his weight off the broken places like a man who had been
sitting like that for twenty-nine days and had stopped expecting the rest of the chair
back.

His jaw was wired.

Specifically: titanium four-point, the kind a Gray Zone field clinic put in when there
was no budget for plates and there was not going to be any budget for plates either.
The skin over the lower mandible had healed inside-out in places. The wire had been
pulled tight before the swelling went down, which was the kind of detail that field
clinics in the Gray Zone got wrong twelve to fifteen percent of the time, depending on
who was on shift, and the kind of detail the patient discovered on day three when the
swelling went down and the wire stayed tight. He breathed through his nose, slow,
even — the way a man breathed who had been breathing through his nose since March, who
had performed his first noseful of breath at the field clinic on the recovery couch and
was now performing his ten-thousandth without any noticeable improvement in technique.

"My husband's name is Liu." Mrs. Chen said it as if it might matter. It did. It
mattered to her. "He was a cook. He was a very good cook."

Liu blinked once at Kyle. The blink was the bow. Kyle returned it, smaller, in the way
men returned bows in rooms where speech was not an even option for everybody present.

The light in the apartment was the color of weak tea, coming from a single overhead
fixture that had been wired into the airshaft duct fifteen years ago by a previous
tenant who had not been an electrician but had been confident, and the apartment had
been lit at the color of weak tea ever since because the bulb the previous tenant had
installed was the only bulb the fixture would accept, and the bulb had been a 2210
clearance item from a closed Wicker hardware store, and there were forty-six of them
left in the world, and Chen Wei-Lin had bought twelve in 2218 and was on her seventh.

A VTOL passed somewhere a long way overhead. Kyle did not see it. The window in the
apartment looked across the canyon at the tower opposite, and the tower opposite was
close enough to read the laundry on its eighth-floor balcony, which was the kind of
laundry that suggested a family of three or possibly four and an aspirational interest
in linen. Behind the laundry, the canyon was full of rain. The rain at this tier was
the warm rain — the upper-tier rain came straight from cloud and was cold, and the
lower-tier rain had been through six tiers of HVAC exhaust on the way down and was
warm — and the rain made its small wet sounds against the metal awning four floors
below them, the specific tinny percussion that the people on the eighth floor had been
hearing for thirty-seven years and had stopped registering as a sound and had begun
registering as the way the city sounded when nothing in particular was happening.

In another window two buildings over, a man was arguing with a ride-share AI about a
routing question. Kyle could not hear him. The argument was visible in the man's
shoulders, which were doing the thing shoulders did when they had been losing a
conversation for four to six minutes. Kyle wished the man well.

"The men who came," Mrs. Chen said.

"Yes."

"There were six. The one I want is named Tien." She gestured with one hand at her
husband, small and economical, the gesture of a woman who did not want to enlarge the
wound by pointing at it. "He hit my husband three times with the back of a wrench. The
first time on the side of the head. The second on the jaw. The third on the jaw also.
After the third he was satisfied."

"Why."

"The license."

She said it as if she had been certain Kyle would understand the word *license* in the
specific sense it was used in this building on this floor. He did. The stall license
was three thousand QUANTA a year, issued through Carrion Logistics LLC, payable in
twelve installments unless you missed an installment, in which case payable in one
installment plus interest plus a visit from a man named Tien, the latter of which was
itemized on the invoice as *Compliance Verification* and which Carrion Logistics LLC's
billing department did not, in this writer's opinion, name with sufficient honesty.

(Carrion Logistics LLC was at the time of this writing the second-largest single
employer in the GLMZ Lower Tiers, the largest being the CorpoNation health system,
which was a separate CorpoNation that had been bought by the same parent CorpoNation,
which was a third CorpoNation, which was Carrion Logistics LLC's grandparent
corporation, which is to say that in the Gray Zone the people you bought your stall
license from and the people who set your jaw afterwards were, ultimately, the same
people, which was the kind of arrangement late capitalism produced with the same
fidelity that a healthy intestine produces stool.)

"You were short this month."

"Eight hundred."

"Why."

"The cold snap. We ran the fryer harder. The gas bill cleared on the third. The eight
hundred would have been in the box on the third. Tien came on the second."

"On purpose."

"On purpose."

A figure moved behind the curtain into the second room. The breathing pattern had been
there since the hallway. Kyle had filed it under *child, female, twelve to fourteen,
has been doing this for some time.* The curtain shifted now and a face appeared at it.
The jaw was her father's, before the wrench. The eyes were her mother's. The expression
was not any one of the expressions a child should be having to make.

She did not come in. She did not retreat. She watched.

Kyle did not look at her directly. Looking at her would put her in the conversation,
and the conversation was not for her.

(More on her later. She is going to be important. Specifically, she is going to be
important in approximately forty-three minutes, although neither she nor Kyle knows
this yet. Kyle is operating on the working theory that she is a witness, and the
working theory is correct, but it is also incomplete in ways the next forty-three
minutes will clarify.)

Mrs. Chen had not turned. Mrs. Chen knew her daughter was there. Mrs. Chen had decided,
possibly tonight, possibly six years ago, that *daughter sees what daughter sees* was
a discipline of a kind Mrs. Chen could not protect against and would not try to.

The damp credits came out of Mrs. Chen's apron in a thin stack, banded with a
hair-elastic. She pressed them into Kyle's palm with both hands. The hair-elastic was
pink. The chip was warm. The warmth was the warmth of the hand. She had been holding
them all evening waiting for the knock. Kyle felt the weight and ran the count: six
hundred and forty QUANTA, half the standing rate, ninety-six QUANTA more than the price
of a one-bedroom Wicker apartment for a week, three orders of pork bone broth and a
glass of cold tea at the stall every weeknight for forty-three weeks.

He did not say any of this. The math was for him.

"I do not have all of it, Mr. Corbin."

"It is enough."

"I do not have all of it because—"

"Mrs. Chen. It is enough."

She had the specific exhaustion of a woman who had practiced the sentence seven times
and had now been told the sentence was not required. The relief did not arrive in her
face. It arrived in her hands — they stopped trembling, second knuckle of the index
finger first, the thumbs after, the rest of the hand on a half-second delay, the way
circulation returned to a limb that had been folded under itself in sleep.

Two stories up, in Unit 1004, somebody started playing a Cantonese pop song from 2218
which had been a hit then and was not a hit now but had been the song the building
played at funerals for the four years it had been a hit, and Kyle could not be entirely
sure whether the person in Unit 1004 had chosen to play it on purpose or whether the
algorithm had served it to them based on some confluence of recent searches the
algorithm did not have permission to disclose.

The song was not, on this occasion, for Liu Chen.

Liu Chen was still alive.

But the song was, in a manner of speaking, on retainer.

"You will be careful, Mr. Corbin."

"I will be efficient."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

She bowed. He bowed. The bows were the contract.
