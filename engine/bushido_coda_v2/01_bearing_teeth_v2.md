# Bearing Teeth

*Protagonist: Kyle Ellen Corbin*

## Part I — Teeth

The freight elevator was the only thing in the building that worked. The resident elevator had been there once. There was a shaft, and there were doors on every floor, and one of the doors on the eighth floor had a piece of paper taped to it that said OUT OF SERVICE in three languages, none of them recent, the corners of the paper curled the way paper curled in a building where humidity was what the building did instead of climate. Kyle rode the freight up. The cage was steel grating and the grating had a fine carpet of pulverized concrete on it from the last load — somebody had moved a refrigerator, or somebody had moved a man, and either way they had brought dust with them.

The building was a welded conversion between two towers, slotted in twelve stories above ground level around 2189 by a contractor who had been bid the job and had done the job and had moved on, and the CorpoNation property registry had quietly removed the conversion from the map two years later to dodge a tax penalty, and ever since then the building had not officially existed and had also continued to be where eighty-six families lived. The math of that did not bother anybody anymore. The smell of the building did. There were thirty-two units stacked four to a floor over eight floors, and each unit had a small flue cut into the back wall feeding the central airshaft, and the airshaft was where the building did all of its thinking out loud. Camphor from the second floor — somebody had a relative who needed it for joint pain. Burnt rice from the fifth, the same burnt rice every Thursday, a smell so reliable Kyle could tell the day of the week from the elevator. And tonight, from somewhere between four and seven, the deep fryer: pork bone, scallion, oil that had been used since around the start of the lunar new year and would not be changed because the oil was the seasoning. Mrs. Chen's stall ran on it.

He was on the eighth floor before he understood that the smell of the stall was coming up the airshaft into the apartment he was about to enter.

Unit 802 was at the end of the hall behind a door painted four times in four different attempts at white. Mrs. Chen answered before the second knock. She did not say his name; she had been working on his name for the duration of the phone call that had set this up and had given up somewhere around the third attempt, and she would now refer to him as *Mr. Corbin*, which was correct in the way that *Mr.* with somebody's middle name was correct. He let it go. The array catalogued her face the way the array catalogued every face Kyle had not turned off the read for, and the read came back without him asking for it: *83% Cantonese, 11% Hakka, 6% noise in the orbital geometry, eyes from somewhere west of the South China Sea three generations back, scar on the left thumb-base consistent with a slip with a cleaver in a kitchen with poor task lighting.* He shelved the read. He had been in her stall once, six hours ago, on the way over, the smell of which was now in her apartment, and the read no longer needed to do work for him in her presence. The hardware ran whether he needed it to or not. He had stopped saying the count out loud at nineteen. He still registered it.

"Come in, please," she said. The English was good. The cadence was a kitchen's cadence — small instructions delivered to small people who had to know exactly what to do next. "There is tea. I am sorry it is only tea."

"It is fine."

The apartment was three rooms and a curtain. The front room was the room. It had two folding chairs and a low table and a television that had not turned on since the year before last and which was now serving as the surface on which the family kept the things the family did not have anywhere else to keep — a bowl of clementines, a stack of envelopes Kyle could not read at the angle he was at, a small carved wooden frog the size of a thumb. The husband was in the corner. Kyle had been told there was a husband, and what had happened to the husband, and so the husband was no surprise to him; the chair the husband was in was a wicker chair the cane of which had broken in two places, and somebody had wrapped the breaks with electrical tape, and the husband sat with his weight off the broken places in a way that suggested he had been sitting like that long enough for it to be how he sat now.

The husband's jaw was wired. Specifically: titanium wire, four-point, the kind a Gray Zone field clinic put in when they did not have the budget for plates and were not going to have the budget for plates either. The skin over the lower mandible had healed inside-out in places — the wire had been pulled tight before the swelling went down. He breathed through his nose, slow and even, the way a man breathes who has been breathing through his nose for twenty-nine days and has stopped expecting his mouth back. He looked at Kyle and did not move. His eyes were tired. He was thirty-four and the tired in his eyes was the tired of a man twice that.

"My husband's name is Liu," Mrs. Chen said. She offered the introduction as if it might matter. It did. It mattered to her, and so it mattered. "He was a cook. He was a very good cook."

Liu blinked once at Kyle. The blink was the bow. Kyle returned it, smaller, in the way men returned bows in rooms where speech was not an even option for everybody present.

"The men who came," Mrs. Chen said.

"Yes."

"There were six. I did not see all of their faces. The one I want—"

She stopped. She put her hand on the back of the second folding chair, the one she was not sitting in. Her knuckles whitened. She was practicing the sentence. She had been practicing the sentence since the call, possibly since the night the sentence had become necessary. Kyle did not help her finish it. The hardware in his skull ran a quiet inventory of her vital signs without his asking — pulse elevated, respiration shallow, micro-tremor in the supporting hand. She was not breaking down. She was building the sentence. He gave her the time the sentence needed.

"The one I want is named Tien. He is the one who did this." She gestured with her free hand at her husband, the gesture small and economical, the gesture of a woman who did not want to enlarge the wound by pointing at it. "He hit my husband three times with the back of a wrench. The first time was on the side of the head. The second was on the jaw. The third was on the jaw also, because the jaw was not yet completely broken. After the third he was satisfied."

"Why."

"The license."

She said it as if she had been certain Kyle would understand the word *license* in the specific sense it was used in this building on this floor in this jurisdiction. He did. The stall license was three thousand QUANTA a year. The license was issued through Carrion Logistics LLC — the same syndicate-friendly intermediary that handled half the unofficial street commerce in this strip of the Gray Zone, the same outfit that, less visibly, ran the body pickup. Mrs. Chen had probably never met an officer of Carrion Logistics LLC in her life. She had met Tien. Tien was the part of Carrion Logistics LLC her transaction surface touched.

"You were short on this month."

"I was short eight hundred."

"Why."

"My husband—" She stopped, and started again. "The gas bill at the stall cleared on the third of the month. The bill was higher this month because the cold snap; we ran the fryer harder; the oil ran longer. The eight hundred would have been in the box on the third. He came on the second."

"On purpose."

"On purpose."

The array catalogued the sequence and offered Kyle, on a channel he had stopped subscribing to several years ago and which the hardware kept open anyway, a phrase that did not belong to him: *the bill is the bill, the boy is the bill, the bill is the boy.* It was not Kyle's phrasing and it was not Kyle's thought. He let it pass. Somewhere in a sealed substrate of the NeoCortex array, a dead test subject was rephrasing the world for him in their own diction. He had learned, by twenty-two, to recognize the texture of the bleed and to keep walking. The boy from the Grind was the anchor. Kyle Vasik. He held that name down with the back of his molars and the thought passed.

"He offered to forgive the eight hundred."

"He offered to forgive the eight hundred."

"On what terms."

She looked at him. He had been told he had grey-green eyes, and there was something gold in the left iris which the array said was the optic-thread bleed and which Pixel said was a tell, and Mrs. Chen looked at his eyes the way a woman looked at eyes when she was deciding how much of the next sentence to spare him because she had decided to spare him some of it.

"On terms I did not accept," she said.

"All right."

"My husband, on the floor — he was on the floor — he agreed with me. He could not speak. He could speak with his eyes. He agreed. Then Tien did the third time with the wrench. He did the third time because we had both said no. The third time was to make sure we understood that no had cost us. So that we would tell other women, and other women would tell their husbands, and the next time Tien came to collect everyone would already know what no cost."

"Pedagogy."

"Yes."

A figure moved behind the curtain into the second room. Kyle had clocked the breathing pattern from the hallway, before Mrs. Chen had opened the door, and had filed it under *child, female, twelve to fourteen, scared, not panicked, has been doing this for some time.* The curtain shifted now and a face appeared at it — the face of a girl who had her father's jaw, the jaw before the wrench, and her mother's eyes, and an expression that was not any one of the expressions a child should be having to make. She did not come in. She did not retreat either. She watched.

Kyle did not look at her directly. Looking at her would put her in the conversation. The conversation was not for her.

Mrs. Chen had not turned. Mrs. Chen knew her daughter was there. Mrs. Chen had decided, possibly tonight, possibly years ago, that *daughter sees what daughter sees* was a discipline of a kind Mrs. Chen could not protect against and would not try to.

The damp credits came out of Mrs. Chen's apron in a thin stack, banded with a hair-elastic, and she pressed them into Kyle's palm with both hands. Warm. The warmth of the chip was the warmth of the hand. She had been holding them all evening waiting for the knock. He felt the weight and ran a count — six hundred and forty QUANTA, half-rate, the standing rate for one-night enforcement on a six-man crew was nominally twelve hundred and eighty, this was exactly half.

"I do not have all of it, Mr. Corbin."

"It is enough."

"I do not have all of it because—"

"Mrs. Chen. It is enough."

She had the specific exhaustion of a woman who had practiced the explanation seven times and had now been told the explanation was not required. The relief did not arrive in her face. It arrived in her hands; they stopped trembling at the second knuckle of the index finger first, then in the thumbs, the rest of her hands following on a half-second delay, the way circulation came back to a limb. Kyle's array, on the channel he could not turn off, catalogued the order of return and filed it.

"You will be careful."

"I will be efficient."

"That is not the same thing."

"No, it is not."

She bowed. He bowed. The bows were the contract. She told him the rest of what he had come to know — the address at the bottom of the eastern strut, the side door that was never locked because nobody was foolish enough to walk in the side door, the front door that was never locked because nobody was foolish enough to walk in the front, the schedule of when Tien drank and when Tien slept and when Tien did the thing he did instead of work, all of which Kyle had already cross-referenced against Sable's last week's update on Carrion Logistics LLC roster movement in the Gray Zone twelve sector. He had known where Tien would be before he had climbed the stairs. He had come anyway because Mrs. Chen needed to give him the address. The address was the gift she had to deliver in order to be the person who delivered it.

He left through the door he had come in. The hallway smelled like the airshaft. The freight elevator was where he had left it. On the ride down, the array, on the channel he could not turn off, said: *the bill is the bill, the boy is the bill,* and Kyle said, in his own mouth, quietly, "no," and the channel went quiet for the rest of the ride.

The bike was where he had left it, two blocks east, under the overhang of a tier-six awning that did most of the work of keeping it dry. The bike was matte black and unbranded and the plate was magnetized to the underside of the rear cowl, which was not legal anywhere, and the plate it currently displayed read like a plate that had been issued to a Carrion Logistics LLC subcontractor in 2206 and had never been cancelled, which was a useful fiction because the surveillance grid the Gray Zone ran was a forty-year-old CorpoNation cast-off and would let a Carrion plate go anywhere a Carrion plate had ever been. The bike rolled clean. He had put more hours into the bike than he had put into most of the people he had known. The bike had repaid the investment. This was not a thing he held against most of the people he had known. It was a thing he had noted, and filed, and stopped examining, because examining it would not change the math of it.

He rode south. The route from the eighth-floor apartment at the West Town boundary to the bottom of the Gray Zone's eastern strut was twenty-two minutes of switchbacks if you took the freight ramps the CorpoNations had stopped maintaining around the time of the second tier rezoning. He took the freight ramps. Each tier dropped him about eight stories. By the third ramp the architecture had stopped pretending to be architecture and had become what the architecture actually was, which was welded industrial grief. By the fifth ramp the rain was warmer — the upper-tier rain was cold because it had come straight from cloud, and the lower-tier rain was warm because by the time it got down here it had been through six tiers of HVAC exhaust on the way and had been re-vaporized and re-condensed twice. He did not roll his visor up. The rain on the visor was rain. The Pulse hub forty stories above him was emitting its low-frequency commute hum — a Mach-six slug bound for Rotterdam, by the timetable, although Rotterdam was a memory the Pulse called by name now without anyone remembering why. A VTOL crossed the gap between two towers a long way overhead, running lights amber, climbing toward the CorpoNation roof traffic stratum. Some board director was late to dinner. Kyle did not file the running lights. He had stopped filing the running lights at some point in his twenties. The rich travelled rich. He travelled the way he travelled. The gap between those two transit modes was part of the city in the way the rain was part of the city, weather you accepted because the alternative was useless.

His left forearm itched under the bracer. The corundum strop sat against the inner forearm — a thumb-thin plate of corundum on a carbon-fiber shell, two titanium-wire braid loops, designed by Seo, made by Seo, given to Kyle by Seo on a Tuesday in 2199 along with the sword. The strop had no electronics in it. It had a face of corundum and a piece of carbon-fiber behind the corundum and that was all it had. The work it did was kinetic. When Silence's flat passed across the corundum face under controlled tension on the draw, the corundum produced a crisp high-frequency stress pulse that propagated through the blade length, and the PZT shingane in the blade harvested the pulse, and the blade came out the saya already at a baseline charge. The gesture, to anyone watching, looked like a corrupted chiburi — the cleaning motion done on the wrong end of the cut, on the draw instead of the sheath. Seo had designed it to look like a mistake. *They will think you do not know how to clean the blade,* he had said, in the room they had used as a dojo at the back of his repair shop, when Kyle was eighteen and had not yet broken anyone's wrist with the sword. *They will think you are sloppy. They will adjust for sloppy. They will not adjust for what you are doing.* Seo had been correct about that. Seo had been correct about many things and had been killed by the things he had been correct about, which was the kind of math the Gray Zone did to people who tried to live by precision.

Kyle parked the bike a block out and walked.

The loading dock sat at the bottom of the eastern strut where the strut met the tier-zero substrate and the substrate was just *concrete* without any further pretense to architectural intent. Six bays. Two of the roller doors were open. The light inside was the amber sodium of overheads that had been installed sometime before LED was a word and had not been replaced because nobody in the building's chain of putative ownership had a budget line for *replace the lights.* The light made everything look like a polaroid in a kitchen drawer.

He came in through the front because surprise was free.

There were six men. They were playing cards on a crate that had been a crate for cargo and was now a crate for a card game, four sitting and two leaning against a stack of dollies. The two leaners were the chrome-arm men: full-shoulder replacement, Hydraulic Solutions ARM-9 series by the wrist articulation pattern — corporate-grade arms, somewhere between fifteen and eighteen thousand QUANTA a side, and the kind of arms a man wore when he had earned them in service to a CorpoNation he no longer worked for and was reluctant to part with. Of the four seated, one had subdermal thoracic plating — the kind you could see, in the right light, as faint geometric ridges along the collar where the seam between dermal layers had not healed correctly. The seam said *low-end clinic, fast install, no aftercare.* One had chrome knuckles and a face the array put at nineteen, give or take, with augmentation that was strictly cosmetic and very fresh. One had no visible work done, which meant whatever he had was internal, which meant expensive, which meant he had made decisions. One had a face that had been rebuilt cheap and tight on the left side — the skin pulled the wrong way over the lower mandible, the parts of his face having run out somewhere along the line, the rebuild done by a clinic that had not had the budget for a full revision and had not been asked to provide one. That face was Tien's. The array logged it without comment.

The laughter died at the tempo laughter always died when the math changed in a room. It died last in the chrome-knuckle kid, who was nineteen and had not yet learned to read tempo.

"You lost, friend?" said the no-visible-work man, who was the boss; the boss had a coat cut for inside-coat carry and a saya inside the coat which the array had clocked on the threshold by the draping; the boss carried a katana and would know how to use it.

"Looking for Tien," Kyle said.

The boss looked at Tien. Tien looked at the boss. The boss looked back at Kyle. The boss had decided to make this take one more sentence than it strictly needed to, which was the decision of a man who had not yet been told that the math had finished resolving.

"Tien is here, friend. Tien is occupied."

"He will not be."

The boss did the thing with his head that men did when they wanted to indicate to the room that something interesting was now happening. The chrome-knuckle kid's right hand began to move toward his waistband, the motion telegraphed at the shoulder a full half-second before the hand committed. *Tier 1 movement,* the array said, in a voice that was almost Kyle's. *Untrained. Unreliable.* The two chrome-arm men shifted their weight a quarter inch each, the kind of shift hydraulic shoulders made when their owners were waking up the actuator buses. The subdermal-plated man set his cards face down on the crate.

Kyle's chest warmed — not the bracer, not the blade, the array. A coal being blown to life behind the sternum. Thirty-two thousand electrodes coming up from the rest cycle to the operating cycle, the firmware allocating from the bio-battery the resolution that the situation required. The room rendered itself in the second channel. Six bodies. Heat. Pulse. The micro-contraction of the kid's forearm flexor. The angle of the saya inside the boss's coat. The exact gap of unprotected actuator housing on the nearer chrome-arm man's shoulder seam, three and a half centimeters of motor bus exposed where the dermal sleeve had retracted to expose the cooling vent. The array put a faint colored frame around the gap. The frame meant *here, if it has to be here.* It had not had to be here yet. It was being held in reserve.

The boss said the next thing slowly. "Tien works for Carrion."

"I am aware."

"Carrion will be upset, friend."

"I am aware."

"You have a name?"

"Kyle."

"Kyle who."

The hardware put up the second channel for him: *He is buying time. He is waiting on something. The pause is for the kid to get his hand into his waistband, not for the boss to learn anything about you.* Kyle did not adjust his stance. The stance was already correct. He had stood, on the threshold, in the line Seo had drilled him into for nine years, the line that did not look like a line because it looked like nothing, the line that was three-quarters of a meter to the left of *square* and a quarter of a turn off-axis, the line from which the right hand could clear Silence over the right shoulder while the left hand cleared Chorus from the low hip, the line that no swordsman who had been told to expect a sword would expect because no school of swordsmanship had ever bothered to map it.

"Kyle from down the street," Kyle said.

Chorus came out of the holster in his left hand. The bird's-head grip cleared without snagging — the holster was a passive friction rig with a thumb-break that was, in practice, not a break at all because Kyle had filed the catch down to a nub two years ago after the catch had cost him a quarter-second one bad night on the Lakefront and he had decided that the quarter-second was worth more than the rare situation in which the gun would fall out of the holster on its own. The cylinder rotated under his trigger finger between shots with the slow deliberate click of a revolver that had been polished by every cleaning since he had owned it. Four shots in three seconds, twelve-gauge buckshot at the chrome-knuckle kid, slug at the subdermal-plated man, slug at the near chrome-arm man, slug at Tien. The sound was concussion, not crack — four heavy thumps that stacked against the eardrum and the sternum and the fillings in the back teeth. The bay's amber overheads strobed twice from the muzzle flash and resumed.

The chrome-knuckle kid's right knee separated. The joint did not bend; it came apart along the ligament line, the way a poorly-glued cabinet door came apart when somebody finally pulled it the right way. He went down sideways. The sound he made was not screaming. Screaming required composure.

The subdermal-plated man took the slug at the left knee at nine feet. His fighting philosophy had been organized around the assumption that nothing got through his chest, and the assumption had not extended to the kneecap, and the kneecap was a sliver of the body the plating did not cover. He folded with the dignified slow collapse of a man whose center of gravity has done him the courtesy of waiting until he has reached the floor to abandon him.

The near chrome-arm man took the slug at the right knee. The arm was hydraulically powerful and the arm was not load-bearing on the leg. The leg was flesh. The flesh failed. He sat down hard against the stack of dollies and the dollies rolled six inches and he did not get back up.

Tien took the slug at the left knee. Kyle had budgeted that shot for the last beat of the count because the array had already mapped what would happen — Tien's center of gravity, the angle of the impact, the way his face would meet the corrugated steel of the drainage channel when the leg gave. The array had been correct. The face met the floor at the geometry the array had specified, and the impact loosened two teeth, possibly three, the array would update after the inspection.

Three seconds. Four shots. Four men down. One slug in the cylinder.

The far chrome-arm man had begun his swing in the half-second between the first shot and the second. This was a correct response from a fighter with hydraulic reach and a head start on the geometry. He had committed before he had registered that his crew was no longer with him. The arm was already coming around at the height where Kyle's collarbone had been. The array read the motor bus exactly the way the array always read motor buses — clean electromagnetic bloom along the deltoid actuator, full lateral extension committed, intercept at the throat — and Kyle stepped under the arc and brought Silence up.

Silence cleared the saya in one continuous draw, right hand over the right shoulder, the blade's flat passing across the corundum strop on the inner left forearm at the bottom of the draw. The corundum face produced its crisp high-frequency stress pulse. The pulse propagated down the blade length and the PZT shingane harvested it and the blade came out of the saya at baseline charge. To anyone watching, the gesture would look like the corrupted chiburi Seo had designed it to look like — a sloppy cleaning motion done at the wrong moment, the kind of mistake an under-trained street swordsman would make. The far chrome-arm man, whose arm was still coming around, had read the literature on Silence and was therefore watching for a glow that the literature claimed Silence produced on the draw. Silence did not glow. The literature was wrong. The man, whose arm was still coming around, watched in the half-second he had remaining for a flare that was not going to arrive and missed seeing Kyle pivot a quarter step left.

Kyle put Silence's edge into the shoulder seam where chrome met flesh. Three and a half centimeters of unprotected actuator housing. The edge passed through the gap the way the edge passed through anything when the angle was right and the line was committed. The arm did not stop. The arm finished its swing into empty air without him. The motor bus was severed at the shoulder, and the rest of the man's body did what bodies did when the load-bearing chrome stopped sending signals. He sat down. He looked at his arm, which was still moving on residual hydraulic pressure but no longer responding to him, and he made a sound that was something less than screaming and more than nothing.

The street, by tomorrow afternoon, would have a new entry in its file on Silence: *cuts chrome.* The street would attribute the cut to whatever ability the street currently believed Silence had — a piezoelectric short, a kinetic disruptor, the carbon-nanotube edge eating chrome like cake. Silence had cut chrome because Silence was a sword and the chrome had a seam and Kyle had put the edge in the seam. None of the street's hypotheses would be the explanation. Kyle had stopped offering the explanation. The people he had explained it to had a way of dying mid-explanation and the explanation had not, when given, persuaded anybody to disbelieve their version of the story. Silence was a sword. The audience was responsible for its own categories.

Kyle turned.

The boss had stood up.

He had not drawn during the four-shot count. He had stood, unhurried, with the calm economy of a man who had sat at a table and watched his crew dismantle and had decided, on his own time, that it was now his turn. The hand that went to his hip came back with a katana. The saya had been inside the coat. Kyle had clocked it on the threshold; the array had not categorized it because the array, in a room full of chrome, had not had a category for *unaugmented man with a sword,* and the array's blind spots were exactly the size of the assumptions Kyle's day-to-day work taught the array to make.

The blade came out at the right angle in the right tempo, point neither high nor low, weight settling into a guard that was not borrowed from any school Kyle could name. He had been trained. Somewhere expensive. Specifically — Kyle would learn this in the next four exchanges — against blades of the kind he had been told Silence was. Kyle could see it in the way the boss's weight stayed off the front foot, in the way the boss was already accounting for an exotic countermeasure that did not exist. The literature on Silence was loud. The boss had read it. The boss had paid for the kind of training a man paid for when he intended to fight a specific weapon at a specific time and was willing to fund the preparation. Kyle filed all of this in the second channel and did not let any of it onto his face.

"Sloppy draw, kid," the boss said.

He had four exchanges in him.

The first exchange tested the line. A short cut at Kyle's right shoulder, no commitment in it, the kind of strike a swordsman used to read his opponent's reach and tempo. Silence rose to meet it. The blades touched edge to flat at a shallow angle. Steel rang, a clean sustained note in concrete with no acoustic mercy. The boss read his information off Kyle's reach and tempo and stepped back without expression. He had also wanted to confirm something else — some signature he had been told to watch for, some flare or pulse or visible change in the blade — and what he had been told to watch for had not arrived. His face did not show this. His weight stayed off the front foot. But the guard moved a quarter inch in a way the array catalogued and filed.

*He is watching for the wrong thing,* said the second channel, in Kyle's diction this time, neutral, no editorializing. *Use it.*

The second exchange pressed. Kyle advanced two steps, the cut coming high and right. The boss met it with a deflection rather than a parry — the literature said you did not take edge-to-edge contact with Silence because edge-to-edge contact was when the bad thing happened, and there was no bad thing but the boss did not know that and would not learn that in the time he had remaining. He slid Silence's edge along his blade's flat to redirect the force past his shoulder. Smart. Conservative. Kyle adjusted. The boss had told him something useful in return: *I will not let you charge me cleanly. I will work the angles.*

The third exchange was the boss's commit. He had read enough, he had decided, and he cut high left, fast, and Kyle's parry was a fraction late because the array's micro-tremor read had been clean but the boss's body had been clean in return — no telegraph, no shoulder bloom, a man who had decided to commit and had committed. Silence took the cut at the proper angle. The boss had already let his blade slide off the parry into a follow-through, and the follow-through grazed Kyle's left forearm — three inches of skin opened above the strop, not deep, the cut shallow enough that the subdermal mesh under the dermis registered the pressure and decided not to engage. Blood came up immediately. The array tagged it without comment: *vein not artery, two centimeters lateral of the radial, will not bleed out, will impair grip on the shotgun side in approximately ninety seconds.*

The boss saw the blood. He understood what the cut meant — that he was inside Kyle's range, that he had landed first, that whatever exotic countermeasure the literature had warned him about either had a tell he had successfully avoided or did not work the way the literature said it worked or had been overstated in the first place by the people who sold the literature. He chose to interpret the absence of a flare as evidence that he had timed it correctly. He chose this because the alternative interpretation — that he had spent money training against a weapon whose mythology had outpaced its mechanism by thirty years — was not the kind of interpretation a man chose mid-duel.

*He is still fighting the literature,* the second channel said. *He is about to find out what the literature was wrong about.*

The fourth exchange was the boss's thrust. A straight committed thrust at Kyle's center mass, full extension, the line clean. Silence came down to meet it not at the edge and not at the flat but at the seppa — the small collar between the tsuba and the blade — and the thrust caught there, blade to blade, locked. Kyle drove forward. He kept the lock. He let Silence's edge ride along the flat of the boss's blade like a skate on ice, the edge tracking down the boss's steel from the tip toward the middle toward the tsuba, and where the boss's blade met the boss's hand, the edge of Silence met the boss's right wrist.

Bone, tendon, the radial and ulnar arteries, the median nerve, the carpal architecture that had held a sword for whatever number of years the boss had been training for this duel. The edge passed through the wrist the way the edge passed through anything when the angle was right and the line was committed. The boss's right hand and the boss's katana fell together to the corrugated steel. The boss looked at his wrist. The boss looked at his sword on the floor. The boss looked at Kyle.

"Oh," the boss said, very quietly.

The brachial artery did most of the work. The body did the rest. He bled out in nineteen seconds. The array timed it. The array timed everything, even when Kyle did not ask, because the array did not have a setting for *do not time things.*

Kyle's hands were steady. They would shake in four minutes. He had learned to use the window.

Twenty-three seconds for the duel. Twenty-six on the dock floor end to end.

He sheathed Silence. A thin line of red on the edge near the kissaki; he wiped it against the boss's coat before the blade seated. Chorus went back into the holster on the low left hip, four chambers spent, one slug still in the cylinder. He registered the count the way other people registered the time.

---

He moved through the bodies, methodical. The badly-rebuilt jaw man was face-down in the drainage channel where the fourth shot had put him, cheek against the corrugated steel, breath fogging in the runoff. Kyle crouched. He found the teeth — two loosened when the man's face had met the floor, one still seated and requiring grip and twist and a specific application of force that Kyle performed without expression. The teeth came into his left hand. Warm. Slick. The smell was blood and saliva. He did not think about what he was doing in terms that required examination. He had been thinking about it on the ride down for twenty-two minutes and he had finished thinking about it before he had parked the bike, and there was nothing left to think about now.

Behind the ventilation unit on the far wall, the breathing pattern Kyle had tracked since the threshold shifted. He had clocked it as *child, female, twelve to fourteen, has been doing this for some time.* He had filed the breathing under *infrastructure* and worked around it the way he had worked around the drainage channels. The breathing was a fixed feature of the room. It was not a variable.

Her father's daughter.

Kyle stood. He walked to the ventilation unit. He did not go behind it. He stood where she could see him — a tall, emaciated, blood-misted shape in the dock's bad amber — and he waited. The breathing stopped. Then a slow movement. A face came around the corner of the unit. Twelve, maybe thirteen. The jaw was her father's, before the wrench. The eyes were her mother's. The array catalogued the face the way the array catalogued every face — *83% Cantonese, 11% Hakka, 6% noise, eyes from somewhere west of the South China Sea, scar on the left thumb-base in mid-formation, suggesting a slip with a kitchen knife at age nine or ten, suggesting she has been working alongside her mother in the stall since she was nine or ten, suggesting—* Kyle shelved the read. The read was not what the moment required. He had used the second channel for the kneecaps and the duel; he did not use it for her.

He opened his hand.

"Present these to your father." His voice was even. He had practiced even for a long time. "I think he will appreciate the gesture."

The teeth fell into her cupped palms. She shrieked — high, thin, a sound that bounced off every hard surface in the dock and came back changed — but she did not drop them. Her fingers closed over them. She looked at her closed hands. She bowed, a full-body convulsion of terror and something that was not gratitude exactly but was shaped like it, the bow of a child who has just been handed an inheritance she had not asked for and is not yet sure what is going to be expected of her in the carrying of it.

Then she was gone, swallowed by the dark beyond the door.

Kyle watched the dark for three seconds.

She had not dropped them.

The array, on the second channel, in a register that was not Kyle's, said: *that is a strong one.* Kyle did not recognize the diction. The diction was three subjects deep in the firmware substrate; Kyle did not have the bio-battery to spare to chase it. He let it pass.

He cleaned the blade. The tremor started in his right hand at the four-minute mark, moved to his left, became briefly bilateral — the crash, the array's invoice arriving, several thousand calories called in twenty-six seconds and the body now presenting the bill. His stomach was a closed fist. He needed protein, carbohydrate, something hot in a bowl, something that required no decisions. He needed to sit somewhere that smelled like something other than what this smelled like.

He walked back to the bike. The Gray Zone's night-architecture closed around him — the flickering overheads at the ramp's intersection, the drip of recycled water from the tier above, the distant compressor whine of a HVAC stack two blocks over running its end-of-shift cycle. The towers leaned over the street the way they did this far down. A second VTOL crossed the gap forty stories up, running lights amber, climbing north. Some board director was still late to dinner. Kyle did not file the running lights.

The bike started on the first turn. He swapped the plate two blocks short of the Wicker boundary, where the surveillance grid changed character, and continued north on a different fiction.

## Part II — Standard Rate

Chen's stall was two blocks south of the freight tier and one alley off the main, and the warm orange glow of its hood lamp was visible through the rain from a block away the way a hearth was visible through the trees in the kind of children's book Kyle had never read but had heard described. He walked the bike the last block. The bike rolled easier than it rode at walking pace because the front wheel was true and the bearings were clean and the chain had been adjusted four days ago. He had spent the day adjusting the chain and he had spent two hours of that day on a tensioner that he could have replaced for forty QUANTA but had instead repaired for two hours of his own time, because the bike was a thing he was willing to spend his own time on, and forty QUANTA was a thing he was not willing to spend on the bike because forty QUANTA was the price of two of Mrs. Chen's bowls and the bike could not eat. This was not a hierarchy he had thought through. It was simply how the math came out.

She was at the counter when he arrived. The apron was clean — a fresh one; the apron she had been wearing in the apartment had been the kitchen apron, and the apron she wore at the stall was the service apron, and the apron she wore at home in front of her wired-jaw husband had been a third apron the existence of which Kyle now retroactively confirmed because Mrs. Chen was a woman who would not have offered service in a kitchen apron. The hands were washed. The small scar at the base of her left thumb — slip with a cleaver, somewhere in her teens, somewhere in a kitchen with poor task lighting — caught the lamp light at an angle the array catalogued without making the catalogue mean anything yet. The daughter was somewhere he could not see. The husband was somewhere he could not see either. The teeth, the array confirmed by absence — no second delivery to make — had already arrived where they were going.

The smell of the stall was the smell that had come up the airshaft into the apartment six hours ago. Pork bone, scallion, oil that had not been changed since the lunar new year. The smell here was the smell as a smell of the work being done, not the smell of the work having been done somewhere and then having travelled. The difference between the two was the difference between a wound and a kitchen.

She did not greet him. She set a bowl down on the counter — pork bone broth, the long-simmered kind, the kind that took fourteen hours to render properly and which Mrs. Chen made in a stockpot that had been in her family for two husbands and four children and three cities, and which was at this moment simmering at the back of the stall on a propane burner Mrs. Chen had bought used in 2218 and had maintained herself ever since. Chili oil already added the way she would remember he liked it for the next four years. He sat on the stool. The stool was the right height for him. She had not adjusted it. He had not adjusted it. The geometry simply was what it was the first time, and the first time was the way it was going to be from now on.

He sat. This was the part the canon would later, when Kyle inventoried the canon to himself in a different chapter, register as the thing that mattered. He ate standing up everywhere else. He sat at Mrs. Chen's. Not because the stool was offered. Because the stool, having been offered, was offered, and the discipline of accepting what had been offered was a discipline Kyle had begun the moment Mrs. Chen had brought the bowl out.

He ate. The broth was hot. He ate the whole bowl and did not speak. She did not refill it. She did not move. When he set the chopsticks down she was still standing exactly where she had been when she set the bowl, and she was looking at him the way she had looked at the chips she had pressed into his hand six hours ago — she had more she wanted to give and no language for the giving, and she was now finding the language.

Cantonese first, low. *Mh̄gōi.* Two syllables doing the work of an entire ledger. Then, in the tongue she had chosen to run her stall in because she did not assume every customer spoke hers: *Free. Always. For you. Forever.*

Her hands were flat on the counter beside the bowl. Her thumb with the scar was the closest thing to him on the wood. She had just offered him the only thing she owned.

The second channel said: *she means it. she is not negotiating. she does not understand that she is offering you a thing you cannot accept.*

Kyle reached into his coat and set a credit chip on the counter beside her hand. Standard rate. The kind of standard rate a man paid when he wanted the transaction to be a transaction and not the beginning of an obligation he had spent his life refusing to accumulate.

"I pay," he said. His voice was level. He had practiced level for a long time. "Every bowl. Standard rate. Every time."

She did not pick up the chip. He did not pick up the bowl. The standoff lasted perhaps four seconds and was the most important four seconds of their relationship, and they both knew it without naming it.

She tried again. Slower this time, the way you tried again with a man you had decided not to insult by giving up on the first refusal. "You took the contract at half. You did the work whole. The bowl is nothing. Forever is nothing."

*Forever is everything,* said the second channel. *Forever is the largest thing she has, and she is offering it because she does not yet know how large it is.*

Kyle did not move the chip. He looked at her in the way the discipline allowed him to look at a woman who had just tried to give him something he could not, in good conscience, accept. The blade was the discipline. The bowl was the discipline. The credit chip was the discipline. He did not say that aloud. He said, aloud, what he could.

"If I do not pay, I do not eat. If I do not eat here, I do not eat. I would rather pay you than not see you again. The chip stays."

The four seconds became five. Then her hand closed over the chip the way her daughter's hand had closed over the teeth — not refusing the gift, accepting that the gift was the form the man in front of her needed the gift to take. She bowed. Not the convulsive bow of the apartment six hours ago. A smaller bow. The bow of a vendor accepting a customer's price. The kind of bow she would give him every night, roughly three hundred nights a year — barring the occasional distant contract, grievous injury, or recon — for the next four years and longer.

He stood. He bowed back, smaller still, the bow of a man who had just successfully refused a kindness larger than he could carry.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Same bowl. Same price."

"Same price," she said. The agreement was the contract. The contract was the bond.

He turned and walked the bike out of the stall's lamp circle. The rain had not stopped. It was not going to stop tonight. The towers overhead leaned in the way they did this far down, and the gap between them was full of the city's wet patient light. The second channel, on the register Kyle could not turn off, registered the moment for the long file the array kept on his life — *Chen, Wei-Lin; noodle stall; standard rate; ritual canonized 2226-03-04 0211 hours* — and Kyle did not argue with the filing because the array would file it whether he agreed with the filing or not.

He laughed.

It was not the laugh the action movies he had never watched had taught the audience to expect. It was a single tight exhalation, the laugh of a man who had just seen the joke and had decided that the joke was funny anyway, the kind of laugh you laughed in private, walking a bike up a slope toward Wicker in the rain, because the alternative was the alternative.

## Part III — The Watcher

Someone had been watching the loading-dock work. Someone had stayed to watch the walk back. Someone had watched the bike pick-up and would watch the route home partially, before the route home crossed into a tier where their telemetry stopped working. The watching was professional — the camera position chosen for the angle, the timing chosen for the moment a man's discipline relaxed after the work was done. Kyle felt the watching the way you felt a draft you could not find the source of. He did not turn his head. Turning his head would tell the watcher what kind of read his array had performed and he was not yet willing to give the watcher that data. The boy from the Grind, who had spent his childhood being measured by people whose names he was never told, had grown into a man who measured back without announcing he was doing it.

The watcher noted the pace. The watcher noted that the pace did not change. The watcher noted that Kyle's hand did not move toward the saya at any point during the walk, that his shoulders did not square, and that the breathing read on whatever telemetry the watcher had access to — and they had access; organizations like the one this watcher worked for had access to telemetry pulled off ambient citywide infrastructure that did not officially exist — stayed at the rest cycle. The watcher noted, in particular, the moment Kyle had walked Silence's edge through the chrome-arm man's shoulder seam without any visible flare, glow, or arc, and the watcher's report would treat this as a calibration data point, because the literature on Silence had been wrong about a lot of things, and the watcher's organization had been paying for the literature, and the watcher's organization preferred their literature to be correct.

The watcher filed all of this in a report Kyle would never read. The report would be reviewed two months later by Hua, the south-arm cell captain of the Lotus Syndicate, who would close the file and pass it to her gang lieutenant Mira along with a second file labeled *Bucktown — first interview — fourteen-piece,* and the second file would lead to the third, and the third to the fourth, and Kyle did not know any of that yet, riding north through the warm rain toward the Wicker boundary because the noodles had been the right temperature and the chip had stayed on the counter and the bike was running clean and the world was still what the world was but it was, this evening, fucked at a tempo he could ride.

Kyle rode home. The noodles would be cold by morning. They always were.
