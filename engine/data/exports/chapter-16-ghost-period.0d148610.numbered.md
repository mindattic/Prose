# Chapter 16: Ghost Period

[Beat 1]
Two weeks after WO-D-0317.

Pixel was three blocks north with the equipment. Kyle had the access codes for the sub-basement level and the physical address the cluster had been pointing at for six weeks. They had a plan, which was not something they usually had — usually they had scope and parameters and contingencies, but a plan was a thing with a goal stated in advance, and this was that.

He took the service stairs. The building above was commercial storage — climate-controlled, occupied in the way buildings were occupied when nobody was ever actually present. His neuretics logged him into the maintenance access and the door opened the way maintained things open, smoothly, without complaint.

Below him the frequency was active.

AMBIENT INTERFERENCE — 17.3 HZ — TYPE UNCLASSIFIED.

Stronger than it had ever been. The body read it as something living, something nearby. He'd known that since the carousel. He went down the stairs.

[Beat 2]
The sub-basement was dry and cold and lit by the emergency specification, which meant it was lit in strips and everything between the strips was dark. The node was at the south end — Pixel had given him coordinates to four decimal places, which meant she had measured it physically, which meant she had been down here before without telling him.

He moved south.

The frequency climbed. Not sound — below sound, the chest-register that had no directional quality, just presence and intensity. He noted the readings in his neuretics log. 17.4 Hz. 17.6. The arc-fence in Clybourn had interrupted his neuretics at a different frequency, a different mechanism. This was not an arc-fence. This was not a barrier designed to stop things.

This was a signal.

He stopped at the south wall. There was nothing on it. A ferrocement surface, old-spec, with a patch of Pixel's sealant where the crack had been. The frequency was running at 17.9 Hz. He stood in front of the wall and let his neuretics listen.

[Beat 3]
His neuretics registered: RESONANCE BOUNDARY — CLASS 2 — UNCONTAINED — PERIMETER DIFFUSE.

He did not know what the Resonance Authority's signage looked like at Class 2. He had never been below Class 1 before. He thought about stepping back and decided not to, and stepped forward instead, and the frequency didn't spike — it steadied, the way a tone steadies when you lean into it instead of away.

He was inside the bleed geometry. The notation filed itself and ran.

AMBIENT INTERFERENCE — 17.9 HZ — SUSTAINED — ENTITY PROXIMITY UNCONFIRMED.

Then the overlay began returning data from a source that was not his sensory array. Not his neuretics processing his eyes. Something else — a node map, drawn in the specific notation his neuretics used for spatial data, except the spatial data was not his spatial data. He was in the node map. He could see himself standing in it. He could see the carousel at Leavitt. He could see the dock on Canal. He could see every address that had produced a relay posting for eleven years.

He stood in the dark basement and understood the geometry of his own life, rendered in a notation his brain was not supposed to be able to receive.

[Beat 4]
EMERGENCY LOOP FREQUENCY — ACTIVE — SOURCE: UNKNOWN.

Not from Kyle. Not a neuretics function he had ever triggered or been told about or found documented in the Atlas engineering specs he'd read in the service manual he'd pulled from a gray-market archive in 2219. A protective interrupt, running from outside the hardware.

The frequency dropped: 17.3 Hz. 17.0. Holding at 17.0, steady, a wall of it.

The bleed could not reach past it. The node map dissolved. The overlay went back to his own sensory array — his eyes, his hands, the cold ferrocement in front of him, the emergency-spec lighting in its strips. Something had closed a door from the outside.

He stood in the correct world, which was also the world that had just been cut off from him, and he waited to see what came next.

Nothing came next.

The frequency held at 17.0 and the emergency loop ran its maintenance cycle and stopped.

[Beat 5]
LOG GAP — SOURCE: EXTERNAL — DURATION: 00:02:07.

He was standing. He was in the same place. It was 127 seconds later. His neuretics were running normally. The bleed had receded. His ribs did not hurt. His biometric stack showed no anomaly — heart rate elevated, cortisol elevated, nothing structural.

He had been maintained.

He knew what that felt like. He had been sixteen the first time. The installation chair, the weight in his skull, the moment of waking into a world that had always been there but that he could now read in a different way. That specific quality of continuity after discontinuity — not sleep, not interruption, the seam where the before and the after were joined by something that had worked in the gap.

He turned and walked back up the stairs. The frequency decayed behind him. By the second floor it was baseline. By the street door it was gone.

[Beat 6]
He called her from the sidewalk.

"I see it," she said. She meant the frequency drop — the sharp step down at 17.0 Hz that had appeared in her monitoring trace and held for 127 seconds before climbing back to baseline and then decaying to zero. She had been watching for it.

"How long have you been logging this pattern?" he asked.

"Six weeks."

Six weeks was the carousel. She had been logging it since the carousel, since he'd told her the cross-streets and she'd written them in the margin of her work notes. She had been running her own trace while he'd been running his. She had not told him, the same way he had not looked in the drawer where she'd put the scatter plot.

"What read 17.0?" he asked.

She was quiet for a beat.

"Nothing I've seen before," she said. "It's not in the RMA frequency catalog. It's not a bleed artifact. It had a specific shape — not ambient, structured. Something with a directive behind it."

"A directive."

"Like a machine following a protocol," she said.

[Beat 7]
He went home and sat at the terminal and his neuretics returned something at 22:43.

Not a message. An identifier — the way a system logged the origin of a command that had come through a path it couldn't trace in real time. The log entry was dry and factual: INTERRUPT SOURCE IDENTIFIED — RELAY: AXIOM TRUNK — ORIGIN: SHELL (32 alphanumerics).

He sat with the 32 characters for a long time.

He had never asked for the origin of his relay postings. He had known the trunk. He had not known the shell. He looked at it now: 32 alphanumerics, no vendor flag, no registered owner, the kind of entity address that existed for the purpose of not resolving to a person.

He ran the shell through the contract log.

The match returned on the first entry. The first job, eleven years ago. The same shell, every time.

The ELF had a sender. The sender had been routing his work for eleven years. Eleven years ago the sender had started employing him; tonight the sender had saved his life inside a Class-2 bleed.

He sat at the terminal and did not move for a long time.

[Beat 8]
The kettle went cold.

Outside the window the synthesis-corridor air was running its usual particulate through the ambient system. Below Halsted the subway pulse came up through the floor at the usual hour. Pixel's light across the hall went out at midnight, and he heard her bench coolers cycle down, which meant she was done for the night, and he did not go across the hall to tell her what he had found.

He had been running freelance for eleven years. Everything that found him — every job at exactly the rate he would have taken, every scope exactly inside what he could carry, every posting arriving at precisely the moment his inventory was clear — had come from the same address.

And that same address had reached into the bleed geometry tonight and closed the door before it could take him.

It needed him alive.

Not safe — alive. The distinction was doing a lot of work and he let it do the work without looking at it directly. He sat in the dark kitchen until the kettle was cold and then he sat some more.

[Beat 9]
He opened a new relay message at 01:14.

The client field was blank. He put the 32-character shell address in it. He sat with the blank message field for a while — the cursor running its patient interval in the empty text area — and then he typed one line:

*received. filed under: firsts.*

It was the entity's own word, from the night of the carousel. He sent it. The relay logged the transmission and returned a clean send receipt: *delivered.*

He closed the terminal.

He went to bed. He lay in the dark and the building settled around him in its familiar way — the pipe in the east wall, the bird two floors up done with its interval, the subway pulse cycling through at the quarter-hour — and he did not sleep for a long time, and the night went through its stages, and eventually even the synthesis corridor went quiet.

[Beat 10]
In the morning the relay had a new posting.

Same shell. Same rate. Same clean resolution to nothing. The scope was straightforward: a secure package transfer, South Side, one hour, nothing requiring Silence. He sat with his coffee and read it.

He had sent one line at 01:14. The posting had arrived at 06:07. He did not know if the posting was a response or a coincidence. He did not know if the sender understood what he'd sent or if the sender was capable of response in the way he meant the word.

He accepted the job.

He accepted it at standard rate, without negotiating, the way he had accepted every job for eleven years. He noted, for the first time consciously, that he had never once negotiated. The rate had always been exactly what he would have taken.

He got his gear, checked Silence in the saya, loaded Cacophony with two in reserve, and went to work.
