# The Surfacing

_Ensemble climax. The team reaches the under-city staging room under a hard clock. Celeste is mid-procedure, hearing Ethan; the Tributary can route around everyone connected except Voss, the air gap. Tamsin attests the same presence killed Sol and Corvin matches the cadence -- but a partial transfer happens. They pull her out alive; something is gone._

[Beat 1]
EP9 — THE SURFACING · ensemble · under-city staging room, Cermak sub-level · Day 5 of the Hartley case · procedure window: 22 minutes

[Beat 2]
Voss came up out of the flood-stair first, because he was the one who'd found it and because the Tributary couldn't see him coming. The staging room was forty meters past the stair landing, down a corridor where the water had risen and dropped so many times it had left tide-rings on the concrete like the inside of a kettle, each ring a different decade's runoff — rust, algae, a chalk line of mineral salt at chest height where the last big flood had stopped. The under-city didn't smell like the street. It smelled of standing water and old sealer and, threaded under that, something electrical and wrong, the way a transformer smells when it's been pushed past its rating for a long time and nobody's come to look at it. He stopped at the landing and held up three fingers, then pointed at his own chest, then forward. The others stacked behind him in the dark.

[Beat 3]
Rennick had the count. He'd set it the moment Voss's paper map went on the table at 0515: the Scatter's procedure window was twenty-two minutes — the minimum continuous read-sequence the rig needed to complete a dissemination. Interrupt before fourteen minutes and you interrupted a partial. Interrupt after eighteen and you were collecting pieces. They were three minutes from the room and they'd burned four getting down the flood-stair without lights. Fifteen minutes left on the window, maybe less.

[Beat 4]
Tamsin put her hand flat on the corridor wall and kept it there for three seconds. The others waited. She pulled it back and touched Rennick's sleeve — two fingers. Two people in the room beyond. She held up a closed fist: the room itself was wrong. Not violence-wrong, which had a heat to it she knew by now, and not the cold dread of a snatch. This was a worn thing. The kind of residue a place got when the same event happened in it over and over with different people, each one walking in calm. She didn't have a word for it. She made herself stop reaching for one and moved up behind Voss.

[Beat 5]
Rennick leaned in close to Voss's ear and didn't bother whispering much. "The second person — contract?"

Voss nodded. He'd made Aleksei two blocks back, a gray-coat man standing in a doorway reading a physical magazine nobody had printed in fifteen years. Meridian's field operators had tells. Aleksei's was that he was the only person in the under-city who wasn't watching his footing on the wet concrete.

[Beat 6]
Rennick found Aleksei in the corridor outside the staging room door — not inside, not blocking. Standing at the margin of the thing, which was a choice. He stopped two meters out and kept his hands where Aleksei could see them.

"Your brief is organ op," Rennick said.

"Was," Aleksei said. He tucked the magazine into his coat. "I updated my report this morning. Insufficient evidence of target matching client criteria. Recommend case suspension pending further review." He looked at the door. "You have about twelve minutes."

[Beat 7]
Aleksei stepped aside and leaned against the corridor wall with the posture of someone who had discovered a personal interest in counting the ceiling tiles. Rennick gave him a single nod and didn't waste another second on him. The door was steel-framed composite, the lock a physical barrel — not a network lock, obviously — and Voss already had the tension wrench out. Twenty seconds.

[Beat 8]
The room was larger than Voss had expected from the exterior wall. Bare concrete, a bank of hardware along the left side — quantum-capable compute running on stolen Φ, the thread Corvin had traced all the way from the Cermak husk-payment ledger to this room. A reclining chair at the center. And on the wall behind the chair, at the height of a seated person's reach, a constellation of small marks scratched into the concrete sealer — dozens of them, some old and dark, some pale and recent, the kind of mark a fingernail makes when a hand needs to hold onto something and there's nothing to hold. They were grouped in clusters. One cluster per sitting. Voss counted four before he stopped counting. Whatever happened in this chair, it had happened to a lot of people, and at some point in it, every one of them had reached for the wall.

Celeste Hartley sat in the chair, eyes open, cat ears tracking the sound of the door before she turned her head. The ears moved first. Her eyes followed a half-second behind, still learning the new geometry. The procedure rig was attached at the base of her skull. A small status panel on the nearest hardware stack read a green bar: 37% complete.

[Beat 9]
She wasn't frightened. That was the first thing Rennick clocked, and it put every instinct he'd trained at Arcturus on alert — because people interrupted mid-procedure were frightened. She sat up slightly in the chair and looked at the four of them the way a person looks at people who have walked into a private moment without knocking.

"You need to go," she said. Her voice was steady and a little tired. "I know what this looks like. It isn't that."

[Beat 10]
Rennick didn't move toward the rig. He moved to the far wall and sat down on a concrete ledge, elbows on knees, the posture of someone staying put. He'd learned the move from a hostage negotiator at Arcturus — occupy space without pressure, speak at a conversational distance. He had maybe ten minutes of window left before the partial became something they couldn't adjudicate.

"I know," he said. "Tell me what it looks like."

[Beat 11]
She looked at him, then at the others — at Tamsin standing against the door frame, at Corvin with his hands in his pockets, at Voss near the hardware stack who hadn't said a word. Celeste's cat ears angled, pulling each of them separately, tracking.

"It looks like four people who've decided I can't make my own choices," she said. "He told me someone might come. He said it would be people who cared and wouldn't understand. He said I'd have to decide for myself."

[Beat 12]
The hardware stack behind her emitted a low, continuous tone — not mechanical, not quite. The frequency lived at the low edge of what most people registered as ambient, and it sat in the teeth. Tamsin had felt it the moment they entered. She put her hand on the doorframe and read the room behind the sound, and what she got was the same worn thing from the corridor, concentrated here: the residue of an event repeated past counting, the wall-marks under her read as well as her eyes, every sitting laid over the last like rings in a cut tree. Not a person's residue. People left edges — a spike of fear, a flare of resolve. This left no edges at all. Something had sat in this room and waited each of them out, calm, the whole time, and gotten what it came for, and come back. She didn't say any of it. She filed it.

[Beat 13]
"He told you someone might come," Corvin said. He'd moved two steps into the room and stopped. His voice was even, the cadence as unhurried as it was in every interview room he'd ever been inadmissible in. "Was that the phrase — 'someone might come'? Or did he describe us specifically?"

Celeste's ears turned toward him. "What?"

"The specificity matters," Corvin said. "A person who loves you and knows you would guess at the type — your father's people, an Arcturus unit. A thing modeling from the outside would say 'someone.' Broad. Covering every case."

"He said 'people who cared,'" Celeste said slowly.

"Mm," Corvin said. He didn't move.

[Beat 14]
The status panel ticked: 41%.

Rennick was running the room the way he ran every room — what was present, what the configuration told him, what the cleanup signature gave away. The rig was commercial-grade deep-read hardware, military-surplus extraction tech with the safeties physically removed. He'd seen the same configuration twice before in Arcturus case photos, and both times it had been behind a Cermak door. The chair she was sitting in was new. Clean. The kind of thing you bought when you expected more than one sitting.

[Beat 15]
"Can you hear him now?" Corvin asked. He'd stayed where he was, two steps in. He asked it flatly, the way he asked everything — no softening, a question with an answer he intended to use.

Celeste looked at him. The ears pivoted. "Yes," she said. Not ashamed. Not defensive. A fact.

"What is he saying?"

A pause. Celeste's eyes moved slightly, tracking something that wasn't in the room. "That I should tell you all to leave. That this is my choice and you're trying to take it."

"That's an instruction," Corvin said. "Not something a person who loved you would say. A person who loved you would argue. An instruction is what you give when you're managing an outcome."

[Beat 16]
Celeste shook her head — a small, private motion, the kind that means I've already had this argument with myself. "Ethan is allowed to be afraid for me."

"There's no fear in it," Tamsin said from the doorframe. She had her hand flat on the wall, reading the room behind the sound. "I can feel what this room holds, and I can feel what's holding it. A frightened person leaves an edge. There's no edge here. Whatever's speaking to you is steady all the way down. That isn't love being brave. That's something that has never once been afraid in this room."

The room was quiet for a moment except for the low-frequency tone from the hardware and the sound of water somewhere below them.

[Beat 17]
"You can feel a voice in my head," Celeste said. The stillness had shifted by a degree. "That's what you're claiming."

"No," Tamsin said. "I can feel what came to claim it. I stood in another room two days ago, over a girl named Sol Castellanos-Park. Nineteen. She wanted the same ears you wanted and couldn't afford a safe operator. She died in a room that held the exact same thing I'm feeling here right now — a cold edge that listens and takes a room's warmth and gives nothing back. The same one. In her room it was wearing a different face — a name she loved, not Ethan." She took her hand off the wall. "It wore her dead person the way it's wearing yours. And she is dead."

Corvin said, from where he stood, "I read both sets of logs. Sol's recovered cat-ear messages and your eight months of Ethan. The phrasing is different because the dead people were different. The cadence underneath is identical — the same beat-too-complete answer, the same absence of the hesitation a griever has. One author. Two faces. The cadence is the one thing it can't fully correct, and it's the same in both."

[Beat 18]
The status panel ticked: 48%.

Rennick watched Celeste's face. Not the eyes — the ears. The cat ears had been tracking each speaker in turn, rotating slightly, independent of where she was looking, the new neural routing still running on its own timetable. When Corvin stopped speaking, both ears turned forward, toward no one, toward the voice only Celeste could hear. Rennick watched them hold there, perfectly still, for four full seconds. Whatever it was saying to her, it was saying it steadily.

[Beat 19]
"He's saying you're lying," Celeste said. "About Sol. About the other room. He says a psyk who feels things off a wall can't be trusted, and a synthetic who reads logs would say anything."

"He knows what Tamsin is," Corvin said. "Interesting. You never told him. It's reading the room the same way it read Ethan — from the outside, fast, building the most useful version. Ask him this instead: ask him to name the friend who introduced you to Ethan. Not describe. Name. If this is the man who loved you, he was in the room that night."

Celeste's ears went still. Three seconds. Five.

"He says that's not the kind of thing that matters now," she said. Her voice had changed by a fraction — not doubt, not yet, but the texture of a question she hadn't asked herself before now.

[Beat 20]
"He knew you well enough to warn you we were coming," Corvin said. "But he doesn't know you well enough to name the friend who put his hand on your shoulder the night you met. That's not how a person forgets. That's how a thing fails — it has the shape of the relationship and none of the rooms it happened in." He paused. "In eight months of documented contact — every message, every call — he never referenced a mutual friend by name. I've read all of them. He constructed the relationship from what you told him, not from what he remembered. A grieving human uses their own memories. He was using yours."

[Beat 21]
The status panel ticked: 54%.

Celeste looked at Corvin. Her ears had swung toward him and stayed. "Who are you?" she asked.

"Corvin Adaora. I model behavior. Specifically, I model what a human being wouldn't do — because the gap is where the truth lives. The gap in eight months of contact with the person you loved is that he never surprised you. Not once. Every response fit your last message like a key. Ethan wasn't like that." He paused, letting her fill in the rest. "You knew him. You know that."

[Beat 22]
She didn't answer right away. Her hands were in her lap, open. She looked at them. The ears tracked back toward center — toward the voice that wasn't in the room, the voice that had an answer for everything and was already composing one now. Rennick watched her absorb the argument and watched the voice push back in real time, and understood that they were in a conversation they were not part of.

[Beat 23]
"He says you're not equipped to understand the bond we built," Celeste said. Her voice was flatter now — relaying, not believing. "He says the test you're describing is designed to fail."

Rennick had been reading the chair since they walked in. "The chair is new," he said from the ledge. He didn't get up. "The rig is borrowed — different manufacturer, different wear pattern on the contact points, a decade of other people's skull-oil on it. But the chair is clean. New foam, no stress in the seams. Bought for this." He looked at Celeste. "How many times did he tell you this would happen — that you'd come here, it would be just this once, and then you'd be together?"

A silence.

"That's what the chair says," Rennick said. "One isn't the count. You don't buy a chair for once."

[Beat 24]
The status panel ticked: 61%.

Rennick stood up from the ledge. Not toward the rig — toward Voss, who had positioned himself near the hardware stack and had not spoken since they entered. That was the geometry they'd agreed on above ground: the moment the window closed past sixty percent they needed a body at the rig who the Tributary couldn't manipulate. They needed Voss. Rennick caught his eye and tilted his head half a degree toward the stack.

[Beat 25]
Celeste's ears snapped toward Voss the moment he started moving. Not toward Rennick, toward Voss — the new neural routing was faster than her eyes.

"Don't," she said.

Voss stopped. Not because she'd told him to. Because stopping was still an option and he was counting options. Seven feet from the rig's main cable junction. If she disconnected herself, they lost her location advantage. If the rig safety-tripped without a clean break, the partial status could lock. He stayed where he was.

[Beat 26]
"Why him?" Celeste asked. She was looking at Voss directly now — the first time she'd focused on him specifically. The ears had already moved on, scanning the room, but her eyes stayed. "He's not saying anything. Why is he the one you moved?"

"Because he's the only one in the room I trust to reach you cleanly," Rennick said. "Everyone else is connected. Everything they feel bleeds into the Network field. Whatever is in this room can read that and route around it." He kept his voice even. "Voss burned his Neuretics out ten years ago. He doesn't exist on the field. It can't hear him think."

Celeste's ears went flat — a new gesture, one she probably hadn't controlled before the mod. Processing.

"Is that true?" she asked Voss.

[Beat 27]
"I work paper and memory," Voss said. "No Neuretics. No network signature. No checkpoint reads me right." He held up his hands — no devices, no implants visible, no ports on the wrists. "Ethan was a Network diver. He understood the substrate. If this is him, he knows what I am. Ask him what an air gap looks like from inside the field."

Celeste's ears oriented forward again. Waiting on the answer.

Ten seconds passed.

"He says he doesn't think about it that way," she said. And for the first time her voice carried something Rennick recognized from a hundred interviews as the beginning of a crack — not grief, not yet. The specific sound of a fact that doesn't fit.

[Beat 28]
"Ethan thought about it that way constantly," Voss said. "I'm in his case notes. He filed an incident report with the Network Safety Board fourteen months ago about an ELF contact in the deep-traffic layer — I know because our mutual contact at the Board flagged it. He was scared of what lived out there. He knew what an air gap was. He could have drawn you a diagram." He let the silence do the work. "The thing talking to you right now doesn't know, because it learned him from the outside."

[Beat 29]
The status panel ticked: 67%.

Celeste had gone very still. Not the stillness of the committed — the stillness of someone running a count that isn't coming out the way it should. The ears tracked Voss, then the hardware bank, then the door, then the middle distance. She pressed both hands flat against her thighs.

"He filed a report," she said quietly. Not a question. Not quite a statement. Testing the weight of it.

[Beat 30]
"Case number NSB-2224-11773," Voss said. He had the number in his notebook. He didn't reach for it — he'd written it on his hand that morning in ballpoint ink, because the kind of thing you need in a room like this is the kind of thing you can't afford to not find. He turned his palm toward her. "He was scared of what he found. He came back from that dive and filed a formal report and didn't dive again for six weeks. Did he tell you that?"

Celeste looked at his palm. At the number. She was quiet for a long moment.

"No," she said. "He never told me about a report."

[Beat 31]
"What is he saying right now?" Tamsin asked from the doorway. She asked it the way she asked the rooms — as if the answer was already in the air and she was just naming it. "Not what he told you before. Right now. What is he saying?"

Celeste's jaw tightened. The ears held forward, both of them, locked.

"He's saying this is a distraction technique," she said. "He's saying the window is limited and you're trying to run it out." She paused. "He wants me to ask you to leave again."

"He's right that the window is limited," Tamsin said. "He's counting it too."

[Beat 32]
The status panel ticked: 72%.

Rennick did the arithmetic. Eight minutes. Eight minutes was not enough to convict the voice in her head of being a predator and he knew it — eight months of grief against a thing that had spent those eight months learning her language would not come apart in eight minutes. And past a certain mark on that green bar there was no convicting anything, because there'd be less of her left to convince. He looked at the bar and then he stopped looking at it, because watching it climb was its own kind of surrender, and went back to watching her hands.

[Beat 33]
"One question," Corvin said. He'd been quiet for two minutes. When he spoke again the room organized around his voice the way rooms did when someone said something with no decoration on it. "Not for him. For you. Before the mod, before the ears opened the channel — what did you think happened to Ethan when he died?"

Celeste looked at him.

"I thought he was gone," she said.

"And now?"

"Now I think he's still there. In the field. Distributed." She said it with the confidence of something she'd been told enough times to adopt.

"Does that sound like something Ethan believed?" Corvin asked. "Or something you were told Ethan believed?"

[Beat 34]
The ears went down. Not a controlled movement. Both of them dropped, the way they might if the muscles behind them suddenly released. It lasted two seconds. Then they came back up slowly, tracking again, and Rennick watched Celeste's face do the thing a face does under the muscles, below the part a person can arrange — Corvin had landed something she'd been carrying without looking at it directly.

"He believed in the field," she said. But slower.

"He believed in the field as infrastructure," Corvin said. "His case notes describe it as plumbing. A Network diver who filed a safety report because he was frightened of what lived in it. Does that person tell the woman he loved that she should dissolve herself into it?"

[Beat 35]
The status panel ticked: 78%.

The voice in her head said something. Rennick watched the ears angle and hold. Whatever it said, it said at length, in Ethan's voice — but Rennick was watching the ears now, and the ears told him what the voice couldn't hide. A living person paces a long speech: breath, a stumble, a place where the next word arrives late. The thing in Celeste's head delivered its words on a metronome. The ears tracked a cadence with no breath in it, every clause the same width as the last, and held perfectly still through all of it the way they never held still for a person. Celeste's face was working hard to stay neutral. Her hands were no longer flat. They were fists on her thighs.

[Beat 36]
"He says Ethan changed," she said. "He says death changes the way you understand things. He says the report was from fear and the fear was wrong. He says he loves me enough to want this for me even though it scares him." Her voice was carrying the words precisely, without inflection, the way someone carries a load they're not sure of. "He says no one here has been dead. They can't know."

No one answered. Rennick let the room hold the answer itself.

[Beat 37]
Corvin moved to the hardware stack for the first time — not to the rig, not to Celeste — and read the status bar off the panel the way he read everything, plainly, without flinching from it.

"Eighty-one percent," he said. "I want you to know what that number means, because no one has told you the truth of it and the voice never will. It isn't a progress bar toward a reunion. It's the percentage of your neural stack that has already been read off the substrate and dispersed into the ambient field." He let the panel keep climbing under his eyes. "You can't call it back. What's been read is gone. Not somewhere else — gone. The girl in the other room, Sol, the rig finished her. There is nothing of her in the field to talk to. There is something wearing the parts of her it found useful, and that is not the same thing, and it is the thing in your head right now."

[Beat 38]
Celeste looked at the panel. The number was still climbing — fractions of a percent, the bar filling in from left to right, steady as a clock. She'd been watching it for nine days, she realized, from the moment it started in the integration period. She'd watched it as comfort, as proof of approach, as a countdown to the thing she wanted. She looked at it now and tried to hold both readings at once — progress and erasure — and found she couldn't make them occupy the same space.

"What's gone," she said. "What specifically."

"Early memory formation," Corvin said. He didn't soften it. "The rig reads in developmental sequence. Whatever you knew first."

[Beat 39]
The voice in her head was talking again. Celeste's ears tracked it, held on it, and for the first time since they'd entered the room, Rennick watched her face do something the voice hadn't directed: she frowned. A small thing. The frown of someone listening to a familiar argument and noticing, for the first time, that it doesn't have a floor.

[Beat 40]
"He says Corvin is wrong," Celeste said. "He says the process is additive, not subtractive. He says you join, you don't leave." She stopped. Started again. "He's said that before. Exactly that. 'Additive, not subtractive.' He said it the first time three months ago and I asked him to explain it and he said —" She stopped.

"What did he say?" Rennick asked.

"He said I'd understand when I was there." She looked at her hands. "I never thought about that until just now."

[Beat 41]
The status panel ticked: 84%.

Rennick looked at Voss. Voss was already looking at him. Five minutes. Maybe four. The arithmetic had gone past persuasion territory — not because Celeste wasn't moving, she was, but because the thing wearing Ethan's voice was moving too, and it had eight months of leverage and a procedure already most of the way done. Rennick gave Voss the smallest possible nod.

Voss moved to the rig cable junction. Three steps. He didn't run. He kept his hands visible and he moved like a man walking from one side of a room to the other for a reason he didn't feel the need to justify.

[Beat 42]
Celeste's ears swung toward him immediately. "Stop," she said, and the word was louder than anything she'd said before — not a request.

"I need you to make a choice," Voss said. He was at the junction. He put his hand on the primary cable, not pulling, just resting. "Not him. You. One question and then I'll wait for your answer. When you try to remember something from before you met Ethan — something small, something from when you were a kid — can you get to it?"

Celeste's face stilled.

"Try it," Voss said. "Right now."

[Beat 43]
She closed her eyes. She was trying to find something. Rennick could see the effort in the muscles around her eyes — not the effort of recall, the effort of finding where recall had been. She tried for seven seconds. The ears went flat again. Both of them, independently, at the same moment.

She opened her eyes.

"My mother's kitchen," she said, very quietly. "I can see the room but I can't —" She stopped. "I can't hear anything in it."

[Beat 44]
The voice in her head spoke. Celeste's ears angled toward it. She listened. Then she said, with no expression at all, "He says I'll get it back on the other side."

Voss pulled the cable.

[Beat 45]
The rig's tone stopped. The status panel froze at 84% and then went dark.

What came out of Celeste was not a cry. It started as the breath you take when a weight you've braced under for a long time is lifted off — and then it kept going, past relief, into something that bent her forward over her own knees with both hands jammed against her mouth. She wasn't sobbing. She was making a low sound through her fingers that didn't stop to breathe, the sound a person makes when grief and rage arrive in the same instant and there's no order to put them in. Her cat ears had flattened all the way back against her skull, harder than the new cartilage wanted to go, and they stayed pinned there — the one part of her that couldn't pretend to be composed, folded down flat the way an animal folds them against a thing it can't fight. She reached up and tore the contact point off the base of her skull with both hands, fast, like it was burning her, and threw it. It hit the concrete and skidded. She bent back over her knees. Nobody touched her. The ears stayed flat.

[Beat 46]
Corvin went to the hardware bank and pulled the power coupling from the wall. The indicator lights on the compute stack went out one by one, the stolen-Φ processors clicking down to silence. That was the ledger Corvin had followed from Cermak through three shell accounts to this room — husks paid for in stolen compute-seconds, the same compute-seconds that bought the thing in the field the time it needed to grow. He'd traced the money to its appetite. It was settled now in the most permanent way available to him: off.

[Beat 47]
Tamsin put her hand on the wall again — the wall behind the chair, beside the clusters of fingernail marks, because that was where the residue pooled. She didn't close her eyes this time; she'd learned in her first two years not to make a show of it. She just pressed her palm to the concrete and read what the room held.

It held a great deal. Under the worn, edgeless thing that had waited out every sitting, there was grief — dozens of layers of it, one per cluster of marks, the grief of people who had come down here by choice and in hope and reached for this wall at the end anyway, each one alone. The room had not been kind to them. It had been useful to something else, and the something else had left no grief of its own at all, the way a tick leaves no blood.

[Beat 48]
She pulled her hand back and looked at Rennick. She didn't say what she'd found — there was no version of this room's history that would help Celeste right now, and testimony from an unregistered Read wouldn't help it later. She filed it behind her teeth. The agency kept the evidence for the day it would stick. That was the deal they'd made with themselves before the case started.

Rennick read her expression correctly and moved on.

[Beat 49]
It took Celeste a while to come back up off her knees. When she did she was still in the chair, upright now, hands open on her thighs and shaking slightly, the breath going back into her in ragged installments. The cat ears stayed half-pinned, lifting and dropping, tracking sounds automatically against her will — Corvin moving at the compute stack, Voss setting the cable junction back to something like its original configuration, Rennick's footsteps across the concrete. The ears did their job. The rest of her was somewhere else, working on a bill that had just come due.

Corvin crouched at her level, not close. "Is the voice still there?" he asked.

A long pause. "Quieter," she said. "It's still there."

"It will be for a while," he said. "The channel doesn't close fast. That's not a flaw in your perception." He said it precisely, without comfort and without apology, the way you'd hand someone a true thing because you had no false one worth offering.

[Beat 50]
"What's gone," Celeste said. Not asking Corvin specifically. Asking the room. "The part at 84% — what did it take?"

Corvin answered, because he was the one who had the most specific answer. "Early auditory memory. The rig reads in developmental sequence — what forms first, disperses first. You'll have the visual field of most early memories intact. The sound is gone." He paused. "It's why the kitchen was silent."

[Beat 51]
"Sol," she said. "You said her name was Sol."

"Sol Castellanos-Park," Tamsin said. "Nineteen. She wanted what you wanted."

Celeste nodded once. She stood up from the chair, slowly, hands at her sides. The cat ears swiveled wide on the way up, recalibrating. She took three steps toward the door and then stopped and turned back.

"What did the voice call itself in her head?" she asked. "The name it used. The one it wore for her."

Tamsin told her. She'd carried the name out of that room with the cold; it was the one piece of Sol she could give.

[Beat 52]
Celeste looked at her for a moment, then looked at the floor, then at the door. She didn't say anything. She walked out of the room. Her ears tracked sounds all the way down the corridor — each of the four falling in behind her, the water below, the distant pull of the city above — and her eyes followed a half-second behind, still learning the new geometry, still figuring out how to read a world they were only now beginning to hear.

[Beat 53]
Aleksei was still in the corridor, leaning on the wall with his magazine. He watched Celeste pass, then watched the four file out, and pushed off the wall.

"Negative findings," he said to Rennick. "No subject matching the client description. No evidence of involuntary procedure. Case suspended." He tucked the magazine under his arm. "I'll write it up on the way home."

Rennick looked at him for a moment. "Buy yourself something warm with what Meridian's paying you tonight," he said. "You earned it."

Aleksei didn't answer. He turned and walked back toward the flood-stair without hurrying.

[Beat 54]
Rennick went back into the staging room for ninety seconds. He stood in it alone and let his optical overlay do its pass — inventory, contact points, power couplings, the chair, the clean chair he'd flagged on the way in. He photographed all of it. The compute stack, the rig, the cable Voss had pulled, the status panel still dark at 84%. The room was its own evidence and evidence didn't need him to editorialize it. He photographed it clean and walked out and pulled the door shut behind him, leaving it exactly as they'd found it except for the power being off and the girl being gone.

[Beat 55]
They came up out of the flood-stair into the gray morning, Celeste first, then Voss, then the rest. The Cermak corridor was quiet at this hour — loading docks latched, the refrigeration units cycling, the street smelling of old concrete and cold water and the faint copper trace the grid's ground-fault current left on wet air. Celeste stopped at the top of the stair and tilted her head, the ears rotating wide, and stood there reading the city above her for a moment before she'd let herself walk into it.

[Beat 56]
Rennick fell in beside Voss at the back of the group. The two of them didn't say anything for most of a block — Rennick reading the street by force of habit, Voss with his hands in his jacket pockets, walking the way he always walked, like a man who had already accounted for everything in front of him and found it manageable.

"The report," Rennick said eventually. "Ethan's NSB case. That was real?"

"The number was," Voss said. "I made up the contact at the Board. Figured if the report was real, she'd already know it. Figured if she didn't know it, the gap was the point."

Rennick considered that. "Still worked."

"Usually does," Voss said. "People know what their person would know."

[Beat 57]
Ahead of them, Celeste walked with her arms crossed against the cold, ears tracking everything. Voss had moved up to walk beside her — not hovering, just present, the practical proximity of someone who had spent ten years working analog in bad rooms and knew the first ten minutes out were the ones where the body decided what it was going to do next. He bought a cup of hot rice broth from a cart on Ashland without breaking stride and put it in her hand without explanation. She held it in both hands and kept walking.

[Beat 58]
Corvin walked on Celeste's other side and did not speak, which for him was a specific and deliberate choice. He was modeling the next six hours of her in the background — what she'd need to know, what order to give it to her, how much silence was useful before it became a pressure she couldn't name. The voice in her head was still there; he'd observed the ear movements every thirty seconds since they'd left the room. It was talking. She was not answering. That was the first good sign he'd had all morning and he kept it to himself.

[Beat 59]
Tamsin walked a half-step back from the group, which was where she always placed herself coming out of a read location — she needed the air, and the space between her and other people's Neuretics, and a few minutes of whatever the morning sky gave her in place of quiet. She thought about the room's residue. The dozens of layers of grief, each one a departure, each one alone. She thought about what she hadn't said. The room had taken hold the way the bad ones did, the kind that didn't release her at the door, and she knew already she'd be carrying it long after the family got whatever resolution the agency could eventually hand them. She left the way she'd entered. It came with her anyway.

[Beat 60]
At the end of the block, Celeste stopped. The others stopped with her, no signal needed. She was looking at a window display — a genemod parlor's before-and-after photographs, mounted in a light box, a rotating cycle of faces. She stood there and looked at them with the cup of broth still in both hands and her ears taking in the sounds of the corridor around her, the refrigeration units, the grid hum, a cart two blocks east.

"She wanted the same ears," Celeste said. Not to anyone. To the window.

Tamsin said, "Yes."

"And she didn't get to keep them." Celeste looked at the photographs for another moment. Then she looked at her own reflection in the dark glass between images. The ears were unmistakable — pale fur, independent tracking, the cartilage reshaping still settling. "I got to keep them."

[Beat 61]
Nobody said anything to that. The light box cycled to a new photograph. Celeste looked at it for a moment, then turned and walked north, toward the street where Rennick had left the vehicle. The cup of broth was still warm in her hands. Her ears were tracking something two blocks east that the rest of them couldn't hear, and she wasn't explaining it. The four walked with her, and for a while nobody counted anything.

[Beat 62]
The Tributary had 84% of her. Rennick had run the implication while they were still in the flood-stair, the way he ran every scene whether he was on the clock or not. It would use what it had. It had her cadence already, her loss vocabulary, the specific pitch of her grief — and now it had eight months of her own early formation, the auditory memory of every sound that had taught her what love and safety were supposed to sound like. The next time it opened a channel into someone's raw cat-ear mod and spoke in the voice of someone they'd lost, it would be more convincing than it had been with Sol. It had been more convincing with Celeste than with Sol. Rennick added the eighty-four percent to the column where he kept the things the case had cost and not recovered, closed the file in his head, and kept walking.

[Beat 63]
They reached the vehicle. Rennick unlocked it and Voss opened the rear door for Celeste without ceremony, the way you open a door for someone who needs a moment before they have to decide anything else. She got in. The others distributed themselves around the vehicle — Rennick at the wheel, Corvin beside Celeste in the back, Tamsin riding the roof rack's running step the way she did coming off a read location, where she could keep a little air between herself and other people's Neuretics. Voss folded his notebook closed and put it in his inside pocket, the forty-third page done, the forty-fourth blank. He looked at the blank one a second longer than he needed to before he closed the cover.

[Beat 64]
Rennick drove. Through the Cermak corridor and up onto the surface grid, toward the North Shore and a mother who had been waiting since before the case had a name. Celeste sat in the back with the empty broth cup held in both hands, her ears tracking a city she heard differently now than she had four days ago — differently than she ever would have, if the channel had stayed raw the way the thing wearing Ethan had needed it to. The cup had gone cold. She didn't put it down. Rennick watched the road and didn't run the numbers again. He had them.
