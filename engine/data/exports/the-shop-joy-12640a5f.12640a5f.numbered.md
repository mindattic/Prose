# The Shop

[Beat 1]
The morning report was one line, in one hand.

06:00 — B. ON LEAVE. SHIFTS COVERED. NOBODY TOUCHES HER MIRROR.

Kyle read it standing in his doorway in yesterday's clothes. On the windowsill, two spent casings had been joined overnight by three more — the whole night's spend, retired in a row, and a freshly loaded Cacophony on the counter beside them with nothing to say about it. Across the hall, Pixel's door stayed closed. She'd been at her bench since 03:00; he'd heard the music come on, very low, around four. Some mornings you help through a wall by leaving the wall alone.

He put on his coat. He put on Silence. The bike's plate cover sat folded on the table where a terminal used to live, and he left it there, and took the stairs, and walked.

The walk to the Loop took fifty-one minutes. The distance was exactly the distance. That was the thing about walking, and it was the reason he was doing it.

[Beat 2]
He knew something was wrong from the top of the underlevel stairs, because the dumpling franchise next to the watch-repair had its shutter down at 09:00 on a working Tuesday, and the only thing that closed a dumpling shop on a Tuesday was the block deciding to be somewhere else for an hour.

The stationery shop's bell was lying on the floor.

Somebody had taken it off its curl of spring steel and set it down — not broken, removed, the way professionals remove a thing that announces customers — and the door stood open, and inside, among the hundred years of drawers, the Continuity Office was doing inventory.

Three of them. Gray coveralls over soft armor, archive cases on dollies, white gloves — actual white gloves, the kind evidence handlers wear — and a fourth at the counter in a gray suit cut from the same bolt as Mr. Able's, with a tablet and the bored cadence of a man reading a manifest to a vendor.

"—seventeen line items, full provenance, originals and duplicates," the suit was saying. "Triple book value, paid on signature. The Office is closing its local accounts, Mr. Vey. It prefers the paper closed with them."

Casper Vey stood behind his counter with his glasses on and his hands folded, and his hands — Kyle catalogued it from the doorway — were perfectly steady. The pot sat cold on its worn ring. No cups were out.

No tea. In three visits across a season, through stolen bikes and customer lists and a war's worth of business, there had always, always been tea.

"Mr. Kyle," Vey said, without turning his head. "You find me in a meeting."

[Beat 3]
The suit turned. The two coverall men by the drawers straightened with the smooth economy of people whose job description had a clause in it, and the third, nearest the door, shifted to put Kyle in his lane.

The suit looked Kyle over — saya, coat, the face from a file he had certainly read — and made the professional choice to continue being pleasant.

"This is a commercial transaction," he said. "It doesn't concern walk-ins."

"He's not a walk-in," Vey said. "He's a subscriber." He unfolded his hands and laid them flat on the counter, and addressed the suit the way he addressed everything, with terms. "Let me restate your offer so the record is clean. The Office wishes to purchase, at triple, every document in this shop touching its local operation: its shells, its desks, its clearing arrangements, its consultant engagements, and its purchases. Including," and his voice did not change at all, which was how Kyle knew, "item seventeen. The juvenile file."

"The Office's paper is the Office's business," the suit said.

"No," said Vey.

The word sat in the shop like a dropped drawer. The suit blinked. Somewhere in his tablet was a number for this contingency and it was not a large number, because in forty years this contingency had never occurred.

"You're declining triple."

"I am declining the sale." Vey took off his half-glasses, folded them, and set them on the counter — a man clearing his hands. "Your office bought a file about a child, for leverage, through a desk you dressed up because you knew I would not sell it to you straight. You made me a party to it. The product of this shop is accuracy, and you used my accuracy to put a wand against a fourteen-year-old's collar." He looked at the suit with no heat whatsoever, which was somehow the coldest thing in the room. "The paper stays. The paper is the only honest witness this trade has. Buying the witness is what guilty institutions do, and this counter does not service that transaction. Not at triple. Not at any multiple. Good morning."

The suit did the math out loud, which Kyle respected. "There are three of us, Mr. Vey, and the dollies are already loaded."

"And the manifest of what's on them is already in four other shops," Vey said pleasantly. "I have twenty-three reciprocity arrangements and I used six of them at dawn. Roll those dollies out that door unsigned and every desk in the trade reads the theft by lunch, itemized, with your faces. The Office can buy a great many things. It cannot buy back being KNOWN." He put his glasses back on. "Also, the gentleman behind you has been to the destination on your tablet, and your office wrote a no-damage clause about him once. I'd review it."

Three sets of eyes did the trip — saya, hands, face, file — and arrived where that trip always arrived.

Kyle didn't move. Moving would have been a second argument and the room only needed one. He stood in the doorway with the morning behind him and let them read the night on him: five casings' worth of it, a yard's worth, twelve tons' worth.

The suit closed his tablet.

"The Office will note your position," he told Vey, in the tone of a man already drafting the memo that explained this away, and the white gloves unloaded the dollies — every folder back in its drawer, supervised by Vey's pointing finger, THAT one goes under C, no, lower, thank you — and they filed out past Kyle one at a time, polite as a tide going out, and the last one stooped, without being asked, and put the bell back on its spring.

It rang when the door shut behind them. The shop exhaled.

"Now," said Casper Vey, and lit the burner under the pot, "tea."

[Beat 4]
He poured two cups and set Kyle's down with the handle aimed outward, and for a while the only sound was the Pulse coming up through the tiles and the pot ticking as it cooled, and when Vey finally spoke he did it the way he did everything: by stating the terms.

"Files involving children are sold at children's rate, to children, for the purposes of children. That is the term. It is the one term I have never negotiated, and eleven days ago the Continuity Office bought item seventeen through a cutout I failed to inspect, because they paid early, and paid exactly, and the presentation was impeccable." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I sold a defective product, Mr. Kyle. First one in forty years. You watched what it cost me to say no to the recall."

"You said no to triple."

"I said no to laundering my own ledger." He drew a single page from beneath the counter — good stock, the ink dark and exact, folded the particular way — and set it between them. "Customer loyalty disclosure. One fact, free, no record of transmission."

Kyle unfolded it.

MR. ABLE. CONTINUITY OFFICE — LOCAL PRINCIPAL.
TEA HOUSE: MERIDIAN PAVILION, THIRD UNDERLEVEL, LAKEVIEW ADJACENT. STANDING RESERVATION: TUESDAYS AND FRIDAYS, 10:00–12:00.
SECURITY: TWO ADVANCE, TWO INSIDE, ONE EXTERIOR, ONE MOBILE. ROTATION: FORTY MINUTES.
HABIT: CORNER TABLE, NORTHEAST. BACK TO WALL.

"I sell maps, Mr. Kyle," Vey said. "Here is a map. What you do at the destination is the destination's business."

It was the same sentence as the first visit, word for word, and both of them sat for a moment with the distance between the two deliveries — a philosophy, the first time; this, now — and the distance measured exactly one girl on leave and a one-line log.

Kyle put the page in his coat. He drank the tea. It was very good, and he said so, and Vey accepted the compliment with the small nod of a man whose product had never been the problem.

"One more thing, since you're a subscriber." Vey's voice went back to commerce, which Kyle understood was the man returning himself to the shelf. "Your file is now the most requested item in this shop. Four standing orders for updates. I will keep selling it — that is the term you walked in under, and terms don't bend for liking a customer." A beat. "But updates are filed at my discretion as to TIMING, and I am a very busy man this quarter. Some drawers may not get refiled until... oh. Spring."

Kyle looked at him. The cleanest hands in the city poured a second cup, steady as a level.

"That's the closest thing to a kindness you've ever said out loud."

"It's inventory management," said Casper Vey. "Get out of my shop. Destinations keep business hours."

[Beat 5]
His work terminal had a posting on it when he got home. He read it with his coat still on.

CONTRACT: STANDING — ADVISORY NOTE.
CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE DESTINATION.
CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE SECURITY ROTATION.
CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE TEA HOUSE'S BOOKING SYSTEM, ITS SUPPLIERS, ITS PAYROLL, AND ITS FIRE CODE COMPLIANCE, WHICH IS POOR.
CLIENT WILL BE ATTENDING.

He stood with that for a long moment — eleven years of paper, and it had never once announced presence; it was a client that existed as receipts, and it had just told him it was coming somewhere — and then the secondary terminal chirped, the logbook channel, a second entry growing under the morning's one line, in a different hand, pressed hard:

14:20 — B. CAME UP TO THE ROOF. SAT WATCH ONE HOUR. DIDN'T LOG ANYTHING. WE DIDN'T EITHER.
14:21 — CORRECTION: LOGGING IT. THIS LOG IS HONEST. SHE CAME BACK. THAT GOES IN THE LOG.

Kyle set Cacophony on the counter next to its five casings, and filled the kettle, and stood at the window while it came up, looking east toward the Loop, where a shop with a brass bell was open for business between a watch-repair and a dumpling franchise, selling everybody to everybody at honest rates, unburned, unburnable — and where, this morning, for the price of one term kept, the most temperatureless man in the city had picked a side and called it inventory management.

Tuesday, the map said. Ten to noon. Corner table, northeast.

The kettle clicked. Two days to plan a tea party.
