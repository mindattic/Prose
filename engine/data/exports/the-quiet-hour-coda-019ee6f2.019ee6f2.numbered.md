# Chapter 6: The Quiet Hour

_Ñu's funeral. Kyle absorbs loss #1 the only way he can: as a cost that has already been paid._

[Beat 1]
Nobody talked on the way back.

Ledger drove. Stash had the cases on her lap, which she didn't need to do — the floor of the van was fine, the locks were fine — but she did it anyway, and nobody said anything about it. The elevated passed overhead in sections, the sodium lights throwing orange bars across the windshield at intervals.

Kyle thought about the reading. Not the woman — the reading itself. The vault-pull Null had taken from the operator's head, the file she'd carried behind her eyes on the dock, the thing War Dog had come to collect. He would not know what was in it. That was the part of the job you didn't keep. The client had paid for extraction. The extraction had been performed. What happened afterward was not in the scope.

He thought about the way she'd moved on the dock. The way she'd counted the geometry the same second he did, without needing to ask. There was a word for that in the trade. He did not use it in the van.

The relay stayed quiet for the whole drive. That was fine. That was correct.

[Beat 2]
Pixel's light was on across the hall.

The particular rhythm of it — two steps and back, two steps and back — the way she moved between her bench and her terminal when she was in the middle of something. He stood in his doorway for a moment. He did not knock. He went to bed.

He woke at 04:00 and lay in the dark until the gray at the window was enough to call it morning. He made coffee. He drank it standing. He did not look at the relay.

By 07:30 he looked at the relay. No new jobs. The previous client had paid in advance — clean rate, clean scope, full deposit before entry. That transaction was closed. That name was done.

He had Mrs. Chen's number. He called it.

"Thursday," she said, before he finished the question. Her voice was already carrying the weight of someone who had done this before. "Six o'clock. Tell whoever she kept."

He did not ask how she knew. She had been running that counter for sixty years. She knew.

[Beat 3]
The rain arrived first — it always does on Halsted, running down the south face of the elevated infrastructure overhead and making a second street-level weather system out of the drip. Kyle came on foot. He had the address right. He checked it twice anyway, not because he doubted it but because standing on the sidewalk was still standing on the sidewalk, and once he went inside the number would mean something different.

The block had been the same block for forty years. The grate pattern on the streetlights gave it away — the older municipal spec, wider spacing, no sensor housing at the center. The buildings were functional and worn in the way things get worn when they are used correctly for a long time. A laundry line between two third-floor windows held three shirts and a child's jacket, all the same off-color from the synthesis-corridor air that drifted east on certain winds. A sundial dog crossed the far end of the block on whatever circuit it kept, indifferent to the rain.

Kyle stood at the vestibule.

There was a handwritten sign taped to the inside of the glass — visible through it, the adhesive tape gone amber with age though the paper itself was new. Ñu's full name. Kyle had never seen it written out before.

He stood at the vestibule longer than he meant to.

It was not unusual, in the work they did, to know someone for years without the full name. The work ran on handle and callsign and the specific weight of a person's silence on a comm line. The handle was the real thing. The full name on the paper was something else — not more or less, just different. A document of something.

He went inside.

[Beat 4]
Forty people Kyle did not know.

Mrs. Okafor had a clipboard. She was moving through the front room the way a good coordinator moves through a site — not hurrying, not pausing, handing a plate here and redirecting foot traffic there, her expression carrying the specific neutrality of someone who has decided that the day will proceed correctly and is simply implementing that decision. She did not need anything from Kyle specifically. He registered this and filed it.

His neuretics registered something else first.

The Psyk was in the corner. Jaw set, both hands flat on their thighs, seated with their back to the wall in the chair closest to the window. The Read was running open — Kyle's neuretics caught the frequency before his conscious attention reached the corner of the room. Nineteen hertz. Not heard, exactly. Felt in the back teeth. The ambient frequency of something spilling over. He noted it. He did not remark on it. The Psyk's jaw stayed set. Their hands stayed flat.

War Dog was at the back wall. Not sitting. Holding a cup of something he had not drunk — Kyle could tell by the way he held it, both hands, cup level, the way a man holds a thing when the thing is not the point. War Dog had been in the work fifteen years longer than Kyle, which meant he had stood in rooms like this fifteen years longer, which meant he knew how to stand in them. Kyle crossed the room and stood next to him. There was no greeting. It was not unfriendly. It was the way men stand together at the thing they would rather not be standing in.

Neither of them remarked on the hum.

On a corkboard near the far window there were photographs. Most were printed on paper feeds — the standard quick-format, slick-edged, from a public output terminal. One was analog. Paper, not plastfilm. A little smaller than the others, the corners soft from handling. Ñu at maybe nineteen, laughing at something outside the frame. The laugh was genuine — full, unguarded, body in it. Kyle looked at it for a while. He reached to take it from the board — and Mrs. Okafor's hand closed gently around his wrist from behind, barely a weight.

"That one stays," she said, not looking at it.

She released his wrist and moved on. He let his hand come back. He did not recognize the laugh. He had known Ñu for six years and he did not recognize that laugh, which meant it had lived somewhere he had not been.

Mrs. Okafor materialized at his left shoulder and handed him a plate without asking. Jollof rice, two pieces of chicken, a fold of flatbread. He took it with both hands. The plate was warm. He stood there with it, next to War Dog, next to the cup War Dog had not drunk.

He did not eat.

[Beat 5]
They came without announcement, through the rain.

Pixel came from across the hall, which meant she'd known. She didn't ask Kyle how that was possible — she had already known.

She sat at his shoulder and said nothing for a long time. Then: "She was good."

"Yeah," Kyle said.

"I know three people who are that good. Now I know two."

He didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that was the right size.

The others came after — faces from jobs Kyle had worked adjacent to, not with; people from Null's side of the ledger, names he didn't have, which was correct, which was how it worked. Forty people in a noodle counter on Halsted, eating broth in a woman's honor whose real name nobody in the room had been given, and all of them understanding exactly what that meant.

[Beat 6]
Four seconds.

The Psyk lost containment and the hum spiked — not louder, deeper, the nineteen hertz thickening into something that sat in the chest. Three people near the window flinched without knowing why, the involuntary micro-flinch of a body responding to something it had no name for. Kyle's neuretics spiked with the frequency, a brief static bloom at the array periphery. He met the Psyk's eyes across the room. The Psyk's jaw was still set, their hands still flat, but their eyes had gone wide and slightly unfocused in the way eyes go when something is running through a person that cannot entirely be contained. Kyle held the look. The Psyk pulled it back — a visible effort, the way a thing is pulled back when it does not want to be pulled. The frequency dropped. The three people near the window settled without knowing they had moved. Four seconds.

War Dog spoke.

"She knew," he said. "Ñu knew the job would end this way. She'd priced it out."

He said it the way a man states a fact that has already been established and does not require argument — not accusation, not absolution, just the accounting. He said the specific amount of the last contract. The number had been acceptable to her. She had decided this.

Kyle said, "She was wrong about the price."

War Dog looked at him. The cup was still in both hands, still level, still not drunk.

"What was the correct price?" War Dog asked.

His neuretics ran the valuation before he could stop it — replacement-schedule, contract record, what filling the gap in the roster would cost. He let it arrive. Then:

"More than she named," Kyle said.

War Dog looked at him for a moment. Then he looked at the cup.

Mrs. Okafor was at the far side, refilling a tray. Someone laughed quietly at something someone else had said about Ñu — a laugh that was not the one in the photograph. The hum ran through it all, low and steady. Four beats of it, keeping time, before it dropped back to the baseline and stayed there.

[Beat 7]
Kyle found the Psyk outside on the curb, both hands pressed over their ears. Not in distress, exactly — in management. Their elbows were on their knees and their posture was careful, the posture of someone who has been here before and knows the protocol.

The hum was audible without augmentation now.

He sat down next to the Psyk on the curb. He still had the plate.

The Psyk didn't look at him. They kept their hands over their ears and stared at the wet concrete of the street, where the rain was coming down clean and the runoff was collecting in the gutter at a steady rate.

"It's not one grief," the Psyk said. Their voice was controlled but close to the surface. "Forty griefs running at once. None of them the same frequency."

Kyle set the plate down on the curb beside him. The jollof rice had gone cold. The chicken was still on it, untouched.

The hum kept running.

[Beat 8]
Mrs. Chen found him at the counter's end when the second round came around. She set a folded envelope beside his bowl without comment — heavy paper, cream, the kind with a watermark pressed into it. The letterhead at the top read *Vey's Antiquity & Stationary*, and below it an address on Dearborn he'd never had a reason to know.

Two words inside, no signature, in a hand that was not Mrs. Chen's.

*Meet me.*

He read it twice. He folded it once and put it in his coat. He finished his broth. The wok came back up like a bell, and the counter filled again with the sound of something ongoing, and he sat in it for a while before he stood and said goodbye to the ones he knew the names of.

He did not know all of them.

That was correct.

[Beat 9]
The hum had stopped before he left the vestibule. By the time he cleared the elevated infrastructure and crossed onto Fullerton, his neuretics had settled to baseline and the cold was doing its ordinary work.

He was four blocks north when the notation appeared:

AMBIENT INTERFERENCE — 18.9 HZ — TYPE UNCLASSIFIED

The same classification as the carousel.

He read it the way he'd read the carousel notation — without stopping, without altering his pace. The frequency was below hearing; it did not announce itself; the body registered something present and nearby, a low animal alertness without an object. He noted the cross-streets and kept walking. The rain was clean. The drainage was working.

The notation held for six blocks, then decayed. His neuretics filed it under the date. The file was two entries now, three weeks apart, different neighborhoods, same classification. He had not made a folder for them.

He got home. The relay had a new posting. Medical transport, short-haul, loading dock in the south Loop — the kind of logistics cover that moved things that were not quite medical. The client field resolved to the same shell it always resolved to. The rate was inside his range. He accepted it, confirmed the 0600 contact window, and filed the parameters.

He went to bed. He did not think about the frequency.
