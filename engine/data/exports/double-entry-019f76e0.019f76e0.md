# Double Entry

The retainer arrived three days before Tally died.

By mid-afternoon on the eighth day he was in the counter district, three subgrades below the transit line. The shaft corridor smelled of reclaimed sealant and old composite. He had found the address by address rather than circuit tag — Tally's counter had no circuit registration. Preference or principle; he did not yet have the data to decide. He knocked. Nothing. He tried the handle. It opened.

The counter was empty. The light was on — the kind of overhead that had not been replaced in years, slightly yellow. A table, composite scarred at the center from years of weight. A shelf. On the shelf, a hard case, and beside it a folded paper — thermal receipt, his name on the outside in block print, the first letters child-large and the last compressed and adult, the same dual-script transition he would later read in the book itself. No embedded tracer in the paper. In 2226 that was either poverty or intention.

He put the note in his jacket without opening it. He looked at the room first. The table's wear patterns: heaviest at the center-left, the posture of someone who wanted to see who was coming. The shelf had four objects: the case, the note, a text — *A Practical Grammar of Gray Zone Instruments, Third Edition* — and a tin that smelled of long matches. She had set up the audit site before she died and placed the tools where she wanted them.

The district was the Low — the ungoverned belt between thirty and three hundred meters. The Fringe Compact, the CorpoNation that administered Gray Zone financial instruments, maintained something closer to a policy position than rule of law there.

He had accepted the terms eight days ago, before breakfast: Φ40, flat rate, disposal clause included. She had thought about what the book might require — and what it might attract. No penalty for findings; that clause was hers.

His own circuit registration was current. He had been code before he was chassis — an accounting and verification system that had acquired personhood under a legislative instrument ratified before the governments that wrote it were absorbed into CorpoNation charters. The obligation survived. It usually did, when there was commercial utility in honoring it: synthetic persons could hold contracts, bear liability, be registered. The Fringe Compact maintained the registry now. They called him a person. There was jurisdiction-specific case law on the question, and he had read it.

He worked the timing from the circuit record while he stood at the table — the city's neuretic overlay, address and identity and payment rail for anyone registered to use it: her filing at 14:23:07, eleven days before today; the death-verification that triggered the standing instruction at 09:44:23, eight days back. The Fringe Compact's relay had needed twenty-three minutes — inherited infrastructure from three administrations before the Schism, unimproved. The arithmetic resolved without remainder. She had been alive when she filed it.

He opened the note.

*The book is in the case. Terms are in the circuit filing. Three claimants will find you; do not go looking for them. The disposal clause is mine — use it if you need to. — G. Ibarra-Voigt.*

Gerta Ibarra-Voigt, who had called herself Tally since before the child-hand section of the book, which he had not read yet. Who had built a retainer for a specific auditor and had not structured the incentives to enforce correct behavior because she had decided he would behave correctly. Who had set up the audit site and placed the disposal clause herself and then died and waited for the trigger to fire.

He crossed to the case. He set his palm flat on the cover — the temperature of the metal, the pressed-steel latches, the weight of a thing that had been sitting here waiting for him. He stayed there for a moment he could not account for to a billing standard. Then he opened the case.

The book was smaller than he would have predicted.

He had predicted, from the volume of entries the fringe registry implied and the forty-year span of the retainer terms, a physical object of some weight — a ledger proper, the kind with a rigid cover and an index spine. What he had was cloth-bound, hand-sewn, the thread that closed the spine the same thread that had started it: he could see both ends where the sewing looped back to the beginning and was tied off in a knot that had held for four decades. The cloth was the color of something that had absorbed forty years of the same counter's light and grease and handling and had stopped being any particular color — not faded, not dirty, just settled into what it was.

He lifted it by the spine. Two fingers, standard examiner's hold. 380 grams. He set it on the composite outside the case.

The smell came immediately: under-counter residue, something fried years ago still present in the composite fibers, something sweet and old underneath, and beneath both a mineral dryness — the smell of a room that had been the same temperature for a long time. He turned it to the overhead light. The cover had no water damage. He checked all four corners: compressed from carrying, not from dropping — carried often, by someone who thought of it as a working object. He opened to the first page.

The handwriting started immediately: columns, three of them, date on the left, amount on the right, notation in the center. Child-hand script in the early pages — large, slightly irregular, the letter spacing of someone still learning what letters were. He did not try to read it. He turned pages instead, corner by corner, checking the binding for loose signatures. Three hundred and twelve pages. The last entry was dated eleven days ago, the ink fractionally proud of the page, not yet fully absorbed.

He went back to the beginning and read.

The child-hand entries were a cipher he decoded by pattern: she had not used standard Gray Zone notation, had developed her own. The structure underneath was consistent: debt identified, debtor noted, terms implied, date set. He cross-referenced the first ten entries against the fringe registry. Seven returned hits — amount, settlement date, witness notation. All accurate. He ran the next twenty. Eleven verified, eleven more with Compact secondary corroboration.

He turned to page forty-one.

He ran the cross-reference. The registry returned nothing.

He tried adjacent entries. Nothing. He ran the whole page-forty-one-through-sixty block, twenty-two entries. Not gaps — absent records. The distinction was specific: a gap left a filing date with no corresponding document. An absent record had never been filed at all. He had built the habit of reading for absence into his standard audit pass because anyone could verify what a registry held. He was paid to notice what it had never held.

He looked at the amounts. They were not small.

He set the pen flat.

Someone had lent to people and had not asked for the money back, and had not written that they were not asking. They had written the debt. They had written only the debt.

The door opened before he could close the cover.

She was tall — the Voigt jaw, recognizable from the fringe registry profile, which he had pulled during transit and which showed: Renn Voigt, 24, counter district, kiosk clerk, no outstanding instruments. Her collar was still warm-damp at the edge. She looked at the book on the composite, then at Ledger, and the order of that told him something.

"Renn Voigt," she said. "Tally was my great-aunt."

He looked at her. The document sleeve under her left arm. The jacket's left cuff repaired in slightly different thread. Ink at the cuticle of her right index finger, the specific residue of kiosk-grade thermal transfer.

"Sit," he said.

She sat across the corner from him, not across the table — close enough to see the page, too far to reach it. He pulled his audit log.

"I need it certified whole," Renn said. "Every page, every entry, the binding intact. Certified by someone the Circuit will recognize." She said it the way you say something you have rehearsed past the point of believing it will work. "Not for sale. Not for claim. I need a document that says the book is what it is."

A connection probe arrived on his circuit tag — unsolicited, no authentication header, origin masked to a Low-zone relay. He declined it without shifting his attention from the document sleeve.

He wrote her name, the date, her claim as stated. Not her interpretation. Her words. He read it back flat, no inflection.

"Recorded," he said. "That's not agreement. That's record."

She watched him write. She did not continue after she stopped. She had filled the first silence because she thought she needed to. She was not filling this one.

He looked at the book, then back at her. She was looking at it the way a person looks at something that already contains information they have — that they have read, or would have read, or have considered reading. Not curiosity. Familiarity.

"I sorted Tally's effects," she said. "After the funeral. She kept her files in a flat drawer in the back."

He wrote that down. She watched him write. She did not continue.

"The flat drawer is visible from the counter," he said.

"Yes."

He let the silence run again. She did not fill it. She had decided how much to say, and she had said it.

The second arrival did not knock.

Broad, Yoruba grandfather in the face, the posture of a man who had spent most of his career letting his body do the negotiating — weight settled, no wasted motion, the stance of someone accustomed to waiting in doorways. He set a flat envelope on the table without sitting, without greeting, and looked at Ledger the way experienced people look at other experienced people: briefly, once, enough.

"Vig sends his regards," the man said. "And his documentation."

Ledger opened the envelope. He counted before he read: eleven sheets, thumb along the corner of each one. Eleven. He set them in purchase-date order and read.

Each page: a purchase receipt with the original seller's notation. Transfer date. Page number in a cipher that matched the book's own notation exactly. The paper aging was consistent across the receipts and consistent with the book's physical evidence — stored together, same location, same temperature, for a long time. Someone had kept these together with the careful attention of a person who knew they would need them eventually.

He found the Compact citation without searching: Fringe Compact Annotations, Third Revised, Section 14, Clause 7. Partial transfer of handwritten instruments, Gray Zone origin, purchase documentation required at time of claim. He read it aloud under his breath, for his log. *Receipts satisfy Section 14, Clause 7.*

Eleven pages. Eleven settled entries in the first forty pages of the book — all within the verified half, all with settlement marks and witness notations. The sum, net of three partial payments recorded in the Compact secondary ledger's margin notation: Φ14,200.

"Vig's position," the man said, and produced a second sheet. Handwritten, signed with an initial only. He set it in front of Ledger without offering it to Renn. Vig wanted what the audit found. The precise figure. Not a quanta more, not a quanta less.

Ledger looked at the initial on the sheet. Consistent across three sheets in different ink — the same hand, different pens, different days. The handwriting of a careful man who had made a habit of the same gesture enough times to commit it to muscle memory. The kind of careful that meant Vig had owned these eleven pages for years and had known, all along, what the gap in the book was.

Renn Voigt had gone still across the corner from him. Her ink-stained hands were flat on the table and she was watching the receipts the way you watch something you already knew existed but had not expected to see today. She had sorted Tally's effects. She had known the receipts were real. Knowing and watching them land on a table were different things.

"Tell Vig the eleven verify," Ledger said. "The figure resolves at end of audit."

The man nodded once, took the envelope but left the receipts, and went.

At the far end of the corridor, past the emissary's retreating posture, a transit vehicle moved at low speed along the access lane — aerostatic, matte panel, tinted. It took the corner without stopping. He noted it and assigned it no weight.

﻿The third arrival knocked.

One knock, knuckle-flat, neither assertive nor tentative — the knock of someone who had established, before they arrived, that they would be admitted. Ledger said nothing. The door opened.

Mid-forties, mid-height, the face assembled by the diaspora from components it had declined to file under any particular national taxonomy. They wore licensed street in a cut that cost what it cost without advertising that it cost it. They carried a flat document sleeve — the professional kind, used for paper instruments under transport — and set it on the table with the deliberateness of someone who had thought about where to put it. The sleeve was clean, but the left corner seam had been pressed flat by use, not damage; a tool that had crossed many tables.

"Quire," they said. Not introduction. Label.

They sat without being asked. They looked at the book first, then the receipts, then at Ledger — the same order Renn had used, and he noted the pattern without assigning meaning.

"The broker wants priced paper," Quire said. Their hands were folded. "Certified entries. What they document, valued and signed. Not the object. The broker works provenance — historical authentication for private collections. The entry needs to be real; it doesn't need to be a current instrument."

He looked at the document sleeve without touching it. He looked at Renn Voigt's hands, flat on the composite. He thought about what a private collection did with authenticated historical debt records from a forty-year counter in the Gray Zone.

"The broker is not a corponation," Quire said.

"No certification, no purchase," he said.

"Correct. Then the broker's interest depends on your findings."

"Also correct."

He wrote: *Quire — broker's representative. Provenance house, private collections, historical authentication. Broker not a corponation; not named. Claim contingent on certification.* He set the pen flat.

Quire looked at the book. "You haven't finished the audit."

"No."

"Do you have a sense of the scope."

"I have preliminary findings. Preliminary findings aren't certification." He looked at them. "You're asking whether there will be anything to buy."

"The broker prefers not to waste their representative's time." The neutral expression settled into place like something it had been waiting to do. "If the audit yields nothing certifiable, the broker would prefer to know before you formally close the retainer. There are other engagements this affects."

He heard the last sentence for what it was: topology. Other jobs. Other books. Quire moved provenance for a buyer with ongoing needs in exactly this category.

"If the audit yields nothing certifiable," Ledger said, "you'll be the first person I tell."

Quire looked at him. "The broker's name isn't relevant to the audit."

"It's relevant to my log. I document principals."

"The broker prefers not to appear in circuit logs. I am the principal for these purposes."

He looked at this for three seconds. Certain principals preferred opacity; he had noted, across fourteen years, the distribution of reasons — privacy, liability, active concealment — and the last category weighted toward certain kinds of priced paper transactions. He had no evidence for which category applied here.

He wrote: *Broker not identified. Quire is principal of record for this engagement.* He underlined the last four words.

He worked through the night because there was no reason not to.

The work itself was the catalog: entry by entry, each one cross-referenced against the fringe registry and the Compact secondary ledger and, where those ran dry, the public record filings from the zone's recorder's office. The settled entries in the first forty pages verified cleanly. Forty-three instruments. Settlement marks, witness notations, amounts, dates, counterparty references — everything the Compact required, everything the registry held.

He turned to page forty-one at three in the morning and ran the cross-reference.

The registry returned nothing. He tried the secondary ledger, the recorder's public filings, the name index with aliases. He ran all twenty-two. Not one had ever been filed anywhere.

He set the query results in his log and held what he had.

He stood. He went to the shelf and picked up the tin of long matches — the heft of it, the dry rattle inside — and set it back down. He went back to the table.

The structure of the absence was what told him something. He had worked with missing records — records that should exist and didn't, filing error, lost document. He had not often worked with records that had never been submitted at all, because a person who kept accounts as precisely as Tally kept accounts would have filed every instrument she intended to be an instrument.

Unless she had not intended them to be instruments.

He looked at the amounts again. Sized, he observed, to what a person in this zone at this period could plausibly owe without the sum becoming threatening. Sized to dignity. She had written the loans at amounts that would not shame the borrower. And she had written them in the book, in the same cipher as everything else, and she had not filed them.

He worked until the auditable portion of the book was fully documented and then he stopped and sat in the room with the two categories of record: the forty pages that meant something to the Compact, and the twenty-two that meant something to someone who was dead.

The Compact's standard on gifts was one sentence.

He found it in the Third Revised at the beginning of the day, had kept it open on the secondary panel while he worked the night through, and by morning he had the full definition committed without trying: *Falsification: misrepresentation of the nature of a transaction in the written record of that transaction.*

He read the twenty-two entries again with that definition in hand.

The nature of each transaction was a loan. She had written it as a loan — amount, debtor, terms implied by the notation structure she had used for every other instrument in the book. The amounts were real. The debtors were real. The debts were real. He could find no entry in the twenty-two that misrepresented the nature of the transaction as written.

He set the book down and stood up.

The room had been the same temperature for three days. He walked to the far wall and back — eight steps, eight steps — and sat down. Renn Voigt was still at the corner. She watched him walk without comment, which was what she always did when he moved, and which he had stopped registering on day two.

That wasn't what she had done.

He knew by now what she had done. The Compact's standard did not have a category for a person who wrote the truth in the private language of their own ledger and did not file it. The Compact's interest was in what got filed. It had no jurisdiction over silence.

He looked at the amounts again. He had run the average: Φ180 across forty-three auditable instruments in the first section of the book. The twenty-two had averaged Φ30. Sized to what a person who was not wealthy enough to refuse could accept without feeling the weight of it every time they looked at their account. She had given forty years of gifts to people who would not take gifts, and she had written them down as the debts they were, and she had filed nothing.

He wrote: *The twenty-two entries are materially accurate records of transactions that were legally structured as loans and practically extended as gifts. The gap between the legal structure and the practical reality is not falsification. It is what she chose.*

Vig arrived in the late afternoon.

He did not arrive alone. He came with two carriers — Gray Zone labor, the careful anonymous kind, positioned in the doorframe without entering, without being told to stay. One of them had the sub-dermal reinforcement collar common to the Low's protection work — the silver edge of it visible at the jaw, the kind of cyberware that communicated function without advertising the manufacturer. Vig himself was shorter than the fringe registry height notation, which sometimes happened with records that predated calibrated measurement: sixty, compact, the face of a man who had built forty years of careful habits around the understanding that he was not large enough to win arguments with his body.

Ledger kept writing. He finished the sentence he was in and set the pen flat.

A probe arrived on his dormant secondary registration — the one he kept current for salvage bids, unused for years. He declined it. Someone was running a methodical sweep of his registered identifiers.

Vig looked at the book. He did not look at Renn Voigt. His right hand came up off his hip and moved two inches toward the composite — toward the book — before he stopped it. He put the hand back. He looked at the book the way a man looks at a property dispute he has been patient about for a long time.

"The eleven verify," Ledger said. "I said so yesterday."

"I know what you said." Vig's voice was the unhurried kind. "I came to know the scope."

Ledger had spent some of the morning on Vig's arithmetic. The two carriers in the doorframe were not structural — Vig was not a man who had come to threaten, because a man who came to threaten a Compact auditor in the middle of an active retainer would need to spend the rest of his life moving between zones, and Vig had spent forty years not moving between zones. What the carriers communicated was that Vig had enough resources to bring carriers, and that he wanted Ledger to know it. The carriers were a price signal.

"Eleven pages," Ledger said. "Φ14,200, net partial payments. Not a quanta more. Not less."

The exhale Vig let out through his nose was almost nothing — barely audible, the involuntary release of a man who had been holding the number in his chest for years and had just heard it spoken aloud for the first time. Then the careful habits took it back. He looked at the book. He looked at Ledger. "The balance."

"Final figure resolves when the audit closes. You'll have it."

Vig looked at Renn Voigt. She looked back at him without particular expression. He looked away first.

He nodded once, and the two carriers turned out of the doorframe without being told, and he went after them.

Beyond the shaft entrance, at the corridor's sightline limit, the same matte-paneled transit vehicle he had noted two days before moved past at the same low speed. Same heading. Same rate. The pattern of a thing establishing a fact about a location.

He had now assigned it weight.

The door opened onto the shaft corridor and the first shot clipped the frame three centimeters above his shoulder. He went left. Cover behind the junction column — composite, load-bearing, adequate. Two shooters at the corridor's far end, one elevated on a service platform, one on the floor. He assessed the distance, the platform angle, the weapon: compact, selector on full auto based on the sound of the first round, Greybox or equivalent, Fringe-supply provenance. The floor shooter was not in a stable position. The one on the platform was stable but exposed from below. He had time before either could reposition to a better angle.

He spent it on Vig.

The exhale Vig had let through his nose — barely audible, involuntary — had told him something. He had spent most of the morning on what exactly, and he thought he had it now.

He came around the column low and put two rounds into the platform support strut. The platform angled. The shooter tried to compensate and did not. He registered the sound of that as the floor shooter's weapon cycled on full auto and missed him by a wider margin than the first shot, which told him something about the floor shooter's baseline accuracy and their current state.

Vig had known the number. The question was when he had known it.

There was a fourth party at his back — he had heard the footfall eleven steps behind him when he opened the door and had read it as coincidence or coordination. Coordination. The intrusion arrived at the same moment: an unauthorized process request against his identity architecture, probing for a root handler, a hardware interrupt, something to seize. He declined the request without interrupting what he was doing. The fourth party's approach slowed as the intrusion met nothing it recognized. A Ghost jacker operating on the assumption that a registered synthetic was addressable hardware. The architecture they were looking for did not exist in the chassis. He was not running from the chassis. The request timed out.

He put a round through the floor shooter's weapon arm at a range where the geometry of the corridor made a different aim inadvisable, and the TA-9 went to the floor. The floor shooter followed it.

Vig had known the number before he arrived. The involuntary release — forty years of holding a figure he could not say aloud, and then hearing someone else say it — had been relief, not shock. A man learning a number does not react that way. A man who has been carrying one and has been told he no longer has to — that was the face he had seen.

The two remaining — the jacker and one who had not committed to the engagement — were moving toward the corridor's far access shaft. He let them go. He noted the direction and the jacker's gait under stress and filed both without stopping to decide what they were for.

He went back inside. He set the door behind him. The book was in the case. The overhead light was still slightly yellow. He stood at the counter's scarred table for a moment and finished the calculation.

Vig had come to know the scope — he had said so. But the scope he had come to know was not twenty-two entries. It was whether those twenty-two were in the book or not. He had already known they were. What he needed to know was whether Ledger knew what they were.

He sat down. He opened the book to page forty-three and went back to work.

Quire came back the next morning.

They set the document sleeve on the table again — same placement, same deliberateness — and said: "The broker would like to revise the scope. Historical certification is not required. Provenance documentation of the physical object would satisfy."

"No," Ledger said.

"The physical object only. Chain of custody from Tally to the estate to the retainer. The broker's interest is in the object's authenticity, not the entries' validity."

"No." He wrote: *Quire — second visit. Broker revised to object provenance only. Declined.*

Quire looked at Renn Voigt, who was sitting across the corner in the same position she had occupied for three days, ink-stained hands flat on the composite. Quire looked at her for two seconds and looked back at Ledger.

"The broker is prepared to be flexible on timing."

"I've recorded your position," Ledger said. "My answer is the same."

Quire picked up the document sleeve and folded it once along the press-crease. Ledger was already reaching for the book — to close it for the day, the motion automatic, the same motion he used every evening — when Quire's hand came down flat on the cover.

Not a grab. A placement. Deliberate, without asking. The sleeve still folded in their other hand. They looked at Ledger.

He was already in motion. He finished it: his palm over their fingers, settling there without urgency, the way you complete a sentence you started before someone interrupted it. Not confrontational. Certain.

Renn Voigt had not moved. She was watching Quire with an expression he had not seen from her before: not the updating-her-model look she'd given Vig, not the stillness she used on him. Something flatter. Something that Quire read, because a second later Quire withdrew their hand.

They tucked the sleeve under their arm. "The broker's name isn't relevant to the audit," Quire said.

"I've already noted that," Ledger said.

Quire went. The door did not close with any particular force.

Renn Voigt watched the door for a moment and then looked at Ledger.

"What did she do with it," she said. Not a question — the tone of someone confirming a shape they had already partially seen.

He told her. The twenty-two entries. The unfiled instruments. The structure — loans written down, amounts sized to dignity, nothing submitted to the registry. He told her the Compact's position on silence and the practical function of what Tally had built over four decades. He did not editorialize. He read from his notes.

Renn Voigt listened with the stillness of a person who had already understood the shape of the thing and was now receiving confirmation of the dimensions.

"Burn them," she said.

Not tactical. Not legal strategy. She said it the way you say something you have known was the answer for years and have been waiting for the right moment to say aloud.

He noted that the Compact's disposal clause allowed authorized destruction of unfiled instruments upon mutual agreement of the estate representative and the auditor, with a log entry as the formal record.

She looked at him. "Yes," she said. "I know."

He did not tell her he had already found the clause on the first day.

He went through the book a third time on the afternoon of the fourth day, this time for completeness, looking for anything he had not already logged.

He found it on page forty-seven.

He had passed the entry twice. The date was twenty-two years back — mid-book, between two auditable instruments, the notation in the same cipher as the twenty-two. He decoded it the way he had decoded the rest: amount on the right, source noted in the center column.

The source read: *For services rendered.*

He had not filed that, either.

He set the pen down. He did not pick it back up.

He had been sitting still for a long time before he noticed the overhead light's faint cycling hum — a sound that had been there for four days and which he had not heard until now. He noticed that his right hand was still on the table, flat, with the pen between his first two fingers and the pen's weight pressing the pad of his index finger into the composite. He had been holding it like that for four minutes. He knew it was four minutes because he was a man who logged his time, and the gap between entries would be four minutes, and if anyone ever audited his audit they would see a four-minute stop on page forty-seven and have no way to know what it meant.

He had not been her auditor twenty-two years ago. He had done three weeks of verification work for someone in her zone at her request, and she had paid him, and he had logged the payment under *source not identified* because he had not, at the time, investigated who had commissioned it. He had accepted the work and the payment and had not looked behind the referral.

She had looked. She had written his name in her private cipher, the amount he'd been paid, and the notation she used for closed instruments: *For services rendered.*

The most accurate thing she had ever written about him.

He picked up the pen. He moved on.

He wrote the certification with Renn Voigt at the table.

He did not ask her to leave. She had been there for four days and she had earned whatever she was there to see. He wrote in the standard Compact format, longhand, the dual-copy form with the carbon sheet underneath:

*I, Ledger, Circuit-registered auditor, retainer reference [date and zone filed], certify the following with respect to the instrument designated: Tally's Gray Zone account book, cloth-bound, hand-sewn, three hundred and twelve pages, notation in private cipher decoded per attached key.*

*The auditable portion of the instrument — pages one through forty, forty-three entries, settlement marks and witness notations as recorded — is materially accurate and consistent with fringe registry records and Compact secondary ledger documentation. The auditable portion is certified complete.*

*Pages forty-one through sixty-two contain twenty-two entries in the same cipher, for which no corresponding filings exist in the fringe registry, the Compact secondary ledger, or the zone recorder's public records. These entries are not instruments of record. They are private notation of private transactions. They are materially accurate records of what occurred. The absence is the document: the choice not to file these entries was not error or omission. It was the record itself.*

*The book is what it is. I certify it.*

He signed his circuit handle. He lifted the carbon sheet and tore it clean along the perforation and set it aside. He set the pen flat.

Renn Voigt was looking at the certification. Her jaw was the same as it had been on the first day — the Voigt jaw, the one the fringe registry had photographed and logged and which had told him, when she walked in, exactly who she was.

"Nobody gets what they came for," he said.

"Nobody gets what they came for." A pause. Her thumb traced the edge of the carbon without looking at it. "Except the twenty-two."

He pulled the pages with both hands.

Twenty-two pages, pages forty-one through sixty-two, separated cleanly at the binding — she had used a thread that was meant for easy removal, which he had noticed on day one and had logged without assigning meaning and which had now resolved into meaning. She had known these pages might leave the book. She had sewn them to go.

He stacked them in order and counted them against his log: twenty-two. He set the stack in the center of the composite table, between the certification and the remaining auditable book, and looked at them. Renn Voigt was watching from across the corner. He had not told her to stay. She had not moved.

He took the portable heat source from his case. He had not used it before on an active retainer; it was there for wax seals and document authentication, and this was not wax or authentication. He set the first page on the ceramic surface.

The name of the first debtor on the first page was Soo-Jin. He did not say it aloud. He read it from his log and made a note in the log — first page, first debtor, notation *disposed per Compact disposal clause, mutual authorization, estate representative and auditor.* The page caught and went.

He worked through all twenty-two in order. Each debtor's name was in his log. Each amount. Each notation that Tally had used to mark the fact that she had written the debt and chosen not to collect it. He did not read any of them aloud. He noted each one in the log with the same entry: name, amount, date, *disposed.*

The mineral smell of paper burning was specific — not like composite, not like anything else in the room. Renn Voigt did not look away from it. Her jaw was still. The ash settled on the ceramic surface. The smell of it stayed longer than the smoke.

He made the final log entry when the last page was gone: *Disposal complete. Twenty-two entries, disposed in full. The act is the record.*

He looked at Renn Voigt. She was looking at the ash.

He made the three deliveries before he closed the retainer.

Vig's carrier arrived first, before the day started, already in the corridor when Ledger opened the door. He handed over the certified summary: eleven pages, Φ14,200 net, settlement marks verified, Section 14 satisfied. The carrier counted the summary sheet's pages — three, plus the attached key — before putting them in the envelope. A careful man. He gave Ledger Φ200 in sealed credit, Vig's idea of a gratuity. Ledger wrote it in his log and took it. He had no opinion about what Vig thought a certified audit was worth. Φ200 was Φ200.

Quire's relay came midmorning. He had expected a relay; what he had not expected was for the relay to look at the sealed notice and then look at him and say, "The broker will want to know if the book is still available." Ledger told them no. The relay considered this for a moment, then said, "If the situation changes." Ledger said it wouldn't. The relay left, which was the broker's answer in the form of a person walking out.

He gave Renn Voigt the carbon copy last.

He set it in front of her without folding it. She picked it up. She held it, not reading. The Voigt jaw was the same as it had been every day. She ran a thumb along the bottom edge where the carbon had transferred the signature, which was the only thing on the page that existed only there.

"Tally would have hated this," she said.

He looked at the book, what was left of it. "Yes."

She folded the carbon once along the center crease — the fold of someone who kept documents, not someone who discarded them — and put it in her jacket's inside pocket. She stood. She put her hands in her jacket pockets and looked at the room once and walked out, and he listened to the footsteps diminish in the corridor until he could not hear them.

He opened his log to the current page. He wrote his name. He wrote the date. He wrote *closed.*

He opened the book a final time.

He had been using two columns across the retainer — the left for account, the right for his running notation. He had used this format for fourteen years. What he did now was add a third column, a narrow strip along the right margin, and label it *gap* in the same notation style as the other headers.

He went through the auditable forty-three entries first, making no entries in the gap column. The auditable section had no gaps. He moved to the twenty-two entries in his log, the ones that had no pages left in the book to refer back to, and he worked through them from the top.

Each entry got a line in the gap column: debtor's name in the left margin. Amount. Date of the original notation, taken from his log. The notation *Disposed per mutual authorization, estate representative and auditor.* It was the most accurate record he could make of what had happened to them, and he made it for all twenty-one.

On the twenty-second entry he stopped.

He looked at the gap column. He looked at the remaining space on the line. He looked at the log entry from the third day — the notation he had made on page forty-seven before the page went to ash: *For services rendered.* It existed now only in his log, in his handwriting. He had not logged the four minutes he had sat with it. He had not logged a lot of things.

He wrote in the gap column: her name. The date of the original entry, twenty-two years back. The amount he had been paid. *Received.*

He set the pen flat.

The overhead was still slightly yellow. The case sat empty on the shelf where she had placed it. Outside, the shaft corridor had been quiet since the third delivery. The book weighed 380 grams. It had weighed that on the first day.

He closed the ledger.

The record was more complete than it had been that morning. It was also incomplete in every column that mattered.
