# The Door Is Unlocked

[Beat 1]
The milking parlor at 04:00 smelled like warm animals and the specific sweetness of automated feed systems processing through the night, a smell she had been breathing since before she could name it. The overhead lights ran on a dimmer at this hour — bright enough to work, dim enough not to startle the herd into a cortisol response that would show up in the yield data. She had set the dimmer herself, at fifteen, after three weeks of watching the overnight yields and identifying the hour-four light-adjustment as the variable. Her mother had let her set it. She didn't explain why the adjustment was correct. She watched her daughter make the calculation and trusted the result.

She was doing the last milking.

The cows did not know it was the last milking. They knew her by footfall and by the specific position she took at each stall, which was slightly to the left of center because she had always preferred the reach angle from the left and the herd had learned her preference and arranged themselves accordingly. She worked through the first eight with the systematic speed of someone who didn't need to think about what her hands were doing, and that was the truth: her hands had been doing this since she was twelve and they didn't require supervision anymore. They required only her presence, which she gave them, while the rest of her was somewhere ahead — already in the city she had been studying for two years, already reading the terminal map she had memorized, already standing on a GLMZ street with two years of accumulated theory about to meet the texture it hadn't been able to predict.

The herd turned over at stall twelve. She changed position and kept working. The automated feed system cycled — a low mechanical sound she'd classified as background before she was eight — and one of the younger cows shifted in a way that meant she was reading the room, checking whether the shift change in the feed cycle was normal, and she put a hand on the cow's flank without looking away from what her other hand was doing, the contact saying: same, nothing has changed, this is the sound it makes, you have heard it before. The cow settled. She moved to stall thirteen.

This was a calf she had delivered herself, in a winter three years ago when her mother had a fever and she was the only other hands on the farm. She had gotten the calf out on her own with the textbook in her lap on the floor beside her, referring to it twice and then setting it down and using her hands. The calf was a three-year-old now and larger than any cow she had expected from that particular birth and had no idea about any of this history, which was fine. Animals did not carry the narratives you put on them.

She finished stall twenty. She checked the feed levels. She confirmed the automated systems were set to continue without her. She had trained the cooperative's relief operator last week, two full sessions, and the operator had been competent, which she'd assessed by watching the hands rather than listening to the self-reporting.

She turned off the dimmer. She walked to the kitchen.

The primary kit was on the table. She had packed it last night — unpacked it and repacked it, checking each instrument against the checklist she had written at fourteen and updated twice since. Once when she added the calibration wands. Once when she replaced the primary bridge interface with the revised model, which she'd done six months before her mother died, while her mother supervised from the doorway of the workroom with her arms crossed and her weight on her right foot, which was how she stood when she was watching closely and didn't want to communicate that she was watching closely. Her mother had trusted her hands before she was done growing into them.

She cleaned her hands at the sink. She ate one piece of bread. She stood at the table and put her hands flat on the surface and looked at the kit case.

Her mother had calibrated a neuretics bridge in this kitchen, on this table, two years before she died. A boy from the next township, seventeen years old. Her mother had done the calibration sequence for two hours and checked it six ways and given him an after-care protocol that she had typed herself because the licensed industry's protocol was wrong in two places and she'd found the errors and corrected them. The boy was fine. The boy had been fine since. The hardware was what had made him fine, and the hardware was in this case, and it was going to GLMZ, and she was going to do with it what her mother had done with it — except in a city where the market for this kind of work was large enough that the work could live.

The boots were beside the door. She had taken them from her mother's closet and padded the toes with folded paper and tested them in the house for three evenings and she was going to wear them to the city. Not because they fit. Because she had decided she would grow into them. The gate latch on the calf pen still hung crooked on its hinge outside in the dark, requiring a half-inch lift before it would slide, and she did not go to touch it before she left. She already knew what it felt like. She would know it until she died.

She picked up the case. She picked up the secondary pouch. She put on her mother's boots.

She walked to the road to meet the county rapid.

## Cedar Rapids Pulse Station, 05:47

[Beat 2]
The station smelled like maintenance fluid and the specific cold of a building designed for throughput — air conditioned not for the people in it but for the machinery under the floor, the kind of cold that didn't have a season in it. She knew the smell. It was the smell of a place that moved things and didn't keep them.

She'd been standing on the platform for fourteen minutes. She knew it because she'd watched the departure board roll the count down from the start, and she'd made herself stop checking it after the third minute, because checking it was a thing her hands did when the rest of her had nowhere to put itself. So she'd stood. Case between her feet, weight balanced over both boots, the way you stand when you've decided not to move until the thing you're waiting for arrives.

Her feet were too small for the boots. They always had been. The toes were packed with folded paper, four sheets to a side, creased and re-creased until the fold held its own shape, and over the fourteen minutes the paper had warmed to the temperature of her feet so that she'd stopped feeling it as separate from herself. The boots were her mother's. The paper made them hers for now. She stood in them the way she'd stood in them in the milking parlor at four in the morning — heels down, knees soft, ready to brace — except there was nothing here to brace against, only a polished floor and a count winding down and the long flat country behind her that she was not going to turn around and look at.

The scrap of paper was in the inside chest pocket of her jacket, folded twice. A name and an address — a referral for an unlicensed calibration cooperative in the gray zone, the only market she trusted. The case sat between her feet. Primary kit — her mother's hands in hardware, eighteen years of calibration history in its scarred shell, the foam molded to the shape of instruments over fifteen thousand hours of use. The handle was worn smooth on one side by a hand that was not her hand, and her fingers found the groove anyway when she picked it up, the way you find a worn place in a threshold without looking down. Secondary kit rode against her ribs in the shoulder pouch.

She thought about the gate latch on the calf pen. Not the house. Not the field. Not anything you'd put on a postcard. The latch. It hung crooked on its hinge and you had to lift it half an inch before it would slide, and her hands knew the lift the way they knew nothing else on earth, and somewhere behind her the latch was still hanging crooked and her hands would never touch it again.

Then the thrum built from below — something below sound that settled into her sternum before her ears registered it, a pressure that came up through the case between her feet and into the long bones of her legs and sat there, vibrating. Her body had understood something before she did, and her weight had already shifted forward over the balls of her feet without her telling it to. She wasn't bracing anymore. She was leaning toward.

The boarding announcement ran in English and then Mandarin and then a dialect she didn't recognize, three voices for a city that was three hundred cities pretending to be one, and the doors along the platform breathed open in a single coordinated motion that she felt as much as saw.

She picked up the case. She stood a half-second longer than she needed to — not because she was afraid, but because she wanted to be the one who decided when the half-second ended.

Then she walked to the door. She did not look back at Iowa. There was nothing back there her hands could reach, and she had stopped, somewhere in the last fourteen minutes, being a person who looked at things she couldn't touch.

[Beat 3]
The slug interior smelled like recycled air and the faint metallic note of electrical insulation running warm - the kind of smell you got from hardware that was working as intended, nothing burning, nothing failing, just current moving through conductor at the rate it was designed to move. She catalogued it without meaning to. Her nose did that.

The seat was narrow. Her bag went overhead in the shared rack, but the case and the shoulder pouch did not. The case sat between her feet. The pouch stayed against her ribs. The woman in the seat beside her had a rolling luggage cart compressed into a thin vertical strip and locked to the wall mounting, and she did not look at what the woman was carrying in the compartment above her, and the woman did not look at her. The first law of the Pulse: you are moving through space at speed, and you are sharing that speed with strangers, and the agreement is to not notice each other.

She understood the agreement. She kept it.

The departure was quieter than she expected. No whistle, no announcement, no theatrical acceleration - just the platform sliding away from the windows with a smoothness that felt wrong until she understood it as intentional, the whole mechanical point of the Pulse being the elimination of the sensation of movement. The city disappeared behind the terminal's curve. Cedar Rapids condensed into three minutes of visible suburban grid and then into fields, and then the fields were moving too fast to read as individual fields and were instead a generalized flatness she had spent nineteen years inside of without understanding it was possible to be outside of.

She watched it until she couldn't distinguish it from the grey of the window at speed. Then she put her head back and let the hum of the magnetic containment fill the back of her skull.

The primary kit's chassis was warm through the case wall. She could feel it against the instep of her left boot - the case's base was vented on that side, and the instruments ran a self-diagnostic at power-up, and she had powered it up on the platform because she was going to leave Iowa and she was not going to leave her mother's instruments in an unverified state when she did. So the chassis ran its cycle and the warmth came through the case and through the leather of the boot and into her foot, and she let it.

Somewhere east of Kankakee the conductor came through. She showed her transit documentation on the screen of the device she had bought secondhand, and the conductor scanned it. Before moving on he looked down at the case between her feet - not nosily, but with the quick professional assessment of someone who had been working the Pulse long enough to read cargo. "Medical equipment?"

"Calibration," she said.

He made a sound that was not skepticism and not approval, just the sound of a categorization completed and saved, and moved to the next seat. She watched him go down the aisle and understood that she had been read as a category, and that the category was not suspicious, and that not being suspicious in a transit context was a kind of credential even if it was not the credential she had come with.

She kept that. Things the zone already knew how to sort.

The documentation was current. The cooperative she was going to was operating in a gray zone that ACS acknowledged rather than policed, because the neuretics calibration market existed at a pressure level that licensing hadn't successfully contained - which was the whole reason she had a referral and a name on a scrap of paper rather than an apprenticeship with a licensed body and a clean work permit. The gray zone was where the work was. She had two years of theory and enough practical skill to recognize what the licensed calibration houses were doing wrong, and what they were doing wrong was charging for certainty they did not deliver.

She had thought about this on the farm for two years. She had thought about it in the milking parlor at four in the morning when the work wasn't interesting enough to occupy all of her and the part left over went to the question of where she should be going. She had come to an answer in the same way she came to most answers: by watching what broke and what held, by eliminating the options that couldn't support the weight of the actual situation. The actual situation was that the licensing industry had taken something from her, and she was not going to work inside it.

The other thing she had thought about, in the milking parlor, was GLMZ. She had read everything she could find: city plans, infrastructure reports, CorpoNation market filings, the transit authority public network documentation, first-person accounts from people who had moved there and written about it. She had cross-referenced them the way you cross-reference technical documents, looking for where the accounts disagreed, because the places where accounts disagreed were the places where one account was wrong and the other was closer to right. She had come away with a mental model she was reasonably confident in and absolutely aware was incomplete.

The incompleteness was the part she couldn't fix from a distance. You couldn't study texture from photographs. You had to be in it.

Outside the window the flatness had been replaced by nothing - the Pulse ran mostly below-grade in the final approach, and there was nothing to see but the lit wall of the tube and her own reflection in the glass, and she looked at herself in it without particular interest, a figure in an oversized jacket with a case between her feet and a look that wasn't nervousness and wasn't certainty and was maybe what the distance between those two things looked like from the outside. She looked away.

The city announced itself before the terminal did. There was a change in the air pressure, subtle, the slug passing through a transition zone between the external atmosphere and the pressurized terminal environment, and her ears adjusted, and then the wall of the tube gave way to a lit shaft that was clearly a vertical structure built around something, and then to daylight of a kind she hadn't seen before: not open daylight but filtered, attenuated, coming down through tier after tier of the city overhead, a light that had been through too many surfaces to have a source anymore.

The terminal resolved into a platform, and the platform was the largest indoor space she had ever stood adjacent to, and she hadn't stood adjacent to it yet - she was still on the slug, bag overhead, case at her feet, the woman with the luggage cart to her right - but she knew already from the glimpse through the windows that the mental model she had built over two years had failed to account for the vertical. The photographs had been lying by omission. The city went up.

She waited until the slug had come completely to rest before she stood, because you did not stand before a vehicle had finished moving. She reached for her bag. She picked up the case. She walked out into the platform and the heat and the light that was not light and the sound that had been running too long to have an edge anymore.

She had been told it would be different from what she expected. She had planned for that.

She hadn't planned for how good it would feel to be right.

[Beat 4]
She'd looked at photographs. Two years of them, studied the way some people study scripture — the transit network committed to memory line by line, every seam point where one CorpoNation's service area handed off to the next, every flag hanging over every district she'd ever read about. She could have drawn the terminal from memory and gotten the platform count right.

None of it had prepared her for the vertical component.

The photographs had been honest about everything except up. The way the city stacked — each tier's underside becoming the ceiling of the tier below, so that looking up was the same as looking up into another street, lit windows and walkways and the soles of someone's feet crossing a bridge two hundred feet overhead. Exhaust heat came down from somewhere above and sat on her shoulders like a hand. Cooking smells, machine oil, and under it the specific chemical bite of industrial solvent — she knew the smell from her mother's kit and didn't expect to find it here, ambient, the whole terminal breathing it.

It was the sound that finally broke the map. The city had been running too long to make a fresh sound anymore — every noise folded back on itself, train tone over crowd over ventilation, until there was no edge to any of it. Her two years of study told her exactly where she was and gave her nothing for how it felt to be there.

Off to the north a tier ended early — a Rift scar, the kind the city walled off and built around rather than through. The support columns closer in wore slow grey blooms of nanite patchwork, continuously deciding not to fall. She catalogued it without alarm. This was just the technical state of the room.

She let the map come back up and walked it. Case in her right hand, the weight of it familiar. Secondary pouch tucked between her left arm and her ribs, where she could feel it shift before anyone else could reach it. She set her pace to the crowd and moved like she had somewhere to be, because the one thing every account had agreed on was that the city read hesitation the way a dog reads a flinch.

So when the weight shifted in the crowd near the exit gates, she didn't decide anything.

She felt it the way she'd learned to feel a calf trying to slip past her in a gate lane — not seen, felt, a wrongness in the distribution of bodies, a pressure where there should have been give. Her right hand closed on a wrist before the rest of her had a name for what was happening. The wrist was thin. A kid's. She held it the half-second it took to confirm the hand had been coming toward the pouch and not toward the gate, and then she opened her fingers and let it go without looking at the face attached to it. Kept walking. Kept the pace. The whole exchange had cost her nothing — no break in stride, no turn of the head, a thing her hands had done while the rest of her was still reading signage.

That was the part that stayed with her.

Not that she'd caught the kid. That a piece of Iowa — a reflex grown in a gate lane at four in the morning, keeping stock from getting past her in the dark — had reached into GLMZ and worked here, unchanged, before she'd had time to be afraid. She'd spent two years afraid she'd arrive empty. That the theory would be all she had and the texture would eat her alive. And the city had reached out in the first ten minutes and tested her at the one thing the photographs could never have taught, and her body had passed the test without telling her it was being given.

She felt the kid watching her go. She didn't turn around. But she let the fact of it sit where she could feel it, next to the case, next to the pouch: she'd been read, and then re-read, and the second reading had moved her out of the column marked *easy* and into some other column she didn't have a name for yet. Fine. Let them file her wherever they filed people they'd decided to leave alone.

She walked toward the gates like the map said to.

[Beat 5]
The transit map she had memorized existed in her head as a clean abstraction: nodes and connectors, color-coded by CorpoNation jurisdiction, with distances rendered in walk-minutes that someone had calculated at some point for some idealized pedestrian. She had studied it until she could reproduce it from memory with the station counts accurate.

The version of the city she was now walking through was the same map and a completely different object.

The exits from the GLMZ terminal opened into a covered transit junction that connected four different surface lines and two elevated platforms and an underground pedestrian corridor that ran southeast into the commercial district of a zone she had labeled, on her mental map, as an interface point between Arcturus Civil Security's administrative territory and a corridor jointly claimed by Shinzo Holdings and a smaller CorpoNation she only knew as a logo: stylized eight-pointed star, recycled from pre-Schism iconography that nobody had bothered to disown. The physical reality of this interface point was a block of concrete and signage and vendors who had set up at the four cardinal corners of the junction in positions that suggested they had been there long enough to have informal territorial claims of their own.

She stood in the junction and turned once to orient herself by the transit signs overhead and then she stopped turning and walked southwest, which was the direction her mental map said to walk.

Fourteen minutes later she stopped on a corner and acknowledged, privately, that she had miscounted a block. Not lost. Offset. She pulled the map back up in her head and walked it forward from the last confirmed position — the elevated line's third pillar, which she had confirmed by checking the station code painted on it against her memorized list — and found the error. She had taken the secondary street when the primary had continued underground for a block. She walked back to the primary and continued.

This wasn't a failure. It was the texture giving her information that the map had abstracted away: there were two routes between nodes where the map showed one, and one of them continued underground, and you had to know which or you got offset. Now she knew which.

The neighborhood shifted from transit-adjacent commercial — the vendor ecology that lived off throughput — into residential in stages that were clear if you knew what to look for. The overhead coverage changed first. In the terminal district everything was covered: permanent awnings, sealed walkway connections, the constant ceiling of tier infrastructure above. Moving west the coverage thinned, and there were gaps where the sky was visible, and the sky had a quality she didn't have a word for yet, not the flat Iowa sky she knew from the farm, but a sky that was aware of the city under it, compressed somehow, closer than sky should be.

The building she was looking for announced itself as The Pivot on a sign that had been installed above the main entrance sometime before the current layer of soot had settled and never cleaned since. Six stories. Ferrocement construction, the kind that had been sprayed in place over a structural frame in the 2170s when the building boom hit a materials shortage and the industry adapted. The walls had the slightly irregular surface that came from spray application, and someone had added a painted mural to the ground-floor face that was clear as abstract at a distance and, up close, as a careful rendering of the transit network for this block's zone, with the current date written in the corner in fresh paint. Someone updated it. That was a data point she kept.

The lobby was unattended. Monitoring hardware in three corners — she checked the sight lines automatically — and a resident access panel and a stairwell door and an elevator that had an out-of-service note attached with what looked like the same tape from a previous out-of-service note, doubled back on itself. She took the stairs.

Second floor. She found 2E at the end of the corridor, which put it at the corner of the building and gave it two exterior walls, which meant two potential thermal-exchange surfaces and a noise environment determined by whatever was on the cross street below rather than the main street. She hadn't known this when she signed the rental agreement. She considered it now. The cross street below was quieter. This was acceptable.

The door opened with the key — physical key, which she appreciated, because physical keys failed in ways she could diagnose and fix, whereas electronic access systems failed in ways that needed someone else's infrastructure. She went inside.

The unit was smaller than the floor plan had suggested, the way units always were smaller than floor plans because floor plans measured wall-center to wall-center and didn't account for the thickness of the walls themselves. She walked the length of it in six steps and the width in four. The window on the exterior corner faced southwest, which meant afternoon light, which meant glare on her workbench in the months she hadn't arrived in yet. She kept that too.

She set the case down in the corner of the room that she had already decided was the corner where the case would go. She put her bag against the wall beside the door. She stood in the middle of the floor and let the unit become her understanding of the unit, not the plan's, not the photographs the listing had included, which had been taken with a wide-angle lens that had flattering opinions about the ceiling height. The real ceiling height was standard. The real light was adequate. The real floor was level, which she knew because she had a habit of checking floors by feel, and this one read even through the boots.

Across the hall, the door to 2D had a latch she could see from her open door: worn, the mechanism showing the slight surface oxidation you got from a contact point that had been touched several thousand times. The door probably opened with less than the full mechanism engaged. She noticed this the way she noticed everything about hardware: automatically, without a plan for what to do with the observation.

She went back inside. She closed her own door. She did not test the lock yet. She was too tired to know what she thought about locks.

[Beat 6]
The super's name was written on a whiteboard by the building entrance, half-erased and overwritten so many times that the board had gone grey under the marker. She didn't read it. He met her at the inner door with a tablet already open to the unit, and he showed her 2E without small talk, which she appreciated more than she'd have been able to say. People who needed to fill the air were people who wanted something from it. He just walked her up one flight and stood back while she looked.

Two rooms. West-facing. The kind of window that would get the last of the afternoon and nothing before it, which meant the morning would be cheap and dim and the evening would cost her in heat. She could live with that. The afternoon light was the part you could work by.

She read the room before she read the price. Two outlets on the long wall, one of them scorched at the upper screw. The other was clean. From the doorway she had a sightline straight across to the window and a partial one to the hall through the gap of the door, and the corner by the radiator was the one place in the room you couldn't be seen from either. She catalogued that without deciding to, the way she'd once known which gate latch in a barn would hold and which would let go if you leaned on it wrong.

The window had a ledge. Wide enough, deep enough. She didn't think the word workbench. She just looked at the ledge a half-second longer than the rest of the room, and her hands, somewhere, already knew where the case would sit. Behind her, the super's weight shifted onto one foot — the small involuntary adjustment of someone waiting for a question that wasn't coming.

She paid first and last. He counted it without making it a ceremony and gave her a physical key — old hardware, brass gone dark, the grip worn smooth on one side where a long line of hands had held it the same way. No fob, no print, no signal to spoof or lose or have read off her at a distance. She closed her fingers around it and felt something in her settle that she didn't examine. Then he left her to it, and the door clicked, and the building's noise resolved into the smaller noise of one room.

She set the case down in the center of the main floor — center, where she could see all of it at once — and turned to close her own door.

That was when she noticed the door across the hall. 2D.

The latch plate was worn in one place: along the upper edge, a bright crescent rubbed into the dull metal where a thumb or the heel of a hand had pressed, again and again, without engaging the full mechanism. Someone opened that door without using the latch the way the latch was made to be used. A key that didn't quite seat. A habit older than the lock. Or a person who came home enough times, tired enough, to teach the door the shape of one hand. She read the wear the way she'd read the scorched outlet — a record of how a thing got used, written by the using.

She didn't know what it meant. She kept it, the way she kept the burned screw and the dead morning light: a fact with no use attached to it yet.

While she was looking, she heard something from inside 2D. Not a sound, exactly — more the particular absence that goes with careful movement, the held quality of a room where someone is keeping still on purpose, aware of the hallway, aware of her. A weight shifting its placement so as not to be a weight. Then it stopped, deliberately, the way a held breath stops being audible the moment you decide to hold it.

She stood there a moment longer than she needed to. Two people, a wall apart, both very still, both waiting. She couldn't have said what she was waiting for. She didn't think whoever was on the other side of 2D could have said either. It was just the particular attention you paid a stranger when you didn't know yet if the stranger was a fact you needed.

Then she stepped back into 2E and closed her own door, and the new key turned in the new lock with the small grinding honesty of metal that owed her nothing yet.

Through the door, very softly, 2D's latch disengaged and re-engaged. One small sound, then nothing. She noted it alongside the latch wear and the odd hours: 2D had heard her key turn and had responded to it. That was a person paying attention to the hallway.

The room was very quiet. Her case in the middle of the floor, the only thing in it that was hers. She lowered herself down beside the case and sat on the bare floor with her back not quite to the wall, knees up, the worn key still in her hand, and let the silence of a room that was not yet hers go on around her without filling it.

[Beat 7]
She did not sleep until after two in the morning.

This wasn't insomnia. The building had sounds she didn't yet know the origin of. A low-frequency vibration in the basement — a pump, cycling on a forty-minute interval — that her body wouldn't classify as safe until it had heard the cycle enough times to know it was the same sound every time. After midnight the street below went from aggregate noise to individual sounds. A cart with a loose wheel at twelve-forty. A conversation in two languages at one-fifteen, arguing direction. A dog at one-thirty that barked twice and then did not bark again, and she held the detail in her mind until she had closed the observation: *found it*, she decided, because a dog that stopped mid-bark rather than trailing off had found a scent and gone to it. She kept the observation and moved on.

She had the secondary kit open on the floor beside her. She wasn't working on it. She was keeping it near her the way she kept things near her that she needed to know were there — the case in the corner, the pouch within arm's reach, the secondary kit open so she could see its face, the faint green standby light the only lit thing in the room besides the window.

GLMZ at night wasn't dark. The tier lighting and the signage and the distant glow of the Loop made a skyscape that wasn't black but a deep diffuse blue-orange. She had grown up sleeping in darkness. She would put something over the window eventually.

At one forty-seven — she noted the time from force of habit — the door of 2D opened and closed. Not loudly. The latch engaged with the specific sound of someone who knew exactly where the mechanism sat and used it that way, and then footsteps went toward the stairwell and did not come back for seventeen minutes.

She kept it: 2D, active at odd hours, exits and returns without visible purpose.

She slept eventually. The pump cycled twice while she was sleeping. Her body had started to classify it as background.

In the morning she would buy calibration fluid and food, and it would be the first morning she had ever woken up in this city, and she would handle it the way she handled new environments: by reading it before she moved in it, by keeping the case between her feet and the pouch against her ribs, and making sure that whatever she did not know yet, she could feel the things she needed to know were there.

[Beat 8]
She needed calibration fluid - the non-ester kind, which meant she was looking for a supplier who understood what she was actually asking for and not just what the word sounded like - and a thermal coupling for the secondary kit's secondary receiver, and food for three days. She gave herself ninety minutes to walk the neighborhood before she bought anything. That was the rule. You did not buy in a place until you had read it, because the price of a thing was never the price of the thing; it was the price of the thing plus everything the seller had decided about you in the first four seconds.

The market ran through the ground floor of three connected buildings and spilled onto the street at both ends. From the outside it looked like one market. It wasn't. She walked it twice before she understood that, and the understanding came not from the goods but from the prices. Two stalls in, a woman was selling cell-grown protein at a number that made sense. Forty meters down, past a support column with a seam of old water-stain running floor to ceiling, the same brick from what looked like the same supplier was a third more. She stood at the column for a moment and felt the market change temperature around her, the way a pasture changed when you crossed a fence the cattle could see and you couldn't. On the near side of the column the vendors made eye contact. On the far side they held it a beat longer, checking her against something. She stayed on the near side after that.

Prices ran in Φ - QUANTA, the city's hard currency - and in a second currency she didn't have a name for yet, a local scrip that regulars used between themselves. The Φ came out for strangers. So the scrip was trust and the Φ was distance, and she was distance, and she would pay in distance and not pretend otherwise.

One stall at the far end was curtained - not hiding inventory, hiding work. Through the gap she caught the bluish spill of a surgical lamp and the particular stillness of someone concentrating. A Splicer running a mobile install. She stopped walking, registered the clean left-to-right of an instrument tray through the gap, and walked on. Her grip on the case had tightened without instruction, and she let it.

She found the food section in the second building, past a narrow passage that smelled like cleaning compound and the specific warm-grain smell of a kitchen running several levels above the ground-floor stalls.
