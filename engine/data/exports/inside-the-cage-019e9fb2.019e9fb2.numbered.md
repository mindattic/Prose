# Inside the Cage

_A dawn walk home through the wet market and the rain that will not quit, a katana left at a door, and the slow, sickening understanding that the hum he has carried for eleven years was never his alone._

## Inside the Cage

[Beat 1]
Below the relay station, 0312. He found the equipment rack by feel — the old kind, bolted to the floor, steel, the bolts themselves worn smooth from decades of hands checking them. He sat on its edge. He did not reach for the katana. He sat with his hands on his knees and waited for whatever came next. Then he turned north.

[Beat 2]
She stepped back and let him through. The workbench was cleared — she had been working on something before she slept, the components pushed to one side in the specific organized disorder of someone who intended to return to them — and she pulled the stool from under the bench and pointed at it, which was all the instruction he needed.

He sat. She put on gloves. She did not turn the music back on.

The silence in the room was the working kind, which was its own form of fullness, and she moved into it with the focus she always had when she was holding a tool near something that mattered. Her quiet running commentary dropped even lower than usual, almost inaudible, almost not for him at all: the fragment's depth, the tissue response, the lateral array's drift she found when she ran the Tier 2 check and corrected without remarking on when or how it had happened.

Twenty-eight minutes. He sat still and let her.

The piezoelectric ceramic was in his coat pocket and he set it on the bench's cleared edge when she finished. She looked at it for one second — the aperture of her attention tightening the way it did when something was exactly right — and said nothing. He stood.

[Beat 3]
The busker was at the far end on an overturned crate, the instrument across his knees — sanxian, possibly, or something older than the word he had for it, three strings and a long neck and a body that had been repaired at the shoulder joint with what looked like epoxy and conviction. The piece was slow. It was in a key that did not have a name in any register his implant carried, which meant it was older than the implant's references or simply outside them, and the sound moved through the courtyard and did not echo the way it should have, the collapsed walls doing something to the acoustics that produced instead a kind of presence, the music occupying the space rather than bouncing off it. Kyle stopped walking.

[Beat 4]
The two men were at the fourth stall. One had a fish cart. The other wanted it. Kyle read the situation in the time it took to take two steps: the man with the cart had a split lip already bleeding into his beard, the other was holding a length of rebar at his side in the loose way of someone who had picked it up and not yet decided what for. The aunt at the neighboring stall was yelling in Mandarin — the specific register of a woman who has seen this particular fight before and is yelling not because she thinks it will stop anything but because silence would mean she had accepted it. The rain came down and took the blood — fish blood from the overturned bucket at the cart's base, man blood from the lip — into the gutter at the same rate, indifferent to the distinction. Kyle's stride did not change. Sable's didn't either. Her right hand was in the tan coat pocket and had been since they left the relay station, and it was not a weapon she was holding.

The hood came down.

[Beat 5]
The stairs at The Pivot had a particular sound at 0356 — the third step from the top, the one with the loose riser that had been loose since the building changed hands in 2198 and would be loose until something structural made it a different problem. Kyle's weight on it produced the same sound it always produced, which was apparently enough. The door to 2E opened before he reached the landing.

[Beat 6]
Pixel in pajamas. Bare feet on the hall's cold composite. The music inside had been running — he could hear it cut when she heard the stair — and her hair was loose and there was a crease on her left cheek from the pillow and she took one look at the blood soaking the collar of his shirt and said: "Again."

[Beat 7]
*So. That.* He kept his hands where they were, flat on the table, and noted that he had to decide to keep them there.

[Beat 8]
He considered what it meant to agree. The hum had been there for eleven years and he had not known it was there, which meant it had been with him in every contract, every room, every choice he had believed was his. Agreeing to help locate the thing was not a neutral act. He knew that. He sat with it for three seconds, which was longer than he usually needed, which was its own kind of answer. "All right," he said.

[Beat 9]
She watched him have it. The implants did not adjust again. Then: 'If it is not looking through your eyes, it is interpreting your senses.' The voice was the same. Level, unhurried, the voice that had given him eleven operational briefings in the two months since she picked him up. 'Either way — the moment you recognize me, it knows we are meeting. So I needed you to read me as a stranger.' A pause that was not hesitation. 'I needed you to choose to follow someone you did not know.' The hum at the base of his sternum picked that moment to clarify, the way a word does when you finally hear how it is spelled, and he held the next breath a half-second longer than the one before it. Kyle looked at the floor. The concrete was old, marked with the ghost-outlines of equipment that had stood here for decades and been removed. Eleven years of the harmonic catalogued as ambient, as background, as *self.* Not self. His left hand moved on the table. He put it back. The question was whether it had been keeping him running or simply watching him run, and he didn't know, and the not-knowing was something he should have asked about a long time ago. 'I do not like,' Sable said, 'being some non-human's puppet.' Flat. The way she said *the risk premium is already factored in.* A thing stated, not performed. She let it sit. Then: 'I can triangulate its physical position. Three locations. Three readings of surveillance traffic against your implants. I have placed the nodes. I need you outside this room behaving exactly as you behave.' Kyle looked up. The ocular implants were still. The aperture locked, which he now understood meant she had moved into a register where every word cost something. 'I need you,' she said, 'to not change.'

[Beat 10]
The song ended the way things end when they have been played correctly — not stopping, arriving. Kyle reached into his coat pocket and produced what was there: forty credits, the same number he had left on Mrs. Chen's counter, which was not a decision he had made consciously and which he did not examine now. He set them on the crate beside the busker. The busker did not look up. Kyle turned. Sable was watching him with the aperture locked, the expression she had when the variables had resolved into something she had not expected and was deciding whether to name it. She did not name it. "That's three," she said, very quietly, and the ocular implants made their small sound and she looked away, at something only the node showed her, at coordinates that were now coordinates rather than a search. Kyle walked, and she walked beside him, and neither of them said anything about the garden or the forty credits or the key the piece had been in, and the busker behind them began, without pause, to play something else.

[Beat 11]
He walked across the hall. Picked Silence up from the wall, the familiar weight settling into his grip, and carried it into 2F and set it on the rack. The hum was at full presence in his chest. The ceramic was already gone from his pocket, which meant it was on her bench, which meant it was already being understood. Kyle sat on the bed's edge in the dark and did not sleep.

[Beat 12]
Sable took two more steps before she understood he was not beside her. He heard the small sound the ocular implants made when they adjusted — the aperture tightening as she looked at him, read him, recalculated. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the busker, who was not looking at either of them, whose attention was entirely on the middle distance above the strings in the way of a person for whom the playing and the listening had become the same act. The hum in Kyle's sternum was there. It was always there now — he would always know it was there now — but the music was also there, and for the length of one song they occupied the same space without canceling each other. Sable's hand moved in the coat pocket. The node reading. The rain came down.

[Beat 13]
The couple was on the third stoop from the corner. A thermal cloth over the bowl, the iridescent kind that kept heat in but cost nothing, the seams repaired twice at least. The man had noodles on his chin and the woman wiped it with a folded napkin — not looking at him, looking at the middle distance the way you look when a thing is so practiced it has stopped requiring attention — and the man kept eating. No thank you. The thank you had been said ten thousand times and was now in the gesture itself, in the way he tilted his chin slightly to meet the napkin rather than pulling away from it. He filed it. The NeoCortex did not file it. He did. Sable walked at his left, her right hand still in the coat pocket, the node reading, and she did not look at the couple.

[Beat 14]
Not a pause. Not the stillness of a person checking their footing or reading a sign. The stillness of a person who has seen something in data and needs three seconds to understand what they are seeing. Kyle did not look at her. He looked at the thermal cloth on the couple's bowl shift as the man reached for the napkin himself this time, belatedly, too late to be the gesture it had been when she did it. He counted the seconds by the flicker of the streetlight, which was reliable in its unreliability. One. Two. Three. Then she was walking again, the same pace, the coat pocket still, the ocular implants making their small sound as they adjusted to the distance ahead. She said nothing. He did not ask. The block continued behind them, and the block did not know that anything had shifted, and Kyle walked and catalogued and felt the hum sharpen again at the base of his sternum, and whatever Sable had read in the node she was carrying now like a stone she had decided not to put down.

[Beat 15]
The brass gasket seated with a sound like a breath held and then released. Kyle stood in the dark and let the silence locate itself in his body. It was in his sternum. Below his sternum. A place he had stopped attending to the way you stop attending to the sound of your own blood moving — it was always there until it was not, and now it was not, and the absence had a shape. Eleven years. He had been sixteen when they finished the installation and he had not been quiet inside since.

[Beat 16]
He did not answer. He unslung Silence from his back and leaned it against the wall outside her door, careful, the friction sheath seated flush against the baseboard, and straightened without looking at her. She did not look at it. The convention did not require acknowledgment to function.

Sable went still.

[Beat 17]
She stopped at the courtyard's western edge where the collapsed buildings gave way to open street. The rain had not changed. It would not change until the processors decided it had served its purpose, and it had not served its purpose yet. She turned — not fully, not the full rotation of a person turning to face someone, but the partial turn of a person who has decided to close a file while still holding it. The ocular implants made their sound. "I'll contact you when the timing is operational," she said. The same voice that had given him eleven briefings. Flat, clean, the risk premium already factored in. "We go together. My read, my call." Not a question. Not a request. The way she said *the deliverable is non-negotiable* — a thing stated so that the stating of it completed the transaction. Then: "Thank you." Two words. She did not touch him. She turned west and walked, and the tan coat went with her into the rain, and Kyle stood at the courtyard's edge and watched her go for three seconds.

[Beat 18]
The man with the rebar did not use it. The man with the cart did not run. They stood in the rain and ran out of whatever had been sustaining the fight — not resolution, not decision, just the body's unwillingness to continue — and the man with the cart took the cart, and the man with the rebar sat down on the curb in the rain and put the rebar across his knees and looked at nothing. The aunt said one more thing, sharp and final, the period at the end of a sentence she had been writing for years, and went back to her stall. The market continued. Kyle and Sable continued. Somewhere in Sable's coat pocket the node was reading, and whatever it was reading was the business of something that had been living in Kyle's chest for eleven years. Kyle walked and did not change. That was the agreement.

*There it is. Still there.*

[Beat 19]
Six blocks. The wet market was still running because the wet market always ran — it did not close for rain, did not close for cold, had not closed for the three-day curfew two years ago, had simply moved two stalls deeper under the corrugated overhang and continued. The fish smell reached him at the edge of it. Then the hum was back, the same hum, and he registered it now the way you register a footstep behind you: something present that had not announced itself.

[Beat 20]
Ten blocks east the courtyard opened between two buildings that had collapsed toward each other and stopped, the way exhausted things stop — not falling, not standing, somewhere between. Someone had run cables across the gap and strung lights, the small kind, and the rain moved through them without caring. The ferrocrete planters were salvaged, four of them, mismatched in height and diameter and arranged with the logic of someone who had thought about where the light would be in the morning. Chrysanthemums. Real ones. White and rust and one the color of old brass, the petals holding the rain's weight the way petals do when they have been tended — not breaking, adjusting. Kyle's NeoCortex catalogued the planters' material composition and moved on. He did not move on.

[Beat 21]
The hum came back as he passed the last planter — the brass-colored chrysanthemum, the one holding the rain's weight without breaking — settled into his sternum with the particular quality of something returning to its place. He registered it the way you register the temperature of a room after a long phone call: the air had always been this temperature, you had simply been elsewhere for a while. The street opened north toward The Pivot. The residential block came up gradually — not a threshold but a gradation, the market giving way to poured concrete faces and the narrower dark of streets that had not been designed for anything except the people who lived on them. The hum adjusted with it. Smaller, he thought, and then corrected himself — not smaller. *More precise.* As if the reduced interference gave it room to sharpen.

[Beat 22]
Red hair. Loose braid, one strand against her cheekbone that had never stayed. The ocular implants — black, machined, the aperture rings that made a small sound when she shifted focus, the sound he had stopped noticing through two months of cracked-terminal audio and had just now, in this room, in this silence, identified as *her* — adjusted once as she looked at him. He had talked to this voice every Tuesday and Friday for two months. He had built a contract file, a behavioral profile, a trust index. He had built two centimeters of a face under streetlight, and the rest arrived now and fit itself over the partial like a key into a lock she had already shown him. He did not move. The cage held. His hands stayed open on his knees, and the muscles across his shoulders did the slow burning work of staying open, and he let them.
