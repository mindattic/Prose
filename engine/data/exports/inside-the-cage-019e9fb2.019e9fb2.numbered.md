# Inside the Cage

_A meeting in a Faraday cage where the hum he has carried for eleven years goes silent for the first time — and what Sable tells him about it cannot be unheard. Then the long walk home, with the thing listening from inside._

[Beat 1]
The brass gasket seated with a sound like a breath held and then released. Kyle stood in the dark and let the silence locate itself in his body. It was in his sternum. Below his sternum. A place he had stopped attending to the way you stop attending to the sound of your own blood moving — it was always there until it was not, and now it was not, and the absence had a shape. Eleven years. He had been sixteen when they finished the installation and he had not been quiet inside since.

[Beat 2]
Red hair. Loose braid, one strand against her cheekbone that had never stayed. The ocular implants — black, machined, the aperture rings that made a small sound when she shifted focus, the sound he had stopped noticing through two months of cracked-terminal audio and had just now, in this room, in this silence, identified as *her* — adjusted once as she looked at him. He had talked to this voice every Tuesday and Friday for two months. He had built a contract file, a behavioral profile, a trust index. He had built an inch of a face under streetlight, and the rest arrived now and fit itself over the partial like a key into a lock she had already shown him. He did not move. The cage held. His hands stayed open on his knees, and the muscles across his shoulders did the slow burning work of staying open, and he let them.

[Beat 3]
*So. That.* He kept his hands where they were, flat on the table, and noted that he had to decide to keep them there.

[Beat 4]
Sable went still — the stillness of someone choosing the exact size of what to say next.

"If it is not looking through your eyes, it is interpreting your senses. Either way — the moment you recognize me, it knows we are meeting. So I needed you to read me as a stranger." A pause that was not hesitation. "I needed you to choose to follow someone you did not know."

The hum at the base of his sternum picked that moment to clarify. He held the next breath a half-second longer than the one before it. Eleven years of the harmonic catalogued as ambient, as background, as *self.* Not self. His left hand moved on the table. He put it back. The question was whether it had been keeping him running or simply watching him run, and he didn't know, and the not-knowing was something he should have asked about a long time ago.

[Beat 5]
"I do not like," Sable said, "being some non-human's puppet." Flat. The way she said *the risk premium is already factored in.* A thing stated, not performed — except that her thumb was pressed against the table's edge hard enough to whiten the nail, and she saw him see it, and did not move it.

She audited her own conclusion before anyone else could get to it: "I do not know what it is. I know what it has been doing. Those are not the same thing, and I am not going to pretend they are."

She set three fingers on the table, spaced apart. A triangle.

"I can locate its physical position. Three readings of surveillance traffic against your implants, three locations. I have placed the nodes. I need you outside this room, behaving exactly as you behave."

Kyle looked up. The ocular implants were still — the aperture locked, which he now understood meant she had moved into a register where every word cost something.

"I need you," she said, "to not change."

[Beat 6]
"You want me to keep being followed," Kyle said, "by the thing that's been following me, so that you can follow it."

"Yes."

"Good. For a second it sounded complicated."

The aperture rings made their small sound. It took him a moment to understand what he was looking at, because he had never seen it through the terminal and had no file for it: Sable, almost smiling.

[Beat 7]
He considered what it meant to agree. The hum had been there for eleven years and he had not known it was there, which meant it had been with him in every contract, every room, every choice he had believed was his. Agreeing to help locate the thing was not a neutral act. He knew that. He sat with it for three seconds, which was longer than he usually needed. "All right," he said.

[Beat 8]
Below the relay station, 0312. He found the equipment rack by feel — the old kind, bolted to the floor, steel, the bolts themselves worn smooth from decades of hands checking them. He sat on its edge. Somewhere behind the panel the volt rats shifted in a cable run, a sound like dry rice poured slowly, and then settled. He did not reach for the katana. He sat with his hands on his knees and waited for whatever came next. Then he turned north.

[Beat 9]
Six blocks. The wet market was still running because the wet market always ran — it did not close for rain, did not close for cold, had not closed for the three-day curfew two years ago, had simply moved two stalls deeper under the corrugated overhang and continued. The fish smell reached him at the edge of it. Then yeast and antiseptic, thin and sweetish, through the cracked door of a splice shop that had not bothered with a sign.

Then the hum was back, and he registered it now the way you register a footstep behind you. He ran the test he had been wanting to run since the cage: crossed to the overhang side of the lane, where the corrugation killed line-of-sight to the camera knot above the splice shop. The hum did not thin. He crossed back through the densest stretch of stall traffic, bodies and steam and dripping awnings between him and every sensor he could name. The hum stayed exactly where it was, in his sternum, undiluted.

Whatever was riding him did not care where he stood. It was not watching him from anywhere. It was watching from inside.

[Beat 10]
The two men were at the fourth stall. One had a fish cart. The other wanted it. Kyle read the situation in the time it took to take two steps: the man with the cart had a split lip already bleeding into his beard, the other was holding a length of rebar at his side in the loose way of someone who had picked it up and not yet decided what for. The aunt at the neighboring stall was yelling in Mandarin — the specific register of a woman who has seen this particular fight before and is yelling not because she thinks it will stop anything but because silence would mean she had accepted it. The rain came down and took the blood — fish blood from the overturned bucket at the cart's base, man blood from the lip — into the gutter at the same rate, indifferent to the distinction. Kyle's stride did not change. Sable's didn't either. Her right hand was in the tan coat pocket and had been since they left the relay station, and it was not a weapon she was holding.

[Beat 11]
The man with the rebar did not use it. The man with the cart did not run. They stood in the rain and ran out of whatever had been sustaining the fight — not resolution, not decision, just the body's unwillingness to continue — and the man with the cart took the cart, and the man with the rebar sat down on the curb in the rain and put the rebar across his knees and looked at nothing. The aunt said one more thing, sharp and final, the period at the end of a sentence she had been writing for years, and went back to her stall. The market continued. Kyle and Sable continued. Somewhere in Sable's coat pocket the node was reading, and whatever it was reading was the business of something that had been living in Kyle's chest for eleven years. Kyle walked and did not change. That was the agreement.

*There it is. Still there.*

[Beat 12]
The busker was at the far end on an overturned crate, the instrument across his knees — sanxian, possibly, or something older than the word he had for it, three strings and a long neck and a body that had been repaired at the shoulder joint with what looked like epoxy and conviction. The piece was slow. It was in a key that did not have a name in any register his implant carried, which meant it was older than the implant's references or simply outside them, and the sound moved through the courtyard and did not echo the way it should have, the collapsed walls doing something to the acoustics that produced instead a kind of presence, the music occupying the space rather than bouncing off it. Kyle stopped walking.

[Beat 13]
The song ended the way things end when they have been played correctly — not stopping, arriving. Kyle reached into his coat pocket and produced what was there: forty credits, the same number he had left on Mrs. Chen's counter, which was not a decision he had made consciously and which he did not examine now. He set them on the crate beside the busker. The busker did not look up.

Kyle turned. Sable was watching him with the aperture locked — the expression she had when the variables had resolved into something she had not expected and was deciding whether to name it. The node needed dwell time at each location to pull a clean reading of the traffic riding his implant. She had planned to manufacture a reason to stop on this block. She had not needed to: he had stood still for one entire song, in the open, for no operational reason at all.

"That's three," she said, very quietly, and looked away — at something only the node showed her, at coordinates that were now coordinates rather than a search. Kyle walked, and she walked beside him, and the busker behind them began, without pause, to play something else.

[Beat 14]
Sable took two more steps before she understood he was not beside her. He heard the small sound the ocular implants made as she looked at him, read him, recalculated. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the busker, whose attention was entirely on the middle distance above the strings, in the way of a person for whom the playing and the listening had become the same act.

The hum in Kyle's sternum was there. It was always there now — he would always know it was there now — but the music was also there, and for the length of one song the two of them occupied the same space without canceling each other.

And then, for four bars near the end, the hum did something it had never done in eleven years. It kept time.

Sable's hand moved in the coat pocket. The node, reading. The rain came down.

[Beat 15]
Ten blocks east the courtyard opened between two buildings that had collapsed toward each other and stopped, the way exhausted things stop — not falling, not standing, somewhere between. Someone had run cables across the gap and strung lights, the small kind, and the rain moved through them without caring. The ferrocrete planters were salvaged, four of them, mismatched in height and diameter and arranged with the logic of someone who had thought about where the light would be in the morning. Chrysanthemums. Real ones. White and rust and one the color of old brass, the petals holding the rain's weight the way petals do when they have been tended — not breaking, adjusting. Kyle's Neuretics catalogued the planters' material composition and moved on. He did not move on. He set his hand flat against the nearest one and found the ferrocement still warm under his palm an hour after the light had gone.

[Beat 16]
The hum came back as he passed the last planter — the brass-colored chrysanthemum, the one holding the rain's weight without breaking — and settled into his sternum like something returning to its place. The street opened north toward The Pivot, the market giving way to poured concrete faces and the narrower dark of streets that had not been designed for anything except the people who lived on them. The hum adjusted with it. Smaller, he thought, and then corrected himself — not smaller. *More precise.*

Then he caught himself reading the camera knots above the intersection before he had decided to look at them — the hum leading his eyes by a quarter-second, or his eyes leading the hum, and he genuinely could not tell which. He stopped. He held his hands open at his sides and checked them. Steady. That was either reassuring or it wasn't, and he walked the rest of the block deciding.

[Beat 17]
The couple was on the third stoop from the corner, a thermal cloth over the shared bowl, the seams repaired twice at least. The man had noodles on his chin and the woman wiped it with a folded napkin without looking at him — a gesture so practiced it had stopped requiring attention. No thank you. The thank you had been said ten thousand times and was now in the gesture itself.

Then Sable stopped. Not the stillness of a person checking their footing — the stillness of a person who has seen something in data and needs three seconds to understand what they are seeing. Kyle counted them by the flicker of the streetlight. One. Two. Three. Then she was walking again, the same pace, the coat pocket still, and she said nothing, and he did not ask. Whatever she had read in the node, she was carrying it now like a stone she had decided not to put down.

[Beat 18]
She stopped at the courtyard's western edge where the collapsed buildings gave way to open street. The rain had not changed. It would not change until the processors decided it had served its purpose, and it had not served its purpose yet. She turned — not fully, not the full rotation of a person turning to face someone, but the partial turn of a person who has decided to close a file while still holding it. The ocular implants made their sound. "I'll contact you when the timing is operational," she said. The same voice that had given him eleven briefings. Flat, clean, the risk premium already factored in. "We go together. My read, my call." Not a question. Not a request. The way she said *the deliverable is non-negotiable* — a thing stated so that the stating of it completed the transaction. Then: "Thank you." Two words. She did not touch him. She turned west and walked, and the tan coat went with her into the rain, and Kyle stood at the courtyard's edge and watched her go for three seconds.

[Beat 19]
The stairs at The Pivot had a particular sound at 0356 — the third step from the top, the one with the loose riser that had been loose since the building changed hands in 2198 and would be loose until something structural made it a different problem. Kyle's weight on it produced the same sound it always produced, which was apparently enough. The door to 2E opened before he reached the landing.

[Beat 20]
Pixel in pajamas. Bare feet on the hall's cold composite. The music inside had been running — he could hear it cut when she heard the stair — and her hair was loose and there was a crease on her left cheek from the pillow and she took one look at the blood soaking the collar of his shirt and said: "Again."

[Beat 21]
He did not answer. He unslung Silence from his back and leaned it against the wall outside her door, careful, the friction sheath seated flush against the baseboard, and straightened without looking at her. She did not look at it. The convention did not require acknowledgment to function.

[Beat 22]
She stepped back and let him through. The workbench was cleared — she had been working on something before she slept, the components pushed to one side in the specific organized disorder of someone who intended to return to them — and she pulled the stool from under the bench and pointed at it, which was all the instruction he needed.

He sat. She put on gloves. She did not turn the music back on. Her quiet running commentary dropped even lower than usual, almost not for him at all: the fragment's depth, the tissue response, the lateral array's drift she found on the Tier 2 check and corrected without remarking on when or how it had happened.

Twenty-eight minutes. He sat still and let her.

The salvaged mil-spec signal relay was in his coat pocket — the part her bench had been improvising around for a month — and he set it on the bench's cleared edge when she finished. She looked at it for one second, the aperture of her attention tightening the way it did when something was exactly right, and said nothing. He stood.

At the door she said it to his back, the thing she said when she could tell a night wasn't finished with him yet:

"Eat something before you chase it."

[Beat 23]
He walked across the hall. Picked Silence up from the wall, the familiar weight settling into his grip, and carried it into 2F and set it on the rack. The relay was already gone from his pocket, which meant it was on her bench, which meant it was already being understood.

Kyle sat on the bed's edge in the dark and did not sleep. The hum was at full presence in his chest, and for the first time in eleven years he listened to it the way you listen to a stranger in your house — deliberately, holding still, mapping it.

Partway into the listening, the texture changed. Very slightly. The way a man shifts his weight when he realizes you have noticed him across a room.

It knew he was listening.

He sat with that until the window went grey.
