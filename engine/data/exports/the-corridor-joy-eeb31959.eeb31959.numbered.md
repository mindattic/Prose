# The Corridor

[Beat 1]
Mrs. Chen set down the second bowl without being asked.

"You're eating fast," she said.

"I have a job at midnight."

She looked at him the way she'd been looking at men with jobs at midnight for seventy years — the whole diagnostic in half a second, verdict rendered and filed — and added a fish ball to the broth without comment. The wok rang its flat note twice. Rain worked the awning. Somewhere down the counter a young woman in a Pulse maintenance coverall was quietly explaining to her ramen that this was not the evening she had planned. The city's complaints were varied and sincere tonight.

Kyle ate the fish ball, paid exact, and was in the alley at 21:45 with the saya over his shoulder and the wet city smell of ozone and freight oil doing its honest best.

His terminal was on the work channel. The posting was clean.

CONTRACT: ESCORT — ONE PERSON, ONE TRANSIT POINT.
SCOPE: SAFEHOUSE (ASHGRAVE FRINGE) TO DOCK 14-C (FERRYSIDE), BY 03:40.
CLIENT OF RECORD: [VERIFIED — SABLE COUNTER-INTELLIGENCE VETTED]
FEE: DOUBLE RATE PAST MIDNIGHT.
NOTES: CLIENT TRAVELS LIGHT. ROUTE AT DISCRETION.

He'd called Sable at twenty hundred, because Sable was the rule, not the override.

"Paper's clean," she'd said. "Client ran a counting house. Nine years. She gave two weeks' notice on a Tuesday and the Tuesday before last was her last shift and she has been in a transit safehouse since Wednesday." A pause with a click of aperture rings in it. "The company she kept books for is not named on the contract, samurai. I asked who the client used to work for and the router gave me a shell."

"Shells?"

"One shell. Which means whoever's watching her thinks the shell is enough. The rate suggests otherwise." Another click. "It's a clean contract. I'm not saying it's a quiet job."

"Standard terms," Kyle said.

"Yours or mine?"

"Both."

The line had closed. He spent twenty minutes on the route, because the route was the whole job, and then he'd eaten two bowls and let Mrs. Chen add the fish ball.

[Beat 2]
The safehouse was a third-floor room above a recycled-materials depot, Ashgrave fringe, in one of the service corridors the city had threaded between its own buildings in 2189 when the ground-level floods came and everyone had to find new ways to move freight at altitude. The corridor was two meters wide, sixty meters long, with conduit runs bolted to both walls and a freight track overhead whose trolleys hadn't moved in a decade. It ran between the depot and the street ramp at the Langley crossing, and it was the most direct route from the safehouse to the dock.

Kyle stood at its entrance for fifteen seconds and did not like it.

He went in anyway, because the route had three alternatives and he'd priced all four. This was still the best.

The woman waiting upstairs was small, late thirties, with a red-and-gray braid over one shoulder and a canvas bag with a broken clasp held shut by a rubber band. She had flour on her sleeves. Not new flour — deep in the weave, years of it, the kind you get from a habit and not an afternoon. Her name was on the contract and he used it.

"Ms. Tanaka."

"You're earlier than I expected," she said.

"We have a long route. There's a place I want to check."

She looked at the saya, then at his face, the way people looked when they were trying to decide if the blade was reassuring or not. She decided. "All right," she said, and picked up the bag.

She didn't ask about the company whose books she'd kept for nine years. He didn't ask why she'd left. The contract had a scope and the scope was the dock, and the dock was everything.

[Beat 3]
He took the long way to the corridor entrance, and the long way told him nothing, which was a kind of answer.

The fringe was quiet in the way fringe blocks got quiet when people who knew things had decided to be somewhere else. A lumen mouse sat on the conduit rail at the corner of Langley and did not run when they passed, and that was texture — the mouse's bioluminescence tracing a pale crescent on the wet pipe as it watched them go — and Kyle noted it and his neuretics noted the noting and filed it where it filed things: the forty-second window, open, waiting.

At 00:17 they reached the corridor's east entrance.

Kyle stopped. Ms. Tanaka stopped beside him. The corridor ahead was dark in the way enclosed spaces were dark when the work lights had been helped off: not a power failure, two screws turned by someone who'd wanted to choose the conditions. He had walked this corridor two days ago, at noon, learning the freight rail's maintenance hooks and the conduit boxes' positions and the exact location of a junction coupling seven meters west of the east entrance that rang like a hammer-strike when you kicked it. He had counted eleven meters to the first door frame — a maintenance alcove, twenty centimeters deep, just enough to funnel whoever stood at its mouth into a narrower problem. He had counted the ceiling clearance at two-point-four meters between the freight track and the floor. He had counted the conduit intervals on the east wall: five anchor bolts, spaced ninety centimeters, each one a handhold.

He said, "Stay a step behind my left shoulder," and walked in.

The corridor smelled of machine oil and old rain.

He got twenty meters before the dark started moving.

[Beat 4]
They came from both ends — the professional move, both flanks committed — and they came quiet, which meant they'd done this in corridors before. His neuretics counted what the dark gave it: breathing, heat gradients, the whisper of soles on wet composite, the small adjustments of bodies learning the space.

Fourteen.

Seven east, seven west, the eastern group already inside twelve meters and closing.

No room here. Two meters wide was a geometry problem with one answer: stay on the centerline and work the stack, because the width made flanking impossible and the length made retreat a gamble. Cacophony was not the answer — not two meters wide with Ms. Tanaka a step behind him, not with fourteen people packed into a choke where a round that missed left nowhere to go. He said it once, flat, to the dark and whoever was in it: "No room for the gun." Then he put his left hand back and she took it, and he moved her to the west wall — conduit-heavy, three large junction boxes she could press her shoulders against — and said, "Don't move from here unless I say so."

Her breathing went high and fast. He didn't have time to help with that.

Silence came off his shoulder.

[Beat 5]
The first man from the east came in with a short blade and the low crouch of someone who had been taught by someone good. Seven meters of corridor behind him; his colleagues stacked at his back, the two-meter width forcing them single file, the man behind him six steps out and waiting his turn. Kyle read the crouch and the ceiling clearance, stepped inside the commitment, and opened a line down the outside of the forearm — wrist to elbow, the way you open a line when you need the hand to stop. The hand stopped. The blade rang off the freight rail somewhere east. The man stepped back into the chest of the one behind him and they sorted the geometry between them for two seconds, which was long enough.

The second man tried to come around the first rather than through him — the smart read, the two-meter choke forcing him into the maintenance alcove opening — and the alcove funnel did exactly what Kyle had counted on it to do, which was put the man's left shoulder into the door frame at the critical moment and half a step before his weight was ready. Kyle closed the distance in the alcove mouth, directed the stumble into the east wall, and the man's own mass delivered him sideways into the conduit boxes. The impact was brief and significant.

Two conduit anchors were chest height on the east wall. Kyle already had a hand on the third when the third and fourth came through.

They'd recalibrated: one high, one low, taking everything the two meters offered. Kyle took the low one's weapon wrist at the seam where the aftermarket brace sat proud of the joint — the hand surrendered the knife and the brace together — while his weight was already shifting onto the conduit anchor, putting his feet off the floor for the half-second it took the high blade to pass under them. He came back down on the high man's leading knee. Something that was not the floor accepted the pressure. The high man's descent was immediate and sincere.

Four men east of center. Three more east, recalibrating in the stack.

He was counting. He knew he was counting. He also knew, in some older part of himself that the neuretics had no language for, that the count was going to break.

[Beat 6]
The western seven came as a unit.

He'd expected that. The corridor gave them no other option — two meters of width meant tactical creativity was a luxury nobody had tonight, which meant volume and speed were the only levers available, which meant they'd committed to both. He moved west at speed before the eastern three had made their decision, putting ground between himself and a pincer that needed him stationary to work. The freight track overhead ran the corridor's full length. He caught the maintenance hook at the three-meter mark with his left hand — not a swing, a pivot point, enough to redirect his feet past the western point man's guard while Silence crossed the opening.

The point man went into the west wall. The man behind him got the western point man's body as his entire tactical situation for the next three seconds.

Then the dark became arithmetic he could not audit in real time.

The width kept them to one-on-one engagements he couldn't refuse — each wave arriving down the single-file press, each man having to decide between pushing through the one in front of him or waiting, and none of them deciding correctly at speed in the dark. A body on the floor changed the floor's geometry. Kyle used the change. An opponent's bulk on the ground was a different kind of step than the composite beneath it, and the man behind had not counted on a different step. The man behind had not counted on Kyle knowing which step it was.

Somewhere in the middle of the western stack a blade found his left forearm — inside, not outside, where the sleeve had ridden up and left skin instead of fabric — and the blade was a good one, a long edge with a good wielder behind it, and the cut was deep. That cost was precise because it landed deliberately: he'd let the exchange take the arm to keep Silence's line clear of the ribcage it was aimed at. A different cost arrived a second later in the same exchange — a body check, the third rib from the bottom, and the rib's assessment was immediate and terminal: cracked. Breathing would be an administrative task for the next several weeks.

Those two he could name. The rest of the count he would reconstruct afterward from what he found against the walls.

The seventh on the western side was a woman with a staff weapon — short-hafted, built for enclosure, which meant she knew what she was doing and knew where she was. She used the corridor's length the way the blades couldn't: standing at the edge of her reach and managing the freight rail from underneath, keeping him off the ceiling anchor until she'd triangulated what he was likely to try. She tagged the rib again, reading the favor he'd started showing it. Kyle worked the angle until he had her staff under the freight track at the moment she needed the ceiling clearance to recover, and a joint lock on the follow-through, and she went down the way professional people went down when the math finished: controlled, without theater, conserving what was left for later.

The corridor was still.

[Beat 7]
Kyle stood in the machine-oil dark and did not count.

This was the fact that lived in the space where counts usually lived: the corridor had not let him count it. He had wins — fourteen people against walls and floor, the dock still reachable, Ms. Tanaka still pressed to the junction boxes — and the wins had prices, and the prices were on his body, not in his arithmetic. The forearm was bleeding through the field strip he'd drawn tight with his teeth during a half-second between the fifth and sixth. The rib had a strong opinion about breathing that he was not going to be able to ignore for long. His neuretics offered a damage summary and he declined to read it, not because he didn't want to know but because reading it would make it exact and exact would make it slower.

"Ms. Tanaka," he said.

"I'm here," she said, from the junction boxes. Her voice had steadied the way voices steadied when the immediate worst was over and the next worst had not arrived yet.

"We're moving. Same position. Stay close."

She came off the wall. In the dark she found his right shoulder by feel and settled into step behind it, and he thought, briefly, that nine years of keeping someone's books was its own kind of education in staying close to the person who was still upright.

He got her out the west end at 01:03.

[Beat 8]
The dock was a Ferryside loading pier — the smell of lake water and compressed fuel and the sub-audible resonance of the Pulse under the eastern breakwater, its 19Hz signature in his back teeth and the wet wood of the bollards. Dock 14-C was a third-tier berth, a mid-range cargo carrier running a mixed freight manifest, and the purser at the gate was a small man with a clipboard who had spent enough shifts at 03:00 to have opinions about paperwork and none about reasons.

Ms. Tanaka stopped at the dock gate.

She turned and looked at Kyle's forearm, where the field strip had soaked through to dark at the edges, and then looked at his face.

"The contract," she said. "Whatever they paid you—"

"Was fine," Kyle said. "Walk in."

She hesitated. She was the kind of person who had kept someone's books for nine years and decided, one Tuesday, that she was done, and that kind of decision had a weight to it that wasn't just fear.

"I should have left three years ago," she said.

"Probably," Kyle said. "Walk in anyway."

She looked at the saya and at the field strip and at his face one more time, and then she walked in. The purser checked the boarding chit, noted the time — 03:34 — and closed the gate.

The ship ran its pre-cast sequence at 03:40 exactly. Kyle watched the running lights pull away from the dock face, the carrier swinging its bow south toward the lake's open body, the flour on Ms. Tanaka's sleeves already gone with her into the dark of the transit berths below.

He stood at the dock rail for two minutes — long enough to be certain, short enough not to name it — and then he called for a medic pickup on the work channel and sat down on a bollard to wait.

His terminal chirped.

STANDING CONTRACT — ADDENDUM.
CLIENT NOTES THE CONTRACT WAS MISPRICED BY THE MARKET.
CLIENT HAS ADJUSTED YOUR HAZARD SCHEDULE. RETROACTIVELY.

He read it twice. He put the terminal away.

[Beat 9]
The medic arrived at 04:15, a quiet woman named Rue with a field kit and the practiced economy of someone who worked the dock district's early hours and had long since learned not to ask about corridors. She cleaned and re-stripped the forearm — the cut ran six centimeters along the inside, deep but tidy, the kind that would stay interesting for two weeks — confirmed the rib was cracked and not broken, taped it firm, and handed him a small packet of analgesics he would not take before a job and could take now.

"You'll favor the arm," she said.

"I know," Kyle said.

"Two weeks minimum. More if you don't favor it."

"I know that too."

She packed her kit. He paid her rate plus the dock-hour premium, exact. She left without asking what the corridor had looked like. Dock medics learned that skill early and kept it their whole careers.

The rib was going to be the louder bill. The forearm would stiffen by morning, which meant the draw would be measured differently for the next two weeks, which meant every job in that window was a different job than the one he'd budgeted for. He sat on the bollard with the rib doing its precise, persistent accounting and thought about Golan.

He'd passed Golan's block on the route to the dock — the parts dealer on Leavitt who had sold him mesh repair supplies for seven years, a man with a second-floor shop and a yellow security light above the door that Kyle could read from the street at a quarter kilometer. He'd looked up out of habit on the approach and the yellow light was off, and through the glass of the street-level lobby Golan had been standing at the inner door watching him, and when Kyle had tried the lobby handle — just the lobby, just the handle — Golan had looked at him for three seconds and not moved.

One door. The corridor's invoice would not be one door.

Fourteen Lotus crew, salaried, professional, hurt badly in a two-meter choke in the dark on a job that had been about one counting-room clerk with flour on her sleeves — that was a debt the city ran on its own schedule, through every vendor and carrier and parts dealer who had a Lotus courtesy arrangement and found the inconvenience of an exception not worth explaining. The GLMZ ran on courtesy arrangements. Kyle had eleven years of them, built into something load-bearing.

He sat on the bollard and let the rib have its opinion and waited to find out how much of it was going to be left in the morning.

[Beat 10]
The lake was grey with the first edge of a dawn that wasn't there yet. South of the breakwater, a running light was very small and getting smaller.

She sailed at 03:40 with the flour still on her sleeves.
