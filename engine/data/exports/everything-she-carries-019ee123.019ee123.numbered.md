# Everything She Carries

*Amara goes to Theodora's appointment carrying a plan to extract the scan data. She leaves carrying less than she came with.*

She woke at 04:22 and lay still for twenty minutes before she acknowledged she wasn't going back to sleep.

The ceiling of her apartment was gray-white in the predawn — a quality she had never noticed in three years of the same room. The decision she had made the night before still held. She dressed in the dark, same routine in the same order, and checked that her personal device had the strip application installed and ready. She had a plan. The plan required her to look like a person who had no plan.

---

She took the transit pod out of Zone 6 at 07:44 and watched the Seam go past: relay beacons on their poles, the breakfast canteen with its white-painted hours. Then managed air registered in her sinuses — cedar and processing — and she was inside.

The lobby of Theodora's arcology was running its morning cycle. The news feed showed a wildfire in the eastern coast corridor and the tagline read ORISON CRITICAL RESPONSE UNIT DEPLOYS. The desk attendant signed her in without looking up — there was a half-eaten pastry pushed to the corner of the desk, the kind of thing that said the shift had started too early. She took the elevator. Fourteen floors.

The elevator doors were reflective. She watched herself stand in them — upright, composed, the portable reader case in its proper hand — and for four seconds she read the lobby below as the reflection of something else: two exit points, the ventilation shaft in the upper-left quadrant, the attendant's position weighted by proximity and concealment. The doors closed. She was looking at her own face.

She stood in the corridor for a moment before she moved toward 14C.

The thought had not been hers. The geometry of the lobby was how someone read a space who moved through checkpoints and could not afford to be caught by surprise. She had stood in lobbies for eight years. She had never read a lobby like that.

---

Theodora Eze was sixty-eight, the textile work spread across her kitchen table in three colors: rust, ivory, a deep saturated green. She had been working on the same piece for four months. "I know exactly what I want it to be," she said, setting water to heat. "But my hands keep making the choice before I can tell them which one." She laughed — a short, involuntary sound — and then looked at her own hands as if surprised by the laugh. "Like they know a different pattern than I do."

Amara set up the portable reader. "When did you first notice?"

"October." She sat down and held her arm out in the practiced way. "I thought it was fatigue. Then I thought maybe my eyes."

"And your last calibration was?"

"September. Everything was within tolerance."

*I know*, Amara thought. *The tech filed a clean record.* She ran the scan.

Theodora got up to check the water while the scan ran. In those ninety seconds, Amara read the tissue: healthy vascularization, the associative mesh conducting at the appropriate frequencies. And underneath the clean readings, in the layer the suppression sub-protocol had decided not to log, the genuine drift — motor pathways losing integration since October, the gap between intention and execution that the system had been noting and then not noting, visit after visit.

She documented the scan. Then she tried to route a copy to an external address.

The reader returned: *Outbound routing restricted. Data classified Cellvault-confidential. Authorized recipients only. This attempt has been logged. Ref: compliance policy 7.4.*

She closed the routing interface and opened the strip application on her personal device. It would take forty-one seconds to separate the classification headers from the session data. She set the device face-down on the table beside the reader, left hand resting on top of it.

At second fourteen, Theodora came back from the counter. "I forgot — do you take honey in your tea? I'm making a cup anyway."

"No, thank you. I'm just finishing the sync." Amara did not move her hand from the device.

"Of course." Theodora sat down and picked up a strand of the rust thread. "My daughter says I should just do a different pattern for a while. Come back to this one fresh." She held the thread to the window. "I don't think that's it. I think I need to understand why this one won't work."

"That makes sense," Amara said. Her hand was still on the device. She had no way to check the progress indicator.

"It stopped raining," Theodora said, looking out.

Amara lifted the device as Theodora looked away and checked the screen.

The progress indicator was gone. But the file was not where she had specified — the application had thrown a secondary exception and logged a second system alert. Cellvault's security layer had caught the strip attempt. The file was missing. She had two compliance log entries and no scan data.

She set the device down and finished packing the reader. "Your tissue is healthy. I want to schedule a follow-up in six weeks rather than six months."

"Of course." Theodora looked at the textile. "I hope by then my hands know what they're supposed to be doing."

At the door, Theodora offered more tea, which Amara declined. In the corridor, she took out her personal device and photographed the embroidery through the still-open door. Theodora didn't see. The photograph showed exactly what Theodora had described: precise, intricate, wrong in a way that took a moment to locate — like a word misspelled by one letter.

She walked to the elevator and went down.

---

She sat in a café in the contested buffer between Zone 5 and Zone 6 at midday. The air came honest — diesel, cooking fat, the specific particulate of a corridor where nobody had finished claiming the infrastructure. A relay beacon tested its signal through the wall. She opened the channel and sent the embroidery photo to Seto through her personal device — not through Cellvault, not logged, the first thing she had moved through an unauthorized channel since she started the anomaly archive.

*I saw what it does to someone today.* She sent the message with the photo.

She added Theodora's entry to the anomaly archive. Thirty-two now, no scan data attached. His reply came: *I know.*

She sat with the two compliance log entries. The first at 09:14:33, the second at 09:17:08. Both in a queue she couldn't see. The photograph was on its way to him. The scan was not.

Something clattered two seats away — a key fob off a table — and she looked up, then back at the device. She closed the archive.

The bleed came after, without warning, arriving in the body before the mind could sort it. A pressure at the base of her spine — the specific ache of someone who had been sitting on a bicycle seat for hours. Not metaphor. Her spine did not know it was wrong. She pressed her back against the café chair and the sensation adjusted but did not leave. She sat with it and tried to determine whether it was his or hers.

She could not.

She ordered a second coffee she didn't need and sat with it, the way she had sat after the lunch with Ciro, and let the café have its own life without her. Outside the window, the buffer zone ran its midday shift: street vendors, relay workers on rotation change, a two-stroke engine idling at the corner. She had sent the photograph. She had two compliance log entries. The scan was not on her device. Those were different things and she was still working out what the difference meant.
