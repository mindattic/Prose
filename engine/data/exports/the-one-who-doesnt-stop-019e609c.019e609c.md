# The One Who Doesn't Stop

_A burned-out freelancer with a dying mind carries a stranger's six-hour-old transplant, and the terrified child it belongs to, in a straight line north across a hostile vertical Chicago: corporate checkpoints, a refinery lockdown, a thin spot where the world stops keeping its promises, and a tunnel war, at a cost greater than the run was ever worth._

## Hegewisch

The shotgun comes up first, which is the contractor's mistake, because it tells Kyle everything about the next half-second and nothing the contractor wanted told. Thirty seconds ago this was a disagreement — an Arcturus man, corporate security of the kind that bills you for the bullet, deciding whether he was going to shoot Kyle tonight. He decided yes. He brought the shotgun to underline the point. Kyle, a man of standards, declines to be underlined: he drops under the blast, feels the pellets shred the air where his chest was, and comes up with Chorus already speaking — the stubby five-chamber revolver-shotgun he keeps low on his left hip. One round. The contractor's trigger hand comes apart at the wrist, and the weapon clatters off to begin a quieter life on the pavement. In the ringing quiet after, Kyle hears the wreck behind him settle: an ambulance that ran the Hegewisch crossroads and lost an argument with a stalled hauler, back doors sprung, one wheel still turning. Inside, in the wreck-light, a small face is watching him. It waves.

"Are you the new one," the small face asks — and Kyle understands the question underneath is a different one. She's eight, maybe nine. Her chest is taped under a flexible biopolymer brace that glows a soft clinical blue through her gown, and she's holding a juice box she hasn't drunk from, the straw still in its wrapper. She is calmer than she has any business being: the flat, careful calm of a kid who's been told that calm is a medical instruction. Kyle steps up into the bay. Driver, dead. Medic in the passenger seat, also not coming back. The gurney tag reads IMANI OKONKWO-LINDQVIST — RECIPIENT, POST-OP, FIRST 6 HOURS, NORTHWESTERN MEMORIAL. Kyle reads it twice. The math does itself: somebody printed this child a new heart tonight, it's six hours old, and it is currently unguarded in the middle of a south-side ditch. He says, "Hi, Imani. I'm Kyle." She says, "I know. My uncle says you're the one who doesn't stop."

He thumbs the tag's emergency contact. One ring. "Vance," a voice says, already moving. Kyle says, "Your driver's dead. Your kid's on the floor of a south-side ditch with a six-hour-old heart wired to a battery. I have a motorcycle." A breath. Then: "What's the brace reading." Kyle looks. "Sinus, ninety-two, paced." "You've got sixty minutes before the vascular scaffold throws a clot. Maybe seventy. And keep her calm — a new graft that races can tear itself off the suture line. The wires aren't decoration, and neither is she." Kyle says, "Northwestern." "Streeterville. Trauma bay nine. I'll hold a slot." Kyle says, "I'm in Hegewisch." Vance does the same math he just did. "Then it's a straight line north and no time to be cute about it. Don't stop for checkpoints. And your straight line clips a thin spot at the old Cinderfall surface, around Thirty-Fifth — a stretch where the world doesn't keep its promises. If the lattice in your skull goes quiet, you're standing in one. Hold the wheel straight even when straight feels wrong, lock your eyes on a far light, and do not look at the edges." Kyle says, "You say that like I've got a habit of stopping." Vance says, "I say it like I've scraped up the ones who did." Vance hangs up.

Here is how Kyle knows it's sixty minutes and not sixty-one — the same way he knew the contractor's trigger a beat early. Everyone in the GLMZ — the drowned-Midwest sprawl that stopped being a dozen cities and started being one long vertical argument — runs Neuretics: compute grown into the skull and worked by thought, no jack, no screen, just knowing. Most people's stay inside the safe envelope. Kyle's is a military NeoCortex tuned so far past it that it is, slowly and without drama, killing him, and he is at peace with that. Somebody built him into a weapon when he was too young to be asked, and he has spent a long time deciding the one thing he actually gets to decide: who the weapon is for. Tonight it's for something that weighs less than his jacket and wants to know how fast the bike goes.

He lifts her onto the pillion, sidesaddle, and threads the medical harness through his belt loop so the battery rides over his kidney instead of swinging free. He ties her wrists loosely through the back of his coat so she won't slide off if she sleeps. Her cheek finds the back of his shoulder like it's been there before. "Hold on," he says — a stupid thing to say to a kid whose hands are too small to grip his ribs, but it's what you say. "How fast," she says. "Fast." "Good." Across his back rides Silence: a matte-black carbon-nanotube katana, no edge that catches the light and no trick to it but the hand that holds it. Chorus sits low on his left where it always sits. The kid is the only new weight, and the only one he's doing math about. The bike comes alive under them — eight cylinders' worth of audacity in a city that doesn't deserve them.

The first mile is gray zone — Hegewisch sprawl, no sovereign in residence, nobody's law and nobody's lights. Kyle takes Avenue O at eighty, threading two rows of collapsed light poles his Neuretics flags and lines up before his eyes have finished arriving. He doesn't signal. There's no one to signal to except a few crows hunched on a dead wire, who decline to comment. Imani's breathing is a small warm patch against his back, even and slow and worked-at, each breath the same measured size as the last. "You're not scared," he says. "I'm not allowed to be," she says. "The doctor said. Scared makes it pump too hard, and it's new, so. I'm doing the breathing." She's reciting; he can hear the doctor in it, the borrowed grown-up calm laid over a kid like a coat three sizes too big. It isn't bravery. It's a child running the one rule she was handed because it's the only thing in reach. "Then keep doing the breathing," Kyle says. He doesn't tell her how good she is at it. Good is not the thing he wants her to be tonight.

## Crucible Genomics

The checkpoint is two guards in soft-helmet armor and a third behind blast glass, all wearing the bored faces of people expecting a manifest. Kyle doesn't have a manifest. He has a child, a battery, and a smile. He brakes hard, throws his hands off the bars in the universal not-a-threat, and gets out the only words that matter: "Post-op transit. Lacuna cooler. Northwestern." The left guard reads the brace. The right guard reads the cooler logo. And the one behind the glass — Crucible internal, white coat under the armor, because in a gene-foundry zone the gate scans the person, not the plate; it's the blood they price, not the bike — runs Imani's face against the registry. Whatever it tells him drains the evening out of his expression. He doesn't make a speech. He looks at the guards, then at Kyle, and lifts two fingers, and the barrier sinks. "Have a good night," Kyle says, and is gone before the man decides whether he's allowed to answer.

A mile and a half of Crucible private artery, manicured, palm-lit, river on the right. Kyle pegs the throttle. The bike makes the unsubtle music of a thing built before noise ordinances. Workers in white smocks look up from the greenhouses and decide not to look long. The corporation has classified Imani as outgoing inventory, which means Kyle is allowed to drive through Eden as long as he keeps moving. He keeps moving. Imani says, "Why are the trees in rows." Kyle says, "Because the company likes to know where things are." She says, "That seems anxious." Kyle has nothing to add. The kid is correct.

The north exit is a sally-gate — a slab of layered ballistic shutter that only lifts for a scheduled manifest, and Kyle's manifest is a lie he told a mile ago. He does not slow. His Neuretics reads the gate the way it reads everything — for the part that's load-bearing and the part that's only confident — and paints the hydraulic spine where the shutter meets its track. He draws Chorus left-handed, thumbs the cylinder to the breaching load, and puts one round exactly there. The actuator gives up its entire career at once; the slab jams a third of the way up, shrieking on a dead track. Kyle flattens to the tank and takes them under it with inches to spare. They drop off Crucible's glass-smooth pavement onto the broken asphalt of a gray zone and the suspension finds out about Chicago all at once. Imani laughs — small, clean, a tuning fork struck somewhere behind his ribs. "Loud part," she says. "Told you," he says.

## The Pullman Seam

Pullman Works glows on his right the way a museum glows — restored row houses, heritage cameras in period housings, an Axiom company town keeping perfect time for people who don't own a minute of it. Kyle threads the gray-zone seam along its edge, the unincorporated strip Axiom never bothered to fence. Heads come up as the engine passes. One kid shouts something the engine eats, but the shape of the word is unmistakably *legend*, and Kyle lifts a hand like he's in a parade. Other heads come up too, and these don't care about the bike. They're fledglings — pledges to the Vultures, the crews that scavenge the dead and the dying, the ones who'll cut the transplant back out of your chest if you miss a payment and sell whatever's left for parts. A fledgling runs salvage on spec — no patch, no blessing — hoping to drag home something worth a place in the flock. A post-op transplant riding pillion through open country isn't a target. It's a winning lottery ticket — the exact score that turns a fledgling into a Vulture. Two of them are already on their bikes and on their phones.

Imani's hand finds the side of his coat and clutches. Not afraid — the grip is steady, intentional — she is *noticing*. She has not been outside Crucible's pavement before. Kyle understands this without being told. He says, "First time off the block?" She says, "First time on the ground." He says, "Well. Buckle up." She says, "Buckle up to what." He says, "Existentially." She thinks about it. She says, "Okay."

## Ashgrave Corridor

The Ashgrave Synthesis Corridor announces itself with a wall of haze you can taste before you see it. The perimeter lights glow the wrong color — too yellow, too low — the way a refinery glows when it is having a feeling. Kyle has a respirator clipped to his jacket; Imani does not. His Neuretics throws the air up as a number he doesn't like and a clock he likes less. He reaches back without slowing, finds her face with his palm, presses his bandana over her mouth. "Breathe shallow. Through this. Not your nose." She nods against his hand. He bites his own respirator's mouthpiece, snaps the seal, and rides into a sky the color of old bruises like he was invited.

Two refinery towers exhale orange fire on either side of him, close enough to read rivet patterns. The air pressure shifts as a vent stack sixty feet up blows downward; the bike yaws; he corrects before the yaw finishes, because the correction arrived in his hands a half-second before the problem did. Imani's grip becomes a small steel claw at his belt. Out loud he says, "We're fine." She says, through the bandana, "I know. I can tell from your shoulders." Kyle has met adults who could not read his shoulders. He is starting to take this kid personally.

Halfway through, the corridor's throat is shut. A leak somewhere upwind has tripped a containment lockdown, and Ashgrave's answer to a containment lockdown is to drop a blast-rated bulkhead across the only lane and let whoever's caught inside hold their breath about it. The bulkhead is built to stop a runaway tanker. It was not built with Kyle's night in mind. He brakes to a crawl in the burning dark, draws Chorus, and lets the Neuretics find the manual-override housing bolted to the right-hand pillar — the one part of a door like this that trusts a human hand. Two breaching rounds. The first peels the cover. The second finds the release. The bulkhead groans and begins to lift, far too slowly, and Kyle is already moving, laying the bike flat and dragging them under the rising lip while Imani buries her face in his back and the toxic dark closes over them like water and is, a heartbeat later, behind them.

He clears the Corridor's north ramp at a hundred and ten, rolls off the throttle on the way down, and spits the respirator's mouthpiece out so it swings free on its lanyard. He reaches back one-handed and tugs Imani's bandana down off her face. "You with me?" She coughs once, twice, then says, "I'm with you. Your shoulders said." He says, "My shoulders are working overtime." She says, "Give them a raise." Kyle laughs so hard he almost loses the line.

## Englewood

Englewood at night looks like a city that's been told a joke nobody is laughing at *yet*. Half the streetlights work; the other half are someone's collateral. Kyle means to take 63rd to Halsted, but 63rd dead-ends in a viaduct that finished collapsing sometime this spring — a slope of broken deck and rebar where an underpass used to be. No way through. There is a way *over*: a tilted slab the locals have braced into a ramp, because in a gray zone every disaster eventually becomes infrastructure. He takes it at speed, the bike going light at the crest, Imani's battery thudding once against his kidney, and lands hard on the far side with thirty-four minutes left and a new ache behind his teeth. Two headlights take the ramp a beat behind him. Then a third. His Neuretics tags them, ranges them, and tells him the only useful thing there is to know: they are matching his speed on purpose. He does the only useful thing back. He goes faster.

## The Cinderfall Anomaly

Halsted north of the tracks runs over the bones of Cinderfall Energy — eleven thermal plants buried under the street, their surface a grid of steam-vent grates and convection shimmer and asphalt re-laid three times this year because it keeps warping in the heat. "Why's the air doing that," Imani says. "Because the company under us is cooking," Kyle says. "Oh," she says — the *oh* of a kid learning the world has a basement. He doesn't tell her the air is about to do something Cinderfall has nothing to do with. Two headlights have hung off his tail since Pullman: patient, matched to his pace, in no hurry, which is its own kind of bad news. Then his Neuretics flags what's ahead instead, and quits having opinions about the headlights. Two blocks on, the streetlights stop agreeing on where they're standing, and the lattice in his skull — which has had a fast, clean answer for every single thing tonight — goes quiet the exact way Vance said it would.

He should turn two blocks west. West is Bridgeport; west is normal. He goes straight, because straight is what the call is for, and at Thirty-Fifth and Halsted the world stops being a place and starts being a condition. The headlight hits something ahead and the something doesn't hit back. His stomach drops a foot without him; the wheels are on the pavement but the pavement is half a second behind where his eyes swear it is. And then the worse thing, the part the warnings never sell hard enough: he *knows* this corner. He has never been here in his life. He knows it the way you know a hallway from a dream you've had since you were small and never once walked on purpose — the grey country behind sleep that everyone visits and nobody admits to in daylight. Behind him Imani makes a sound he has never heard a child make. Not fear. Not pain. *Recognition.*

Warpature, the Network calls it — a thin spot, the sediment of the mass quantum-entanglement programs they ran and buried sixty years back, surfacing now wherever it pleases. The maps mark it LATENT in a small gray font and the insurers won't write a line on the block. Kyle's Neuretics tries to map it and gets garbage back: distances that won't hold still, a clock running three directions at once. So for once in his overclocked life he flies on the one instrument the lattice can't sharpen and can't replace — the old animal at the base of the skull that every person still carries under all the hardware. He obeys the rules survivors keep, the ones Vance recited and the ones older than Vance: constant speed, eyes locked on a far streetlamp, don't look at the edges, don't trust a turn — because in here *right* is sometimes *left*, and *left* is sometimes *down*, and the only direction that stays honest is the one you already committed to. "Don't look left," he says. "I already wasn't," Imani says, in a voice gone very small and very old.

At the edge of his vision something moves that shouldn't: a shape with one shadow too many, and the extra shadow arrives a half-beat *before* the shape, like an echo that forgot which way time runs. He doesn't look at it straight. The dark keeps it smeared, and he understands — the way you understand a pressure drop in your teeth — that looking at it directly is not a thing a mind comes home from. Imani's breath snags, his only warning, and he throws the bike *right*, which is the wrong word for the direction it actually goes. The miss should have been a foot. It's less than an inch. He feels it pass like a current turning over in deep water, and a line of cold opens along his ribs under the coat — clean, deep, not bleeding yet, because in here the blood runs half a second behind everything else. Then the far lamp is closer, the throttle holds, and a block on the world remembers its own rules and the pain arrives all at once and warm. And behind him is the thing he's been dreading worse than the aberration: the brace at his kidney starts to chirp, fast and climbing, because Imani's borrowed calm has finally come apart. She's crying now, the real thing, no doctor's voice left in it, her whole small body shaking against his back — *I saw it, I saw it, it knew my name* — and her new heart is sprinting in its six-hour-old cradle exactly the way it was never to be let. This is the version of tonight that kills her, and it isn't a knife or a bike or a Vulture; it's a frightened child's pulse. So Kyle does the one thing the situation allows and talks: low, flat, boring, the voice you'd read a tax form in — about the bike, about the loud part coming up, about anything with edges that stay where you leave them. "You didn't see it," he says, which is true in the only way that counts — nobody keeps it. "By morning you won't be able to find it. That's the one mercy the things like that have." A beat. "Breathe with the engine. In on the low note." The chirping slows. Slows. Steadies. "You lied," she says, wrung out, when she can. "You said don't look left and there wasn't a left." "There wasn't," he agrees. "That was the point." "You're hurt." "I'm fine," he says. It's the first time he's lied to her, and she's too tired to catch it.

## The Lobby War

Twenty-one minutes. He hits the south edge of the Loop, where municipal law has strong opinions about his speed except municipal law has been on wildcat strike for nine days and currently has *no* opinions, which Kyle considers a generous gift. The fastest line through is *through* the Wabash Stack — a service tunnel under the building's whole footprint, six lanes wide, badge-locked at both ends. The Loop's municipal floors own the lower entry; the corponation owns four and up; between them sits a standing feud the locals call the Lobby War. Tonight the feud is not standing. Nine days of strike has turned it hot, and the tunnel mouth is a barricade with people behind it who have run clean out of patience for one another and would be glad to spend the surplus on him.

He goes in anyway, because the long way costs eleven minutes he's currently bleeding through. The tunnel is cathedral-tall and full of scrolling ads, a slow river of light overhead, and halfway down it a corporate security line has a municipal picket pinned behind an overturned cleaning rig: strikers on one side, the company's hired guns on the other, both too far gone in nine days of grievance to clock a motorcycle until it's the most interesting thing in the room. Kyle thumbs Chorus to the flash-bang load and puts a round into the ceiling above the corporate line. The tunnel goes white. He doesn't get through clean — two seconds of blindness is two seconds of frightened people firing at memory, and one of them is unlucky enough to be lucky: a round furrows the meat across the top of his shoulder, not through, a hot graze that nearly tears Chorus out of his grip and paints the sleeve dark. He keeps the median. He keeps the throttle. A thermal-biometric reader catches his face on the way out and does what Stack policy says to do with an unbilled civilian — lets him pass, opens an invoice. Behind him Imani is watching the rivers of light, not the blood she can't see. "It's beautiful," she says. Kyle gets his breath back around the burn in his shoulder before he answers, so it lands level. "Don't tell the advertisers," he says. "It'll go to their heads."

## River North

River North is a thirty-second wedge of gray zone where the river district gives way to the high towers, and most of it is underwater — the Chicago River hasn't been managed in eighteen years, and Lower Wacker is a canal now. He takes the upper level, a bridge that Tollgate Systems owns and charges credits to cross. Kyle does not have a Tollgate badge. Kyle has twelve minutes and an opinion about Tollgate.

The Tollgate barrier is a swinging steel boom. He hits it at twenty-five. The boom snaps clean off its hinge and cartwheels into the river like an unimpressed sword. The bike scrapes the railing, throws sparks into the dark, recovers. A camera reads his face across the span — not the bike, the bike is nobody, it is *him* the system wants — and bills the only identity it can match: a credit account that has not existed since 2207, which Kyle made for exactly this kind of evening and then deleted. The invoice will get an automated rejection. The rejection will be filed. The filing will be archived. The archive will be deleted in a routine purge. The whole exchange will take six weeks and cost Tollgate seventeen credits in processing. He laughs, which pulls at the cold line under his coat. Imani says, "Why are you laughing." He says, "Because the most expensive thing I did to them tonight was the paperwork." She says, "Good."

## Streeterville

Streeterville is a forest of glass towers wearing crowns of advertising, the Waxwing Spire tallest among them, and at its feet the brutalist slab of Northwestern Memorial — still standing since the twenty-tens because the corponation makes too much money knowing precisely when each patient inside it dies to bother tearing it down. Kyle sees Trauma Bay Nine's red light from six blocks out. He counts it down — six, five — and at four the fledglings finish the hunt they opened in Pullman. They have had the entire north side to get ahead of him. A bike slides out of a parking structure and lays itself across his front wheel on purpose.

The world turns over. Kyle gets a hand behind Imani's head the way you cradle a dropped glass and takes the road with his own shoulder so she takes none of it. His bike goes skating into a pillar and stops being a bike. He comes up with his back to a loading dock and Imani tucked behind his knees, and his Neuretics counts the headlights fanning into a half-circle: six riders, chains and a cut-down pipe and at least one gun, all of them young, all of them wanting to be the one who carries a fresh transplant home to the real Vultures by morning. "Stay behind me," he says. Imani says, "I'm counting." He says, "Count slow."

He gives them the one chance honesty requires — "Walk away and keep your hands" — and none take it, because a fledgling who walks away stays a fledgling. So Kyle goes to work, a child at his back he will not let them past. The chain-rider comes first and overcommits; Kyle steps inside the arc, lets the chain bite into his forearm instead of his skull, and puts the rider down with a short brutal economy that ends with the man studying the asphalt up close. The pipe-swinger he takes at the knee and then the jaw — alive, unconscious, a distinction he still bothers to make. The one clawing for a pistol gets an electric-bola round from Chorus and a long bad minute alone with his own nervous system. Then the rib the thin spot opened tears wider on a hard pivot, a wrong and sudden heat that empties half the air out of him, and for the first time all night Kyle is a half-step slow. He pays for it at once: a knife opens the back of his shoulder, a boot finds the bad rib, and he eats both standing, because stepping clear would have left a lane to Imani and there is no version of tonight where he leaves a lane to Imani. He stays between every one of them and her. Under the work the lattice runs past every red line it has, spending him to keep the read fast enough, and he can feel the cost of it gathering behind his eyes like weather. When it ends, four are down and two are taillights, and Kyle is upright in a way that is costing him everything he has left.

The bike is dead and the bay is three blocks off, and three blocks is nothing — he has bled more than this for less. He sheathes Silence, holsters Chorus, and picks her up: harness, battery, the whole small weight of her. When she settles against his chest something old turns over in him without asking permission — a body remembering a night it was small and the arms that didn't come — and he files it where he files everything, which is *later*, which is *never*. He is not going to set her down. That's the whole of it; the reasons can wait in line. Her hand fists in his collar. "You're hurt," she says again, and this time he doesn't bother with the lie. "Yeah." "Tell me about the loud," she says — which is the kindest thing anyone has done for him in a year — so he does, the whole way, one foot in front of the other, the red light of the bay getting bigger.

Vance is already in the lane with the gurney — tall, brown skin, hair tied back, scrubs over a body that has run a lot of miles today — and doesn't waste a syllable. "On the gurney." Kyle is already lowering her. The harness comes off in two practiced motions. Imani's hand stays in his coat a half-second after the rest of her is on the gurney, and when he works it loose she asks the question every kid eventually has to put to somebody. "Will I see you again?" Kyle doesn't lie to her; he's been lied to by kinder people than himself, and it never once helped. "No," he says. "Probably not." She takes it the way she's taken everything tonight — straight, no flinch, filed somewhere for later. "Thank you for the loud," she says. "Anytime, kid," he says — the one gentle lie he lets himself, and the only one she lets him have. "Tell the heart I said hi."

Vance reads the brace, the wires, the cooler — then reads *him*: the hand pressed to his side, the dark spreading at the forearm and the shoulder, the boot that won't take full weight. "Forty-one minutes," she says. "I had you at fifty-five." It is an old argument between them; she has put him back together before, and they both already know how the next part goes. Kyle says, "I cheated." She says, "You're bleeding into my ambulance lane. Stay. Half the people in my bay tonight are in better shape than you." Kyle is already turning away. "Don't worry about me," he says. "I'll just rub some dirt in it."

He walks. The bay door drops behind him and its red light goes white, which is the hospital's way of saying they have her, which is the only confirmation he was ever going to get that it worked. No alarm follows him out. No name. That's the shape of the work: you do the thing, the thing closes behind you like water, and the city goes back to being the city without ever noticing the hole you came through. Sixty minutes ago a man with a shotgun wanted to underline a point. Kyle has, in his fashion, finished the sentence.

He turns south, on foot, the long way, because south is no longer urgent and there's no bike left to make it quick. Behind his eyes the overclocked lattice runs hot and spends another thin slice of whatever he has left, and he carries that the way he carries everything — without complaint, because tonight it bought a stranger's kid a heartbeat she gets to keep. She'll forget his name by spring. That's the deal, and it's a good deal, and he'd take it again before she finished asking. He has somewhere to be. He always does. So he goes — not fast, for once, and not in a straight line, but going, which is the only direction he has ever really had.
