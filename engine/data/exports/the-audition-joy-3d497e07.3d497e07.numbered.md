# The Audition

[Beat 1]
The rain had been making itself at home since midnight, and by 02:14 it had moved the furniture and settled in. West Town at that hour was a series of arrangements between the streetlights and the water — what the light kept, what the dark swallowed, who was standing in which column. Kyle walked north on Wood because Wood ran straight and he had developed a personal affection for straight streets after the corridor two nights ago, where straight had meant fourteen crew coming at him in sequence instead of simultaneously, and the difference had mattered.

The rib talked when he twisted. It had been talking since day one of the corridor job and it had grown, in the manner of unwanted conversationalists, from a whisper to an opinion. The rest of the damage was administrative — bruising that had resolved from red to the institutional yellow-green of a problem being processed, a shoulder that worked at ninety percent and stopped asking questions at eighty. Corridor wounds. They were their own category.

He counted the bracket at two blocks out, because the discipline counted things.

One high: the thermal window on the third floor of the print shop, glass gone just cold enough to be wrong, a warmth inside that was not the building's warmth. Someone with a visual line to the whole cross-street, stationary, patient. A watcher running a rubric.

One in the doorway across from the laundromat, standing the way a person stands when they have been standing a long time and are being extremely casual about the fact that their weight was on the wrong foot.

One at the corner that Kyle was approaching, pretending to read something on a dead terminal.

One down the block, far enough to be the anchor.

One on the rooftop of the tailor's shop, which he got from the rain pattern — the way rain fell differently where something stood in it.

One he didn't have yet, which meant one he wasn't supposed to have yet, which meant the sixth position was the runner and the runner was already in place behind him, because a runner doesn't wait at the bracket, a runner waits at the exit.

Six positions. One of them was designed to leave.

Kyle did the arithmetic on the stun drone while he was still a full block away, which was the margin the arithmetic required. The drone would be somewhere overhead, maybe two, held back until he committed to the cross-street where the geometry closed. The doorway, the corner man, the anchor: a standard closing pattern, textbook, three points. The rooftop was the override position, the one that could move. And the watcher in the thermal window was the one running the rubric, which meant this was being graded.

He put both hands in his coat pockets — left on the saya's mouth, right closed around air, not Cacophony, because Cacophony was for when the arithmetic came out different — and walked into the bracket at normal pace, because a man who changes his pace when he spots surveillance tells the surveillance everything.

The cross-street smell was wet concrete and the ghost of a food stall that hadn't been there since afternoon, and from somewhere up the block a turret cat was sitting on a window ledge watching the rain the way the dedicated watched anything.

[Beat 2]
The drone dropped at 02:16, forty centimeters short.

Kyle knew what a stun drone at full discharge looked like and sounded like — he'd met three, paid for the education twice, the third time had been someone else's problem — and what this one did was not that. The discharge hit the wet pavement in a cone that stopped at his boot heel, the arc tracing the surface water and then dying, the sound a half-note lower than a real discharge, the light forty percent of what the specs said. The drone itself held at three meters and cycled immediately for a second pass that it did not make.

An audition crew throttles its emitters. They weren't trying to drop him. They were testing how he reacted to a charge that looked real and wasn't, and they were doing it with maybe two seconds between the dropped drone and the first of the ground crew moving, which meant the test had a graded response window.

Kyle did not move, which was one of the answers. He tilted his head thirty degrees and looked at the drone, which was the more interesting one.

"Evening," he said, to the drone, which could not respond, and to the bracket in general, which could.

The corner man came first — young, moving fast, and furious in the way that people are furious when they're twenty-two and loyal and someone they respect is being studied by a stranger. He had an arm. Not chrome, not aftermarket — the real thing, a full replacement starting mid-humerus, the kind the body rejection catalogs listed at eight thousand Φ on a good year, this one running higher because it had secondary-market augments in the forearm, tactile-override arrays, pressure-indexed grip. Kyle clocked the manufacturer's seal at the shoulder joint as the young man closed the last three meters and swung.

KT. That was the quality of chrome: Kinetix TerraForm — the market name for the Warptech Systems division that did precision replacement arms for industrial accidents and people who wanted to be more than industrial accidents. The emergency lockout was a three-finger combination on the exterior surface of the wrist, because the certification body required manual override accessible to a third party in case the wearer went into medical distress. Kyle had a thirty-second window once the arm was in range.

He took the swing on his forearm, stepped inside it, took a bruise he had expected, and found the lockout sequence.

The arm stopped.

Not dropped — locked, mid-extension, fingers splayed, caught in the gesture of a blow that wasn't landing, a chrome hand held out like an offering. KT said something that required punctuation Kyle wouldn't put on paper, and Kyle said:

"Kinetix TerraForm, the emergency medical lockout is a liability clause, not a warranty gap. You should ask Lotus to bill Kinetix for the upgrade, at a minimum."

He let go. The arm unlocked. KT stepped back with the expression of a man who has just been told something humiliating, true, and expensive, in that order.

[Beat 3]
Rook came from the doorway, which was the approach pattern Kyle had expected, and Rook was what his name advertised: broad-shouldered, honest in the way that physical scale makes honest, moving with the economy of a man who'd spent years not starting things he couldn't finish. The BCI port was at the temple, left side, the older Meridian Conduct series — Kyle knew them from the identifier housing, a flattened oval of matte gray that sat proud at the skull, the kind that had a hardware reset built into the external casing because the early Conduct units had overloaded and needed physical intervention to stop.

Hardware protects itself. The logic is baked into certification: a BCI that could be locked by software could be locked by hostile software, and hostile software was a category that the 2218 standards body had found intolerable. The Conduct series had a mechanical failsafe: a physical shock to the housing would trigger the reset routine and put the wearer into a five-second proprioceptive blank.

Rook had his left arm coming up for a guard, so his left side was closed, which meant Kyle had to work the right, which was the longer route but not by much.

Silence came off the hip for the first time.

Not to cut — to use the pommel, which weighed 340 grams and had a striking radius Kyle had measured through eleven years of arguments with people's equipment. The pommel found the BCI housing at the exact angle the array had been pricing, and Rook blinked twice, stepped left when he'd intended right, put his hand out and found the laundromat door instead of Kyle, and sat down with his back to it, working through the reset sequence with his eyes half-focused on a wall that wasn't cooperating.

"Five seconds," Kyle told him. "You're fine."

"Yeah," Rook said, from somewhere inside his own reset. "I know."

Silence went back. The rib made its opinion known about the draw-and-replace motion. Kyle filed the opinion and moved.

[Beat 4]
Nines was the one he'd flagged last, which was Nines's job — industrial wrist brace, pneumatic assist, the kind the freight yards used for sustained-load operators and the kind that developed its own opinions about direction once the pneumatic channel pressurized wrong. The brace locked along a single axis to distribute load, which meant that if you could intercept the lock while the pneumatic was fighting the frame, the brace's own assist force would do the work for you.

The anchor position had held through the drone discharge and both entries, which was professional. Nines waited until Kyle was dealing with the right side and then came across the street at a pace that accounted for rain, which was careful, and led with the braced arm, which was honest.

Kyle let him commit the extension, took the inside line, and used Nines's own weight to put the braced arm behind Nines's back. The pneumatic fought the frame, the frame locked, and Nines did a complete inventory of the street's surface in about four seconds while Kyle held the brace at the angle that kept the joint disagreeing with itself.

"Locked out," Kyle said, releasing. The brace cycled and freed. Nines sat with it a moment, one knee on the wet pavement, and did not say anything, which was the professional response.

The drone had gone for altitude. The rooftop position hadn't moved, because the rooftop position was the override and knew when it wasn't needed. Somewhere behind Kyle, the sixth position — the runner — was having the simplest task on the street: waiting to carry the news. Kyle put his back to the runner deliberately, which was the correct signal.

You've earned the exit. Take it.

He heard Dex's footsteps going east at a run, the way a young man runs when he's been given the best part of any job, which is the part where he gets to say what happened.

[Beat 5]
The thermal window on the third floor opened at 02:23.

Mira came down, not from the building's front door but from the fire access on the east side, and she came alone, which meant she'd watched the whole arc and decided it was over, which meant her rubric was complete. She was forty meters out when Kyle first put her properly into the read: medium height, operational gear that was functional first and second and didn't bother with a third priority, a carry stance that said the piece on her hip was neither a prop nor an afterthought.

She stopped at eight meters. Standard conversation distance for someone who wasn't trying to demonstrate indifference to it.

"Kinetix's lockout is contractor-certified," she said, without preamble, because she had been watching and her rubric included what had been said. "The arm costs eleven thousand two hundred Φ with the augment array."

"I know the price," Kyle said.

"I know you know." Her voice had the tightness of someone updating a rubric that hadn't expected to need updating, not the tightness of distress. Those were different things and the distinction mattered. "Rook's port was a Meridian Conduct first-series, they issued the mechanical-reset advisory in 2221, he's been meaning to get the housing seated deeper."

"Add the seating charge to the bill."

She looked at him for a long moment the way a person looks at something that's behaving slightly differently than the model predicted.

Then she drew and fired. One round, eight meters, into the upper chest, because she had watched the corridor reports the same way any professional watched someone's recent work, and she knew what was there and what it would cost.

The mesh took it. The percussion distributed across the upper thorax in the way of a very decisive conversation with a door — full-contact, brief, leaving both parties informed. Kyle took three breaths that were more deliberate than usual. The rib weighed in at volume.

Mira watched him keep walking, one step, two, the stride unchanged, and she dropped the weapon against her thigh and then lowered it to the wet pavement. She left it there, muzzle toward the drain.

"That was an audition," she said.

"I worked that out," Kyle said.

"Cacophony has five rounds. You didn't pull it."

"The audition was for the blade and the body. Five rounds, zero fired. I'll note it for the record."

She almost smiled. It didn't quite complete. "Freelance roster, West Town and adjacent. Lotus-territory contracts through your fixer, who I assume is the Húlijing outfit. Carrier rates, standard."

"No further crews."

"No further crews," she agreed, and that was the one line that meant they were actually negotiating and not just reciting terms. She had the authority to offer it and she'd been expecting him to ask. "The arrangement is provisional, sixty-day reassessment. Standard for new roster."

"Your crew's hardware goes on Lotus's account," Kyle said. "The arm, the port, the brace. List price. You billed them to an audition; they walk home whole or there's no arrangement."

The tightness in her voice made one small adjustment. Not surprise — recalculation.

"I can authorize the arm," she said. "The port and the brace I need to clear."

"Clear them. Tonight. I'll be at Chen's at seven-thirty for the confirmation."

"They'd have shot you for free, you know," Mira said, and that was the one human line, the one that wasn't rubric, the one she'd been carrying through the whole arc of the bracket and had decided she was allowed to say now that the terms were closing.

"I know," Kyle said. "Tell Lotus their hardware is better than their audit of its own vulnerabilities. That's a courtesy, not an invoice."

She reached into the inner pocket of her coat and set the roster marker on the planter rim at her right: a lacquered oval, deep red with a gold five-petal blossom pressed into its surface, the size of a thumb, and heavy, heavier than a thing that size had any reason to be. The weight of a formal thing. Kyle picked it up.

"Any Lotus counter in the city seats you," Mira said, back on professional ground, the human line filed and done. "You pay carrier rates like everyone on the roster. The bowl's better than the politics."

"It usually is."

She retrieved her piece from the wet pavement, cleared and holstered it, and turned to go. Three steps. Then, over her shoulder, into the rain, without turning back:

"The Narrows desk bought your file last year, freelancer. You should ask the stationer who ELSE subscribes."

Her footsteps went north and the bracket dissolved: Rook walking his reset out in a slow circle, KT flexing the arm through its range and swearing quietly at the manufacturer's specifications, Nines working the brace through two full cycles to confirm the pneumatic had reset. Professionals filing their after-action. Kyle stood on the wet cross-street with the lacquered marker in one hand and a bruise over his upper thorax that was going to have a great deal to say over the next three days and watched them all go.

The turret cat on the window ledge had not moved. It had the institutional patience of something that had been watching long before Kyle arrived and would be watching long after he left.

WIN: a third of the city formally reopened, carrier rates, one lacquered marker that weighs more than it should. COST: sixty days on a reassessment clock, one mesh bruise, and a name in the Narrows' subscription list that he is going to have to do something about. OUTSTANDING: confirm hardware authorization, seven-thirty, Chen's counter.

He took the long way home.

[Beat 6]
At 02:41, between the Pulse strut and the laundry on Damen, his terminal chirped. The work one.

CONTRACT UPDATE — ASSET SCHEDULE, SUBJECT: KYLE.
CLIENT HAS NOTED THE EXECUTION OF THE LOTUS ROSTER ARRANGEMENT, EFFECTIVE 02:23.
CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE TERMS.
CLIENT HAS APPENDED THE MARKER TO THE SUBJECT'S ASSET SCHEDULE.
VALUATION: ONE BOWL, RECURRING.
ADDENDUM: THE AUDITION CREW'S MEDICAL CLAIM (ARM, PORT, BRACE) HAS BEEN FILED WITH LOTUS ACCOUNTS.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT ADVISES THAT "RECURRING" IS THE LOAD-BEARING WORD.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT ALSO NOTES THE RIB. THE RIB HAS BEEN NOTED SINCE DAY ONE.

Kyle read the addenda in the rain, the marker in his coat pocket against the old folded plate cover he still carried from the alley, one rectangle of lacquer and one of steel, two different proofs that the city occasionally paid what it owed.

He typed into the amendment field, which he was doing more often:

NOTED ABOUT THE RIB. MIND YOUR OWN ARCHITECTURE.

The reply came back in six seconds.

ACKNOWLEDGED.
GRATUITY WITHHELD — ITEMIZED: IMPERTINENCE.

He put the terminal away and walked the last four blocks with the Pulse hum coming up through the concrete and the turret cats watching from their ledges and the rain making its arrangements with the city's lights, and thought about the Narrows desk, and about a man with half-glasses and clean hands and a drawer marked CLIENTS, UNSOURCED, and about the fact that there was now a second subscriber.

The rib had opinions about the pace. Kyle let it talk.

That was tomorrow's problem.
