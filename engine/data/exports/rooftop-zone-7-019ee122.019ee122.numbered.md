The relay drop was on the third floor of a building that had once held a shipping broker's annex, before the Zone 7 consolidation folded the brokers into a single tower and left their old offices to whoever wanted them. Seto had found it eight months ago. The deadpoint was a function of position: at street level the signals from the Kessler mesh tower and the corrugated roof of the loading bay two addresses over ran into each other and degraded anything time-sensitive. Two floors up, the building stepped back from the loading bay's shadow and the signal resolved.

He came up the stairwell at 18:09 and crossed through the access door onto the utility walkway. He clipped his receiver to the parapet edge and waited. The last package through this relay had been Amara's ΓÇö she'd checked the architecture before letting anything sensitive move through it, the kind of question that told him what kind of operator she was.

The light came in low and amber off the mesh towers, the kind of industrial light that made freight yards beautiful. Between Zone 6 to the west and Zone 8's administrative tower blinking north, the Zone 7 transit-corridor Seam ran three square kilometers of relay towers, repair shops, and canteen windows that belonged to nobody and worked for everybody. From up here the air thinned at the boundary lines where two CorpoNations stopped managing their edges. Neither one's filtered climate reached the gap, and you breathed the actual city: diesel particulate, the iron smell of rain on hot pavement, the chemical undertone of whatever the building's ventilation was losing to. The city the arcology feeds showed only as disaster, between curated segments, with nothing you could smell.

Sixty-three seconds. The same as always.

He pulled the data through, confirmed the relay handshake, and was writing the timestamp into his log when the door behind him opened.

He had heard the latch. He did not turn at once. The footsteps told him it was one person, moving without effort to be quiet, which meant they were not trying to be quiet, which meant they wanted him to hear. He turned.

Ekow Ato came out from behind the HVAC housing the way a man comes through a door he has already decided to come through. Not fast, not slow, hands at his sides and open. The housing was the only cover on the walkway, and it sat at the angle that held both the access door and the parapet in view. He had been there before Seto climbed the stairs. He had let Seto finish the work and then chosen his moment.

That was information.

Seto had heard the name from two people on the Gray Zone circuit. NGRA veteran, Northern Ghana Republic. Freelance contractor, not muscle ΓÇö the distinction mattered. The machete on his hip had a handle worn smooth with use and nothing decorative on it.

Two Seam professionals conducting a professional interaction, and they both knew the choreography: open hands, the angle, the wait. Ekow had worked environments where the negotiation before contact mattered more than anything the contact accomplished. So had Seto. Neither performed. The machete stayed at the hip and the hands stayed open, and that was the vocabulary they were both reading.

"You've been pulling correlations from GLMZ health data against private courier records," Ekow said. He said it the way a man states a logistics problem, not an accusation ΓÇö a known position. "Walk away from the Orison work."

Seto filed it. "What is the Orison work."

Not a denial. An actual question.

Ekow looked at him for a moment. His chin came up a fraction ΓÇö not suspicion, reassessment. He had expected a denial, and Seto had given him a gap. Now he was deciding what to put in it.

"Proprietary neuretic data," Ekow said. "Stolen. Moving through the Gray Zone courier network." A pause. "Through you, specifically."

Seto thought about the correlations he had been running ΓÇö health-record gaps, calibration-visit clusters, dead data corridors in the crescent adjacent to Orison's service footprint. He thought about the relay drop he had just completed, a timing handshake for a package whose contents he had not touched in eleven days.

"I'm not moving anything," he said. "I've been mapping silence ΓÇö information that used to flow and doesn't anymore. Specific corridors. Specific knowledge." He let it land. "What exactly did they tell you was stolen."

Ekow did not answer at once. He looked at the receiver still clipped to the parapet, the open log on the handheld, and the way none of it looked like anything other than what it was: a courier running a signal check on a route he had run for eight months. A professional reading a room he had already read once and finding it different the second time.

"Gray Zone intermediary," he said. "I didn't ask the chain."

"You should."

It came out flat, not as a judgment. It was the advice Seto would give anyone. If you didn't run the chain yourself, you were working on someone else's premise, and someone else's premise meant someone else's risk assessment and someone else's interest in the outcome.

Ekow reached into his jacket, slow and telegraphed, and Seto tracked the hand without moving. What came out was a white handkerchief, folded in quarters. Ekow held it without looking at it and turned it once between his fingers, not unfolding it, only the motion of something his hands knew how to do while the rest of him thought.

Seto waited. He did not interpret the handkerchief.

"I have seven days left on this contract," Ekow said. He was looking at the skyline now, toward the mesh tower on Kessler. "Make it easy for me. Don't be where I can find you for seven days."

He put the handkerchief back in his jacket ΓÇö one look at it before it went in, the folded white square, then done ΓÇö walked to the access door without looking at the receiver still clipped to the parapet, and stepped through. The latch caught behind him. His footsteps went down the stairwell at the same pace as before, and Seto listened until he could not hear them.

He stood on the walkway and did not move.

He went to the parapet edge and gripped the rail. Below, a truck was pulling out of the loading dock, running lights on, moving slowly east.

Seven days. He could reroute four stops, respace the Zone 7 schedule, push his relay points west for a week. He had done longer gaps on less notice.

*Walk away from the Orison work* meant Orison knew someone was correlating ΓÇö not what had been found. If they'd known that, they would have sent something with a different mandate than a seven-day window. Seven days was a suppression budget, not a resolution budget: let it go cold, move the problem to another quadrant. The data he already had was not part of the deal.

He pulled up the channel.

*Someone's watching the Orison queries. I need to know how much you've accessed through the Cellvault system.*

A pause ΓÇö not hesitation, calculation. Then she sent the scope ΓÇö text only, no tags, the way she relayed data ΓÇö and he read it.

His hand came off the parapet rail.

The scope was larger than anything he had given her material for. Amara had pulled correlations he had not asked for, extended the pattern into sub-records he had not queried, built a picture more complete than what he had assembled across eight months of route work. Not reckless ΓÇö thorough, the kind of thorough that doesn't know yet it's also a flag. She had gone deeper than the ask without adjusting her exposure profile, the way someone works when they're treating a problem from the outside when they're already in it. That was what his body registered. Not the professional liability. The distance.

He set down the professional piece. The distance didn't go with it.

*How long until your next maintenance visit at the Zone 5 facility.*

A brief pause.

*Eight days.*

Seven days on Ekow's contract. Eight days until Amara's visit. One day of margin.

He looked at the skyline. The mesh tower on Kessler carried a service indicator near the top, amber, not red. On the next street a truck sat at the loading dock with its running lights on, the driver waiting on something that was not ready yet. The amber indicator blinked.

*Move your schedule,* he sent. *Push it by two weeks.*

He did not explain. He let the instruction sit. The driver got out of the truck, stretched, walked to the dock door, and knocked twice.

She had not answered yet. He kept the channel open.

Seto picked up his kit and went back through the access door.

The repair shop two doors east had its roller door up, and the smell of hot solder reached him on the evening air, clean and specific, the smell of work being done by someone who knew how to do it. Zone 7 Seam at 18:30, shift change: freight workers heading for the Pulse or the grid, their day finished and filed, night operators arriving with their thermoses and the lunches folded in cloth bags they had carried for years. Nobody in a hurry. Nobody without a destination. He got on the bicycle and joined it.
