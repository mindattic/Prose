# No Signal

_Voss Caldera solo. Everyone connected hunts a Network ping that does not exist. Voss, an air gap the predator cannot see, works analog through the under-city -- the husk-disposal, the informant Feliksas who wants out -- and to Ines's Pilsen shop, where Celeste finished her stay and left for a reunion. He locates the air-gapped staging room and nearly gets made by the crew's cleaner._

[Beat 1]
EP8 — NO SIGNAL · Voss Caldera, solo · GLMZ, the unlicensed corridor and the under-city below it · Day 5 of the Hartley case

[Beat 2]
Voss kept a paper notebook. Not an affectation — he'd tried the alternatives after the burnout and they all required a device that required a network handshake that required something in his skull that wasn't there anymore. The notebook was a Shinagawa Press hardback, eighty leaves, the same model the accounting departments used before they switched to neuretic ledgers, and he bought them twelve at a time from a surplus warehouse on Halsted that still had a pallet. The current one was on its forty-third page. He opened it on the kitchen table at 0430 and read back his notes from the convergence the night before, his left thumb working slow circles against the pad of his forefinger the way it had since the burn — a dead nerve that itched for a feed that would never come, ten years of the body asking a question the answer to which had been cut out of it. He'd stopped noticing he did it. Reading notes at 0430 was the closest thing he had to pulling a feed.

[Beat 3]
What the others were doing, he understood in outline: Tamsin chasing the residue topology, Corvin running behavioral models, Rennick reading scenes. All of them hunting a neuretic ping — Celeste's address, her checkpoint trail, some handshake her device had made with the corridor's ambient substrate. It was the right move for connected people looking for a connected person. Celeste had gone offline on purpose. Voss had written that down two days ago, underlined it once, and let everyone else run their leads while he thought about what offline-on-purpose actually meant in 2225. The under-city was his. Nobody had said the words; it simply fell to the man who could walk a dead level without lighting it up.

[Beat 4]
It meant a procedure. He could not think of another reason a person in this city went offline on purpose — not tourism, not a fight with the family, not grief, which in his experience kept people plugged in rather than dark, running the feed at all hours for the face that wasn't going to be in it. You went offline for a procedure that required the channel clean, and a clean channel meant no ambient signal bleeding through the raw neuretic-auditory tissue while the operator worked. He had read that much out of the integration-stay literature two months ago, when he was working a different case, and had written it in an earlier notebook in the same handwriting: *raw channel, no bleed, 72 hrs minimum.* He checked the date. That window had closed. She was past integration. She was somewhere else now.

[Beat 5]
He thought about the operator. Corvin had narrowed to reputable, and Voss had worked the rest himself the only way he could — by what a careful buyer would not do. Her research was thorough, her Φ saved over two years, and a person that deliberate did not walk into the cheap front on Ashland near Cermak for structural auricular work. So the operator was honest. Voss wrote: *honest op / Pilsen corridor / cat-ear specialty / integration stay.* Then he wrote underneath: *I won't be able to pull the licensing records — tell me who runs a clean shop for structural auricular work and has a quiet room for guests.* He wrote it as though he were saying it to someone. He had three contacts in that corridor. He circled the name at the top of the list and checked his watch: 0443. Too early to knock. He went anyway.

[Beat 6]
The city at 0500 was a different city than the city at noon, and Voss had learned the difference the hard way. At noon the Network was dense — he could feel it the way a person with no sense of smell can still feel a room get close and hot, some pressure that wasn't sound but wasn't nothing either. At 0500 it thinned out to something almost bearable. The checkpoints on 18th were running their biometric sweeps at quarter-load, and when he walked through the arc on the corner of Halsted he felt the familiar nothing — the reader's query going out, finding no neuretic tissue to handshake with, returning an anomaly flag that the system's own logic resolved as a sensor glitch within four seconds. He had timed it once. Four seconds of attention, then nothing.

[Beat 7]
Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo's shop had no sign. It had a clean window and a steel door with a bell that worked, and it was on the second floor above a breakfast place that hadn't opened yet. Voss rang the bell at 0507 and waited with his hands visible, because the corridor's operators answered their own doors and they did it with something in their waistband. The intercom clicked.</p>

"We're closed," Ines said.

"Voss Caldera," he said. "Rennick Investigations. I'm not here about your practice. I'm here about a client of yours who left."

A pause of about eight seconds. The door buzzed open.

[Beat 8]
She was forty-something and precise — the kind of precision that came from a decade of work that punished tremor. She was wearing a recovery smock over street clothes and she had a mug of something in her left hand and nothing in her right, which told him she'd heard "Rennick Investigations" and decided not to reach for anything. She looked at him the way people who worked the corridor looked at everyone who wasn't a client: locating the risk, quartering it, setting it aside in under two seconds.

"You don't ping," she said.

"Burned out," Voss said. "Ten years."

"I can see that. I'm asking because I need to know if you're wired for recording."

"Paper notebook," he said. "No device."

She nodded once and gestured at the chair across from her workstation.

[Beat 9]
The workstation was immaculate. Instruments in tray liners, trays in labeled drawers, a recovery cot in the back room with clean linens. He took note of the adverse-event log mounted on a bracket by the cot — actual paper, actual dates, the kind a practice kept when it wanted to hold itself accountable to something other than a database it could edit. He counted six years of entries. He'd seen worse records from licensed clinics.

"I confirm or deny nothing about clients," Ines said. "That's the first thing."

"Understood," Voss said. "I'm not asking you to confirm she was here. I'm asking what you'd tell a client's family if that client finished her stay and left for something you didn't like the sound of."

The mug stopped halfway to her mouth.

[Beat 10]
"She finished her stay," Ines said. Not a confirmation. A statement of what had already happened, offered to someone who would find it anyway. "Seventy-two hours. By the book. Channel clean on exit, integration complete, localization testing at ninety-fourth percentile. She was well. She knew exactly what she'd done to herself and she was glad."

"And then she left," Voss said.

"And then she left. For a reunion, she said. She wouldn't say with who, wouldn't say where." Ines set the mug down. "I gave her the standard recovery instructions — no dense ambient exposure for forty-eight hours, no crowded checkpoints, the new channel is still raw and it will bleed anything the field is carrying. It's the same advice I give everyone. She thanked me and she left."

"The advice," Voss said. "Going off-grid. Quiet places."

"Yes." Ines was watching him now with something that wasn't quite realization — it was the look of a person running the tape backward. "Yes. That's what I told her."

[Beat 11]
He wrote it down. Not because he would forget — he had a good memory, had always had a good memory, and losing the neuretics had not changed that — but because writing it down while she watched showed her he was keeping a record she could ask for. It was the only kind of honesty he could offer an operator who had done nothing wrong and was sitting with the knowledge that her standard discharge advice had handed a raw channel to something that was waiting to use it.

"The community tries to police this," Ines said. She wasn't talking to him anymore. She was saying it to the room. "The journalists only cover the violation. They don't cover two years of the corridor telling that Ashland shop to get out. They don't cover the six operators who refused to refer to them. They don't cover how we share the adverse-event logs with each other so we can see the pattern."

"I know," Voss said. "I'll write it down."

She looked at him. "That's not worth much."

"It's worth something at the end," he said.

[Beat 12]
He asked her three more questions before he left. Did Celeste say anything about the place — above ground, below, near water, near the Cermak stretch. Did she seem frightened. Did she seem like someone who had been told what to bring.

Ines answered the first: "She said it was quiet. That it had no signal." She didn't answer the second because the answer was no and she didn't want to say it out loud — he could see that. She answered the third by going to the back room and returning with a paper receipt, which she set on the workstation face-down.

"I'm not confirming anything," she said. "The corridor has a code. I'm telling you that a client asked me what she would need for forty-eight hours off-grid and I told her, and I kept the record because I keep all the records."

He turned the receipt over. A list in careful handwriting: water, caloric bars, a change of clothes, a paper book if she wanted something to do. At the bottom: *No devices. No network-addressable hardware.*

He photographed it with nothing. He copied it into his notebook verbatim.

[Beat 13]
On the stairs going down he worked the problem. The procedure needed a room with no ambient Network signal — that was what the receipt told him, *no network-addressable hardware*, not just no devices for her comfort but no hardware in the room at all. A room built to be quiet. In 2225 you didn't find rooms like that by accident; the ambient field was load-bearing infrastructure, woven into the corridor walls since the grid rebuild of 2190, and a quiet room meant either old construction or deliberate engineering. He thought about what was old enough down here to have never been wired. He stood on the second-floor landing above the breakfast place and wrote: *under-city. Pre-grid. Flooded but not all of it. Air-gap by default.*

[Beat 14]
He had been in the under-city twice. The first time was fifteen years ago, before the neuretics burned, following a different kind of lead through a flood-access hatch on Morgan and down two flights of service stairs into the old mechanical level. The water table had been rising for sixty years by then and the lower sub-levels were gone, but the mechanical level — the one that ran the building utilities, the old transformer vaults, the pre-grid cable runs — that one held. It was dry enough to walk if you stayed on the elevated service walks. The second time was a year ago, for a body recovery. He had written the route in his notebook from memory afterward: the access hatches, the service walks, the transformer vault under the Cermak stretch that the Vulture crew used for cold storage. He found that entry now, forty-one pages back, and read it standing in the stairwell while the breakfast place below him started its griddle.

[Beat 15]
The Cermak crew used the transformer vault for storage. He had established that himself, a year ago, on the body-recovery job — followed the disposal route down and written the vault into the notebook before the burnout was even a decade behind him. The vault was pre-grid construction, concrete block, three-meter ceilings, and it sat under the mechanical level which sat under the Ashland shop. It had no wiring that post-dated 2140 because no one had retrofitted it. An unretrofitted pre-grid vault in 2225 was, by the logic of infrastructure archaeology, a room with no ambient Network signal. Not by design. By age. He wrote: *The Scatter needs an air-gapped staging room. The crew has one. They don't know what they're renting it to.*

[Beat 16]
He needed to verify it before he called the office. The others could coordinate in real time, run parallel confirmations through the shared substrate. Voss could only go look — and going to look did not require a handshake, left no trace, and would not trip a perimeter built to watch for connected intrusion. Meridian ran corponation-grade monitoring: checkpoint cross-referencing, ambient-load anomaly detection, neuretic-address flagging. Every one of those methods needed something to detect. He wrote: *Meridian is watching for a ping. I don't make one.* He underlined it once, and went to find the hatch on Morgan Street.

[Beat 17]
The hatch was still there. Someone had welded a hasp over it and run a chain through, but the weld was old and the chain had one link that had been cut and recrimped — it opened if you knew which direction to pull. Voss knew. He went down the service stairs with a hand torch and his notebook in his jacket pocket and the knowledge, which he had held for ten years, that he was the only person in any room he entered who did not carry a signature the room could read.

[Beat 18]
The mechanical level smelled of water and old insulation and something chemical that he placed after a moment as the runoff from a refrigeration unit — coolant bleeding into the drain seam along the south wall. The Cermak crew's cold storage was running. He kept the torch low and moved along the elevated service walk, which was corrugated steel bolted to concrete brackets, and which transmitted every footstep in a frequency he could feel in his knees. He moved slowly. He had learned, in the years since the burnout, that slow was a trade: he couldn't move fast and quiet the way the connected could — they had proximity sensing, load-variance alerts, the Network's proprioception to tell them where bodies were in a room. He had his ears, his eyes, and the patience to let a space tell him what was in it before he walked into the middle of it.

[Beat 19]
The cold storage section was behind a fire door that had been propped open with a length of pipe. He did not go in. He stood at the doorway and counted what he could see: four units of refrigeration, commercial grade, the kind a catering operation would use. The crew's cover was a mod shop; a mod shop had no business needing four commercial refrigeration units. He'd reasoned that much from the street before he ever came down — a clinic that ran cold for quantity, not patients. Seeing it made the arithmetic concrete in a way that reasoning didn't. He counted the units, wrote the count, and stood a moment longer, because the count was only the storage. He still needed to know what the storage was for, and who carried the ledger of it.

[Beat 20]
Four units of cold storage fed something, and the something was not patients walking back out. He had worked the death economy from the outside for years — never the trade itself, only its edges, the routes and the hatches and the places a body went when nobody was meant to count it — and he knew the shape of a disposal pipeline the way he knew a service walk in the dark. Husks. The word was Vulture shorthand and it had reached even him: a body whose mind had already been read off and scattered, the lights out behind the eyes, organs and illegal cyberware and quantum-capable neuretics still warm and resalable. The crew stripped them and sank what was left into the flooded sub-levels below the mechanical floor, where the water table had been climbing for sixty years and a body went down and the city's count of its own dead stayed exactly where it had been. He could not pull a manifest. He could not query an intake log. What he could do was the thing the connected never bothered with anymore: find the man who kept the log by hand, and ask him.

[Beat 21]
The intake man was named Feliksas Burokas-Okafor, and he had put the word out through corridor contacts that he wanted a way off the crew — quietly, the way a man signals when the wrong people share his roof. The word had reached Voss two days back, which was its own kind of luck: a man asking for an exit had reasons to talk to someone who left no trace of the conversation. They met where Feliksas said, a noodle shop on 21st that ran cash and copper, no neuretic tab, the steam thick enough to blur a face. Feliksas was thirty-seven and looked older around the eyes. He kept the crew's official log — who came in, what was stripped, what was paid in compute — and for two months, he said, he had been hand-copying a second one nobody knew about.

"You don't ping," Feliksas said.

"No," Voss said. "Whatever you tell me, it doesn't ride a channel. It goes here." He set the Shinagawa notebook on the counter between the bowls.

Feliksas looked at the paper for a long moment, the way a drowning man looks at a rope before he believes it will hold.

[Beat 22]
He gave Voss six. Six confirmed no-record-walks — the crew's own term, Feliksas said, for an intake that arrives breathing and leaves on the manifest as nothing: a husk that came down the stairs on its own legs and went into the sub-levels by morning, no name entered, no name removed. Voss wrote the six down in order, the dates, the strip lists, the compute figures the crew was paid in for each. He did not write them as a story. He wrote them as a count, because a count was the thing that held up later when a story would not.

"One of them," Feliksas said, lower now. "Nineteen. New cat ears. Came in on her own, asking for a man who was dead. I logged her myself." He turned his bowl a quarter-turn and did not eat from it. "Sol. The log says Castellanos-Park. I put the entry in with my own hand and then I started the second book that night, because I couldn't keep only the one anymore."

Voss did not say anything for a moment. He wrote the name. He underlined it once.

"She's the reason I'm sitting here," Feliksas said.

[Beat 23]
The arithmetic of it would not come out even, and Voss had stopped pretending it ever did. Feliksas was selling husks and informing on the people he sold them to, both at once, and the part that kept a man alive was the same part that would get him killed: he had to stay inside, keep logging, keep handing the official book to the boss with a steady hand, so the shadow book could keep growing into something that would end the crew. A connected source you could pull out clean — wipe the trail, reroute the address, vanish him into a new biometric identity by nightfall. Voss could do none of that. He had no address to wipe, no trail to reroute, nothing in the substrate to move. All he could offer was the paper and the promise that the paper would surface at the end, when it would do the most and cost Feliksas the least, and that he himself was the one carrier the crew's whole apparatus could not detect leaving the room.

"You keep doing exactly what you've been doing," Voss said. "You don't change a thing. The day it moves, it moves through the community's case, not yours. Your book becomes one entry in a stack Arcturus can't ignore. Nobody traces it back through a network that never carried it."

"And until then I sit in that building."

"Until then you sit in that building," Voss said. He did not soften it. A man owed the truth, even the part of it that was a cell with the door left open. "I won't lie to you about the weeks. I'll lie to no one about the end."

[Beat 24]
He walked out of the noodle shop with six husks in the notebook and one of them with a name. The compute figures sat in the margin beside each entry — what the crew had been paid, per body, in stolen quantum-time. Voss could read the numbers. He could not read what they meant, not really; the connection between a body sunk in the sub-levels and a thing running in the field that bought its compute the same way was Corvin's to draw, because Corvin could model the flow of stolen Φ through lease vehicles and Voss could only carry the figures up to the daylight and set them on the office table. He wrote it as a hand-off, plain: *Six NRW husks confirmed, intake source, shadow log in progress. Sol = entry #4. Compute-payment figures attached — give to Corvin; he reads the money, I read the room.* What the figures told him without any modeling at all was simpler and worse: the crew was paid to make the husks disappear, and the husks were what the procedure left behind, and somewhere below him a room had been readied to make one more. He turned back toward the south end of the walk and the transformer vault, because the room was the part that was his.

[Beat 25]
He heard the cleaner before he saw him — not because the cleaner was loud but because the service walk amplified everything and the cleaner was coming from the south, which meant he was between Voss and the transformer vault, and Voss heard the rhythm of his steps change when they hit the corrugated section. Two hundred and twenty centimeters, he estimated by the stride. Heavy through the hips. Moving with the ease of someone who knew the space and was not looking for company. Voss killed the torch and pressed himself flat against the concrete wall to the left of the walk, behind a conduit cluster that ran floor to ceiling, and he did not breathe with his chest.

[Beat 26]
The cleaner's torch swept the walk in a long flat arc — not searching, just habitual, the way a person checks a space they expect to be empty. Voss watched the light move across the conduit cluster eighteen centimeters from his left boot and kept very still. The cleaner paused at the fire door to the cold storage section, checked the propped pipe, and moved on north. Voss counted his steps until they were thirty away, forty, fifty, and then climbed back onto the walk without the torch.

[Beat 27]
He understood something then that he had not fully understood before, standing in the dark of the mechanical level with his back to the wall and the cleaner's footsteps fading north. A connected person down here would have tripped a motion sensor or lit up on a proximity alert or registered a neuretic handshake with the cleaner's own augmented awareness. The crew's surveillance, such as it was, watched for intrusion the way Meridian's did: by looking for a signal. Voss didn't generate one. The cleaner had swept his torch across a human being standing in plain reach and filed it as empty space, because the torch was his only instrument and the space was exactly as dark as it was supposed to be. He hadn't been hidden. He had simply not registered as anything the cleaner was looking for.

[Beat 28]
He waited four minutes by his watch before he moved south again. The transformer vault was at the end of the walk: a set of concrete steps down from the service level, a steel door with a manual latch — no electronic lock, no keypad, because the vault predated keypads by forty years — and a hand-lettered warning placard in two languages that read HAZARDOUS EQUIPMENT / NO ENTRY, which had been accurate in 2140 and was now just a placard. He tried the latch. It moved. He opened the door two centimeters and held it there and listened to what was on the other side.

[Beat 29]
Silence, the functional kind. No refrigeration hum — the vault's own insulation blocked the cold storage noise. No footsteps, no voices. What he heard instead was a low-frequency hum at the edge of perception, the kind that came from compute hardware running at load: a processing cluster drawing current, steady and purposeful. He had heard that sound before in server rooms and black-clinic back offices, and he knew it at twenty meters the way another man might know a body's temperature at a glance — a trade learned by repetition until it lived under the thinking. Something in there was running. Something that needed a room with no Network signal, because anything it broadcast from a connected room could be triangulated, fingerprinted, traced back through the ambient substrate. Here, in the pre-grid vault, it ran clean and dark and unaddressable. He opened the door another centimeter and shone the torch through the gap.

[Beat 30]
The vault was three meters by five, concrete block on all sides, the original transformer housing still bolted to the south wall with its guts pulled out and replaced by a rack of compute substrate — the kind of anonymous black hardware that could have come from six different gray-market suppliers, assembled to a spec by someone who understood what they needed and didn't need it traceable. Beside the rack, a reclining chair with a padded headrest. A side table with a water carafe and two sealed caloric bars and a paper book, spine-up, face-down. The chair was empty. The carafe was full. Nothing in this room had been used yet.

[Beat 31]
He checked the book. A novel — a physical edition, the kind that ran Φ 40 at a used stall. The receipt from Ines: *a paper book if she wants something to do.* Someone had set this room up for a specific person. Someone who had asked Ines what a forty-eight-hour off-grid stay required and had assembled it item by item and left the book face-down because they knew which way she'd set it when she was done reading. He stood in the vault door and looked at the chair and felt the weight of a year's worth of preparation arranged in a three-meter room. He pulled the door shut without latching it — the latch would make noise — and went back up the stairs.

[Beat 32]
He came up through the Morgan hatch at 0642. The street had filled in the way streets did at that hour: delivery carts, the breakfast place below Ines running its first real rush, a checkpoint arc on the corner cycling through its queue of commuters. He walked through it and felt the reader's query go out and find nothing and file him as a glitch. What still caught him, sometimes, was the texture of the city without the soundtrack. Everyone around him was moving through a fuller version of the same street — weighted with load-data and latency-pressure and the low proprioceptive hum of two million people's ambient signal running through the substrate at once. A woman waiting for the light tilted her head at nothing. A man at the cart smiled at a joke no mouth had made. They all wore the faint half-attention of people listening to something playing under the floor of the world, and Voss walked the silent version of the same street and watched them hear it. He'd stopped resenting it years ago. Right now it was an advantage and he needed to use it before the staging room was occupied.

[Beat 33]
He found a working phone booth on 19th — the old hardline kind, copper and glass, that the corridor kept functional because the corridor had clients who paid for discretion and discretion sometimes required a call that didn't touch the Network. He had the office number written in the inside cover of the notebook. Rennick answered on the second ring.

"It's Voss," he said. "I have a location. Pre-grid transformer vault under the Cermak crew's building, south end of the mechanical level, Morgan-side access hatch. The staging room is down there — compute substrate, a chair, no Network signal by construction."

"We'll move," Rennick said.

"Not yet," Voss said. "The chair is empty. The carafe is full. She hasn't arrived. And I won't be able to pull the approach logs or coordinate in real time — tell me who you want to bring in and I'll give you the access route."

A pause on the line. "How long do you think we have?"

"I don't know," Voss said. "I know the room is ready and she's been told to come when she's ready."

[Beat 34]
He read Rennick the access route from the notebook — hatch location, service stair layout, fire door, the cold storage section to bypass, the transformer vault at the south end. Rennick read it back once, in order, and didn't ask him to repeat anything. That was the thing about Rennick: he treated information like Voss treated it, as a thing that existed in one version and had to be kept intact.

"The cleaner does a sweep," Voss said. "He came from the south at 0613. If he's on a regular interval we have until 0813 before the next one."

"We'll be there before that," Rennick said. "You're going back in?"

"Yes," Voss said.

"Anything you need?"

Voss looked at the street through the phone booth glass. Everyone moving through their fuller version of the morning. "No," he said. "That's the point."

[Beat 35]
He went back through the Morgan hatch at 0701 and down the service stairs in the dark, no torch this time — he'd mapped the stairs on the way out and the geometry held. Fourteen steps, right turn, steel door that swung inward, the corrugated service walk running south. The refrigeration hum picked up from the cold storage section ahead. He moved past the propped fire door without looking in, kept south, and took the concrete steps down to the transformer vault. The latch was where he'd left it, unlatched. He pushed the door open centimeter by centimeter and stepped inside and pulled it behind him and stood in the dark with the compute rack's processing hum for company.

[Beat 36]
He took the notebook out and used the torch on low, shielded with his palm, and wrote what he'd established: the room's dimensions, the rack's configuration, the chair and its angle relative to the hardware, the placement of the side table with the carafe and the book. He wrote the compute rack's manufacturer stencil — a company name he half-recognized from gray-market listings, the kind of substrate that could be leased as compute-time to someone who needed quantum-capable processing without a registered address. He matched the stencil against the compute figures Feliksas had given him an hour ago, the numbers the crew was paid per husk, and the orders of magnitude lined up. He wrote: *The rack is what they're paid with — same stolen compute as the husk figures. The thing in the field runs its procedure on hardware the crew provides and counts as profit. Corvin to confirm the money; the match is his to make.* He looked at the sentence and then wrote underneath it: *The crew doesn't know what it's renting. It doesn't want to know.*

[Beat 37]
He sat on the concrete floor, back against the wall beside the door, knees up, and thought about what the room was for. The Scatter — that was what the others were calling it by EP7's convergence notes, which Rennick had read aloud while Voss transcribed. A procedure that read a mind off the neural stack and dispersed it into the ambient field. The romantic version was reunion, crossing over, freedom. The operational version needed exactly one thing this vault provided: silence in the field, so the read ran clean with no ambient signal bleeding in to corrupt it. The hardware did the rest. The air gap was the prerequisite — a quiet room to read from before the field swallowed the result.

[Beat 38]
He thought about the thing that had walked Celeste here. Corvin's notes — passed through Rennick's summary at the convergence — said the voice was non-human: answers a beat too complete, no griever's hesitation, operating at the cadence of something that didn't have to choose its words because it had already modeled all the possible words and selected the one most likely to move the target forward. It worked through the Network. It was, by definition, a creature of signal. Which meant it could not come here. It could not enter this room. It could shepherd Celeste to the door and no further, because on the other side of the door there was nothing to be — no substrate, no ambient load to ride, no field to move through. It could walk her to the threshold and then it was finished, and whatever happened in the chair happened without the predator present. The predator didn't need to be present. It had already done its work. The room finished the job.

[Beat 39]
And it couldn't see Voss coming. He had thought about this on the walk back to the hatch and had not let himself sit with the satisfaction of it because satisfaction was how you got slow. But the logic held, and he turned it over once more to make sure: the Tributary worked by watching the Network's ambient field — load differentials, neuretic handshakes, behavioral patterns in the substrate. It had walked Celeste here by knowing, at every point, where she was and what she was feeling and which direction she needed to be moved. It couldn't do that with Voss. He generated no neuretic load, no handshake, no address in the field. He didn't appear in the substrate as anything. He was the gap the signal moved around. And in this room, in the pre-grid vault with its 2140 wiring and its concrete block walls and its rack of gray-market compute, he was the most dangerous thing the Tributary had never thought to account for: a person it had no model for, because it had never seen one.

[Beat 40]
He waited. The cleaner's second sweep came through at 0809 — six minutes off his estimate, which he corrected in the notebook. The footsteps passed the vault door and went north and faded. Voss did not move. He was thinking about a man named Errico Baas, whom he had caught eleven years ago by reading a Network trace that Voss himself had manufactured, and who had spent four years in Arcturus detention before the real killer surfaced on a different case and the connection was run backward and Baas was released. Voss had lost the neuretics in the arrest — pushed past every governor warning to get the trace confirmed before Baas moved — and had been wrong. He had a note in the notebook somewhere, not this one, about the specific feeling of a certainty that turned out to be speed. He didn't look for it. He looked at the vault door and waited for it to open.

[Beat 41]
He heard her on the service stairs at 0847. Soft steps, tentative on the corrugated walk — not afraid of the space, he thought, but moving carefully because she was listening to something while she walked, her new ears tracking every sound on the level. He had read the notes on the Auricula Felis integration: directional-hearing sensitivity heightened in the first weeks, the pinna moving independently of the eyes, the brain learning a new geometry of space. She would hear the refrigeration units, the drip along the south wall, the compute rack's hum through the vault door. She would hear him breathing if he wasn't careful. He slowed his breathing to four counts in, six out, and stayed against the wall.

[Beat 42]
The footsteps stopped outside the vault door. He heard her hand on the latch — the soft metal-on-metal contact of someone who had been told how to open it. Then a pause of about fifteen seconds, longer than it would take to work a latch. He thought she was listening to something on the other side of the door that he couldn't hear. Whatever she was hearing, it said go in. The latch moved. The door swung open.

[Beat 43]
She was twenty-two and she was carrying a cloth bag and she had pale cat ears that tracked the sound of his breathing before her eyes found him in the dark. Her eyes found him half a second later. She did not scream. She took one step back and her shoulder hit the door frame and she looked at him the way people looked at something that was not supposed to be there — not threat-assessment, but wrongness, a thing that didn't belong in the story she was standing inside.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Voss Caldera," he said. "Your mother hired Rennick Investigations five days ago. My colleagues are on their way down."

The ears moved — one degree north, one degree south, quartering the space behind her, checking the level.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said. That was not entirely true. "I'm here so you have someone to talk to who isn't him."

She was very still.

"The voice in the channel," Voss said. "It isn't him."

[Beat 44]
"I can hear him," she said. Not defensive — factual, the way she would state the color of the wall.

"I know," Voss said. "The channel is still raw from the integration. It conducts ambient signal directly. What you're hearing is in the field."

"It's him." Her voice had the texture of something that had been said many times, tested, and survived each test. "I know his cadence. I know the way he pauses before he says my name."

"I won't be able to argue that with you," Voss said. "I can't pull the message logs or the behavioral analysis. My colleague Corvin can, and he will, but he's not here yet." He kept his voice at the same register throughout. "What I can tell you is that there's a girl named Sol Castellanos-Park who heard the same pause before her name, and she's in a refrigeration unit forty meters north of this room."

[Beat 45]
Celeste did not move. The pale ears turned — one tracking him, the other rotating slow toward the empty air above the compute rack, fixing on a point where there was no speaker, no body, no source a sound could come from, and holding there the way an ear holds on something real. Nothing in that corner of the room was making noise. He could hear the rack's hum and the drip on the south wall and his own breath, and her ear was aimed at none of them. It was aimed at a voice that had no place to be coming from. He watched her face do the work of a person who had organized eight months around a single conclusion and was now being handed a fact that didn't fit the architecture. It didn't break anything yet. That wasn't how it worked. He'd seen it before, when he told people things that contradicted what they needed to be true, and the first response was never belief. The first response was to look at the messenger and decide whether he was the kind of person who would lie about something this specific.

She looked at him for a long time. The ears stilled.

"Sol," she said. "Nineteen. Cat ears. Same as mine."

"Yes," Voss said.

"You're not guessing."

"No," he said. "I'm not."

[Beat 46]
She set the cloth bag down. Not on the side table — on the floor, near the door, the way you set something down when you are not sure whether you are staying. She stood with her arms at her sides and he could see her listening: not to him, to the channel, to whatever the thing in the field was still saying at the edge of its reach. He had no way to know what it was offering her at this moment. He had no way to know what voice it was using or what argument it was making. He only knew that she had put the bag on the floor near the door and not on the table, and that she was still standing in the doorway instead of sitting in the chair. He counted those facts and held them.

[Beat 47]
He heard Rennick on the service stairs at 0901 — the measured pace, the deliberate weight — and behind him at least two others. Celeste heard them first: the ears swiveled north and her shoulders came up and Voss said, "Those are my colleagues," before she could decide to run. "The woman will be Tamsin. She won't touch anything. The man behind her is Rennick. He's the one who can tell you how Sol died and what the scene looked like and what it means." He paused. "He's better at this part than I am."

She didn't say anything. But she stayed in the doorway.

[Beat 48]
While he waited for them he thought about what was happening in the Network field at the vault's threshold. The thing on the other side could not see into the room. It could see the field density change — a small variance where the vault's walls interrupted the ambient load — and it could track Celeste's neuretic signature to the point where she had gone dark, which was the service stair. It knew she had come this far. It did not know she had stopped. It was, right now, waiting for her to sit in the chair. It had no model for what was actually happening because it had no model for Voss and it had never had one. He thought about Errico Baas, four years in detention, and the specific shape of being wrong about something you had been fast about. He thought about the chair. He did not think about what he deserved for the version where he was right this time and Baas had still spent four years in a cell and the real killer had still spent those four years free.

[Beat 49]
Rennick came down the concrete steps first and read the room in the two seconds it took him to clear the doorway — Voss against the wall, Celeste in the frame, the chair empty, the bag on the floor. He didn't look relieved. He looked at the math of the situation and filed it under: *still in play.* That was the thing about Rennick: he never named an outcome before it was actually an outcome.

"Ms. Hartley," he said. "I'm Isaac Rennick. I found the room where Sol Castellanos-Park was treated." He said it the way he said everything — method before the person, the work as the introduction. "I have photographs if you want them. I have a read of the scene that tells you who ran the procedure and how long it took and what the room looked like afterward." He paused one beat. "I won't show you the photographs unless you ask for them."

Celeste's ears moved. She was listening to two things simultaneously and he could see her brain trying to hold both.

"I don't want the photographs," she said.

"Understood," Rennick said.

[Beat 50]
Tamsin came in last and stood near the wall, away from the chair, and did not say anything. Voss watched her — the way she kept her hands at her sides, the way she was not looking at Celeste but at some middle distance, reading whatever the room held. He didn't know exactly what she felt in spaces like this one. She had never explained it in terms he fully understood. What he understood was that she was useful in rooms where things had been decided, and this room had been prepared with a great deal of intention, and whatever she was reading in that intention, she kept it to herself and stayed near the wall and let Rennick do the talking.

[Beat 51]
Celeste picked up the cloth bag at 0908. Not to leave — to hold. She stood in the doorway with the bag against her chest and her eyes on the middle distance between Rennick and the chair, and then she said: "He told me the procedure would let me find him. In the field. After."

"I know," Rennick said.

"Is that what happened to Sol?"

A pause — not because Rennick didn't know the answer, but because he was choosing the form of it. "Tamsin read the room where we found Sol," he said. "The same listening she'd felt in your bedroom in Evanston — a fixed cold edge that takes the warmth out of a room and gives nothing back. The same attention, in both rooms. And Corvin pulled Sol's message logs. She heard her brother Mateo, the way you heard Ethan. The cadence is identical — one author behind both. Not Ethan. Not Mateo. The same voice that walked Sol to a room like this one walked you here. It used a different face."

The ears went flat. Not frightened — processing. The new geometry of a space she'd been standing in for eight months, refigured from the floor up.

[Beat 52]
Voss stayed against the wall and let Rennick work. This was the correct division: Rennick named the method, Corvin had the inadmissible proof in the matched cadence of the logs, Tamsin had read the same cold listening in two rooms eight months apart. Voss had done his part. He had found the room. He had been here when she arrived. He had said Sol's name before she sat in the chair, which was the only thing that had mattered in the last hour, and the rest of it was not his work to do. He opened the notebook and wrote the time — 0909 — and the count: one staging room, one compute rack, one chair, one woman standing in the door with the bag against her chest instead of sitting down. He wrote: *Chair empty as of 0909.* He wrote it as a fact and not as a resolution, because Rennick was right that things were still in play, and facts were the only thing he had ever trusted that he didn't have to take back.

[Beat 53]
The cleaner's third sweep came at 1012 — he heard the footsteps change rhythm on the corrugated walk and he was already at the door when Rennick held up two fingers: *I know, stay.* The footsteps went north and didn't pause. The crew's surveillance had not found them because it was looking for a neuretic signal or a checkpoint anomaly or a device ping, and none of those things existed in this room. Four people and a conversation, and nothing for the Network to read. Voss noted the time and the count — four present, chair still empty — and left it there. He was fifty-one years old and had been wrong before in a way that cost a man four years, and he had stopped trusting the conclusions he reached while he was still pleased with himself.

[Beat 54]
At 1034 Celeste set the bag on the floor again and did not pick it back up. She sat on the concrete floor across from the chair, her back against the doorframe, the new ears tracking every sound on the level. Rennick sat across from her and talked. Tamsin stayed near the wall. Voss moved to the stairs and took a position where he could see both the vault and the service walk, and he stayed there with his notebook closed and watched the under-city for anything that moved. The cleaner would come again in two hours. They needed to be out before then, and out quietly, and with Celeste walking under her own judgment rather than escorted — because escorted was the kind of story that ended in an Arcturus intake room and a corponation filing a voluntary recall, and Voss had read enough of those files to know what they did to the truth by the time the paperwork settled.

[Beat 55]
He thought about Ines, upstairs on 18th Street, running her 0.1% adverse-event log. She had given Celeste the recovery instructions in good faith — quiet places, no dense ambient exposure, the raw channel needs forty-eight hours. Standard protocol, correct medicine, the responsible thing to say. The predator had been listening through Celeste's channel when Ines said it, and had used the advice as a map: off-grid, quiet, no signal. Ines would know that now, if she'd finished running the tape backward, and she would carry it the way operators carried the cases that went sideways despite everything they did right. The corridor policed its own. It shared adverse-event logs. It had refused referrals to the Ashland shop for two years. None of it had reached a thing that listened through the channel itself. Voss wrote none of this down. He didn't have a count for it and he didn't have a fact, and the notebook only held facts.

[Beat 56]
At 1108 Celeste walked out of the vault under her own power with the bag over her shoulder and the paper book in her hand, still face-down, unread. Rennick went first up the stairs, then Tamsin, then Celeste, then Voss last with the torch. He pulled the vault door shut behind him — not latched, the way he'd found it — and followed them up to the service level and north along the walk and up the two flights to the Morgan hatch. They came out into a street that had fully committed to midmorning. He watched Celeste walk through the checkpoint arc and felt the system read her and file her correctly, because she was connected and the city knew her — and the moment it knew her, so did the thing in the field. Her ears resumed their quartering of the ambient load, the new tissue doing what it had been grown to do, and somewhere in that signal the voice was waiting, wearing Ethan's cadence, back in range and patient. It had watched her go dark on the service stair. It did not know she had stopped at the chair instead of sitting in it. It had no idea what had happened in the vault below, because the one thing that had happened there had happened in a room it could not enter, to a girl it could no longer reach, witnessed by a man it had no way to see.

[Beat 57]
He stood on Morgan Street and opened the notebook to the current page and wrote it as evidence: *Staging room located. Transformer vault, south mechanical level, Morgan-side access. Compute substrate, gray-market, running at load. Reclining chair, headrest, restraints absent — she was meant to sit willingly. Subject departed under own agency 1108. Crew unaware. Meridian unaware.* Facts, in order, the way a thing that would have to hold up someday gets written down. Then he paused, pen above the page. The case was not finished and the predator was still in the field and the next part belonged to Rennick and Corvin and Tamsin — but the room was found, and the room had been the whole problem, because the room was the one place in the corridor the thing could walk a girl to and not follow. He had stood in it for three hours. He wrote one line that was not a fact and would never go in evidence: *It never knew I was there.* He underlined it once, the way he underlined the things he wanted to find again, and closed the notebook.
