# ATTE AB OFF

_Cross-district truancy investigator Yemina Fola investigates three children who vanished from schools in the Pilsen Veil — each one preceded by a neuretics burnout flag, each disappearance logged as an unexcused absence before anyone connected the cases. The schools were under three different corp contracts; nobody had connected them because nobody else could see all three. When she sweeps the third school's bathroom with a resonance sensor, she finds two traces: an echo above the child's usual seat where the bleed's signal tuned him, and a shadow in the bathroom stall where he crossed. In her car afterward, someone else's hands complete a search query in her neuretics: 47 missing children, GLMZ-wide, 22 months. The 48th entry is her own name. The onset date is six weeks ago._

## Attendance

[Beat 1]
The relay towers on 63rd Street had been singing since before Maris was born, a flat insect drone at 1,247 hertz that her grandmother had named after the cicadas of a park that no longer existed, and that Maris had stopped hearing somewhere around her ninth birthday, the way you stop hearing your own breathing. This morning it was back. Not louder. Just present, the way a tooth is present after the dentist touches it — a frequency she felt in the bridge of her nose and the fillings she did not have, standing in the doorway of the relay-tower maintenance shed with her thermos of recycled coffee and her clipboard that was an actual clipboard, plastic and twenty years dead, because the union ran attendance on paper so Tessera couldn't backdate a shortage and dock the whole crew.

*Same tone the wallet makes when it hits zero. Two beeps, then nothing. Don't think about it.*

She thought about it. She always thought about it the second she told herself not to, which her mother had called a gift and her ex had called something else, and she pressed the pen to the line beside her certification number — not her name, the towers didn't care about her name — and signed in for the cycle. Around her the other three signed too, Okonkwo and the new kid whose handle she hadn't learned and Reyes, who was nursing a hand wrapped in medical tape and who looked at the singing towers with the same flat suspicion she felt and said nothing, because you didn't say it out loud where the Ear could file it.

"Four for four," she said, marking the count, and the words came out wrong because the count wasn't four. The new kid was here. Okonkwo was here. Reyes was here. The fourth slot on the sheet belonged to Devlin, who had signed in at 0500 every cycle for six years, whose mug was still on the shelf with cold coffee in it, and whose line on the clipboard was blank, the pen-stroke she'd expected to find already there simply not there, the paper white where six years of muscle memory said it shouldn't be.

*Don't fill it in for him. Whatever else you do today. Don't put his number on a line he didn't sign.*

[Beat 2]
She set the clipboard against the shed's doorframe and crossed to the shelf where Devlin's mug sat, and she did not touch it, because the coffee in it had a skin and the skin meant hours, and hours meant he had been here before her, which the sign-in sheet said he had not. Behind her Okonkwo had gone still in the particular way men went still when they were doing math they didn't want the answer to, and Reyes had stopped picking at the tape on his hand. The new kid asked where Dev was, and his voice did the thing new voices did out here, that little upward hook at the end that the towers ate before it cleared the door.

"He's around," Maris said, which was a lie shaped like a fact, and she hated the practiced way it came out, the union-meeting way, the talking-to-the-Ear way.

*The mug's still warm to the eye. He poured it. He poured it and then his line stayed white. Both of those are true and that's the problem, that's the whole problem.*

She walked the catwalk to the base of Tower Three, the one whose drone sat lowest in her teeth, and the frequency climbed half a step as she approached, which it was not supposed to do, which it had never once done in eleven years of her standing under it, and she stopped with her boot on the first rung of the ladder and her hand on the cold rail and looked up the spine of the thing into the gray nothing where the top of it should have been and was not — not fog, the morning was clear, just a place where the tower entered something and the something did not give it back. Her clipboard was still leaning on the doorframe forty meters behind her with three signatures and one white line, and she understood, in the bridge of her nose and the fillings she did not have, that she was going to climb, and that climbing was the wrong call, and that she was going to do it anyway because Devlin had a daughter and somebody had to be able to say where the line went blank.

## Chapter 1

[Beat 3]
The first rung took her weight with the small complaint of cold steel, a tick of contraction noise that the drone swallowed before it reached her ear, and she went up the way the union taught — three points always, breathe on the even rungs — past the junction box at four meters where someone had scratched a tally into the powder coat years ago, and past the conduit clamp at nine where the maintenance tag still read her own certification number from the inspection she'd signed off in the spring, her ink, her hand, proof she had been here in a world where Devlin's line stayed white. The frequency climbed another half step. It was in her molars now, the ones she had and the ones she didn't, and the gray above her did not recede as she rose toward it the way distance was supposed to. It held its distance exactly. It waited the way a thing waits when it has already decided.

*Tessera's manual says report anomalous EM and descend. Page forty. I could be on the catwalk in ninety seconds writing it up by the book and nobody could touch me.*

She kept climbing. Below her Okonkwo's voice came up the spine of the tower thinned to almost nothing, calling her name and then the word *down*, the only part of the sentence the drone let through, and she did not answer because answering meant stopping and stopping meant deciding, and she had already decided at the bottom with her boot on the first rung. At eighteen meters the rail under her palm went cold in a way that wasn't temperature, a cold with a clean vertical edge to it like a line ruled on the air, and her breath came out visible though the morning was not cold, the white of it drifting up past her face and into the gray and not coming back out the other side, dispersing into the something the way the top of the tower had.

*The mug had a skin on it. He poured it. He poured it and then he went up here, didn't he, he went up here ahead of all of us and that's why his line's white, because there's no rung you climb back down from this and sign your number on a sheet.*

She made herself look up past her own knuckles into the place where Tower Three stopped being Tower Three, and the drone was no longer a drone but a pressure shaped like a sound, a thing the bridge of her nose understood before any part of her that used words could, and her hand on the next rung would not close. It simply would not close. The fingers stayed half-curled in the air a centimeter off the steel, refusing her, and she hung there at twenty meters with three points of contact and a fourth that had stopped taking orders, and forty meters below and behind her the clipboard waited against the doorframe with its one honest blank line, and she understood that whatever she did with the next thirty seconds, she was not going to be able to say where the line went blank. She had climbed up to find that out and the finding was that there was nothing to find. Only the cold edge, and her breath going up and not coming back, and Devlin's daughter, somewhere across the Gray Zone, still not knowing she should start waiting.

[Beat 4]
She brought her free hand off the rail — not toward the next rung, toward her own face, the back of her wrist against her mouth to feel her own breath, to confirm there was still breath, and there was, warm against the tendons for the half-second before the cold edge took it and walked it up into the gray. The fingers of her other hand were still half-curled, still refusing, a centimeter of dead air between flesh and steel that her arm kept reporting as solid and her eyes kept reporting as nothing. Both true. Both at once. The mug's skin and the white line. The rung and the no-rung. She had spent her whole working life standing under a thing that hummed and never asked what the hum was made of, and now she was inside the question and the question had a temperature.

*Eleven years and I never once put my hand somewhere the rung wasn't. Three points always. That's the rule and the rule is for falling. There's no rule for this.*

The drone shifted — not louder, not the half-step climb she'd been tracking up the spine, but a settling, the way a held note resolves down into something you can almost stand inside, and in the resolving she heard, or her molars heard, a fold in it, a place where the one tone became two and the two were not in agreement. She knew that disagreement. She had stood beside it at 0500 for six years. It was the pitch of Devlin's voice when he hummed without knowing he was doing it, the tuneless thing he did filling thermoses, and it was up here now, threaded into the pressure that was not a sound, neither lost nor kept, just folded in, the way her breath was folded in, the way the top of the tower was folded in.

*Don't. Don't make it him. It's a frequency. Page forty says report and descend. It is not his voice. It is a frequency that wants to be his voice because I came up here wanting something to say to a girl I've never met.*

Her hand closed. Not the dead one — the other, the one at her mouth, it closed into a fist she pressed flat against her own sternum and held there, three points of contact and a fourth that was just her, just her own ribs under her own knuckles, the one honest thing left at twenty meters. Below and behind, the clipboard against the doorframe with its white line. Above, the gray that held its distance and waited. And between them her, fist to chest, breathing out into a cold that took the breath and gave back nothing, deciding — not whether to climb, she knew now she would not climb, the body had ruled on that — but the smaller, worse thing: whether what she carried back down the spine would be the truth, which was that there was nothing to find, or the lie shaped like a fact, the union-meeting lie, the one she'd already practiced once this morning on a new kid whose handle she still didn't know.

[Beat 5]
The descent took longer than the climb, the way down always does when the body has stopped pretending it has a choice, and she went rung by rung with the three points she'd been taught and the fourth one held now, given back to her at fourteen meters where the cold edge let go all at once, no fade, a clean line crossed downward into air that was just air, into a morning that was just a morning, and the drone behind her settled back into a drone and the fold in it went out of her molars and into the unremarkable hum she had stood beside for eleven years without once asking what it was made of. Okonkwo was on the catwalk when her boots found it, a hand out, not touching, the careful not-touching the union taught for someone coming off a height wrong, and his mouth was already shaping her name into a question she could see the front of before she heard the back.

"You good?" he asked.

She did not answer that one either. She picked the clipboard up off the doorframe where she'd left it and she felt the cheap composite of it under her thumb, the corner worn soft by a thousand grips before hers, and the one honest blank line waited under the pen the way the gray had waited above her, patient, already decided about nothing.

*Write report and descend. Write anomalous EM, page forty, ink and a number, and it's true, every word, and it tells the girl nothing, and that's the mercy, that's what a fact is for.*

"Okonkwo." She heard her own voice come level, the level she'd practiced. "You been up a tower long enough — you ever figure out the difference between what a thing is and what it's made of?" She watched him not understand it, watched it slide off him the way it would have slid off her an hour ago, before the rung and the no-rung, before her own breath went up and didn't come back. "Because the report's going to say what it is. EM anomaly. Clean. And that's the part anybody can read." She clicked the pen. "And what it's made of doesn't go on a sheet. There's no line for it. There's never been a line for it, and I signed eleven years of these thinking the lines were the whole tower."

[Beat 6]
Okonkwo took the pen from her when she held it out, and he held it the way you hold a tool someone's handed you wrong-end-first, looking at the line and then at her face and then at the line again, and the not-understanding was still on him, but it had gone quieter, the way a man goes quiet when the joke he was waiting for doesn't come. The wind came across the catwalk grating in a long flat sheet that smelled of lake and rust and the ozone the towers shed, and below them the Gray Zone went on doing its sideways morning, the maglev's faint four-second-tolerant rumble somewhere east, a thousand thermoses being filled, none of them by Devlin.

*He's going to write it the way I'd have written it. He's going to read the lines and think they're the whole tower. And there's no part of me that gets to tell him different without sounding like the cold edge got into me, like I came down wrong, like I'm the anomaly on page forty.*

"It's an EM read," he said finally, careful, giving her the out, signing his own number under hers because that was the rule, two hands on every height incident, and she watched him write the digits clean and square. "That's what it is. We log it, they send a crew with better gear than a clipboard, and we don't have to be the ones who know."

"That's the part anybody can read," she said again, and she let it sit there between them on the grating, not a correction, not a lesson — she didn't have one, she'd gone up looking for one and come back with a temperature instead — just the shape of a question she now had to carry down the spine and across the Zone to a girl who'd been humming the wrong tune her whole life without knowing whose it was. She took the clipboard back. The white line had two numbers under it now and the thing it didn't say was still the whole reason she'd climbed. *And what it's made of doesn't go on a sheet.* She clicked the pen closed and started for the ladder down, and behind her the hum held its distance, patient, made of something, decided about nothing.

[Beat 7]
The ladder down off the catwalk was the easy part, the part the body could do without her, and she let it, hand over hand into the stairwell's caged dark where the tower's own light gave out and the service strips took over, that low maintenance amber that turned everyone's skin the same wrong color. Okonkwo came after her at the union spacing, two rungs and never one, and she could hear his breathing through the cage, the working unevenness of a big man taking stairs he'd taken a thousand times, and it was just breathing, it was nobody's voice, and she made herself listen to it as the plain thing it was until they reached the landing and the door with the magnetic seal and the panel that wanted a credential she had and the girl downstairs did not.

*Halloran. The handle's Halloran. The kid this morning with the wrong tune, that's the name she gave me before I went up, and I let it slide off because I had a tower to climb. And she's the one. She's the one humming his note and she doesn't know there's a name under it, doesn't know there's a man who filled thermoses, doesn't know the report's going to tell her it's an EM anomaly and she's going to read the line and think it's the whole tower like I did.*

She put her thumb to the panel and it took her, the seal letting go with that small pneumatic sigh that was the most honest sound the building made all day, a door deciding she was allowed, and through the gap the stairwell gave onto the receiving bay where the new kid sat on an upturned crate with her boots not quite reaching the grating, swinging them a little, humming — there it was, the fold, the disagreement, the two tones that weren't in agreement coming out of a mouth that had never stood beside a tower at 0500 and didn't know what it was repeating. Achebe stopped in the doorframe with the clipboard against her hip and the two clean numbers on the white line and the whole unwritten reason for the climb sitting behind her sternum where she'd held the fist, and she did not yet know which thing she was going to say, the fact or the other, and Okonkwo's breathing came down the cage behind her and waited too.

"Halloran." The kid looked up, the humming stopped mid-fold, and the silence where the second tone had been was louder than the tone had ever been. *Now. Either it's a frequency on page forty or it's a name, and the second you give her the name you can't take it back, and there's no line on any sheet for what that costs her.* "That tune you do." She heard her own voice find the level again, the practiced level, and hold it one word past where the mercy would have been. "Where'd you pick it up?"

[Beat 8]
The kid shrugged the way the young shrug, the whole shoulder, like the question weighed nothing. "Always known it. My mom, maybe? She didn't sing it though, she just — " Halloran made a small motion with her hand, flat, side to side, the fold of it, the disagreement, doing the thing the voice did without a voice. "She used to do that when she was thinking. With her hand. I thought everybody knew it." She tipped her head. "Why? Is it wrong? Did I get it wrong?"

*She thinks the question is whether she's got the tune right. She thinks there's a right. Tell her EM anomaly. Tell her page forty. Hand her the line and let her read the line and let her think it's the whole tower, and she goes home humming her mother's hand for fifty more years and nobody made her stand at the foot of a name.*

Achebe felt the clipboard against her hip, the two clean numbers, the worn-soft corner under her thumb, and she felt the cold edge that had gotten into her on the rung that wasn't there, the temperature she'd carried down the spine of the tower, and she heard Okonkwo's breathing settle behind her in the door, plain, nobody's voice, the most honest thing in the bay. "No," she said, and her voice held the level one word, two words, three — "you didn't get it wrong." And then the level cracked along a seam she'd signed eleven years pretending wasn't there. "It's got a name under it. The hum. There's a man — there was a man — and that fold you do, that's his, and I came down a tower to not tell you that, and I'm telling you anyway, and I don't have the next part. There isn't a next part. There's just the name."

The kid's boots stopped swinging. The bay went quiet the way the receiving bay never went quiet, the loading seals ticking, the maglev's far rumble holding its four-second tolerance somewhere east, and Halloran looked at her with a face that hadn't decided yet whether it had been given a gift or a wound, the two of them not in agreement, the fold of it, and Achebe stood in the doorframe having spent the thing she'd climbed to keep and finding she was lighter and worse for it both at once.

[Beat 9]
"What name?" Halloran asked, and there it was, the question Achebe had handed her without a back half to it, the kid already reaching for the thing the way the young reach, both hands, no notion of the weight.

*She wants it whole. She wants a face and a date and a reason, and I gave her a fold of a hand and a temperature off a rung that wasn't there, and now she's going to carry the gap the way I carried the fist, and the gap is heavier because it's hers and she didn't climb for it.* Achebe came off the doorframe one step, the clipboard knocking soft against her hip, the two clean numbers riding down with her. She crouched so the kid's swung-still boots and her own eyes were on a level, the way you do for the small ones, the way she hadn't done in years because the small ones weren't usually hers to do it for.

"I don't have all of it," she said, and she made herself say it plain, not the practiced level now, just the level a person uses with another person. "I have a piece. Filled thermoses, somebody told me once, a long time before your time on the crew. Stood at the foot of this tower at 0500 doing the work nobody put a name to either, until the day the tower kept the only part of him it could keep, and that's the part you've got in your mother's hand and never knew." She held the kid's face with her own and did not blink it away. "His name was the thing I came down to not say. So I'm going to do the next worst thing to saying it, which is I'm going to find the rest of it before I hand it to you, and then it's whole, and then it's yours to do whatever you do with a whole thing. That's the trade. That's all the trade I've got."

Behind her Okonkwo's breathing came off the stairwell cage and into the bay, plain, unhurried, a big man done with the climbing part, and he didn't say a word, because there wasn't a word, and the loading seals ticked their slow tick, and the kid put her flat hand down against her own knee, still, not folding anything, holding the disagreement for the first time instead of singing it.

[Beat 10]
The kid sat with the flat hand on her knee a long moment, the stillness new on her, ill-fitting, the way a coat is too big before you grow into it. Then she did the thing Achebe had not budgeted for, which was the worst thing, which was that she nodded — not the gift-nod and not the wound-nod but the third one, the one that took the trade as offered and meant to hold Achebe to it. "Okay," Halloran said. "Find the rest. I can wait." And she could. That was the part that got under the cold edge already sitting in Achebe's spine. The young can wait the way only the young can, certain there's road enough behind the waiting to make it worth something.

*Now I've signed a second line and there's no thumb-panel for this one, no seal, no door deciding I'm allowed. I climbed to keep a name shut and instead I cracked it open and promised her the whole jar, and I don't have the jar, I have a fold of a hand and a temperature and a word — thermoses — that some half-cut rigger handed me at a bar in a year I can't pin. That's the inventory. That's what I went and made a debt against.*

She straightened out of the crouch slow, the knees telling her about the eleven years and the tower both, and she put the clipboard's worn-soft corner under her thumb again because her hand wanted a thing to do that wasn't the fold. The two clean numbers were still on the white line. They were going to stay clean. Page forty would read EM anomaly the way it always read EM anomaly, and that was the lie she was allowed to file and the truth she'd just spent stood in the bay between her and a kid with her boots gone still, and the lie was lighter on paper and heavier everywhere a sheet didn't reach.

"You can wait," Achebe said, and she heard the practiced level try to come back into her voice and she didn't let it, held the door shut on it the one way she had left. "But don't go quiet on it while you wait. Hum it. Keep the fold going." Behind her Okonkwo shifted his weight off the cage at last, the grating taking him, and his breathing did the thing breathing does when a man's decided to follow you somewhere he thinks is a mistake — went even, went patient, went along. "Because right now that hand of yours is the only piece of him anybody's still carrying out loud, and I'd rather not find the rest of him just to learn the last of it stopped while my back was turned."

[Beat 11]
Achebe turned for the bay door before the kid could put another question into the gap, because a second question would want a second piece and she was out of pieces to spend. Okonkwo fell in off the grating the way she'd known he would, the patient even breath of a man who'd decided her mistake was now also his, and the two of them went out past the loading seals into the corridor's lower hum, the maglev holding its four-second tolerance somewhere east of the both of them.

*Thermoses. That's the whole thread. A word a rigger gave me at a bar I can't name in a year I can't pin, and I just leveraged it against a child's mother's hand. I've signed worse with better paper.*

"Where do you even start," Okonkwo said, not a question the way he said it, flat, the way he laid down anything he already knew the bad answer to. She didn't slow. "Old Harbor. The forge man keeps names. Kofi. If somebody filled thermoses at 0500 at the foot of a tower for years, a blacksmith on the waterfront heard the man's hands before he heard his face." That was the thread and she pulled it and felt it hold and felt, under the hold, exactly how thin it was — one strand, one bar, one half-cut word — and she kept walking on it anyway because she'd promised the jar and a promise was just a line you signed where no panel asked your thumb.

The corridor opened on the freight ramp and the wet came up to meet them, the redirected rain doing its slow political work on the concrete, and her boot found the first puddle and broke a long strip of someone's signage across the surface — red, then white, then her own dark shape walking through both, brighter in the water than it had any business being. She did not stop to look at which version was the true one. She had a thread to pull and a kid behind her humming a dead man's hand into the ticking quiet, and somewhere ahead of her a man at a forge who might or might not remember the sound of a stranger's grip from a year nobody could pin, and that was the road now, the whole of it, signed and unsealed and hers.

[Beat 12]
The freight ramp gave out onto the open mouth of the Gray Zone and the wet politics kept on with their redistribution, sheeting off the corrugated overhangs in the steep efficient angle the processors liked, and Achebe pulled her collar up against none of it because the collar had given up keeping water out two winters ago and now only kept the idea of it. Okonkwo walked her left side the way he always took the left, the bad-sightline side, the side that meant if a thing came at her it came at him first — an old habit from a crew that didn't exist anymore, a courtesy paid to a rank she'd stopped holding before he'd ever met her. She let him keep paying it. It was cheaper than telling him to stop.

*Old Harbor's a four-hour walk if the lifts are Q-dry, and they're always Q-dry on the freight side, and Kofi doesn't answer a pinged stranger, Kofi answers a face he's already weighed once. So that's the first cost. I go in person, I lose the morning, and the morning's the only thing I had clean.*

"You're going to walk it," Okonkwo said, still not a question, laying the bad answer down flat between them like he was reading her page forty over her shoulder. She broke the second puddle and her dark shape went red-then-white again under her boot, brighter than the woman casting it, and she did not look down to settle the argument of which one was the real one because she already knew the water always won that and she didn't have the morning to lose to it twice. "I'm going to walk it," she said. "You're going to go back up and sit with the kid until the hum holds on its own, because a promise this thin doesn't survive me finding out somebody let her go quiet while I was on the waterfront getting a name out of a man who counts hands." He stopped. She felt him stop without turning, the patient even breath catching once on the decision and then going along, the way it had gone along in the bay, the way it would go along all the way to whatever this cost the both of them.

"That's not the trade you made," he said. "You made the trade. Not me, and not her, and not Kofi." She kept walking, because if she turned he'd see the thing the practiced level was built to cover and she'd spent her whole supply of plain talk on a six-year-old's boots an hour ago and had none left to spend on a man who already knew. "No," she said to the rain ahead of her, to the thread, to the one bar holding. "But somebody's carrying the whole jar now whether they signed for it or not, and that's the part I never could file right. You hand a person a thing to keep and you've handed them the keeping of it too, and the keeping's the heavy half." The puddle ahead held the whole sky's worth of redirected nobody-else's-weather, red and white and her in it, and she stepped through her own brighter self without slowing and went toward Old Harbor on the one strand she had.

[Beat 13]
Okonkwo did not go back up. She heard the grating of his boots fail to turn behind her, heard them choose her direction instead, the patient even breath closing the gap she'd just told him to leave open. He came up on the left side again, the bad-sightline side, and matched her stride into the wet without a word, and the word he wasn't saying was louder than the rain.

"I told you to sit with the kid," she said.

"You told me," he said. "And I heard you. And the maglev's still got CONDUCTOR holding it to four seconds, so the kid's got a feed full of city humming at her whether I'm there or not, and you've got a four-hour walk on a thread you said yourself was one strand. Pick which one of those needs a man with a bad knee more." He didn't break stride to say it. He laid it down flat the way he laid down everything, the bad answer already weighed and priced. "I'm not letting you spend the morning alone, Achebe. That part's not on your page. That part's on mine."

*He's wrong about which of us the keeping breaks first, and he's right that I'd let it break me to keep from filing it. Both true. Both on the same sheet. I never could make those two numbers stay on opposite sides of a line.*

She kept walking, because turning would cost her the practiced level she had nothing left to rebuild it with, and the puddle ahead held the redirected sky and the red-and-white signage and now two dark shapes instead of one, hers and his, both brighter in the water than the two tired people casting them. She stepped through the both of them without slowing and let him keep the left side, let him pay the courtesy to a rank she'd stopped holding, and said nothing, because saying *thank you* would mean admitting she'd needed the company, and she'd already spent her whole supply of plain talk on a six-year-old's boots an hour ago. So the thanks stayed unsaid between them, the way the worst of the file stayed off page forty, and the two of them went toward Old Harbor on the one strand and the bad knee and the morning neither of them had clean anymore.

[Beat 14]
The waterfront found them before the buildings did — Old Harbor announced itself the way it always had, by the smell first, the lake coming up under the redirected rain with a cold that had a clean vertical edge to it, an old indifferent cold that predated every processor that had ever told the weather where to go. Achebe's teeth registered it before her mind named it: the lake didn't care about the morning she'd lost or the thread she carried or the jar she'd signed for. The lake had been here before the sea wall failed and before Axiom declined to fix it and before there was a corponation to decline anything, and it would be here after, and it sheeted the broken concrete with its own water that had nothing to do with anybody's politics.

The forge was a cargo container with a chimney built from dead HVAC duct, and the hood glowed orange against the gray, and a man stood in the mouth of it with a hammer and did not look up when two shapes broke the far puddle and came across the open ground toward him. That was Kofi. That was the man who answered a face he'd weighed once and a pinged stranger never, and she had walked four hours of bad lift and worse knee to put her face in front of him cold, unweighed, on a single strand of half-cut word.

*He counts hands. That's the whole reason he's worth the morning — a man who counts hands remembers the one he's looking for. But a man who counts hands counts mine too, and mine's empty, and an empty hand at a forge buys nothing.*

He set the hammer down on the anvil's horn, slow, and turned the pale work of his face toward them, and the heat of the forge moved between her and him in a wall she could feel from ten feet out. "I know him," Kofi said, and lifted his chin at Okonkwo on the bad-sightline side. "Saints crew, the old one, before the bay went bad. Him I'll talk to." Then the eyes came to her, weighing, and did not finish weighing, and that was its own answer. "You I don't know. And I don't take a name off a stranger who walked here meaning to ask me for one. So you've got the wrong half of the trade in your hand, and you spent your whole morning carrying it."

## Chapter 2

The wanting it on the books.* She pressed the good hand flat against the dry wall and held it there, not for balance, just to feel a surface that wasn't water, and the wall held her weight the way the third panel had, the way everything down here held her — provisionally, expecting nothing, ready to forget.

The rain went on falling in its thin redirected line a half-pace to her right, falling where the math said it should and not on her, and she did not move back into it to prove anything and she did not move further from it to spare herself. She just stood at the seam between the wet and the dry with the wrist that would not close, listening to the building breathe its throttled nineteen-hertz nothing, and she understood that she would climb the rest of the way out and the climb would not absolve her and the morning would come on at five with the water and she would still be the person who had walked north away from the door so the grandmother could keep the open file instead of the closed one.

*No-fee,* she told the dark, one more time, knowing it for the lie that kept the body moving, and she lifted the good hand off the wall, and she went up.
