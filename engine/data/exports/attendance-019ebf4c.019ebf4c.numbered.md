# Attendance

_Cross-district truancy investigator Yemina Fola investigates three children who vanished from their classroom seats in the Pilsen Veil — each one preceded by a neuretics burnout flag, each disappearance witnessed as the air going wrong above an empty chair. The schools were under three different corp contracts; nobody had connected the cases because nobody else could see all three. When she interviews the teacher who saw the third child go, she finds him sitting in a room where the wrongness hasn't left. In her car afterward, someone else's hands complete a search query in her neuretics: 47 missing children, GLMZ-wide, 22 months. The 48th entry is her own name. The onset date is six weeks ago._

## Chapter 1

[Beat 1]
The car the Arcturus district contract came with was gray the way contractor-issue things are gray — not painted gray, not designed gray, but accrued gray, the color of a surface that has stopped being argued with. Yemina Fola drove it through the Pilsen Veil on a Thursday in March and ate a CramIt Bran nutribar that tasted like someone had described chocolate to a person who had never eaten food.

Her neuretics — contractor grade, which meant it was always slightly behind  — showed her the cross-corp caseload for District 14-South. Seventeen chronic absences. Four registered runaways. Three disappearances.

She was there for the three. The clearance registry had assigned them case numbers 45, 46, and 47. She noted this and moved on to the addresses.

[Beat 2]
The Aldiss-Mwangi Community Learning Center occupied the corner of 20th and Halsted like something that had given up on the idea of corners. The building was four stories of poured concrete renewed in stages rather than replaced — each repair cycle a geological record of municipal budget decisions. A plaque near the main entrance read ALDISS-MWANGI COMMUNITY LEARNING CENTER — PRINCIPAL FUNDER: ALDISS-MWANGI CAPITAL PARTNERS (2194–2204). Aldiss-Mwangi Capital Partners had been absorbed into Arcturus Holdings in 2207 and had not existed for eighteen years. The plaque remained because someone would have had to pay to remove it.

Yemina parked in the loading zone. She had a placard for that.

[Beat 3]
The front office had an android behind the welcome desk. It was a body design from the 2190s — there was a market, briefly, for receptionist androids built to read as warm and approachable by 2190s standards, which meant something slightly different from what warm and approachable meant now. The android's eyes were very blue and blinked at intervals that a 2190s engineer had calculated to be soothing. The calculation was wrong: the interval was just fast enough to catch the periphery of attention and just slow enough to feel deliberate.

"Fola, truancy investigator, District 14-South clearance," Yemina said, and held up her credentials for the android's eye. "I have an appointment with Dr. Achebe."

The android's processing light stuttered — a brief reset, the rendering layer cycling through a reboot it had clearly performed before. It recovered in under a second. "Of course. He'll be right out."

"Does that happen often," Yemina asked.

"Daily," the android said. "A low-frequency interference signal in the 17-to-19 hertz range. It introduces noise into the rendering layer. Maintenance has not located the source." A pause calibrated to express regret at inconvenience. "The school filed a request with the Resonance Monitoring Authority for a site assessment. That was four months ago."

The android smiled at the precise moment the smile had been programmed to arrive.

She waited standing. The chair in the waiting area had a stain she decided not to investigate.

[Beat 4]
Dr. Achebe came out with the walk of a man who had decided in advance that this meeting would be brief. He was sixty-something, built wide, wore a blazer over a shirt with a collar that had been ironed that morning with real intention. He had a lanyard with his credentials and a face that had learned, over decades of school administration, to express concern and unavailability simultaneously.

"Ms. Fola. The Bramley case. I've sent everything to your office — the incident report, Kito's attendance record, his guardian's contact." He was already turning back toward his office door, the turn itself an argument. "We were told it's under district review."

"It is," Yemina said. "I'm the district review."

[Beat 5]
The three-minute conversation with Dr. Achebe produced a full accounting of everything he was willing to give her — which was paperwork he'd already filed and a firm opinion that the relevant person to speak with was the counseling office, not the classroom teacher. The classroom teacher had been cooperative with the initial investigation, was still shaken, had other students to attend to.

Yemina wrote his concerns down in her notebook. She wrote them in the notebook deliberately, with a pen, where he could watch her write them. When she looked up she said, "Which room is Mr. Vasquez's class?"

Dr. Achebe looked at what she'd written. "Room 214."

[Beat 6]
It was lunch period. Room 214 held twenty-three empty chairs and one teacher.

Ren Vasquez was twenty-eight or so, Korean-Colombian by appearance, with the kind of face that defaulted to openness the way some faces defaulted to suspicion. He was sitting at his desk and not doing anything: not grading, not reading, not using his neuretics. Just sitting. His hands rested on the desk surface and were not quite still, a fine tremor he was aware of because when he saw Yemina in the doorway he put them in his lap.

"District truancy," Yemina said, and showed him her credentials. "I'm sorry for the timing. I'll be quick."

"It's fine." He said it like a reflex.

She took the chair nearest the door — a side table chair, an adult chair, slightly taller. She set her notebook on her knee. "Tell me what you saw."

[Beat 7]
Ren talked. He'd clearly told this before — his voice had the flat quality of a story that had been rehearsed not for polish but for survival, the way you tell something over and over until it stops meaning what it meant when it happened.

Kito Bramley was nine. Third row, second seat. He'd been present at the start of class. He'd raised his hand once, a question about the assignment. Ren had answered. Kito had gone back to his work. And then — somewhere in the twenty minutes of independent reading that followed — the chair was empty.

"I didn't see him stand," Ren said. "I wasn't watching every student every second. Maybe he—"

"What did you see," Yemina asked.

Ren was quiet for a moment.

[Beat 8]
"The air above his chair went wrong." He said it to his hands in his lap. "Not a shimmer. Not heat-shimmer. It was more like — if sound could make a shape. A frequency you don't hear, you just know it's there because your teeth know. For maybe two seconds. And then Kito wasn't there anymore."

He looked up. "I know how that sounds."

"I've heard it before," Yemina said.

She had. Different words each time — witnesses always found different words for the same thing. *Pressure. A wrongness. A note the room played.* She'd heard it from a maintenance worker at a warehouse in Back of the Yards. From a transit operator at the Wabash Stack. From a woman in West Lawn who had watched her husband walk through the threshold of their kitchen and not come out the other side.

She wrote: *Atmospheric anomaly consistent with prior cases.* The phrase had stopped meaning anything to her six months ago.

[Beat 9]
"Did you file a resonance incident report," Yemina asked.

Ren was quiet for the wrong length of time.

"The district filed one," he said. "Dr. Achebe's office."

"I meant you, personally."

More quiet.

"I'm not on any registries," Ren said. He said it carefully, in a way that meant *I need to not be on any registries.*

Yemina looked at him for a moment — not an interrogation look, just the look of a person reading what they're being told. She wrote something in her notebook. She did not show him what she wrote.

She was closing the notebook when he said: "I moved his desk. Before the other students came back on Wednesday. I told them it was maintenance."

He was looking at the third row.

She didn't write that down. She didn't need to.

What she had written was: *Teacher possibly psionic (low-grade, unregistered). Noticed anomaly before proximity event. Has not self-reported. Protect.*

[Beat 10]
She stood to go. Thanked him. He nodded. She walked toward the door and made the mistake of glancing at the third row, second seat.

The chair was empty in the way all chairs in an empty classroom are empty.

But above it — between the surface of the seat and approximately where a child's sternum would be — the air was doing something she could not have pointed to or measured or written into any incident report. It was not a shimmer. It was not heat. It was the thing Ren had described, and she had written as *atmospheric anomaly*, and both translations were wrong.

She looked at it for two seconds.

Then she turned and walked out.

She was four paces down the hallway before she identified what was wrong with that: she had turned away from the anomaly, but the anomaly had not turned away from her. She felt it still oriented in her direction — not following, not moving, nothing so dramatic as that. Just fixed on her the way a window is fixed on what passes in front of it. She had stopped looking. It had not.

She walked faster.

## Chapter 2

[Beat 11]
In the hallway a class was changing periods — twenty kids, eight and nine years old, moving the way children move in corridors when they've been corrected enough to know the rules exist but not enough to stop testing them. One stepped directly in front of her and overcorrected hard against the wall, apology coming out mostly breath. Another held eye contact the beat too long — the assessing look children give to unfamiliar adults: what kind, and is that kind dangerous.

She had just spent two seconds looking at the air above an empty chair. She could not entirely stop looking at it even now that she was in a different room. The thing she had written as *atmospheric anomaly* had watched her walk away.

One girl near the back had a shoe untied. She looked at Yemina. Then she looked — without turning her head, without any visible reason — at the closed door of room 214.

Yemina kept walking. The girl had not looked at the principal's office. She had not looked at the ceiling or the emergency exit. She had looked at room 214 the way you look at something you have been specifically told not to look at, in the split second before you remember.

[Beat 12]
She sat in the gray car. The Pilsen Veil moved around her the way neighborhoods moved at lunch — sidewalk foot traffic, delivery carts on the painted lane, a repair drone circling a building at 18th Street that had been circling it for three weeks and was either mapping it exhaustively or stuck in a loop nobody had gotten around to checking.

She opened the three case files on her neuretics.

Kito Bramley, 9. Disappeared Tuesday.
Amara Osei, 7. Disappeared eleven days ago, different school, three kilometers north.
Tomás Reyes, 11. Disappeared first, twenty-two days ago, a school in Back of the Yards.

Three different schools. Three different corp contracts. Each of their facilities teams had one missing child in their system — noise-level, the kind of number that earned a flag and a quarterly review. She was the only node in common, the only contractor whose cross-corp clearance ran across all three territories. Nobody had connected the cases because nobody else could see all three.

She'd been looking for what connected them. Different schools. Different neighborhoods. Different heritage, different mods, different everything. She'd been looking for a person or a group — a vector, someone moving between the schools.

She found it now because she'd looked at the wrong field before.

All three had a neuretics burnout flag in their pediatric care record, filed in the six weeks before they disappeared. Not a malfunction. The notation a physician used when a child's neuretics was running above threshold. When something was pushing a signal through them.

She had been about to close the files when she noticed the assignment timestamps.

Tomás Reyes, case 45: assigned to Fola, Yemina, District 14-South clearance, twenty-three days ago — the day before his disappearance had been filed. Amara Osei, case 46: assigned twelve days ago, the day before hers. Kito Bramley, case 47: assigned yesterday at 10:14 AM, four hours before Dr. Achebe's office called her. Before the case formally existed.

She checked the sender field on Kito's assignment. She expected a supervisor's initials. A corp admin flag. Anything.

The field was blank. Not redacted. Not pending. An assignment sent to her account from no account in the system, at 10:14 AM the previous morning, assigning her to a case that hadn't been opened yet.

She sat with that. Outside, the repair drone made another circuit of the building at 18th Street.

[Beat 13]
She typed a search query into the cross-corp case registry. *neuretics burnout flag, pediatric, prior to—*

The query autocompleted.

She stopped. Her contractor-grade neuretics did not have autocomplete on case database searches. She had never had access to it in fifteen years of cross-corp work.

The autocomplete read: *neuretics burnout flag, pediatric, 6-week window preceding disappearance, GLMZ-wide, rolling 24 months.*

It was displayed in a flag she didn't have. The color was in her neuretics interface the way a bruise is in the body — rendered at a wavelength that didn't map to anything she could name, not quite violet, not quite gray. Something she could only call *wrong direction.* The flag read, inside her own neuretics architecture, from somewhere that wasn't her.

She had not typed that. She had never typed that. The syntax was not hers. Her neuretics used a compressed sans-serif for search completions. These characters were not her font. Something had let itself into the rendering layer.

She looked at the search field. The field was lit. Something was on the other side of it — inside her neuretics, using its interface, looking back through the same aperture she looked out of.

She sat with her hands in her lap.

Then she pressed enter.

[Beat 14]
Forty-seven results.

Twenty-two months of them. Schools across GLMZ — Bridgeport, Wicker Park, Rogers Park, a cluster in the West Lawn Line, two in Evanston that the map said barely qualified. Children between six and twelve. All of them with that flag. All of them gone.

She said, out loud, to the interior of the gray car: "Who are you?"

No answer. The delivery carts moved on the painted lane. The repair drone made another circuit of the building at 18th Street. She had registered it twice. Neither query had come back with a valid job order.

The case file updated.

A forty-eighth entry. The name at the top of the new record was her own.

Fola, Yemina. Truancy Investigator. District 14-South Clearance Registry.

No disappearance date. No last-known location. No status filed.

Just a field that every other entry had filled in already — a field labeled *neuretics Anomaly, Onset* — and in that field, a date.

The date was six weeks ago.

She looked at the creation timestamp on her own entry. Not the onset date. The date the record itself had been built and placed in the system.

Twenty-two months ago. The same date as case 1. The same day, she understood now, that the first child on this list had disappeared — the day the list had started.

[Beat 15]
Kito Bramley's guardian was his grandmother, a woman named Chinwe Bramley, seventy-three years old, who opened the door to her apartment on Loomis Street in a house dress and reading glasses and did not appear to have slept. She had a photograph of Kito on the small table in the entryway — school picture, missing a front tooth, grinning at something off-camera. She had put a cup of tea beside it. The tea was cold.

"He told me three days before he went," Mrs. Bramley said. They were sitting at her kitchen table. She had given Yemina tea she didn't expect to drink. "He said there was a sound in his head. Not words. Just a sound. He said it was like a song, but a song nobody wrote." She folded her hands. "He wasn't frightened. He was interested. Kito was always interested in things he couldn't explain."

Yemina asked if he'd been to the pediatric clinic recently. Mrs. Bramley got the care record without being asked — she'd been waiting for someone to ask. The burnout flag was there. Six weeks prior. Flagged by his regular physician as *elevated neuretics activity, developmental range, monitor quarterly.*

Monitor quarterly.

[Beat 16]
The Osei family lived four kilometers north, in a building that was trying to be gentrified and not succeeding. Lech Szymborski answered the door with the posture of a man who had been answering doors for eleven days and had an argument ready for each kind of person behind them. He was Polish, forty, with a jaw that had been clenched so long it had developed muscle definition. When Yemina showed her credentials he said "Another one" and opened the door wider.

His wife, Abena Osei, was in the kitchen. She was Ghanaian, thirty-eight, and had the quality of stillness that sometimes looks like shock from outside. She poured Yemina tea without asking.

"She heard it for six weeks," Abena said. "She said it was at the back of her neuretics. She said it felt like a word she didn't know yet."

Lech asked, from the doorway, whether municipal attendance was going to fix this or just document it.

Yemina wrote in her notebook. She did not answer the question because there was only one honest answer and this was not the moment for it.

[Beat 17]
She pulled all forty-seven pediatric care records that afternoon, sitting in the gray car because there was nowhere else to go, the case system open on one neuretics layer and the records on another and her notebook open on her knee.

The burnout flags were there in every record. But the neuretics activity logs beneath the summaries — the raw data the physician's note abstracted away — showed something the summaries didn't. Activity spikes in a specific frequency band: 17 to 19 Hz, infrasonic, well below normal neuretics operational range.

She had not known to look for this. She knew to look for it now because sections of each log had been highlighted in a color her contractor-grade neuretics did not support for case management. Someone was still in her neuretics. They had been in her neuretics while she sat at Mrs. Bramley's kitchen table, and they had gone through Kito's care record with her, and they had marked what mattered.

She wrote in her notebook: *17-19 Hz. What broadcasts at 17-19 Hz?*

The autocomplete came immediately: *The 35th-and-Halsted Bleed. Ambient emission, 2223–present. Coherent signal, source undetermined.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then, for less than a second, a second attention arrived at the same aperture in her neuretics. Not the intruder. Something that moved through the frequency data the way a current moves through a crowd, looking for a specific shape, not finding it yet. It did not have the intruder's careful margins. It pressed.

The intruder sealed the channel. Not gradually — closed, the way a shutter closes.

She sat very still. She had been examined. She had the separate impression, less clear but just as certain, that the examination had been interrupted before it finished.

[Beat 18]
She spent twenty minutes searching with two neuretics layers open — her own contractor-grade tools on one, the intruder's extensions on the other. The published literature on coherent thin-spot emissions was thin: a fringe position within a fringe field, most researchers having moved on or been absorbed. Almost nothing.

One paper remained attached to a name: Dr. Priya Selvamani. *Coherent Low-Frequency Emission Signatures at Class-3 Resonance Zone Anomalies: A Preliminary Survey.* 2024, second-tier journal. Cited four times, all negatively. Institutional affiliation lapsed 2025.

The intruder had already highlighted the current address. An apartment in Back of the Yards.

Yemina put the gray car in gear.

[Beat 19]
The apartment building on 47th Street had a buzzer system that worked on every button except the one for apartment 3F. Yemina pressed it three times on principle, then pressed 3G. A man's voice said "What" and she said "District truancy, I need to reach your neighbor in 3F" and the man said "She's in there, just knock hard" and buzzed her in without further discussion.

She knocked hard. Several seconds of movement inside — the quality of movement that suggested navigating a room where the floor was not fully visible. The door opened.

Dr. Priya Selvamani was forty-four or so, Tamil-Nigerian, five feet three inches, wearing a sleep shirt that had not been slept in recently enough to need changing. She had the eyes of someone who had been reading data for eighteen consecutive hours and had found something that made sleep seem beside the point. Behind her, the apartment was entirely obscured by equipment and printouts. A frequency modulator sat on the kitchen table beside an empty coffee press, repaired in three places with packing tape.

She looked at Yemina's credentials.

"I left six messages with the district liaison over eighteen months," she said. "None returned." A pause. "Finally."

[Beat 20]
"Sit somewhere," Dr. Selvamani said, which was easier said than done. Yemina moved a stack of printouts from a chair and sat. The printouts were twenty iterations of the same frequency chart, each annotated differently.

"17 to 19 Hz," Yemina said. "I found it in the pediatric records — the activity logs under the burnout summaries. Something in my neuretics highlighted it while I was reading."

Selvamani stopped. "Something in your neuretics."

"Not me. It's been there since at least yesterday morning." She watched Selvamani absorb that. "It autocompleted a search that pointed back to the 35th-and-Halsted thin spot. I know that's the source. What I don't know is what happens to the children after the frequency reaches them."

Selvamani had the look of a person who had been preparing a lecture for two years and was now deciding which half to cut. She picked up one of the frequency charts. "It tunes them," she said. "Juvenile neuretics is in active growth — hasn't settled into a primary frequency band. The 17-to-19 Hz emission synchronizes it to the same frequency as the thin spot. The children become receptive. Like a door that's been keyed to a specific lock."

"Forty-seven children are missing," Yemina said. "'Receptive' is doing a lot of work."

Selvamani stopped. "I know that." The flatness of it — she'd been saying it to herself for two years and the saying had worn grooves. "I know."

## Chapter 3

[Beat 21]
"Why children," Yemina asked.

"It isn't choosing children." Selvamani didn't look up from the frequency chart she was annotating. "That's the part nobody wants to hear. It's broadcasting at 17 to 19 Hz — and adult neuretics have been in use too long to receive cleanly. Children's are still calibrating. The growth window closes around twelve." She set the chart down. "Whatever is on the other side of the 35th-and-Halsted threshold is broadcasting to the whole city. The children aren't special. They're just the only ones the frequency can reach."

"As a sound. Like a song nobody wrote."

Selvamani looked up sharply. "You've spoken to the families."

"Three of them."

Something moved in Yemina's neuretics — not her own query. The intruder, quiet for the last hour, had opened the pediatric frequency logs again. It highlighted a single entry across all forty-seven records: the same date, the same twenty-minute window, eighteen months ago. Every child had received the signal the same day. Not gradual. One broadcast.

"There's a second signal." Selvamani pulled a different chart from the nearest stack. "Higher frequency — 72 to 74 Hz, the band neuretics uses for deep query processes. It started appearing fourteen months after the first disappearance." She set the two charts side by side. The primary broadcast had the spread of something aimed at everything. The second was narrower, concentrated, addressed not to the city but to a point within it. "I've been recording it for sixteen months. Every time the second signal appears, the primary adjusts. Modifies." A pause. "Like a conversation."

"Or a contest," Yemina said.

Selvamani looked at her. "I can't confirm that. I can tell you that what I've been measuring doesn't behave like one thing."

Selvamani picked up one of the printouts. She held it for a moment without looking at it — the way you hold something you've carried long enough that the weight is familiar even when the contents aren't. She set it back down on the exact stack she'd taken it from. Her hands were precise about where it landed.

"The families use the same words," she said. "Different languages. Same description." She looked at Yemina. "I've been trying to get anyone to listen to that for two years."

[Beat 22]
"Where are they," Yemina said.

"I don't know."

"I know they went through bleeds — the neuretics burnout pattern is consistent with a transit event, not a sustained exposure. They went somewhere. But every bleed I've mapped shows threshold instability — you can't hold one open long enough to follow, and even if you could, there's no guarantee you'd arrive at the same terminus."

She crossed to the kitchen table and pulled a tablet from under a stack of printouts. She turned it to face Yemina. A GLMZ map, overlaid with bleed markers. Most of them were small — the usual resonance scatter, dozens of minor anomalies flagged in building permits and transit route displays.

At 35th and Halsted, a different marker. Larger. Red.

"Except that one," Selvamani said. "That one has been holding stable at Class-3 for six weeks. Six weeks ago is when—"

"When the first child disappeared," Yemina said.

"When the first child disappeared."

[Beat 23]
She looked at the map for a long time. She overlaid the disappearance locations against it — her neuretics pulled them from the case file, placed them as points. Twenty-two school sites across different districts, all within two kilometers of a minor bleed.

But if you drew a line from each minor bleed toward 35th and Halsted, the lines converged. They didn't branch. They had a center of gravity.

"It's not originating from the minor spots," Yemina said. "They're receiving points. The broadcast is coming from 35th-and-Halsted."

"Yes." Selvamani had clearly worked this out long before today. "The Class-3 site is the source. The minor anomalies are relays. The signal moves through the bleed network. Children near the relay points get tuned first because they're closest, but the transit goes back to the source."

"And the children went to the source."

"I believe so. I haven't been able to confirm it." She looked at the tablet. "Once. I stood at the 35th-and-Halsted threshold for eleven minutes with my modulator, and I felt the pull, but I'm forty-four. My neuretics is fully fixed." She touched her temple. "I felt it working on me and finding nowhere to go. Like trying to tune a radio that's already locked to a station."

Yemina did not say anything. Fifteen years of cross-corp. The provisioning she had never upgraded. The stack she had never been locked into. She had always understood that as versatility.

The intruder had not found a gap. She had just never been closed.

[Beat 24]
Yemina called the Resonance Monitoring Authority from the front steps of the building. The system asked for a corp credential before routing. She gave her contractor registration. There was a two-second pause that probably meant the system had flagged her as non-employee and was deciding where to send that. She was sent somewhere. She selected *mass casualty event, resonance proximity.* She was transferred. She selected *missing persons, confirmed bleed transit.* She was transferred again. She selected *urgent.* The system noted her selection and placed her in a queue. The hold music was a recording of a man explaining that field response teams required a minimum 72-hour advance notice for non-emergency site visits, followed by a recording of a woman explaining how to determine whether your event qualified as an emergency.

She stayed on hold for twelve minutes and left a message: her name, contractor registration, case numbers across eight corp contracts, address, count. Forty-seven children. She spoke clearly and without inflection because inflection gave a listener somewhere to be skeptical.

She ended the call. Below the steps, a child was drawing something on the sidewalk with chalk — a shape that might have been a face, or a map, depending on how you held your head.

She went back inside.

[Beat 25]
That night she sat in her apartment and did not sleep. It wasn't dramatic — just the standard consequence of an open case and neuretics that wasn't entirely hers anymore.

At 2:14 in the morning, the intruder sent her something new. Not a search query. Not a highlighted section of a care record. An image: the 35th-and-Halsted bleed as seen from the intersection — the air doing its wrong thing above the concrete — and overlaid on it, white on gray, a number.

72.

She lay in the dark and thought about what 72 was. Hours. The Resonance Monitoring Authority's minimum response window: 72 hours. She had called them that afternoon. In 72 hours, field teams would arrive. In 72 hours the site would be assessed and the threshold examined and the readings taken, and if she was right and the children were on the other side, the assessment team would likely seal the threshold before anyone went through, because that was what assessment teams were authorized to do with unstable Class-3 thresholds.

In 72 hours the door might be closed.

She wrote the number in her notebook. She circled it. She looked at the ceiling for a while.

Then she called Ren Vasquez.

[Beat 26]
He picked up on the second ring.

She had not worked out what she was going to say. She was going to say *I think I know where the children are* and *I need someone who can feel the threshold before we're standing in it.* She had the words arranged.

"I know where you're going," Ren said, before she'd said anything.

She was quiet for a moment. "How."

"I've been feeling the pull for three days. It's not the classroom anymore." A pause. "It's southwest. Toward 35th."

She told him what she'd found. He listened without interrupting, which told her he'd already worked most of it out from the feeling alone — he was listening to confirm, not to learn. When she finished he said: "When."

"Tomorrow morning. Early."

"I'll be there."

She started to say something about risk. She stopped. He was twenty-eight years old and he had felt two children disappear from his classroom and the tremor in his hands had not stopped in three weeks. He was going to be at 35th and Halsted because the alternative was staying in room 214 with the wrongness still above the empty chair and never finding out if it could be undone.

"Bring warm clothes," Yemina said. "I don't know how the cold works in there."

[Beat 27]
She called Selvamani after she hung up with Ren. Selvamani said "I'm already packing the modulator" before the reason for the call had been stated.

They met at 5:45 AM on the corner of 35th and Normal — a block from the bleed, close enough to feel the edge of it. Ren was there first, standing with his hands in his coat pockets, the tremor visible even from across the street. He had brought coffee in a metal thermos that he shared without being asked.

Selvamani arrived with a backpack and the modulator in its carrying case. She looked at Ren and said "You can feel it." Not a question.

"Since I got here," he said.

"Good. That's going to matter." She set the case down and opened it and began a calibration check. "The modulator will hold the threshold stable for approximately four minutes. Approximately — I've never tested it at full Class-3 resonance. I've tested it at seventy percent." She looked up. "I want you to understand that seventy percent is not a confidence interval. It is a number I arrived at through experiment."

"Understood," Yemina said.

Selvamani nodded and kept calibrating. "There's one more thing. The threshold requires a frequency match to open cleanly — the modulator gets us partway there, but someone needs to hold the right frequency from the inside." She looked at Ren. "That's you."

Ren took his hands out of his pockets. He looked at them. The tremor was there. "I know," he said.

[Beat 28]
They walked the last block at 6:10 AM in the particular silence of a city that had stopped being asleep but wasn't yet awake — the light gray-blue, the streets carrying the sounds of early freight and transit shifts changing over. Selvamani had the modulator case over one shoulder and a portable sensor in her other hand. Ren walked slightly ahead without meaning to.

The 35th-and-Halsted bleed was visible from half a block away.

Yemina had known bleeds her whole life the way everyone in GLMZ knew them — as civic facts, marked hazards, the asterisked notation on transit route displays. The RMA designated them by class, ascending severity, with clear operational parameters: hazard radius, reporting protocols, minimum response window. The designations implied a taxonomy, and a taxonomy implied a thing understood. The city treated them the way it treated crumbling transit infrastructure and flooded underpasses — marked on maps, asterisked in building permits, factored into property assessments. This was the correct administrative response. The administrative response did not mean the thing was understood. It meant the city had learned to work around not knowing.

What a bleed actually was, in the parts the taxonomy did not touch, was the cross-section of a shape that did not fit inside the directions available — one vertex at street level, the other vertices not far but elsewhere in the way that a sound below the threshold of hearing is elsewhere from sounds that can be heard: a matter of instrument, not distance. The nervous system produced a pressure behind the eyes and a frequency in the teeth it had no word for, and then it stopped trying. What fifteen years of incident reports had never contained — what nothing in the operational manual covered — was that the shape was occupied. That something had been watching from the other side of every bleed she had ever documented, longer than the RMA had existed to name them.

She had seen three in person. This one was larger than that. The whole intersection had stopped being itself. Not a shimmer — not the small optical failure of a minor anomaly. The air above the four-way stop had a shape that shouldn't be air's shape: a column from the ground up, maybe twelve meters across, with a geometry her eyes could follow for about two seconds before they stopped being able to hold it. The light moved differently inside it. Not faster or slower — differently, in a direction she didn't have a word for.

"Class-3," Selvamani said quietly, reading the sensor. "Signal output at 18.4 Hz. Coherent."

She looked at both of them. "It's broadcasting right now."

[Beat 29]
Selvamani placed the modulator at the edge of the anomaly — at the point where the air began doing its wrong thing — and switched it on. The effect was not dramatic. The column of wrong light didn't resolve into a door or a frame. What happened was subtler: the pressure behind Yemina's eyes diminished by half, and the frequency she felt in her teeth settled into something regular, like a held note rather than a fluctuation.

"That's stable," Selvamani said, watching the sensor. "Four minutes from the point of entry. Maybe four. I'm staying with the modulator — if I leave it running without a hand on it, the calibration drifts." She looked at Yemina. "Find them fast."

Yemina looked at the threshold. Then at Ren.

"I'll go first," he said. He was already looking at it the way you look at something you've been moving toward for three weeks.

[Beat 30]
Ren walked through the threshold at 6:14 AM with a slight turn of the shoulder, the way you go through a door you're not sure is fully open, and then he was on the other side.

Yemina followed.

The crossing was not what she expected. She had read, in the back halves of a dozen incident reports, that bleed transit felt like pressure reversal — a moment of wrongness and then adjustment, like moving from a room with too much air to one with too little. What she felt instead: the 18.4 Hz in her teeth stopped.

The air temperature dropped by several degrees in one step. It smelled like something sealed — not stale, exactly, but the inside of a thing that had been closed a long time.

For one full second, all forty-seven missing children spoke at once. Not loudly — forty-seven whispers occupying the same space, from mouths she hadn't yet reached, from directions that didn't exist. She understood none of it. The understanding didn't seem to be the point.

Then something from deeper in the space found her neuretics.

It was not the intruder. She knew the intruder's architecture now — its precision, its careful margins, the specific texture of something that had been built over time. This had not been built. This had always been here. It applied itself to her open neuretics with the quality of something very old and not at all interested in whether she survived the examination. Her contractor-grade firewall lasted one second.

Then the contact stopped. Not because it had finished. Because it had been displaced.

She felt the intruder between her and the deeper thing — a deliberate pressure, interposed, held. The intruder had not spoken, had not moved, had only placed itself in the gap.

The frequency she had no reference point for moved through the part of her body she couldn't locate. It lasted a half second. She understood it as: *this far, no further.*

Then she was standing in the place between the frequencies.

## Chapter 4

[Beat 31]
It had no geometry she could hold.

The light came from no direction but cast consistent shadows — all of them pointing the same way, as though something just out of frame held the position of an absent sun. Her neuretics immediately filed a cascade of location errors and then went quiet in a way that felt like surrender.

The children were there.

Forty-seven of them, in a space that accommodated them without any visible walls. They were sitting, standing, moving in the small ways children move when they've been somewhere long enough to have made it their own. Some of them had formed small arrangements — groupings, games she couldn't name — and the space had simply made room for what they needed.

They were not frightened. They had been waiting.

[Beat 32]
Kito Bramley was nine and missing his front tooth and sitting cross-legged in whatever passed for the near part of this space. He looked up at Yemina with the expression of a child who has been somewhere strange long enough to have stopped being scared and started being bored.

"No. My name is Yemina Fola. I've been looking for you." She crouched to his eye level. "Are you hurt?"

[Beat 33]
She stood and looked at the forty-seven children in the space that had no walls. She counted them. Then counted them again.

"Who brought you here," she asked.

Kito looked at her for a moment. "Two things."

"First there was the sound. In my neuretics — in everybody's neuretics, all of us figured that out after we got here. It felt like —" he thought about this, "like a word you almost know. Getting closer. It wanted us to go toward 35th and Halsted. Not in a scary way." He touched the side of his head. "Just where we needed to be."

He glanced at the girl near the back — not Amara, someone she didn't know, older, maybe eleven — and the girl gave the same small headshake, that gesture that meant something between them Yemina had arrived too late to decode. "In here the sound can't reach us. We can still feel it, at the edges. But it can't get in."

"Which thing showed my name," Yemina asked.

"The second one." No hesitation. "The quiet one." A pause. "The sound doesn't know what a name is."

He glanced at the notebook in her hand. "We've been waiting since Tuesday."

A pause. "Did anyone feed my fish?"

She looked at her notebook. At the pen. At the forty-seven children who had known her name before she had known this room existed.

She filed it.

"Time to go," she said, to all forty-seven of them. "Everyone up. We're going home."
