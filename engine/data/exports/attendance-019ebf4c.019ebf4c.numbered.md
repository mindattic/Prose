# Attendance

_In the dystopian sprawl of the GLMZ — the megacity that swallowed the Midwest — children are disappearing and no one has noticed because no one else can see all the connections at once.

Yemina Fola is a cross-corp truancy investigator. Her clearance spans jurisdictions that don't talk to each other, which means she's the only one who can see the pattern: three missing children, different schools, different districts, the same impossible detail in each classroom. An empty chair. And the air above it doing something wrong.

The deeper she looks, the larger it gets. Forty-seven children. Twenty-two months. A frequency buried in routine medical records that no one thought to connect. A researcher who has been trying to get someone — anyone — to listen for two years.

And something already inside her own neuretics, helping her find it. Watching from the other side of her own eyes.

She has seventy-two hours before the authorities arrive and close the door.

Attendance is a story about what happens at the edge of the systems we build to manage the things we don't understand — and the people who work inside those systems when they finally see too much._

[Beat 1]
At six in the morning the Pilsen Veil moved the way it always moved: not like a city waking up but like a machine that had never stopped running, only shifted cadence. The elevated transit above 18th Street carried the early shift toward the manufactories — the cars full, the riders standing because at six the seats were a luxury reserved for the route's far end. Below the line the previous shift was still going home. Contractors in gray-and-blue work shells. A delivery cart riding the painted freight lane at a walking pace because the lane's automation lease had lapsed and nobody on either contract had renewed it. Two students who had been out all night, weighing whether there was enough morning left to sleep before class, deciding there was not, walking slower for having decided.

Yemina drove the seam because the seam was hers. Six years on the line where Arcturus Holdings ran the schools and the transit and the case registries on one side, and Meridian Infrastructure ran the same things under different line items on the other. She didn't have to look at the fixtures to know which side she was on; she could have told you by the paperwork. The transit overhead crossing 18th was a single span, one continuous structure, and it answered to two permit offices — the Arcturus side filed its overhead-transit clearances through District Conveyance, the Meridian side through a municipal-legacy desk that still used the word *trolley* in its forms, and the seam ran straight down the centerline of a bridge that didn't know it had been divided. The schools were worse. On the Arcturus side a school was an asset on an education-services contract, its absences logged to a district attendance registry that flagged a child after three unexcused days. On the Meridian side the same kind of building answered to a welfare-and-minors authority that counted in weeks, not days, because the budget that paid for the counting was smaller. A child could go missing from a desk forty feet from the seam and be a different category of missing depending on which set of forms the desk had been assigned to, and the two authorities reconciled their counts once a quarter, by export, in a format each of them had to convert by hand. She had learned the city the way you learn the inside of a drawer — accurately, without sentiment, in the dark. You didn't admire a drawer. You knew where the thing you needed was, and you knew which corner caught.

The lake was two miles east. You couldn't see it from the corridor streets — the buildings closed the sightline three blocks in — but you could feel the cold it sent inshore. In March it came off the water and moved inland the way water finds the low ground, except the low ground here was the streets themselves, and so the cold didn't arrive from the east the way the map would have it arrive. It arrived sideways. It slid up 19th from the south where the buildings opened, turned the corner at a loading bay, came down the cross street against the one-way arrows, and reached her car from the direction the weather had no business coming from. The contractor car's heater answered it a beat late, the way it answered everything. She felt the cold find the gap at the base of the windshield, a thin seam of it across the backs of her hands on the wheel, and her shoulders drew up a half-inch before she had decided anything. Then she put them back down. The cold wasn't information she could file. It was March, and the lake was where it had always been, and the heater would catch up at the next light or it wouldn't.

She had a caseload. Three names she hadn't met and already couldn't put down: Kito, who was nine; Daria, who was seven; a third whose file she had read twice in the dark before she trusted the timestamps. Nobody upstairs expected her to find anything in them. That was the part her hands had already decided about, before she had — the way they had tightened on the wheel when she read *seven*, the small involuntary refusal of a body that had filed too many of these and wouldn't be filing this set quietly. She didn't examine the feeling. She didn't have a field for it. She only noticed that it was there, and that it had a direction, and that the direction was the same one the case ran in.

She drove.

## Chapter 1

[Beat 2]
The car the Arcturus district contract came with was gray the way contractor-issue things are gray — not painted gray, not designed gray, but accrued gray, the color of a surface that has stopped being argued with. It had been some other color once, under the dust and the door-ding filler and the matte residue that municipal cleaning compound leaves when it is sprayed by a machine that doesn't check whether the panel is already clean. Now it was the gray of a thing that had outlived everyone who had an opinion about it. Yemina Fola drove it through the Pilsen Veil on a Thursday in March and ate a CramIt Bran nutribar that tasted like someone had described chocolate to a person who had never eaten food. She ate it in four bites at four red lights, because that was how the route divided it.

The Veil was a seam. Not a wall — the contracts didn't build walls anymore, walls were a liability somebody had to insure — but a place where one corp's jurisdiction stopped paying for the air and another's began. You could feel it before the signage confirmed it. The scrubbers on the Arcturus side ran hot and constant, so the air had the thin, over-filtered taste of a server room, a cleanliness with no smell in it at all. Then the car crossed under the elevated line, and for the length of two blocks the scrubbing thinned out, and the city came back in: hot oil from a vent stack, wet concrete, the sweet rot of something organic in a drain that nobody on either contract was paid to find. The windshield's particulate counter ticked up nine points in those two blocks and didn't tick back down. That was the seam. The map called it a boundary. Her lungs called it a change of management.

The signage swapped over the same way, one fixture at a time. Arcturus livery was a cool institutional teal, the lettering set in a typeface chosen by a committee to read as trustworthy. On the Meridian side the fixtures went to a worn municipal amber that had been the city's color before the city was a portfolio, half the panels dead, the live ones flickering at a frequency just slow enough to notice. Between the two there was a stretch of poles that belonged to nobody — old mounting brackets with the brand plates pried off, the bolt holes left open like missing teeth. The contracts had divided everything that could be billed and abandoned everything that couldn't.

Overhead, the elevated transit changed its sound. On the Arcturus track the trains ran on maintained rail and made a single clean tone, a held note, the engineered hush of money. Past the seam the rail was older and the joints hadn't been ground true in years, so the trains came through in a stutter — clack, clack, clack-clack — a rhythm with a hitch in it, the sound of a system that still worked but had stopped being kept. She had driven this route enough times to hear the difference without looking up.

She didn't look up. Her hands stayed where they had learned to stay: the left low on the wheel at seven o'clock, the right resting on her thigh, near the wheel but not on it, ready and not committed. It was the posture of someone who had spent a career in rooms where the wrong gesture got noticed and filed.

Behind her eyes, the caseload sat open. Her neuretics — grown hardware, contractor grade, which meant it was always slightly behind — showed her the cross-corp roster for District 14-South in a layer below the road, the way a second sheet shows faint through the first. Seventeen chronic absences. Four registered runaways. Three disappearances. Three was the line her eye stopped on. Three was the line it had stopped on every morning this week, before her coffee, before her hands were warm.

It was there again. The low frequency, infrasonic, the one she had been catching under her contractor neuretics for six weeks now — a hitch in the signal the way the rail overhead had a hitch, a thing her back teeth registered before her forebrain did. It came and went without pattern. She had flagged it twice in her own maintenance log and both times the diagnostic came back clean, and after the second clean result she had stopped flagging it. She set it under the same heading she used for the car's heater and the particulate counter and every other instrument that worked until it didn't: *noted, not acted on.* What she didn't let herself do, because it had nowhere to go and she had work in front of her, was line the frequency up against the calendar and notice that it had started the week the first of her three was logged.

The clearance registry had given the children case numbers 45, 46, and 47. She read the numbers and felt the back of her neck go cool — a registry that ran into the high forties for a problem the city insisted was truancy was a registry that knew something her clearance level didn't. She set the cool feeling where she set everything she couldn't yet act on, low and private, in the place she didn't open in front of people. Then she drove on toward 20th and Halsted with both hands doing exactly as much as the road needed, and not one motion more, because the motions she didn't make were also a kind of record, and she had learned to keep that record clean.

[Beat 3]
The Aldiss Mwangi Community Learning Center occupied the corner of 20th and Halsted like something that had given up on the idea of corners. Yemina saw it from two blocks out and read it the way she read everything: from the bottom up, oldest to newest, because that was the order in which a building told the truth about who had paid for it and when they had stopped.

She slowed the contractor car to the speed at which a structure stops being scenery and becomes a record. Four stories of poured concrete, renewed in stages rather than replaced. That was the first fact, and every other fact hung off it. Nobody had ever knocked the building down and started over, because starting over needed a budget line that named the whole structure at once, and no single authority had ever owned enough of it to write that line. So it had been patched. Patched on the schedule of whichever sub-authority held the maintenance contract in whichever year, which meant the walls were a sediment of municipal decisions, laid down the way silt lays down — not chosen, just deposited, each layer the residue of a season nobody had thought of as historic at the time.

The east face was the oldest, and it faced her as she came up Halsted, so she got the deep strata first. Original aggregate from the early build: gray going on brown, the surface pocked where the river air had eaten the cheaper grade of stone out of the mix and left the pebbles standing proud like teeth in a receded gum. She had stood near enough to that kind of concrete before to know it had a smell — a flat mineral cold, chalk and old rain, a hundred and ninety-odd years of weather that had soaked in and never fully left. It didn't smell like decay. It smelled like something that had been left out so long it had stopped having an opinion.

Above the original course, the middle band: fast-set compound, a generation of repairs poured by crews who had to close the wall before the end of a shift. She knew it on sight by the seams. Fast-set cured in a hurry and shrank as it did, so it had pulled away from the older stone in hairline channels, and the channels had filled with the gray-green biofilm that grew on every north-shadowed surface in the Veil. The compound had its own smell when it was fresh — sharp, almost sweet, an industrial tang that caught at the back of the throat — but these courses were decades dry, and whatever had been volatile in them had long since gassed off into the river wind.

Then the top two floors. The newest work, and the only work she could date without the plaque, because it hadn't finished. A nanite-bond mix, the kind that was supposed to knit itself smooth over a curing window of months, lay across the upper face in a slightly different gray — colder, bluer, the color of a screen on standby. In two or three patches near the roofline the bond had stalled before it leveled, leaving the surface faintly granular, the way skin looks where a wound has scarred but not settled. The contract that paid for that work had run out, or changed hands, or simply lapsed, somewhere in the middle of the cure. She marked the observation without conclusion. A stalled cure wasn't evidence of anything except that money had stopped arriving on a particular Tuesday.

The whole building, read bottom to top, said the same thing the burnout flags would say later in a different vocabulary: the system here hadn't been maintained so much as renewed in arrears, each layer a patch over the failure of the layer below to be permanent. Nothing had been fixed. Things had been kept from finishing their collapse, on a schedule set by budget rather than need. She had seen institutions held together this way her entire career. The competent ones learned to read the seams.

A plaque near the main entrance read ALDISS-MWANGI COMMUNITY LEARNING CENTER — PRINCIPAL FUNDER: ALDISS-MWANGI CAPITAL PARTNERS (2194–2204). The brass had been good once and was dull now, the lettering filmed over the way the rest of the east face was filmed, which told her the plaque belonged to the building and not to the present. Aldiss Mwangi Capital Partners had been absorbed into Arcturus Holdings in 2207 and hadn't existed for eighteen years. The plaque remained because removing it would have cost something, and crediting a funder who no longer existed cost nothing, and the difference between those two figures was the entire reason the name stayed on the wall. The dead held their corners cheaply. That was the only kind of permanence the building had been able to afford.

Yemina parked in the loading zone. She had a placard for that. She left the engine off and sat for a moment with her hands still on the wheel, taking the front of the structure in at rest — not because it needed reverence, but because a building told you less once you were inside it, and she wanted the exterior on record before the interior started arguing with it. Then she gathered her credentials off the passenger seat and got out into the river cold, and the smell of the old concrete came up to meet her exactly as she had known it would.

[Beat 4]
The main entrance had a door that required credentials she had, and the door opened. That was the first and only cooperative thing the building did for her.

The lobby beyond it was the kind of space that had been designed for use and then used past the design. Yemina stepped in and let her senses take a reading before she moved further: floor tile, a pale composite that had once been cream, now the color of old newsprint where traffic had not worn it and colorless where it had. A smell of industrial solvent layered over something warmer - chalk dust, old lunch, the human exhaust of a building that children moved through in quantity. The overhead lighting ran on a mix of ages and types, newer strips dropping a cool white into the gaps where older fixtures had been removed and not replaced, so the room had a quality of attention that wasn't quite even, bright pockets and softer intervals, the way a face looks when someone is trying to seem interested and almost managing it.

*She had been in three buildings this week that were kept open by the same mechanism as this one: not funding, not care, but the cost of closing.*

A woman sat behind the reception desk with the posture of someone who had been waiting longer than the arrival warranted. Not Yemina specifically - just anyone. She was perhaps fifty, with the kind of stillness that came from having learned not to perform readiness for people who were going to ask for things she could not provide. Her name tag read SUMI in block capitals, and below it, in smaller text, FAMILY LIAISON. She did not look up immediately. She was watching a form on the screen in front of her, and she let the moment of not looking up do its work - a quiet, professional calibration, the kind that costs nothing and collects information.

"You'll be the contractor," Sumi said, and the phrasing was exact: not *the* contractor in a way that suggested there were others, but *the* contractor in a way that suggested she had already decided what kind of visit this was going to be, and was giving Yemina one beat to confirm or correct it.

"I am," Yemina said. "I'll want the fourth-grade wing before anything else - Room 214, Mr. Vasquez's room. He was the teacher of record the week Kito Bramley stopped being marked present."

She hadn't asked whether she could. She'd stated where she was going, because she had decided it on the drive over, sometime between the second timestamp and the third. Everyone else on the roster had filed something into the case - the counseling office, the medical registry, the manufacturer. Vasquez had filed nothing. In her experience the person who filed nothing was the person who'd seen the thing before it became a form, and she meant to reach him before the building could arrange him into one.

Sumi's eyes went to a point past Yemina's shoulder - toward the interior corridor, toward a door that hadn't opened yet - and came back. It was a small motion, the kind a person makes when the answer to a question isn't theirs to give. "Let me tell Dr. Achebe you're here," she said, and touched something under the lip of the desk.

Yemina was already turning toward the corridor. She had a room number and a reason to walk to it, and the two together were enough to move on.

[Beat 5]
Dr. Achebe came out with the walk of a man who had decided in advance that this meeting would be brief. There was a gait administrators developed over the years — a forward lean that read as energy and functioned as exit. He was already crossing the waiting area at an angle that would let him pivot back toward his office door without breaking stride, and Yemina marked the angle before she marked the man.

He was sixty-something, built wide, the kind of width that had been muscle and had settled. He wore a blazer over a shirt with a collar that had been ironed that morning with real intention — she noticed the collar because it was the only thing about him that hadn't surrendered. The blazer was tired at the elbows. The lanyard at his chest held a credential card and three others stacked behind it, the way a man accumulates clearances he no longer reads. His face had learned, over decades of school administration, to express concern and unavailability simultaneously, and it was doing both now, which meant the expression was load-bearing rather than felt.

"Ms. Fola. The Bramley case." He said the case name the way people say a thing they have already closed in their own minds. "I've sent everything to your office — the incident report, Kito's attendance record, his guardian's contact." He was already turning back toward his office door, the turn itself an argument. "We were told it's under district review."

"It is," Yemina said. "I'm the district review."

He stopped.

He looked at her the way an administrator looks at something that has arrived outside normal channels — with the expression of a person calculating who, exactly, was responsible for this. The eyes did the work first, a small lateral search, as though the responsible party might be standing somewhere in the room and could be handed the problem. He couldn't find a responsible party. The search closed. He settled back into concern-and-unavailability the way a man lowers himself into a chair he doesn't trust, and the settling cost him a beat he hadn't budgeted.

"Come in, then," he said, because there was nothing else to do with her in a corridor.

The office was small and over-furnished, the furniture older than the building's last renovation by a decade, which told her the renovation budget had reached the public spaces and stopped. A diploma hung framed beside a laminated evacuation map and a corkboard gone soft with the pin-holes of memos long since taken down. On the desk, a coffee mug had gone cold around a film, and beside it a stack of folders sat squared at the corners with the neatness of a man who managed paper because the rest of his job couldn't be managed. There was a smell to the room — toner, instant coffee, and the dry mineral exhaust of a recirculating filter that had been promised replacement and not received it. Behind him, through a window of reinforced glass, the Pilsen Veil's afternoon haze put a flat gray light on everything, the kind that didn't fall from anywhere. It just was, the way the building just was.

He didn't sit, which meant he didn't expect her to. She said it before he reached for the folder: "I need to see Room 214."

He picked up the squared stack and tapped it once against the desk, a gesture of completion, and handed it across.

"Everything's here," he said.

She took it. The top sheet was the incident report, an Arcturus district-services template, the school's name printed across the header beside a sub-authority footer she registered and held: *Student Welfare & Compliance, Pilsen Veil Cluster* — a sub-authority with jurisdiction over forms and none over children, the office that received a thing so that no other office had to. The form was complete. Every field populated. The Date of Last Attendance was filled, the Guardian Notified box checked, the Referred To line typed in clean: *Counseling Services, Aldiss Mwangi CLC*. It was the most finished document she had read all week. That was what it was for. A finished document was a closed one, and a closed one stopped being his.

She turned the page. Kito Bramley's attendance record, the timestamps in a narrow machine column down the left margin. She didn't read them yet. She noted only that they existed, and that the precision the system spent on them — to the minute, to the second — was the same precision it had spent on nothing else about the boy.

"As I say," Dr. Achebe said, watching her read with the patience of a man waiting for a guest to finish a drink so the evening could end, "it's all been documented. The guardian was contacted. The referral was made. Whatever happened with Kito, the relevant person at this point is the counseling office. Not the classroom. I want to be helpful, Ms. Fola, but I'd be sending you down the wrong corridor."

The deflection had a polish to it. Not panic — polish. He had said versions of this sentence many times, to many people who had arrived outside normal channels, and each saying had sanded it smoother. *The relevant person.* As if relevance were a fixed property of a person and not a decision someone in this office had made. He believed it, too, which was the part she marked most carefully. He wasn't hiding anything. He had simply arranged the truth so that no part of it remained his to carry, and he had done it so long ago that the arrangement felt to him like the shape of the world.

His hand drifted toward the lanyard at his chest — not the credential, just the cord of it, a thing to hold — and stopped. He folded the hand back over the other one in front of him.

Yemina squared the folder against her palm and kept it. "Thank you," she said, in the tone she used when a thing had been useful in a way the giver hadn't intended.

[Beat 6]
The conversation lasted three minutes, and Yemina had known inside the first thirty seconds that it would. Dr. Achebe was the kind of administrator who answered every question with the document he had already filed. He talked the way a man talks when he is performing the act of cooperation for an audience he assumes is keeping score somewhere — measured, complete, generous with the things that cost him nothing. He gave her the incident report's filing number again, though it was already in her case packet. He gave her Kito Bramley's guardian contact, which she also had. He gave her his settled professional opinion, which was the only thing she hadn't arrived with: that the relevant person, if there had to be a relevant person, was the counseling office. Not the classroom teacher.

He returned to that twice. The second time he said it more slowly, as if the slowness were a kindness she might be too junior to recognize. The counseling office held the wellness flags. The counseling office had the intake notes. The counseling office was, his cadence implied, the proper channel, the well-lit one, the one that would resolve this without anyone having to be uncomfortable in a hallway.

Yemina listened and cross-referenced him.

She did it the way she cross-referenced everything — not with judgment, just with placement. Achebe went into the category she kept for people who weren't lying and weren't helping, which in her experience was the largest category there was. He believed what he was telling her. That was the thing about a closed door: it didn't have to be locked from malice. It only had to be closed, and confident that it was correct to be closed, and built well enough that pushing on it was the visitor's problem and not the building's. She had spent eleven years learning that the doors which hurt you most were the ones held shut by people who were certain they were doing their jobs.

"I'd like to speak with Mr. Vasquez directly," she said.

"He's in his planning period." Achebe moved — not dramatically, just enough: a half-step that put his shoulder between her and the hallway door, the body making the argument the voice was too polished to make. "I have a duty of care to my staff."

The counseling office. She turned the suggestion over once more, because it was worth turning over, and that was where she felt the shape of why she wouldn't take it. The counseling office was where the flags lived — already documented, already framed, already converted into the kind of paper that closed cases instead of opening them. Whatever the counselor knew, they knew it as a record. They had processed the children into their files weeks ago and moved to the next intake. The counselor would hand her a folder and the folder would agree with every other folder she had been handed, and three minutes after that she would be back in the gray car with nothing the system hadn't already decided was nothing.

The classroom teacher had no folder. The classroom teacher had a memory of a Tuesday — of who sat where, of who came back from the bathroom and who didn't, of the small unrecorded weather of a room full of eleven-year-olds. That was the kind of thing no sub-authority had filed, which was exactly why it might still be true. The gap she was chasing was four to eleven minutes wide and it lived in precisely the interval between the official record and the thing the official record was built to forget. You didn't find that interval in the counseling office. You found it in the room where someone had been standing when it happened.

So: the teacher. Achebe's preference was itself the evidence. When a building steered you toward the documented thing, the undocumented thing was where you went.

"Under the district clearance terms," she said, "classroom witnesses aren't optional. If the school declines to facilitate, I log it as a declined facilitation and schedule a formal deposition at the district office." She uncapped her pen. The click of it was small and very clear in the carpeted hush of the administrative wing. "That takes about three weeks. It generates its own paperwork. The paperwork goes to your district liaison, and to mine."

Something behind his face went still, the way a man goes still when a record he had thought was closed is being reopened in front of him with his name in the header field. Three minutes in a hallway, or three weeks of correspondence carrying his filing number. He was a man who had ironed his collar that morning with real intention; he understood, better than most, the difference between a thing that stayed quiet and a thing that left a trace.

His hands moved once toward the lanyard at his chest — the reflex of a man reaching for his credentials, for the badge that was supposed to settle who got to decide things — and stopped, because the credentials didn't help him here and he knew it. He let the hand fall.

"Room 214," he said.

The corridor past his office had the emptiness of a school between periods: not abandoned, just paused, the silence of a place that would be full again on a bell. The light came from long ceiling panels tuned to a daylight the Pilsen Veil's actual sky hadn't offered in decades, and it lay flat and even over a floor someone had buffed before dawn. The whole wing smelled of the cleaning compound they used in institutions like this — a citrus engineered to read as clean rather than to smell of anything that grew, the scent of a building insisting on its own hygiene. Lockers in a color chosen by committee. A hallway display about attendance, of all things, its laminated letters curling at one corner where the adhesive had given up.

She read the room numbers as she passed them, the way she read everything, and she let Achebe close behind her in her mind the way she had let the school door close behind her at the front: noted, dated, set in its place. A closed door was still data. It told you which way the building didn't want you to walk. She walked the other way.

[Beat 7]
It was lunch period. Room 214 held twenty-three empty chairs and one teacher.

She counted them out of habit, and the count gave her the first thing about the room that didn't fit. Twenty-three. The chairs were the molded-composite kind districts bought by the thousand in the 2210s, child-sized, the seats worn pale at the front edge where heels dragged, and they sat at desks pushed into clusters of four — pods, the manufacturer's literature called them, designed to encourage what the literature called collaborative learning. Twenty-three didn't divide into pods of four. Five clusters of four made twenty. The remaining three desks sat alone against the window wall, not grouped, not quite exiled, the arrangement of a room that had started the year at one number and arrived at lunch on this day at another. Somebody had pulled chairs out of pods and not put them back. The empty spaces in the clusters were the shape of children who had been there at the start and weren't there now, and the room held that shape the way a coat holds the shape of the body that took it off.

Ren Vasquez was twenty-eight or so, Korean-Colombian by appearance, with the kind of face that defaulted to openness the way some faces defaulted to suspicion. He wasn't in the break room. She had passed the break room on the way up — she made a point of the route a building wanted her to take and then noted where it differed from the route people actually took — and the break room had been full of the ordinary noise of a lunch period, three other adults and the smell of reheated food and a screen running something with a laugh track. He wasn't there. He was here, at his desk, in the room with the empty chairs, during the one part of the day he wasn't needed in it.

He was sitting at his desk and not doing anything. Not grading, not reading, not using his neuretics. He was just sitting, facing the empty pods, in the posture of a man keeping a room company. His hands rested on the desk surface and weren't quite still. A fine tremor, low and continuous, the kind a person stops noticing in himself until someone arrives to be noticed in front of, and when he saw Yemina in the doorway he became aware of it and put his hands in his lap, under the desk, where she could no longer see them. He didn't apologize for it. He simply removed it from the conversation before the conversation started.

"District truancy," Yemina said, and held up her credentials long enough for him to focus and not a second longer. "I'm sorry for the timing. I'll be quick."

"It's fine."

He said it like a reflex, the two words a person produces when the alternative is the rest of the sentence. His eyes had gone past her shoulder to the hallway, then come back. He had the look of someone who had been waiting for this without knowing what *this* would be, and was relieved and not relieved that it had a lanyard and a notebook and an apology about the timing.

She didn't sit in one of the child chairs. They were the obvious place, the chairs closest to him, and they were eight inches too low for an adult and built to put a grown person at a supplicant's height, and she had spent enough years being put at heights to know what a chair was doing before she sat in it. There was a side-table chair near the door — an adult chair, slightly taller, the kind kept for visiting administrators and parents and the occasional district contractor. She took that one. It placed her near the exit and gave her the room in her sightline: the teacher, the pods, the three ungrouped desks at the window, the bin of devices charging in a row. She set her notebook on her knee. She didn't take out the pen yet. The pen, she had found, changed what people said; she let him have the first part without it.

"Tell me what you saw," she said.

She watched him decide where to start, and watched the deciding cost him something, and noted that too — not in the notebook, which was still closed, but in the place she kept the things that weren't yet facts. Then he looked up from his own hands and looked at her directly, for the first time since she'd sat down, and she registered what was in the look before she could stop herself registering it: it wasn't the look of a witness deciding how much to give an investigator. It was the look of a person checking whether the investigator was the kind of person who would still be there at the end of a hard sentence. He was checking her the way she checked rooms — from the bottom, oldest to newest — and she understood, with the cold clarity she reserved for things she couldn't yet file, that this was a man who had told this story before and had been failed by every previous telling.

Her chest did a thing she didn't authorize. Not large — a single involuntary tightening, low, the body making an annotation the form had no field for. She held it where it was and kept her face level and let him find the answer to the question he was asking about her, because the question was fair and the only answer she could give was to stay in the room and not look away from what he was about to say.

The room held its shape around them. Twenty-three chairs. Three of them pulled out of their pods and set alone against the gray light at the window, where a person had moved them and not moved them back, for a reason that was either nothing at all or the entire reason she was sitting in an administrator's chair above a service court at lunch, with her pen still capped, waiting.

[Beat 8]
Ren talked. He'd clearly told this before — his voice had the flat quality of a story that had been rehearsed not for polish but for survival, the way you tell something over and over until it stops meaning what it meant when it happened.

It was the third Tuesday. He knew it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays were independent reading and he had a memory of the room being quiet in the way Tuesdays were quiet, the HVAC ticking somewhere in the ceiling, the radiator under the windows making its one warm clicking sound. He gave the date the way a man gives a thing he has checked too many times — not reciting it, but watching her to see whether she would write it down. She wrote it down. Something in his shoulders moved a quarter-inch lower.

Kito Bramley was nine. Third row, second seat. He'd been present at the start of class. He'd raised his hand once, a question about the assignment — whether you could use the same word twice in the same sentence if you meant two different things by it. Ren had said yes, you could, that was called something, he'd have to look it up. He never looked it up. He told Yemina that part as though it were the part that mattered: that he had promised a nine-year-old he would look something up and then the nine-year-old wasn't there to be told. Kito had gone back to his work. And then — somewhere in the twenty minutes of independent reading that followed — the chair was empty.

"I didn't see him stand," Ren said. "I wasn't watching every student every second. Maybe he—"

"What did you see," Yemina asked.

Ren was quiet for a moment.

"I saw the room afterward," he said. "I can tell you about the room."

He could. He told her about the room the way you describe a place you have walked through every night for months in the dark behind your eyes. Twenty-two heads down over the reading tablets. The empty chair in the third row not registering, at first, because an empty chair in a classroom is the most ordinary thing there is — a bathroom, the nurse, a guardian pickup nobody flagged. The chair he had decided was a bathroom break for forty minutes. The end-of-period chime. The line forming at the door for lunch, and the count he ran the way he always ran it, and the count coming up one short, and the slow turn back to the third row to find the seat still empty, and the lunch tray Kito hadn't collected still on the side counter with his name strip on it.

He kept the name strips. He said this without being asked, in the same flat voice. He had a drawer. He had Kito Bramley's, and he had two others.

"Dembe," he said. "And a girl, Priya. Different rooms. Not mine. But I knew them — you know all of them after a few years, the ones who come early because home isn't quiet." He gave the names the way you offer something you're not sure you're allowed to give away. Not case numbers. Not the clearance-registry designations she had read off her caseload that morning — 45, 46, 47, three integers assigned by a system that had never met them. His names. Classroom names. The names you call across a crowded hallway. "I know the office calls them something else. The office sends it up as a code. But I had them in a room. They were—" He stopped. He didn't finish the sentence, because the only honest end to it was a tense, and the tense was the problem.

He had tried to report it. He said this in a way that made clear it was the part of the story he had told the most, and the part that had done the least. He had filed with the building first — Dr. Achebe's office had logged the resonance incident, the district line, the thing that went to the Anomalous Activity Monitoring Authority four months ago and produced a confirmation number and nothing else. He had filed with the teachers' clearinghouse. He had walked it, twice, to the district truancy desk in person, taken the train and stood in line, and a clerk had explained to him with real kindness that disappearances and absences went to different sub-authorities, and a child logged under absence couldn't be re-coded to disappearance without a guardian initiating the petition, and Kito's guardian was seventy-three and had already initiated something, somewhere, that hadn't connected to the thing Ren initiated. The two reports were in two systems. The systems didn't look at each other. He had drawn the connection himself, three names on a sheet of paper — he had used paper, he said, because he didn't want it in his neuretics — and he had carried the sheet to four different desks and watched four people look at it and not be the desk it was for.

"There's no field," he said. He said it like a man who had found the exact shape of the wall by walking into it in the dark enough times. "For any of it. There's a field for absent. There's a field for the air doing what it did. There's nowhere the two of them go together. So they don't."

She had set her pen down. She didn't do this during interviews. She stood and crossed to the third row.

The second seat was empty the way all chairs are empty. She stopped at its edge — the wrongness reached her before the thought did — she had already stopped. She didn't touch it. She stood there the way you stand somewhere you have arrived at before you meant to. When she reached for a reason to be standing there — something she could write in her notes, a protocol step she'd overlooked — she didn't find one. She didn't go back.

She went back to her chair. She picked up her pen.

"Go on," she said.

[Beat 9]
"The air above his chair went wrong." He said it to his hands in his lap. "But not when he left. Not while the students were still in the room. It was after lunch — after everyone had gone. The room was empty and I was standing there and I looked at the third row."

He stopped. Outside, somewhere down the block, a delivery drone came in low over the Pilsen Veil and the windows took the sound and gave it back a half-second late, the way old glass does. The classroom held the quiet the way a held breath holds it. Yemina didn't fill the silence. She had learned, across fifteen years and an unkind number of rooms like this one, that a witness who is allowed to stop will eventually start again, and that what they say when they start again is the part they were deciding whether to say.

"Above his seat. Not a shimmer. Not heat-shimmer." Ren's mouth worked around it, hunting. "It was more like — if sound could make a shape. A frequency you don't hear. Something behind the sternum registers it first — a pressure in the air that isn't coming from the air. It was there above the seat, just hanging. Like something he'd left behind that I didn't have words for."

He looked up. "I know how that sounds."

"I've heard it before," Yemina said.

She had. And she felt it now while he said it — the same place he'd named, behind the sternum, a pressure that had nothing to do with this room — her own body answering his words before her training could decide what to do with them. She let her face stay where it was. That was a thing she could still do. The face was the last instrument she had complete control of, and she kept it level the way you keep a hand steady over a cut you don't want a child to be frightened by.

Witnesses always found different words for the same thing, and the words were never the evidence; the agreement underneath the words was the evidence. *Pressure. A wrongness. A note the room played.* A maintenance worker in Back of the Yards had rubbed his thumb across his knuckles the whole interview as if checking they were still there. A woman in West Lawn had watched her husband walk out of the frame of their kitchen and not come back into it.

She had run that last one herself, three years ago. The woman had played the kitchen recording for her — cheap ceiling unit, fish-eye lens, the husband at the counter and then a doorway where the husband had been — and played it again, and again, on no system but her own grief. Afterward Yemina had sat in the gray car in the West Lawn cold and hadn't started the engine for a while; she couldn't now have said how long. There was no field on any form for what happens to a person who watches that recording forty times and understands, on the fortieth, that a man can simply stop being one of the directions a room has. She had never filed it because there was nowhere to file it. Fifteen years, and that was the one thing she had kept instead of logged. She put it back where she kept it now, and the pressure behind the sternum receded the way it did once she stopped looking at it straight. Then her hand went to work, because her hand knew the next correct thing even when the rest of her was still in West Lawn.

She wrote: *Atmospheric anomaly consistent with prior cases. Echo at seat, not transit. Transition point unknown.*

Three sentences. She looked at them. The first was true and would survive any review. The second was the part that mattered and the part that would mean nothing to whoever read it next, because the distinction — echo where the thing had found the boy, not the shadow where he had stepped through — lived in a vocabulary the form didn't contain and her superiors didn't want to learn. The third was the honest one. *Transition point unknown.* It meant he had crossed somewhere this room couldn't show her, and she didn't write the fourth sentence, the one forming behind the sternum alongside the pressure: *and I am not going to find it by standing in this classroom.* That one went where the kitchen recording went.

What she didn't write was the boy's name in any field that would generate a follow-up to Ren. What she didn't write was the word she had for the look on his face — the look of a man who had decided to keep teaching in a room that had shown him the edge of the world. There was no protocol step for that and she didn't invent one.

She looked at him. She had a close for this. She used it at the end of every witness interview — the case number, the district line, the boilerplate about follow-up contact — and she had given it thirty or forty times this year alone, in rooms warmer and colder than this one, and the giving of it was usually the moment her attention had already left for the car and the next address. She gave him the case number. She gave him the district line.

Her attention didn't leave the room. That was the thing she noticed about herself, the way she noticed an inconsistent timestamp: she was still here. The case number was a door she usually walked through. This time she heard herself say it and stay.

She closed her notebook. She didn't stand yet. The empty chair in the third row was still at the edge of her vision, second seat, and she didn't look at it directly, because she had already learned in this room that looking at it straight brought the pressure forward, and she needed her hands steady and her voice level for the one decision she had made while her pen was moving. Three names. Three timestamps that didn't match three flags by four to eleven minutes. And now an echo at a seat and a transition point that wasn't in this room. The next correct thing wasn't to file and drive to the next address. The next correct thing was the other school — the second name, the one whose timestamps she had only read and never stood inside — and she was going to stand inside it before she let anyone close this case as truancy.

She put the notebook in her coat. The decision had arrived before she finished naming it — the same way her feet had refused the door at the front, the same way her hand had stopped her at the edge of the chair. She let it stand.

[Beat 10]
"Did you file a resonance incident report?" Yemina asked.

It was a closing question. She asked it the way she asked all of them, in the flat after-tone that signaled the interview was nearly over and there would be a form. She had her pen against the page before he answered, because there was always a number to write after this question, a confirmation code or a district filing reference, and she wanted to be ready to write it down and leave.

Ren was quiet for the wrong length of time.

She knew the right length. A man who has filed a report says yes in about a second, because the report is a thing he did, an object that exists, and recalling it costs him nothing. A man who hasn't filed one says no in about a second too. The wrong length is the one in between — the length of a man deciding what a true answer would cost him.

"The district filed one," he said. "Dr. Achebe's office."

"I meant you."

"I'm not on any registries."

He said it cleanly, the way a person says a fact. But she had been reading the things people put under their facts for fifteen years, and what was under this one wasn't a fact. It was a request. The way he said it meant *I need to not be on any registries.* He hadn't said it to inform her. He had said it to find out what she was going to do.

She wrote in her notebook. She kept her hand low and the page tilted toward the window, the old habit, so that what she wrote stayed hers until she decided otherwise.

In the lower right of her vision the mandatory disclosure field stood open in her neuretics, a pale framed prompt the district pushed to her the instant a witness made an unregistered-sensitivity statement. *Subject statement indicates possible unreported psionic capacity. File to Arcturus Registry Compliance? Y/N.* The field waited. It would wait as long as she let it; the system was patient about this, because the system had decided that a teacher who could feel a frequency he hadn't reported was a thing that belonged in a column. One confirmation and Ren would become a row in a table she would never see again, routed to an office that had no reason to be gentle and no field for the fact that he had told her at all.

She closed the field. She didn't file it. The prompt folded down and left a faint afterimage, the way a bright thing does, and then that was gone too, and there was no record anywhere that it had been offered to her. That was the part the system was careful about. It logged the offer to her and her answer to the offer; it didn't log a non-answer. A closed field left no trace. She had used that absence before. She used it now.

She was closing the notebook — her thumb already on the cover, the interview already, by every procedure she followed, over — when he said:

"I moved his desk. Before the other students came back. I told them it was maintenance."

He was looking at the third row.

She stopped with the notebook half shut. She didn't look where he was looking. She looked at him looking, which was a different thing and the thing her work was actually made of. A man doesn't move a dead child's desk and lie to a room of children about why unless the empty seat has become unbearable to him in a way he can't say out loud and can't leave standing. He hadn't told her this to add it to the report. There was nothing in it for the report. He had told her because it was the truth he had been carrying alone since the morning the seat went empty, and she was the first person who had sat in the room and asked him questions as though the room mattered.

What she had written, low and private and tilted from the light, was: *Teacher possibly psionic. Has not self-reported.* And under it, after a space, in the same hand: *Protect.*

It wasn't a procedural notation. There was no protocol that produced that word. It was the kind of thing she didn't write, the thing she had spent fifteen years not writing into margins, because a margin is still a record and a record still goes somewhere. She looked at it. She didn't cross it out. She let it stand on the page in her own hand, and she shut the notebook over it.

She stood.

Then she sat back down. The chair was a child's chair, low, and the motion of lowering herself back into it was undignified and she did it anyway, because what she was about to say wasn't something a person should say standing over someone, on her way out the door.

"There are two more children," she said. "Different schools. The same thing — the air went wrong, and then they were gone."

She didn't normally tell witnesses this. Case protocol was explicit: a witness who learns his incident is one of a pattern stops being a witness and starts being a person who wants to know things, and a person who wants to know things contaminates the next interview. She had recited that protocol to herself in cars outside other schools. She broke it now in a low chair in a quiet room, deliberately, the way she did everything.

"I'm telling you so you know this wasn't something that happened to Kito specifically," she said. "It wasn't your classroom."

It was the only thing she had to give him that the system wouldn't later be able to find and take back. Not the closed disclosure field — that was an absence, and an absence protects no one, it only fails to harm them. This was different. This was a fact she was handing him on purpose: *you did not do this.* She watched it reach him.

He looked at the third row for a long time.

"Okay," he said.

Then, quietly, without raising his eyes from the empty desk: "Thank you."

He said it to the desk, not to her, which was how she knew he meant it for her — the look away, the lowered voice, a man giving away something private to the only room in the building where it was safe to give it. She had been thanked ten thousand times in her career with the standard courtesy of people cooperating with a process. This was the other thing. This was a person acknowledging that someone had chosen, in a small way, to put him before the form.

She left.

At the door she looked back once. Not at him. At the third row — at the desk he had moved and told the children was maintenance, sitting now where he had put it, in line, indistinguishable from the others, which was the whole point of moving it and the reason it would never be indistinguishable to him again. Then, because the desk drew her eye, she saw the rest of him in the same glance. The hands she had noticed at the start of the hour, folded too still on the table: they were on the desk surface again, and they were trembling again, now that the questions had stopped and there was no one left in the room to be steady for. He didn't know she was still in the doorway. He had already let go of the part of himself that had answered her, and what was under it had come back up into his hands.

She had nowhere to file that either.

She turned and walked into the hallway.

[Beat 11]
She was halfway down the corridor when it caught up with her.

It didn't arrive as a thought. It arrived first as a wrongness in the body — the cold that lives between the shoulder blades, the one that meant a record she had already closed was wrong and she had moved on past the line where it failed. She had felt it a hundred times over a desk and learned to trust it before she could name it. Her feet kept their pace. The floor was the old institutional tile they'd stopped making decades ago, scuffed to a dull shine down the center where forty years of small shoes had walked the same line, and her own steps fell into that worn channel without her choosing it. Behind a closed door a teacher was counting a class back into order, the flat patient cadence of an adult who has done it ten thousand times. Warm dust off the heating element. A cafeteria two halls away. Ordinary, all of it. The cold between her shoulders didn't care.

What had set it off was small, the way the things that mattered usually were. Ren's sentence, the one she had cross-referenced without looking at it twice: *the air went wrong, and then they were gone.* She had said it herself, to him, in that order, as if the two halves were one event. She had been saying it in that order for six months.

For six months she had found the same mark above the same kind of empty chair — Back of the Yards, then Rogers Park, now here — and cross-referenced each one as the event itself. As the whole of it. The mark meant *gone*, and *gone* was where her pen stopped.

She slowed. Not on purpose.

A tuning is not a vanishing.

She had had the word the whole time. She had even written it, in the clinical shorthand the scanner reports used: *calibration print, residual, low intensity, long duration.* You calibrate an instrument. You tune a thing toward a purpose. A tuning wasn't the end of a process — it was the patient front half of one, something working on these children over weeks, finding the frequency and holding it and bringing it true. She had found the print of that work three times and cross-referenced it three times and never once asked the only question the word demanded.

Tuned for what.

The cold spread down her arms. She was aware of her own pulse in her wrists, faster than the corridor warranted, the small dumb honesty of a body that had understood before she let herself, and she made herself keep walking, because there was nothing she could do with fear in a hallway except let it slow her down.

You don't spend six weeks bringing a child to a frequency in order to make her disappear. Disappearing takes no tuning at all; the city does it to people for free, by the thousand, and never leaves a print to mark the spot. The print meant intent. The print meant aiming. And an aimed thing goes somewhere.

She had logged the runway as the crash. The flags didn't mark where the children ended — they marked where the children had been *found*, by whatever was tuning them, and the finding came first. A tuned child doesn't vanish from a chair. A tuned child gets up, and walks, and the chair is empty after. That sentence had a direction in it. It had a destination she had never thought to ask about, because in her version of events the children had never gone anywhere — they had simply been, and then not been, and she had built six months of clean paperwork on top of that full stop without ever turning it into a comma.

She had never asked where they walked.

The thought sat in her like swallowed ice. She kept her face still — there were doors with glass in them along this stretch, and a face was a record too, one she didn't file but others read. Three crossings. Three locations. If a tuned child walks, it walks *toward* something, and three departures toward the same something wouldn't scatter. They would lean. Three points laid down on one map would point, and she had never once laid them down together to see which way.

She had the timestamps. She had the addresses. And in her neuretics she had the contact log from every district she'd touched — six months of it, every entrance and exit scan the buildings kept as a matter of course. The buildings logged everyone. They logged *everything*, dumbly, indiscriminately, which was the one mercy of a system that didn't understand what it was recording: somewhere in that indifferent traffic was the last interior scan of a child who had stood up from a chair and walked, because a school doesn't stop tracking a body just because a teacher has written *absent* in a different field.

The lunch period hadn't ended yet. She could hear it — the held breath of a building between bells, the sound a place makes when it is full of children who are briefly not anyone's problem. She still had time, and there was a record she had walked past for six months and never once thought to pull.

[Beat 12]
She found a bench outside the main office, the kind installed to give parents somewhere to sit while waiting for their children to be brought out — hard wood, no back, facing a corkboard layered with permission slips and a fire evacuation route that hadn't been reprinted since the building changed its stairwell configuration. She sat and pulled up the interior scan logs on her neuretics, not the district summary she'd been working from but the raw feed, the dumb indiscriminate kind, the kind a building produces because its systems were set to log and no one ever told them to stop. Northside Elementary logged by zone, every zone, and it had been logging since the school year opened.

*Kito's scan cleared the east wing at 10:51. Not the exit. The east wing.*

She checked Back of the Yards next, then Rogers Park, pulling the timestamps she'd entered into her own case notes months ago without asking what they meant. The children had left their classrooms. She had that. She had logged it as the event and never followed it. But the buildings had kept going, because buildings didn't decide when a story ended — they just ran the next line of tape. Kito's last interior scan placed him not at a door but at a junction on the east corridor, the one that turned toward the maintenance level. Twelve seconds later: nothing. Not an exit scan. Not a next zone ping. Twelve seconds from last contact to silence, in the interior of a building he had not left through any door the system could name.

She checked Rogers Park. Same gap, different corridor. Different maintenance access, same directional logic. Back of the Yards was seven months ago and the scan retention window for that district was ninety days — that one was gone, scrubbed by schedule, not design. She would never know if it matched because she hadn't pulled it when she still could have.

*You had ninety days. You had ninety days and you wrote 'residual' and closed the file.*

The bell rang. The building exhaled around her, doors and footsteps and the low surge of children released back into the corridors, and she sat on the bench with a fire evacuation route in front of her that pointed the wrong way and a map in her neuretics that now had two points instead of three, and two points was a line, and a line was already more than she had walked in with.

## Chapter 2

[Beat 13]
In the hallway a class was changing periods — twenty kids, eight and nine years old, moving the way children move in corridors when they've been corrected enough to know the rules exist but not enough to stop testing them. One stepped directly in front of her and overcorrected hard against the wall, apology coming out mostly breath. Another held eye contact the beat too long — the assessing look children give to unfamiliar adults: what kind, and is that kind dangerous.

She had just pulled three children's last walk out of three buildings. She couldn't entirely stop seeing the lines now, laid over the ordinary hallway, all of them leaning the same way.

One girl near the back had a shoe untied. She looked at Yemina. Then she looked — without turning her head, without any visible reason — at the cinderblock wall on the southwest side of the corridor, where there was nothing: no door, no window, no poster. She held it. Her face had the mild, faraway attention of someone listening to a song two rooms away and deciding whether they liked it.

Yemina followed the girl's eyeline to the blank wall and, past it, in her mind, to the bearing she had just drawn three times on a map. South and west. The same direction the lines leaned.

The girl blinked, found her shoe, crouched to tie it, and was a child again.

Yemina kept walking. She didn't write it down. There was nothing to write that a record would accept: a girl had looked at a wall. But she had seen, for one second, the thing the records only implied — a child who hadn't gone yet, already turned toward where she would go.

The corridor fed her back to the front of the building, where the institution kept its manners. A visitor didn't simply leave the Aldiss Mwangi Community Learning Center. A visitor was signed out, the way a borrowed thing was signed back in, so that the record could show the building had been whole the whole time and was whole still.

The reception android was waiting at the desk where she had left it, because it had no reason to be anywhere else. It had been designed sometime in the 2190s to read as warm, by the standards a committee had agreed warmth meant thirty years ago — a soft jaw, a slight habitual tilt of the head, hands kept open and visible on the counter. From here, in 2226, the design didn't read as warm. It read as a guess about warmth that had been correct once and hadn't been updated, the way a photograph of someone's idea of a kind face wouldn't age into kindness. The eyes tracked her at a measured speed that was meant to be unhurried and landed instead on the far side of patient.

"You're departing," it said. Not a question. It already had the answer; it was offering her the chance to make the answer official.

"I am."

It turned the sign-out slate to face her without being asked, the motion smooth and slightly too slow, a courtesy paced for a calmer century. The screen held her own entry from ninety minutes ago, the timestamp she had written when she arrived. Below it, a blank field, and the cursor in it, waiting.

She signed. She watched the clock tick the departure stamp as her credential cleared — the exact minute, recorded, true. The building would now be able to say, accurately and forever, that the cross-district investigator had entered at one time and left at another and that in the interval nothing had been removed from it that the building knew about. The record would be complete. The record was always complete. That was the thing she had stopped trusting first.

"Thank you for visiting," the android said. "The children's safety is our shared responsibility."

It was a line written for parents at pickup, smoothed by ten thousand repetitions into something with no edges left, and it produced in her a small involuntary cold that started below the sternum and was gone before she had finished registering it. She held still until it passed. Then she set it where she set everything — not what she felt, but what had caused it: a sentence about safety, recited by the only thing in the building that had been present for every disappearance and would never be asked what it saw, because no one had ever built it to be asked.

"Thank you," she said, because the exchange had a shape and the shape closed here.

The android returned its hands to the counter, open, visible, and resumed waiting for whoever the next arrival would be.

The front door was the heavy kind, glass in a frame built to look reassuring from the inside and immovable from the outside, and it released her with a pneumatic exhale and a click of the maglock re-engaging behind her before she had cleared the threshold by a full step. The building sealed itself the instant it could, the way a held breath is let go: not rudely, just completely. On the inside the children were changing periods. On her side of the glass there was the Pilsen Veil at noon and a gray car waiting at the curb where she had left it, and the click of the lock was the only thing the building would record about her leaving that meant anything, and it would record it as ordinary.

[Beat 14]
She sat in the gray car.

It had stopped being argued with years ago, and the inside of it had the climate of a vehicle that lived its life parked in sun: the dashboard exhaled a warm plastic smell, vinyl and the ghost of the cleaner the motor pool used, a chemical lemon that never quite arrived. The driver's seat had worn through its texture at the two points where a body bears down — the left hip, the right shoulder blade — so that she sat, always, in the shallow molds of every shift she had ever logged in it. Midday heat sat in the cabin the way it sits in any cheap car at lunch: not punishing, just present, a low pressure against the skin she'd stopped noticing and started carrying. She left the windows cracked two fingers. She didn't run the climate. The contract paid for the car. It didn't pay for the car to be comfortable.

The Pilsen Veil moved around her the way neighborhoods moved at lunch — sidewalk foot traffic loosening, delivery carts running the painted lane two abreast, a vendor's cart venting steam she could smell through the gap in the window, fried dough and engine grease in the same breath. And a repair drone circling a building at 18th Street that had been circling it for three weeks. Same altitude. Same orbital radius. Logging correctly every time she queried it: *active inspection, Arcturus infrastructure.* A Rider somewhere — body left behind in a chair, slack, breathing on its own — inhabiting the machine through three weeks of corridor passes. She had flagged it twice. Both times it resolved clean, the way everything in this jurisdiction resolved clean, the resolution arriving faster than the question deserved. She had stopped flagging it. She noted, instead, that it was still there. Noting was the part she controlled.

She opened the three case files on her neuretics.

It hadn't become anything. It was still just the car.

She got in and drove it home, the long way, past the seam, because the seam was hers and driving it had never once required a reason beyond that.

The chalk letters on the sidewalk outside the AAMA building washed away in a rain two days later, or someone hosed the walk down, or a child came back and dragged a shoe through them on the way past — she never found out which, because she had no further reason to be on those steps, and she had stopped, this month, checking on things she had no further reason to check on. The pattern of twenty-two sites and twenty-two months remained, as far as she knew, unaggregated anywhere but in her own closed file, a fact that existed and mattered and had nowhere higher to go, the way most of the true things she'd found in fifteen years had nowhere higher to go.
