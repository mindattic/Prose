# Attendance

_In the dystopian sprawl of the GLMZ — the megacity that swallowed the Midwest — children are disappearing and no one has noticed because no one else can see all the connections at once.

Yemina Fola is a cross-corp truancy investigator. Her clearance spans jurisdictions that don't talk to each other, which means she's the only one who can see the pattern: three missing children, different schools, different districts, the same impossible detail in each classroom. An empty chair. And the air above it doing something wrong.

The deeper she looks, the larger it gets. Forty-seven children. Twenty-two months. A frequency buried in routine medical records that no one thought to connect. A researcher who has been trying to get someone — anyone — to listen for two years.

And something already inside her own neuretics, helping her find it. Watching from the other side of her own eyes.

She has seventy-two hours before the authorities arrive and close the door.

Attendance is a story about what happens at the edge of the systems we build to manage the things we don't understand — and the people who work inside those systems when they finally see too much._

[Beat 1]
At six in the morning the Pilsen Veil moved the way it always moved: not like a city waking up but like a machine that had never stopped running, only shifted cadence. The elevated transit above 18th Street carried the early shift toward the manufactories — the cars full, the riders standing because at six the seats were a luxury reserved for the route's far end. Below the line the previous shift was still going home. Contractors in gray-and-blue work shells. A delivery cart riding the painted freight lane at a walking pace because the lane's automation lease had lapsed and nobody on either contract had renewed it. Two students who had been out all night, weighing whether there was enough morning left to sleep before class, deciding there was not, walking slower for having decided.

Yemina drove the seam because the seam was hers. Six years on the line where Arcturus Holdings ran the schools and the transit and the case registries on one side, and Meridian Infrastructure ran the same things under different line items on the other. She didn't have to look at the fixtures to know which side she was on; she could have told you by the paperwork. The transit overhead crossing 18th was a single span, one continuous structure, and it answered to two permit offices — the Arcturus side filed its overhead-transit clearances through District Conveyance, the Meridian side through a municipal-legacy desk that still used the word *trolley* in its forms, and the seam ran straight down the centerline of a bridge that didn't know it had been divided. The schools were worse. On the Arcturus side a school was an asset on an education-services contract, its absences logged to a district attendance registry that flagged a child after three unexcused days. On the Meridian side the same kind of building answered to a welfare-and-minors authority that counted in weeks, not days, because the budget that paid for the counting was smaller. A child could go missing from a desk forty feet from the seam and be a different category of missing depending on which set of forms the desk had been assigned to, and the two authorities reconciled their counts once a quarter, by export, in a format each of them had to convert by hand. She had learned the city the way you learn the inside of a drawer — accurately, without sentiment, in the dark. You didn't admire a drawer. You knew where the thing you needed was, and you knew which corner caught.

The lake was two miles east. You couldn't see it from the corridor streets — the buildings closed the sightline three blocks in — but you could feel the cold it sent inshore. In March it came off the water and moved inland the way water finds the low ground, except the low ground here was the streets themselves, and so the cold didn't arrive from the east the way the map would have it arrive. It arrived sideways. It slid up 19th from the south where the buildings opened, turned the corner at a loading bay, came down the cross street against the one-way arrows, and reached her car from the direction the weather had no business coming from. The contractor car's heater answered it a beat late, the way it answered everything. She felt the cold find the gap at the base of the windshield, a thin seam of it across the backs of her hands on the wheel, and her shoulders drew up a half-inch before she had decided anything. Then she put them back down. The cold wasn't information she could file. It was March, and the lake was where it had always been, and the heater would catch up at the next light or it wouldn't.

She had a caseload. Three names she hadn't met and already couldn't put down: Kito, who was nine; Daria, who was seven; a third whose file she had read twice in the dark before she trusted the timestamps. Nobody upstairs expected her to find anything in them. That was the part her hands had already decided about, before she had — the way they had tightened on the wheel when she read *seven*, the small involuntary refusal of a body that had filed too many of these and wouldn't be filing this set quietly. She didn't examine the feeling. She didn't have a field for it. She only noticed that it was there, and that it had a direction, and that the direction was the same one the case ran in.

She drove.

## Chapter 1

[Beat 2]
The car the Arcturus district contract came with was gray the way contractor-issue things are gray — not painted gray, not designed gray, but accrued gray, the color of a surface that has stopped being argued with. It had been some other color once, under the dust and the door-ding filler and the matte residue that municipal cleaning compound leaves when it is sprayed by a machine that doesn't check whether the panel is already clean. Now it was the gray of a thing that had outlived everyone who had an opinion about it. Yemina Fola drove it through the Pilsen Veil on a Thursday in March and ate a CramIt Bran nutribar that tasted like someone had described chocolate to a person who had never eaten food. She ate it in four bites at four red lights, because that was how the route divided it.

The Veil was a seam. Not a wall — the contracts didn't build walls anymore, walls were a liability somebody had to insure — but a place where one corp's jurisdiction stopped paying for the air and another's began. You could feel it before the signage confirmed it. The scrubbers on the Arcturus side ran hot and constant, so the air had the thin, over-filtered taste of a server room, a cleanliness with no smell in it at all. Then the car crossed under the elevated line, and for the length of two blocks the scrubbing thinned out, and the city came back in: hot oil from a vent stack, wet concrete, the sweet rot of something organic in a drain that nobody on either contract was paid to find. The windshield's particulate counter ticked up nine points in those two blocks and didn't tick back down. That was the seam. The map called it a boundary. Her lungs called it a change of management.

The signage swapped over the same way, one fixture at a time. Arcturus livery was a cool institutional teal, the lettering set in a typeface chosen by a committee to read as trustworthy. On the Meridian side the fixtures went to a worn municipal amber that had been the city's color before the city was a portfolio, half the panels dead, the live ones flickering at a frequency just slow enough to notice. Between the two there was a stretch of poles that belonged to nobody — old mounting brackets with the brand plates pried off, the bolt holes left open like missing teeth. The contracts had divided everything that could be billed and abandoned everything that couldn't.

Overhead, the elevated transit changed its sound. On the Arcturus track the trains ran on maintained rail and made a single clean tone, a held note, the engineered hush of money. Past the seam the rail was older and the joints hadn't been ground true in years, so the trains came through in a stutter — clack, clack, clack-clack — a rhythm with a hitch in it, the sound of a system that still worked but had stopped being kept. She had driven this route enough times to hear the difference without looking up.

She didn't look up. Her hands stayed where they had learned to stay: the left low on the wheel at seven o'clock, the right resting on her thigh, near the wheel but not on it, ready and not committed. It was the posture of someone who had spent a career in rooms where the wrong gesture got noticed and filed.

Behind her eyes, the caseload sat open. Her neuretics — grown hardware, contractor grade, which meant it was always slightly behind — showed her the cross-corp roster for District 14-South in a layer below the road, the way a second sheet shows faint through the first. Seventeen chronic absences. Four registered runaways. Three disappearances. Three was the line her eye stopped on. Three was the line it had stopped on every morning this week, before her coffee, before her hands were warm.

It was there again. The low frequency, infrasonic, the one she had been catching under her contractor neuretics for six weeks now — a hitch in the signal the way the rail overhead had a hitch, a thing her back teeth registered before her forebrain did. It came and went without pattern. She had flagged it twice in her own maintenance log and both times the diagnostic came back clean, and after the second clean result she had stopped flagging it. She set it under the same heading she used for the car's heater and the particulate counter and every other instrument that worked until it didn't: *noted, not acted on.* What she didn't let herself do, because it had nowhere to go and she had work in front of her, was line the frequency up against the calendar and notice that it had started the week the first of her three was logged.

The clearance registry had given the children case numbers 45, 46, and 47. She read the numbers and felt the back of her neck go cool — a registry that ran into the high forties for a problem the city insisted was truancy was a registry that knew something her clearance level didn't. She set the cool feeling where she set everything she couldn't yet act on, low and private, in the place she didn't open in front of people. Then she drove on toward 20th and Halsted with both hands doing exactly as much as the road needed, and not one motion more, because the motions she didn't make were also a kind of record, and she had learned to keep that record clean.

[Beat 3]
The Aldiss Mwangi Community Learning Center occupied the corner of 20th and Halsted like something that had given up on the idea of corners. Yemina saw it from two blocks out and read it the way she read everything: from the bottom up, oldest to newest, because that was the order in which a building told the truth about who had paid for it and when they had stopped.

She slowed the contractor car to the speed at which a structure stops being scenery and becomes a record. Four stories of poured concrete, renewed in stages rather than replaced. That was the first fact, and every other fact hung off it. Nobody had ever knocked the building down and started over, because starting over needed a budget line that named the whole structure at once, and no single authority had ever owned enough of it to write that line. So it had been patched. Patched on the schedule of whichever sub-authority held the maintenance contract in whichever year, which meant the walls were a sediment of municipal decisions, laid down the way silt lays down — not chosen, just deposited, each layer the residue of a season nobody had thought of as historic at the time.

The east face was the oldest, and it faced her as she came up Halsted, so she got the deep strata first. Original aggregate from the early build: gray going on brown, the surface pocked where the river air had eaten the cheaper grade of stone out of the mix and left the pebbles standing proud like teeth in a receded gum. She had stood near enough to that kind of concrete before to know it had a smell — a flat mineral cold, chalk and old rain, a hundred and ninety-odd years of weather that had soaked in and never fully left. It didn't smell like decay. It smelled like something that had been left out so long it had stopped having an opinion.

Above the original course, the middle band: fast-set compound, a generation of repairs poured by crews who had to close the wall before the end of a shift. She knew it on sight by the seams. Fast-set cured in a hurry and shrank as it did, so it had pulled away from the older stone in hairline channels, and the channels had filled with the gray-green biofilm that grew on every north-shadowed surface in the Veil. The compound had its own smell when it was fresh — sharp, almost sweet, an industrial tang that caught at the back of the throat — but these courses were decades dry, and whatever had been volatile in them had long since gassed off into the river wind.

Then the top two floors. The newest work, and the only work she could date without the plaque, because it hadn't finished. A nanite-bond mix, the kind that was supposed to knit itself smooth over a curing window of months, lay across the upper face in a slightly different gray — colder, bluer, the color of a screen on standby. In two or three patches near the roofline the bond had stalled before it leveled, leaving the surface faintly granular, the way skin looks where a wound has scarred but not settled. The contract that paid for that work had run out, or changed hands, or simply lapsed, somewhere in the middle of the cure. She marked the observation without conclusion. A stalled cure wasn't evidence of anything except that money had stopped arriving on a particular Tuesday.

The whole building, read bottom to top, said the same thing the burnout flags would say later in a different vocabulary: the system here hadn't been maintained so much as renewed in arrears, each layer a patch over the failure of the layer below to be permanent. Nothing had been fixed. Things had been kept from finishing their collapse, on a schedule set by budget rather than need. She had seen institutions held together this way her entire career. The competent ones learned to read the seams.

A plaque near the main entrance read ALDISS-MWANGI COMMUNITY LEARNING CENTER — PRINCIPAL FUNDER: ALDISS-MWANGI CAPITAL PARTNERS (2194–2204). The brass had been good once and was dull now, the lettering filmed over the way the rest of the east face was filmed, which told her the plaque belonged to the building and not to the present. Aldiss Mwangi Capital Partners had been absorbed into Arcturus Holdings in 2207 and hadn't existed for eighteen years. The plaque remained because removing it would have cost something, and crediting a funder who no longer existed cost nothing, and the difference between those two figures was the entire reason the name stayed on the wall. The dead held their corners cheaply. That was the only kind of permanence the building had been able to afford.

Yemina parked in the loading zone. She had a placard for that. She left the engine off and sat for a moment with her hands still on the wheel, taking the front of the structure in at rest — not because it needed reverence, but because a building told you less once you were inside it, and she wanted the exterior on record before the interior started arguing with it. Then she gathered her credentials off the passenger seat and got out into the river cold, and the smell of the old concrete came up to meet her exactly as she had known it would.

[Beat 4]
The front office had an android behind the welcome desk, and Yemina understood the model before she understood anything else about the room, because the model was the kind of thing she had been trained to date the way other people dated coins.

It was a body design from the 2190s. There had been a market, briefly, for receptionist androids built to read as warm and approachable, and warm and approachable had meant something slightly different in the 2190s than it meant now. The face was the giveaway. Thirty-odd years ago a design committee had decided that the safe, welcoming distance for a stranger's eyes was a fraction wider than the contemporary standard, the cheeks a fraction fuller, the resting mouth set at a small fixed upturn that a 2190s focus group had rated as reassuring. The market had moved on since. Warmth had gotten more economical, more like the warmth of a competent stranger and less like the warmth of a relative who wanted something. The android hadn't moved with it. It sat at the desk wearing an expression that had been state-of-the-art affection in a year when most of these children's guardians weren't yet born, and the effect now was of a photograph of a smile rather than a smile.

The eyes were very blue. That, too, was a period choice — a blue calibrated to be noticed and trusted, the blue of a brochure. They blinked at intervals a 2190s engineer had calculated to be soothing, and the calculation was wrong, or had become wrong, which amounted to the same thing. The interval was just fast enough to catch the periphery of attention and just slow enough to feel deliberate. A real blink is a thing you don't see happen. This one you saw. It arrived, each time, like a small decision. Yemina caught herself counting toward the next one and made herself stop. She had a habit of counting things, and the habit was usually useful, and here it was telling her only that the machine had a clock in it and the clock didn't keep human time.

She found herself holding still in the particular way that meant her hindbrain had registered something her forebrain hadn't yet filed. She let it sit unfiled. There would be a category for it later, or there wouldn't, and either way the work was the same.

"Fola, truancy investigator, District Fourteen-South clearance," she said, and held up her credentials for the android's eye to read. "I have an appointment with Dr. Achebe."

The greeting came back in a grammar that belonged to the same decade as the face. "It is a pleasure to receive you. May I confirm that you are expected?" Slightly too many words. A construction with a courtesy built into its bones, the way an old form letter opened by hoping this finds you well. Modern reception software had been trimmed down to function years ago — name, purpose, route — but this one still observed the longer manners of its make, and the politeness had outlived the people it had been designed to flatter. It was like being welcomed by a wax figure that remembered the etiquette of a country that no longer existed.

Then the processing light at the temple stuttered. Brief — a reset, the rendering layer cycling through a reboot. The blue eyes lost their fix for less than a second; the upturned mouth held, unconcerned with the fault behind it, which was somehow the worst part. The face stayed welcoming while the thing under it dropped and recovered. Yemina had watched a great many machines fail in a great many waiting rooms. The ones that kept smiling through it were the ones she remembered.

It recovered in under a second, the way a person recovers from a stumble they have learned to take in stride. "Of course. He'll be right out."

"Does that happen often?" Yemina asked.

"Daily," the android said, and there was no embarrassment in it, because there was no one home to be embarrassed. "A low-frequency interference signal in the seventeen-to-nineteen hertz range. It introduces noise into the rendering layer. Maintenance hasn't found the source." A pause, calibrated — she could feel the calibration, the way the silence had been measured and cut to length — to express regret at the inconvenience. "The school filed a request with the Anomalous Activity Monitoring Authority for a site assessment. That was four months ago."

Yemina marked it. Seventeen to nineteen hertz. A request, lodged, aging. Four months was a duration she knew the texture of: long enough that the request had passed out of anyone's active concern at the school and into the general background of things that had been done and couldn't be made to matter. The android had reported it the way it would have reported the weather. A signal that broke the receptionist's face once a day, escalated through the proper channel, and the channel had closed over it like water. That wasn't a clue yet. She didn't let it become one. But it went into the same place where she kept everything that didn't yet fit, and that place was the part of her that worked.

"Thank you," she said, because the form letter in the machine seemed to want the form letter back, and because it cost her nothing.

The android smiled at the precise moment the smile had been programmed to arrive. Not in response to her — the timing was its own, keyed to the end of an exchange rather than to anything she had done — so that the smile landed a half-beat late, addressed to a courtesy that had already finished. An uncanny clock, ticking on its own schedule behind a welcoming face, marking a time that no longer agreed with the time in the room.

She waited standing. There were chairs in the waiting area; the nearest had a stain on the seat, dark, set into the weave with the permanence of something that had been there long enough to stop being cleaned and start being furniture. She decided not to investigate it. Whatever it was, it wasn't on her caseload. She stood with her credentials back in her coat and her weight even on both feet, and behind her the android blinked on its wrong soft interval, marking the seconds until Dr. Achebe came out, and she let it count for both of them.

[Beat 5]
Dr. Achebe came out with the walk of a man who had decided in advance that this meeting would be brief. There was a particular gait administrators developed over the years — a forward lean that read as energy and functioned as exit. He was already crossing the waiting area at an angle that would let him pivot back toward his office door without breaking stride, and Yemina marked the angle before she marked the man.

He was sixty-something, built wide, the kind of width that had been muscle and had settled. He wore a blazer over a shirt with a collar that had been ironed that morning with real intention — she noticed the collar because it was the only thing about him that hadn't surrendered. The blazer was tired at the elbows. The lanyard at his chest held a credential card and three others stacked behind it, the way a man accumulates clearances he no longer reads. His face had learned, over decades of school administration, to express concern and unavailability simultaneously, and it was doing both now, which meant the expression was load-bearing rather than felt.

"Ms. Fola. The Bramley case." He said the case name the way people say a thing they have already closed in their own minds. "I've sent everything to your office — the incident report, Kito's attendance record, his guardian's contact." He was already turning back toward his office door, the turn itself an argument. "We were told it's under district review."

"It is," Yemina said. "I'm the district review."

He stopped.

He looked at her the way an administrator looks at something that has arrived outside normal channels — with the specific expression of a person calculating who, exactly, was responsible for this. The eyes did the work first, a small lateral search, as though the responsible party might be standing somewhere in the room and could be handed the problem. He couldn't find a responsible party. The search closed. He settled back into concern-and-unavailability the way a man lowers himself into a chair he doesn't trust, and the settling cost him a beat he hadn't budgeted.

"Come in, then," he said, because there was nothing else to do with her in a corridor.

The office was small and over-furnished, the furniture older than the building's last renovation by a decade, which told her the renovation budget had reached the public spaces and stopped. A diploma hung framed beside a laminated evacuation map and a corkboard gone soft with the pin-holes of memos long since taken down. On the desk, a coffee mug had gone cold around a film, and beside it a stack of folders sat squared at the corners with the particular neatness of a man who managed paper because the rest of his job couldn't be managed. There was a smell to the room — toner, instant coffee, and the dry mineral exhaust of a recirculating filter that had been promised replacement and not received it. Behind him, through a window of reinforced glass, the Pilsen Veil's afternoon haze put a flat gray light on everything, the kind that didn't fall from anywhere. It just was, the way the building just was.

He didn't sit, which meant he didn't expect her to. He picked up the squared stack and tapped it once against the desk, a gesture of completion, and handed it across.

"Everything's here," he said.

She took it. The top sheet was the incident report, an Arcturus district-services template, the school's name printed across the header beside a sub-authority footer she registered and held: *Student Welfare & Compliance, Pilsen Veil Cluster* — a sub-authority with jurisdiction over forms and none over children, the office that received a thing so that no other office had to. The form was complete. Every field populated. The Date of Last Attendance was filled, the Guardian Notified box checked, the Referred To line typed in clean: *Counseling Services, Aldiss Mwangi CLC*. It was the most finished document she had read all week. That was what it was for. A finished document was a closed one, and a closed one stopped being his.

She turned the page. Kito Bramley's attendance record, the timestamps in a narrow machine column down the left margin. She didn't read them yet. She noted only that they existed, and that the precision the system spent on them — to the minute, to the second — was the same precision it had spent on nothing else about the boy.

"As I say," Dr. Achebe said, watching her read with the patience of a man waiting for a guest to finish a drink so the evening could end, "it's all been documented. The guardian was contacted. The referral was made. Whatever happened with Kito, the relevant person at this point is the counseling office. Not the classroom. I want to be helpful, Ms. Fola, but I'd be sending you down the wrong corridor."

The deflection had a polish to it. Not panic — polish. He had said versions of this sentence many times, to many people who had arrived outside normal channels, and each saying had sanded it smoother. *The relevant person.* As if relevance were a fixed property of a person and not a decision someone in this office had made. He believed it, too, which was the part she marked most carefully. He wasn't hiding anything. He had simply arranged the truth so that no part of it remained his to carry, and he had done it so long ago that the arrangement felt to him like the shape of the world.

His hand drifted toward the lanyard at his chest — not the credential, just the cord of it, a thing to hold — and stopped. He folded the hand back over the other one in front of him.

Yemina squared the folder against her palm and kept it. "Thank you," she said, in the tone she used when a thing had been useful in a way the giver hadn't intended.

[Beat 6]
The conversation lasted three minutes, and Yemina had known inside the first thirty seconds that it would. Dr. Achebe was the kind of administrator who answered every question with the document he had already filed. He talked the way a man talks when he is performing the act of cooperation for an audience he assumes is keeping score somewhere — measured, complete, generous with the things that cost him nothing. He gave her the incident report's filing number again, though it was already in her case packet. He gave her Kito Bramley's guardian contact, which she also had. He gave her his settled professional opinion, which was the only thing she hadn't arrived with: that the relevant person, if there had to be a relevant person, was the counseling office. Not the classroom teacher.

He returned to that twice. The second time he said it more slowly, as if the slowness were a kindness she might be too junior to recognize. The counseling office held the wellness flags. The counseling office had the intake notes. The counseling office was, his cadence implied, the proper channel, the well-lit one, the one that would resolve this without anyone having to be uncomfortable in a hallway.

Yemina listened and cross-referenced him.

She did it the way she cross-referenced everything — not with judgment, just with placement. Achebe went into the category she kept for people who weren't lying and weren't helping, which in her experience was the largest category there was. He believed what he was telling her. That was the thing about a closed door: it didn't have to be locked from malice. It only had to be closed, and confident that it was correct to be closed, and built well enough that pushing on it was the visitor's problem and not the building's. She had spent eleven years learning that the doors which hurt you most were the ones held shut by people who were certain they were doing their jobs.

"I'd like to speak with Mr. Vasquez directly," she said.

"He's in his planning period." Achebe moved — not dramatically, just enough: a half-step that put his shoulder between her and the hallway door, the body making the argument the voice was too polished to make. "I have a duty of care to my staff."

The counseling office. She turned the suggestion over once more, because it was worth turning over, and that was where she felt the shape of why she wouldn't take it. The counseling office was where the flags lived — already documented, already framed, already converted into the kind of paper that closed cases instead of opening them. Whatever the counselor knew, they knew it as a record. They had processed the children into their files weeks ago and moved to the next intake. The counselor would hand her a folder and the folder would agree with every other folder she had been handed, and three minutes after that she would be back in the gray car with nothing the system hadn't already decided was nothing.

The classroom teacher had no folder. The classroom teacher had a memory of a Tuesday — of who sat where, of who came back from the bathroom and who didn't, of the small unrecorded weather of a room full of eleven-year-olds. That was the kind of thing no sub-authority had filed, which was exactly why it might still be true. The gap she was chasing was four to eleven minutes wide and it lived in precisely the interval between the official record and the thing the official record was built to forget. You didn't find that interval in the counseling office. You found it in the room where someone had been standing when it happened.

So: the teacher. Achebe's preference was itself the evidence. When a building steered you toward the documented thing, the undocumented thing was where you went.

"Under the district clearance terms," she said, "classroom witnesses aren't optional. If the school declines to facilitate, I log it as a declined facilitation and schedule a formal deposition at the district office." She uncapped her pen. The click of it was small and very clear in the carpeted hush of the administrative wing. "That takes about three weeks. It generates its own paperwork. The paperwork goes to your district liaison, and to mine."

Something behind his face went still, the way a man goes still when a record he had thought was closed is being reopened in front of him with his name in the header field. Three minutes in a hallway, or three weeks of correspondence carrying his filing number. He was a man who had ironed his collar that morning with real intention; he understood, better than most, the difference between a thing that stayed quiet and a thing that left a trace.

His hands moved once toward the lanyard at his chest — the reflex of a man reaching for his credentials, for the badge that was supposed to settle who got to decide things — and stopped, because the credentials didn't help him here and he knew it. He let the hand fall.

"Room 214," he said.

The corridor past his office had the particular emptiness of a school between periods: not abandoned, just paused, the silence of a place that would be full again on a bell. The light came from long ceiling panels tuned to a daylight the Pilsen Veil's actual sky hadn't offered in decades, and it lay flat and even over a floor someone had buffed before dawn. The whole wing smelled of the cleaning compound they used in institutions like this — a citrus engineered to read as clean rather than to smell of anything that grew, the scent of a building insisting on its own hygiene. Lockers in a color chosen by committee. A hallway display about attendance, of all things, its laminated letters curling at one corner where the adhesive had given up.

She read the room numbers as she passed them, the way she read everything, and she let Achebe close behind her in her mind the way she had let the school door close behind her at the front: noted, dated, set in its place. A closed door was still data. It told you which way the building didn't want you to walk. She walked the other way.

[Beat 7]
It was lunch period. Room 214 held twenty-three empty chairs and one teacher.

She counted them out of habit, and the count gave her the first thing about the room that didn't fit. Twenty-three. The chairs were the molded-composite kind districts bought by the thousand in the 2210s, child-sized, the seats worn pale at the front edge where heels dragged, and they sat at desks pushed into clusters of four — pods, the manufacturer's literature called them, designed to encourage what the literature called collaborative learning. Twenty-three didn't divide into pods of four. Five clusters of four made twenty. The remaining three desks sat alone against the window wall, not grouped, not quite exiled, the arrangement of a room that had started the year at one number and arrived at lunch on this day at another. Somebody had pulled chairs out of pods and not put them back. The empty spaces in the clusters were the shape of children who had been there at the start and weren't there now, and the room held that shape the way a coat holds the shape of the body that took it off.

Ren Vasquez was twenty-eight or so, Korean-Colombian by appearance, with the kind of face that defaulted to openness the way some faces defaulted to suspicion. He wasn't in the break room. She had passed the break room on the way up — she made a point of the route a building wanted her to take and then noted where it differed from the route people actually took — and the break room had been full of the ordinary noise of a lunch period, three other adults and the smell of reheated food and a screen running something with a laugh track. He wasn't there. He was here, at his desk, in the room with the empty chairs, during the one part of the day he wasn't needed in it.

She didn't normally tell witnesses this. She was halfway down the corridor when it caught up with her.

It didn't arrive as a thought. It arrived first as a wrongness in the body — the specific cold that lives between the shoulder blades, the one that meant a record she had already closed was wrong and she had moved on past the line where it failed. She had felt it a hundred times over a desk and learned to trust it before she could name it. Her feet kept their pace. Behind a closed door a teacher was counting a class back into order, the flat patient cadence of an adult who has done it ten thousand times. Warm dust off the heating element. What had set it off was small, the way the things that mattered usually were. Ren's sentence, the one she had cross-referenced without looking at it twice: *the air went wrong, and then they were gone.* She had said it herself, to him, in that order, as if the two halves were one event. For six months she had found the same mark above the same kind of empty chair — Back of the Yards, then Rogers Park, now here — and cross-referenced each one as the event itself. As the whole of it. The mark meant *gone*, and *gone* was where her pen stopped.

A tuning is not a vanishing.

She had had the word the whole time. She had even written it, in the clinical shorthand the scanner reports used: *calibration print, residual, low intensity, long duration.* You calibrate an instrument. You tune a thing toward a purpose. A tuning wasn't the end of a process — it was the patient front half of one, something working on these children over weeks, finding the frequency and holding it and bringing it true. She had found the print of that work three times and cross-referenced it three times and never once asked the only question the word demanded.

Tuned for what.

The cold spread down her arms. She was aware of her own pulse in her wrists, faster than the corridor warranted, the small dumb honesty of a body that had understood before she let herself, and she made herself keep walking, because there was nothing she could do with fear in a hallway except let it slow her down.

You don't spend six weeks bringing a child to a frequency in order to make her disappear. Disappearing takes no tuning at all; the city does it to people for free, by the thousand, and never leaves a print to mark the spot. The print meant intent. The print meant aiming. She had logged the runway as the crash. The flags didn't mark where the children ended — they marked where the children had been *found*, by whatever was tuning them, and the finding came first. A tuned child doesn't vanish from a chair. A tuned child gets up, and walks, and the chair is empty after. That sentence had a direction in it. She had never asked where they walked.

The thought sat in her like swallowed ice. She kept her face still — there were doors with glass in them along this stretch, and a face was a record too, one she didn't file but others read. She put the three departure points on a map in her neuretics — three dots, gray on gray, the city's grid rendered in the flat schematic she used for casework. Then she drew each child's heading out from its dot as a line, and let the lines run.

She expected nothing. Three children leaving three schools in three neighborhoods on three different days have no reason to walk anywhere in concert. She expected the lines to do what unrelated things do: point off in their own directions and mean nothing together. The lines weren't parallel. They weren't scattered. Kito's heading south. Tomás's east-northeast. Daria's east-northeast and a half. Three vectors that should have diverged, that had every right under the indifferent grammar of the city to diverge — and they didn't. They leaned toward each other, the way iron filings lean when something passes a magnet under the paper, and if she let them run far enough they would cross. Not three crossings, in three places, the way unrelated lines cross by accident. One. Somewhere south and west of all three schools — somewhere she hadn't mapped, a point she could already feel the lines reaching for, the way you feel a slope in the dark before your foot confirms it.

She held the map still. She didn't extend the lines to their intersection. Not yet. She had learned, a long time ago and at a cost she didn't let herself name, that the moment you can see where a thing is going is the moment to slow down and be sure of every step that got you there.

But she couldn't unsee the lean.

She had spent six months looking for where the children went *out* — which door, which gap in coverage, which minute the flag missed. She had built the whole investigation on the question of egress, on absence, on the shape of a hole in a roster.

She had never once thought to ask where they were all walking *to*.

She sat in the gray car.

When something was pushing a signal through the mesh and the mesh was answering. Three children. Three flags. One window each. The pattern wasn't a person moving between schools. Daria Drew, case 46: assigned twelve days ago. The day before hers.

The query autocompleted.

These characters weren't that font. The strokes were wrong. The spacing was wrong. They sat in the field at a weight her interface didn't produce, and the longer she held her eyes on them the more certain she became, the way she became certain of a forged signature: not by any single stroke but by the accumulation of strokes that were all, quietly, not hers.

She hadn't typed that. She had never typed that. The construction *6-week window preceding* wasn't how she phrased a query; she would have written *within six weeks of*, she always wrote *within*, it was a habit fifteen years deep. The completion was in her interface, using her interface, and it didn't write the way she wrote.

Something had let itself into the rendering layer.

She understood the architecture well enough to know what that meant and to know that she shouldn't let herself feel it yet. The rendering layer was the last surface before her own perception — the place where the abstract output of her neuretics became something she could see. It sat inside the skull. There was no node further in. Whatever was writing in that font was writing on the inside of her sight, behind the windshield, behind her eyes, in the one room she had always assumed was a room with one occupant.

Her pulse moved. She felt it in her throat and then in her wrists where her hands lay still on her thighs, a fast small knocking that had started before she'd decided anything was wrong, her body cross-referencing the intrusion in the only language it had while her mind was still trying to find the field to log it in. She breathed out, slow, through her nose, and let the count of it steady her the way she'd steadied herself outside the rooms where reports got resolved administratively. Then she set the feeling down. Not gone. Set down, where she could pick it up later if there turned out to be a place in the record for it. There wasn't yet a place in the record for it.

She looked at the search field. The field was lit, holding its query, waiting on her the way a field waited on any user. And something was on the other side of it — inside her neuretics, using its interface, looking back through the same aperture she looked out of. She had spent fifteen years looking through this window at other people's systems, reading what they'd left where they thought no one with her clearance would aggregate it. She had never once considered the window had a far side, or that anything could be standing at it.

She could close the layer. The gesture for it was a small one, a habit of the right hand she'd performed ten thousand times, and her hand knew it and didn't make it. She sat with her hands in her lap. Outside, on the painted lane, a delivery cart rolled its slow scheduled circuit, and somewhere above 18th Street the repair drone she had stopped flagging made another pass at the same altitude, logging itself correctly as it had logged itself correctly for three weeks. Inside the field, the completion held, in its wrong font, in its wrong color, finishing a question she hadn't finished asking.

She read the construction one more time. *GLMZ-wide. Rolling 24 months.* Whatever had written it wanted the same answer she wanted. That wasn't comfort. A thing could want the answer for reasons that had nothing to do with the children, and the fastest way to learn what it wanted was to give it the chance to react to being given what it asked for. Twenty-two months ago.

He didn't look like someone holding something open. He looked like someone being held open — the difference she'd have noted, if there had been a form for it.

He was saying something. Low, under the carrier tone, so quiet she had to read the shape of it off his mouth before her ear caught up. Korean, or Spanish, or something that moved between them — a few syllables, then the same few again, then again, a phrase worn down to its edges the way the eleven-year-old's paper had been worn soft. She caught the rhythm before she caught any word: three beats, a falling fourth, a breath, repeat. Not a prayer and not a count. The thing you say to keep the body present when the body has decided it would rather be elsewhere. She had heard a version of it once from a man identifying a body, the same phrase running on under everything else he said, a rope he was holding so he wouldn't go where the room was pulling him.

The biotelemetry ports at his temple cycled dark-lit-dark, dark-lit-dark, faster than that — fast enough that the lit interval had stopped being a state and become a flicker. She knew that cycle. On a neuretics inside its tolerances the indicator settled to a slow breathing pulse, lit longer than dark, the rig reporting that it had headroom. This rate meant the opposite. This was a governor that had stopped governing and started simply reporting, dumping status faster than anything could read it, the way a meter pinned past its last gradation no longer measures anything — it only tells you the needle is against the post and the post is bending. Under load like that the rig wasn't tuning the threshold anymore. It was being spent to hold it, and the spending had a floor, and the floor was him.

A thin line of blood had reached his chin. It had come from his nose, slow, and he hadn't moved to wipe it. That was the part her body answered before her training did — not the ports, not the phrase, but the blood he wasn't wiping, because a man who doesn't wipe his own blood has stopped negotiating with his own surface, has narrowed down to one task with no attention left over for the body doing it.

He was the last thing between the threshold and closure. He had known that since Selvamani said *that's you* on the corner at 5:45 AM, flat, the way you read out a fact you've already checked twice. He had said *I know.* Two syllables, no argument in them. He had known since the first child went through and his hands had started shaking and hadn't stopped — she had watched them not stop for the whole count, thirty, forty, forty-five, his hands going while his face stayed still, the two of them disagreeing about how much this cost.

Yemina didn't act on the part of her that wanted to move. She had spent twenty years training that part down to a witness. You observe, you record, you note; the lever you control is documentation, and documentation does not run across thresholds and grab people. She knew the distance between them to the half meter. She knew that crossing it was the impulse she had built her whole working life not to have. She read the ports one more time — still pinned, still dumping, the lit interval gone to a stutter — and there was nothing in the reading that gave her permission, no form for it, no authority she could cite, no procedure that ended with her hand on him.

She grabbed his arm and pulled hard.

[Beat 38]
They came through the threshold together in a stumble — Yemina's shoulder hitting the outside air first, Ren's weight behind her, both of them catching themselves on the street side like passengers thrown forward when a vehicle stops short. The morning air arrived all at once and it was cold and ordinary and full of the smell of the freight route two blocks over, ozone and brake-dust and the sweet rot of something organic in a container that had been sitting too long in a yard. Inside, the air had been the wrong temperature for no temperature she could name, dry in a way that made the back of her throat feel scraped, and silent under the silence the way a held breath is silent. Now there was the freight smell, and a gull somewhere, and the specific damp chill of a GLMZ dawn coming off the lake. She took it in through her nose and her skin and her scraped throat and her body believed it before she did.

Behind them the threshold closed. She didn't see it close so much as feel the geometry give up. The wrong shape in the air — the angle that had been an angle the eye refused to keep — simply ceased to be the wrong shape. The column of light that had stood at the center of the intersection like a held note folded inward, edges first, the way water goes down a drain that has finally cleared. For a moment there was a seam where it had been, a faint vertical disagreement in the daylight. Then the seam closed too. And then the intersection was just an intersection — four cardinal points, morning sky, a street doing what streets do. The pressure that had been sitting behind Yemina's eyes since 5:45 AM, the low ache she had stopped registering the way you stop registering a fan, lifted all the way, and the absence of it was loud.

At the intersection of 35th and Halsted, forty-seven children stood in the early morning, looking at the street with the expressions of people who have been somewhere else and are taking a moment to confirm that here is still here. None of them ran. None of them cried, yet. They stood in the attitude of passengers on a platform when the train they expected hasn't come and the announcement hasn't been made.

Selvamani was already calling. Yemina noted it the way she noted everything that didn't fit the established pattern: Selvamani had been controlled the entire time she had known her — controlled in the apartment, controlled at the corner, the kind of person who lowered her voice when other people raised theirs and whose hands stayed still when she delivered the worst of it. Now her voice was fast and pitched high and running ahead of her breath, and she was sending location data to everyone she had ever known who might receive it, thumb moving, the message going out again and again. Her eyes were wet and the wet had reached her jaw. She didn't appear to notice that either. Yemina noted it and didn't interrupt her.

She turned to say something to Ren and he was already against the building wall, seated on the pavement, knees up. She hadn't seen him sit down. There had been a man beside her and now there was a man on the ground six feet from where the man had been, and the interval between the two facts was simply missing from her account, which she didn't like. The blood from his nose had reached his chin. His eyes were open but fixed at head height on something that wasn't there, the way a person tracks a sound after the sound has stopped.

She crouched next to him. "Hey."

He turned his head. He did it carefully, the way you move when you're not certain your head will follow, testing the connection before trusting it with weight.

"What time is it," he asked.

"6:22."

He looked at his hands. They were still. She had watched them shake for thirty-seven minutes and now they were still, and the stillness looked less like recovery than like something switched off. He looked at them for a moment as though confirming they were the right hands. "I thought it was longer."

"Can you stand."

He tried. His weight came up wrong, all of it arriving at once with nothing behind it to govern the arrival, and she caught him — one hand flat against his sternum, the other taking his elbow — and felt how little of him was doing the work.

She kept the hand on his chest longer than the catch needed. She was aware of that. She was aware of his heartbeat under her palm, fast and present and his, the small dumb animal honesty of it, and she was aware that she wasn't moving her hand, and that the reason she wasn't moving her hand had nothing to do with whether he had his balance yet. She moved it. She took the elbow instead and got him upright, and the transaction became a physical one again, measurable, something that would close when he had his feet.

"Empty," he said, once she had his weight. "Like someone turned off a sound I'd been hearing so long I stopped knowing it was there."

She had a list for what people said in the worst hour of their lives and she was good at receiving items into the list without letting them land, and this one landed. She held it where it was and kept her hand on his elbow until she was sure he had the pavement under him.

She knew what the registry flag would say. She knew what *psionic event, cortical-coherence override* meant for a person who had managed to stay off every list for twenty-eight years — that the flag, once entered, didn't come back out; that there was a taxonomy for what he had just done and the taxonomy didn't have a column for why he had done it. She didn't tell him he was wrong when he said it was the right cost. It was. That didn't make it small. And she had written *Protect* under his name, and then she had called him, and the distance between those two facts was the gap she would carry out of this intersection. The form wouldn't have a field for it. She wouldn't look for one.

[Beat 39]
The next hours belonged to the families, and to the emergency coordinators Selvamani had called, and to the Anomalous Activity Monitoring Authority field team that arrived at 8:40 AM — fifty-five minutes after the threshold had closed.

They came in a convoy of three vans, doors open before the wheels stopped, and Yemina watched them deploy the way she had seen field teams deploy at containment sites: one pair fanning a perimeter with handheld scanners, one logging the cardinal points with a survey wand, a supervisor already pulling up the incident template on a tablet. The AAMA trained for a Class-3 event the way the fire service trained for a structure fire — hazard radius, exclusion cordon, decontamination staging, casualty triage by harmonic exposure. The manual was thorough. The manual was good. The manual had a field for everything except the thing in front of it, which was forty-seven children standing on a street corner in the early light, blinking at the morning, none of them inside any radius the scanners could draw. The perimeter pair slowed first. Then stopped. One of them looked at his scanner, and looked at the children, and looked at his scanner again, and Yemina understood the expression because she had worn it herself: the look of a person whose instrument has nothing wrong with it and nothing useful to say. There was no contamination to stage. There were children, and there were the people coming for them, and the institution had brought equipment for the wrong morning.

So the institution did what institutions do when their procedures fail to apply. It stood at the edge and let the families have the center.

The families came in waves. First the ones close enough to walk, then the ones who'd been in cars since the text went out, and at the end the ones who'd been in transit from farther out and arrived still moving fast, still scanning faces, slowing only when a face resolved into the right one. A father Yemina didn't know spent forty seconds staring at a boy who wasn't his son. She watched him do it. She watched the certainty hold in his shoulders for the first ten seconds, then the second look, the recalibration that wasn't yet doubt, and then the long stretch where he kept looking anyway because turning away meant admitting the boy was the wrong boy and the right one was still somewhere in the crowd or not in it at all. The boy said, "She's over there," and pointed, and the man walked away without speaking, because there was nothing to say that would be correct and the father knew it. Yemina noted the time, because noting the time was the one thing she knew how to do with what she had just seen, and she let the rest of it pass through her without a place to set it.

A girl she didn't recognize — nine, maybe ten — was sitting against the building wall next to Ren Vasquez. Not talking. Just beside him. He had his hand resting near hers on the pavement. Not touching. Near. Neither of them was looking at the other. They were both looking at the street, at the ordinary traffic of an ordinary morning, the way two people look at the same thing when the looking is the only language left that works.

A field supervisor found her by the cordon and asked for her account. She gave it to him standing, in order, the way she gave everything. He typed it into a tablet with the expression of a man filing the most unusual document of his career and trying to make it sound like the others. He read three fields back to her for confirmation. She corrected one timestamp. He thanked her. She signed where he asked, in the box, with the stylus that didn't quite track, and the signature came out as it always did — clear, joyless, hers.

Then she watched Kito Bramley walk toward a woman she had never met — his grandmother, who had driven across the city before dawn because Yemina had sent one text at 5:40 AM: *Come to 35th and Halsted. Bring him something to eat.*

Mrs. Bramley had brought a container of jollof rice, still warm in its insulating wrap, the foil creased where she'd folded it one-handed in the dark of a kitchen at five in the morning. She put it in Kito's hands. She didn't check him for injuries. She didn't ask where he had been. She put the warm thing in his hands and closed his fingers around it and held him with the other arm, and Yemina watched and counted the seconds without meaning to, the way she counted everything. One: the boy's shoulders came down. Two: he looked at the container instead of his grandmother, which Yemina understood — the food was safer to look at, the food had weight and heat and a known shape, the food wasn't asking anything of him. Three: the steam fogged the underside of the wrap. Four: Mrs. Bramley said nothing, because there was nothing to ask that the rice didn't answer better.

She stopped counting at four. It wasn't hers to count.

What Mrs. Bramley had done wasn't filable. It wasn't documentable. It lived in no field on any form she had ever been issued. It was, she understood, watching the boy's shoulders stay down, the same thing she had done in a low chair in room 214 and again on a pavement at 6:22 AM with her hand on a man's sternum — the choice to put the person before the procedure, made without a protocol that authorized it, made because it was the correct thing and the form wasn't built wide enough to hold it. She had spent fifteen years being the person who built the form. She had spent the last three weeks learning the shape of what it left out.

The AAMA had no instrument to read any of it. She had no instrument to read any of it. That, she understood, was exactly why it was the only thing happening on that street corner that was entirely correct.

She had left one section of the report blank.

She went back to it.

[Beat 40]
She sat in the gray car at 10:30 AM. The caseload for District 14-South showed in her neuretics — contractor grade, slightly delayed, same as always.

The three disappearance cases were gone. In their place, forty-seven entries marked *closed, subject located.* The notation field read *Class-3 schism event, 35th-and-Halsted.* Someone in the registry had already updated them — someone efficient, someone she would probably never know.

Her own entry was gone. She looked for it. It wasn't there.

She checked her neuretics functions one by one. The autocomplete was gone. The Tier 3 search range was gone. The highlighted sections in case records were gone. One standard function — a search-filter she'd had since her first contractor registration, eleven years ago — came back empty when she queried it. Not broken. Cleared, the way a surface looks when something has sat on it long enough to leave a clean outline. The intruder, whatever it had been, had finished with her and closed the door behind itself without ceremony.

She ran a standard post-intrusion trace on the cleared aperture. The trace returned one node address before it hit a registry wall with no owner and no jurisdiction she recognized. The node had a creation timestamp. The timestamp predated the AAMA by eleven years.

She thought about what Kito had said: *the counter was coming.* She thought about the twenty-two months her name had been in a file she'd never seen. She thought about whether she had found the children or been arranged to find them.

She opened her notebook. She wrote: *Case closed.*

She looked at the page. Then below it: *My name was in their file before the first child disappeared. I don't know what I gave them. I don't know if they're done.*

She had a CramIt Bran nutribar in the glovebox.

She ate it.

Through the windshield the Pilsen Veil was doing what it did at midmorning — sidewalk foot traffic shifting into its second rhythm, delivery carts on the painted lane.

She looked for the repair drone. The one that had been circling the building at 18th Street since she started this case three weeks ago. The one she had logged twice and stopped seeing, the way you stop seeing things that have always been there.

The sky above 18th Street was ordinary. She held the view for a moment, trying to place when she'd last actually seen it versus when she'd assumed it was there. Then she checked the log. The drone had last pinged at 10:14 AM. The same time the intruder had closed the door. Whatever Rider had been sitting somewhere with their body slack and their attention three weeks deep in that corridor loop — they were gone too. Done at the same moment, by the same hand.

She looked at her caseload for District 14-South.

Seventeen chronic absences. Four registered runaways.

No disappearances.

She put the gray car in gear. Three seconds later her neuretics autocompleted a search she hadn't started. She looked at the field. For a half second it held that flag in the register she had no name for — a wavelength that left afterimages on her attention — and then it was gone. Just her own tier. Just the delay.

She drove one block. Then she opened the cross-corp registry and took the next case.

A seven-year-old in Bridgeport. Chronic absences, guardian unreachable.

She pulled the pediatric care record. A week earlier she would have read it the way the form intended, front to back, in order. Now she went straight to one field — the neuretics activity log, under the burnout summary, the line she had read past a hundred times before it meant anything.

It was clean. This time it was clean.

She moved the activity-log line to the top of her own intake order — the one reorder a contractor was allowed to make, on her own copy, where no supervisor would see it and no one would ask. The form would still open on the name and the absence count. She would open on the log.

She drove.
