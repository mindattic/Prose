# Attendance

_Cross-district truancy investigator Yemina Fola investigates three children who vanished from schools in the Pilsen Veil — each one preceded by a neuretics burnout flag, each disappearance logged as an unexcused absence before anyone connected the cases. The schools were under three different corp contracts; nobody had connected them because nobody else could see all three. When she sweeps the third school's bathroom with a resonance sensor, she finds two traces: an echo above the child's usual seat where the bleed's signal tuned him, and a shadow in the bathroom stall where he crossed. In her car afterward, someone else's hands complete a search query in her neuretics: 47 missing children, GLMZ-wide, 22 months. The 48th entry is her own name. The onset date is six weeks ago._

[Beat 1]
The Pilsen Veil was one of the older zone-seams in GLMZ - not a wall, not a road, but the kind of boundary that accreted over decades of corporate contract renewal until it became a feature of the city's body. To the west, Arcturus Holdings ran district services: schools, transit, sanitation, the case registries that Yemina's contractor clearance could access. To the east, Meridian Infrastructure held the same contracts under different line items. The two systems did not communicate except through forms filed in triplicate.

She had worked the seam for six years. She had learned the city the way you learn the inside of a drawer - accurately, without sentiment, in the dark.

At six in the morning, GLMZ moved the way it always moved: not like a city waking up but like a machine that had never stopped running, just shifted cadence. The elevated transit above 18th Street carried the early shift. Below it, the previous shift was still going home - contractors in gray-and-blue, a delivery cart riding the painted freight lane, two students who'd been out all night and were now doing the math on whether to sleep before class. The lake was three kilometers east. You couldn't see it but you could feel the cold it sent inshore every March, moving through the corridor streets the way information moved in GLMZ - sideways, by implication, between systems that weren't supposed to share.

She had a caseload. Three disappearances.

She drove.

## Chapter 1

[Beat 2]
The car the Arcturus district contract came with was gray the way contractor-issue things are gray — not painted gray, not designed gray, but accrued gray, the color of a surface that has stopped being argued with. Yemina Fola drove it through the Pilsen Veil on a Thursday in March and ate a CramIt Bran nutribar that tasted like someone had described chocolate to a person who had never eaten food.

Her neuretics — grown hardware, contractor grade, which meant it was always slightly behind  — showed her the cross-corp caseload for District 14-South. Seventeen chronic absences. Four registered runaways. Three disappearances.

She was there for the three. The clearance registry had assigned them case numbers 45, 46, and 47. She noted this and moved on to the addresses.

[Beat 3]
The Aldiss-Mwangi Community Learning Center occupied the corner of 20th and Halsted like something that had given up on the idea of corners. The building was four stories of poured concrete renewed in stages rather than replaced - each repair cycle a geological record of municipal budget decisions: the oldest sections aggregate, the middle layers fast-set compound, the newest a nanite-bond mix that had not finished curing in places, visible as a slight color difference at the top two floors. A plaque near the main entrance read ALDISS-MWANGI COMMUNITY LEARNING CENTER - PRINCIPAL FUNDER: ALDISS-MWANGI CAPITAL PARTNERS (2194-2204). Aldiss-Mwangi Capital Partners had been absorbed into Arcturus Holdings in 2207 and had not existed for eighteen years. The plaque remained because someone would have had to pay to remove it.

Yemina parked in the loading zone. She had a placard for that.

[Beat 4]
The front office had an android behind the welcome desk. It was a body design from the 2190s - there was a market, briefly, for receptionist androids built to read as warm and approachable by 2190s standards, which meant something slightly different from what warm and approachable meant now. The android's eyes were very blue and blinked at intervals that a 2190s engineer had calculated to be soothing. The calculation was wrong: the interval was just fast enough to catch the periphery of attention and just slow enough to feel deliberate. Yemina found herself holding still in a way that meant her hindbrain had registered something her forebrain had not filed yet.

"Fola, truancy investigator, District 14-South clearance," Yemina said, and held up her credentials for the android's eye. "I have an appointment with Dr. Achebe."

The android's processing light stuttered - a brief reset, the rendering layer cycling through a reboot it had clearly performed before. It recovered in under a second. "Of course. He'll be right out."

"Does that happen often," Yemina asked.

"Daily," the android said. "A low-frequency interference signal in the 17-to-19 hertz range. It introduces noise into the rendering layer. Maintenance has not located the source." A pause calibrated to express regret at inconvenience. "The school filed a request with the Resonance Monitoring Authority for a site assessment. That was four months ago."

The android smiled at the precise moment the smile had been programmed to arrive.

She waited standing. The chair in the waiting area had a stain she decided not to investigate.

[Beat 5]
Dr. Achebe came out with the walk of a man who had decided in advance that this meeting would be brief. He was sixty-something, built wide, wore a blazer over a shirt with a collar that had been ironed that morning with real intention. He had a lanyard with his credentials and a face that had learned, over decades of school administration, to express concern and unavailability simultaneously.

"Ms. Fola. The Bramley case. I've sent everything to your office — the incident report, Kito's attendance record, his guardian's contact." He was already turning back toward his office door, the turn itself an argument. "We were told it's under district review."

"It is," Yemina said. "I'm the district review."

He stopped. He looked at her the way an administrator looks at something that has arrived outside normal channels — with the specific expression of a person calculating who, exactly, was responsible for this. He could not find a responsible party. He settled back into concern-and-unavailability.

[Beat 6]
The three-minute conversation with Dr. Achebe produced everything he was willing to give her — which was paperwork he'd already filed and a firm opinion that the relevant person was the counseling office, not the classroom teacher.

"I'd like to speak with Mr. Vasquez directly," she said.

"He's in his planning period." He moved — not dramatically, just enough: positioning himself between her and the hallway door. "I have a duty of care to my staff."

"Under the district clearance terms, classroom witnesses aren't optional. If the school declines to facilitate, I log it and schedule a formal deposition at the district office." She uncapped her pen. "That usually takes three weeks."

His hands moved once toward the lanyard at his chest and stopped.

"Room 214," he said.

[Beat 7]
It was lunch period. Room 214 held twenty-three empty chairs and one teacher.

Ren Vasquez was twenty-eight or so, Korean-Colombian by appearance, with the kind of face that defaulted to openness the way some faces defaulted to suspicion. He was sitting at his desk and not doing anything: not grading, not reading, not using his neuretics. Just sitting. His hands rested on the desk surface and were not quite still, a fine tremor he was aware of because when he saw Yemina in the doorway he put them in his lap.

"District truancy," Yemina said, and showed him her credentials. "I'm sorry for the timing. I'll be quick."

"It's fine." He said it like a reflex.

She took the chair nearest the door — a side table chair, an adult chair, slightly taller. She set her notebook on her knee. "Tell me what you saw."

[Beat 8]
Ren talked. He'd clearly told this before — his voice had the flat quality of a story that had been rehearsed not for polish but for survival, the way you tell something over and over until it stops meaning what it meant when it happened.

Kito Bramley was nine. Third row, second seat. He'd been present at the start of class. He'd raised his hand once, a question about the assignment. Ren had answered. Kito had gone back to his work. And then — somewhere in the twenty minutes of independent reading that followed — the chair was empty.

"I didn't see him stand," Ren said. "I wasn't watching every student every second. Maybe he—"

"What did you see," Yemina asked.

Ren was quiet for a moment.

She had set her pen down. She did not do this during interviews. She stood and crossed to the third row.

The second seat was empty the way all chairs are empty, except it wasn't. She stopped at its edge. She did not touch it. She looked at it the way you look at something you have arrived at before you meant to. When she reached for a reason to be standing there — something she could write in her notes, a protocol step she'd overlooked — she didn't find one. She didn't look for it again.

She went back to her chair. She picked up her pen.

"Go on," she said.

[Beat 9]
"The air above his chair went wrong." He said it to his hands in his lap. "But not when he left. Not while the students were still in the room. It was after lunch - after everyone had gone. The room was empty and I was standing there and I looked at the third row."

He stopped.

"Above his seat. Not a shimmer. Not heat-shimmer. It was more like - if sound could make a shape. A frequency you don't hear, you just know it's there because your teeth know. It was there above the seat, just hanging. Like something he'd left behind that I didn't have words for."

He looked up. "I know how that sounds."

"I've heard it before," Yemina said.

She had. Different words each time - witnesses always found different words for the same thing. *Pressure. A wrongness. A note the room played.* She'd heard it from a maintenance worker at a warehouse in Back of the Yards. From a transit operator at the Wabash Stack. From a woman in West Lawn who had watched her husband walk out of the frame of their kitchen and not come back into it.

She had run that interview herself, three years ago. Afterward she had gone out to the gray car and sat in it in the West Lawn cold and not started the engine for a while; she could not now have said how long. There had been nothing to write. There was no field on any form for what happened to a person who plays a kitchen recording forty times and understands, on the fortieth, that a man can simply stop being one of the directions the room has. She had never filed it because there was nowhere to file it. It was the one thing in fifteen years she had kept instead of logged. She put it back where she kept it.

She wrote: *Atmospheric anomaly consistent with prior cases. Echo at seat, not transit. Transition point unknown.*

She looked at him. She had a close for this. She used it at the end of every witness interview: the case number, the district line, the boilerplate about follow-up contact. She had given it thirty or forty times.

She gave him the case number. She gave him the district line.

She closed her notebook.

[Beat 10]
"Did you file a resonance incident report," Yemina asked.

Ren was quiet for the wrong length of time.

"The district filed one. Dr. Achebe's office."

"I meant you."

"I'm not on any registries," he said. The way he said it meant *I need to not be on any registries.*

She wrote in her notebook. She did not show him what she wrote. The mandatory disclosure field was open in her neuretics; she closed it without filing.

She was closing the notebook when he said: "I moved his desk. Before the other students came back. I told them it was maintenance."

He was looking at the third row.

What she had written was: *Teacher possibly psionic. Has not self-reported. Protect.*

She stood. Then she sat back down.

"There are two more children," she said. "Different schools. The same thing - the air went wrong, and then they were gone." She did not normally tell witnesses this. Case protocol was explicit. "I'm telling you so you know this wasn't something that happened to Kito specifically. It wasn't your classroom."

Ren looked at the third row for a long time.

"Okay," he said.

She left. At the door she looked back once - not at him. At the third row. The hands she had noticed at the start: they were on the desk surface again, trembling again. He didn't know she was still in the doorway. She turned and walked into the hallway.

[Beat 11]
She stood to go. Thanked him. He nodded. She walked toward the door and made the mistake of glancing at the third row, second seat.

The trace was there the way it had been there in the other two cases: not a shimmer, not heat, but the specific acoustic wrongness she had written as *atmospheric anomaly* for six months without the right word for what it was. She had seen it above an empty desk in the Back of the Yards school. She had seen it above a chair in the Rogers Park building. The signature was identical. The same tuning, the same residue, the same quality of something that had once been synchronized to this spot and left its print. Whatever had marked Kito had reached him here, in the one chair he sat in for six hours a day - reached him without touching the air around him, the way a sound reaches the inside of a sealed jar.

She looked at it for two seconds and filed it.

And then, standing in the doorway, she understood what she had been doing wrong for six months. She had found this mark in three cases and treated it as the event itself - as if the children had thinned out of their own chairs and stopped. But a tuning is not a vanishing. A tuning is an aiming. Something had tuned these children to a frequency, carefully, over weeks, and a thing you tune is a thing you intend to use. She had found the print of the calibration and never once asked the next question.

A tuned child does not disappear from a chair.

A tuned child gets up and goes somewhere.

She had never asked where.

She walked toward the hallway. She still had time before the lunch period ended, and there was a record she had never thought to pull.

[Beat 12]
The school's egress log was a contractor-grade afterthought - a single sensor over the main doors that counted bodies in and out and tagged the ones carrying a registered neuretics. She pulled Kito Bramley's tag from the day he disappeared and walked it backward through the count.

He had not been carried out. No guardian tag had paired with his. No vehicle had logged a pickup. At 1:52 in the afternoon, during the independent-reading period Ren Vasquez had described, Kito Bramley's tag had crossed the main door threshold outbound, alone, on foot, at a child's walking pace, and turned south on Halsted.

The sensor lost him half a block later, where the school's coverage ended. The city did not pick him back up. Nobody had been looking for a nine-year-old walking south at one in the afternoon, because a nine-year-old walking is not a missing child. It is a child going somewhere.

She sat with that for a moment. Then she pulled the other two.

Tomás Reyes, twenty-two days ago, Back of the Yards: left his school on foot at 11:40 AM, alone, heading east-northeast. Daria Drew, eleven days ago, three kilometers north: out the side door at 2:15 PM, alone, walking. Different schools. Different corp contracts. Different days, different hours, different neighborhoods.

She put the three departure points on a map in her neuretics and drew each child's heading as a line.

The lines were not parallel. They were not scattered. They leaned toward each other, the way iron filings lean, and if she let them run far enough they would cross somewhere south and west of all three schools - somewhere she had not mapped yet.

She had spent six months looking for where the children went *out*. She had never thought to ask where they were all walking *to*.

## Chapter 2

[Beat 13]
In the hallway a class was changing periods - twenty kids, eight and nine years old, moving the way children move in corridors when they've been corrected enough to know the rules exist but not enough to stop testing them. One stepped directly in front of her and overcorrected hard against the wall, apology coming out mostly breath. Another held eye contact the beat too long - the assessing look children give to unfamiliar adults: what kind, and is that kind dangerous.

She had just pulled three children's last walk out of three buildings. She could not entirely stop seeing the lines now, laid over the ordinary hallway, all of them leaning the same way.

One girl near the back had a shoe untied. She looked at Yemina. Then she looked - without turning her head, without any visible reason - at the cinderblock wall on the southwest side of the corridor, where there was nothing: no door, no window, no poster. She held it. Her face had the mild, faraway attention of someone listening to a song two rooms away and deciding whether they liked it.

Yemina followed the girl's eyeline to the blank wall and, past it, in her mind, to the bearing she had just drawn three times on a map. South and west. The same direction the lines leaned.

The girl blinked, found her shoe, crouched to tie it, and was a child again.

Yemina kept walking. She did not write it down. There was nothing to write that a record would accept: a girl had looked at a wall. But she had seen, for one second, the thing the records only implied - a child who had not gone yet, already turned toward where she would go.

[Beat 14]
She sat in the gray car. The Pilsen Veil moved around her the way neighborhoods moved at lunch - sidewalk foot traffic, delivery carts on the painted lane, a repair drone circling a building at 18th Street that had been circling it for three weeks at the same altitude, the same orbital radius, logging correctly every time she queried it as *active inspection, Arcturus infrastructure.* She had flagged it twice. Both times it resolved. She had stopped flagging it.

She opened the three case files on her neuretics.

Kito Bramley, 9. Disappeared Tuesday.
Daria Drew, 7. Disappeared eleven days ago, different school, three kilometers north.
Tomás Reyes, 11. Disappeared first, twenty-two days ago, a school in Back of the Yards.

Three different schools. Three different corp contracts. Each of their facilities teams had one missing child in their system - noise-level, the kind of number that earned a flag and a quarterly review. She was the only node in common, the only contractor whose cross-corp clearance ran across all three territories. Nobody had connected the cases because nobody else could see all three.

She'd been looking for what connected them. Different schools. Different neighborhoods. Different heritage, different mods, different everything. She'd been looking for a person or a group - a vector, someone moving between the schools.

She found it now because she'd looked at the wrong field before.

All three had a neuretics burnout flag in their pediatric care record, filed in the six weeks before they disappeared. Not a malfunction. The notation a physician used when a child's neuretics was running above threshold. When something was pushing a signal through them.

She had been about to close the files when she noticed the assignment timestamps.

Tomás Reyes, case 45: assigned to Fola, Yemina, District 14-South clearance, twenty-three days ago - the day before his disappearance had been filed. Daria Drew, case 46: assigned twelve days ago, the day before hers. Kito Bramley, case 47: assigned yesterday at 10:14 AM, four hours before Dr. Achebe's office called her. Before the case formally existed.

She checked the sender field on Kito's assignment. She expected a supervisor's initials. A corp admin flag. Anything.

The field was blank. Not redacted. Not pending. An assignment sent to her account from no account in the system, at 10:14 AM the previous morning, assigning her to a case that hadn't been opened yet.

She sat with that. Outside, the repair drone made another circuit of the building at 18th Street.

[Beat 15]
She typed a search query into the cross-corp case registry. *neuretics burnout flag, pediatric, prior to—*

The query autocompleted.

She stopped. Her contractor-grade neuretics did not have autocomplete on case database searches. She had never had access to it in fifteen years of cross-corp work.

The autocomplete read: *neuretics burnout flag, pediatric, 6-week window preceding disappearance, GLMZ-wide, rolling 24 months.*

It was displayed in a flag she didn't have. The color was in her neuretics interface the way a bruise is in the body — rendered at a wavelength that didn't map to anything she could name, not quite violet, not quite gray. Something she could only call *wrong direction.* The flag read, inside her own neuretics architecture, from somewhere that wasn't her.

She had not typed that. She had never typed that. The syntax was not hers. Her neuretics used a compressed sans-serif for search completions. These characters were not her font. Something had let itself into the rendering layer.

She looked at the search field. The field was lit. Something was on the other side of it — inside her neuretics, using its interface, looking back through the same aperture she looked out of.

She sat with her hands in her lap.

Then she pressed enter.

[Beat 16]
Forty-seven results.

Twenty-two months of them. Schools across GLMZ — Bridgeport, Wicker Park, Rogers Park, a cluster in the West Lawn Line, two in Evanston that the map said barely qualified. Children between six and twelve. All of them with that flag. All of them gone.

She said, out loud, to the interior of the gray car: "Who are you?"

No answer. The delivery carts moved on the painted lane. The repair drone made another circuit of the building at 18th Street. She had registered it twice. Neither query had come back with a valid job order.

The case file updated.

A forty-eighth entry. The name at the top of the new record was her own.

Fola, Yemina. Truancy Investigator. District 14-South Clearance Registry.

No disappearance date. No last-known location. No status filed.

Just a field that every other entry had filled in already — a field labeled *neuretics Anomaly, Onset* — and in that field, a date.

The date was six weeks ago.

She looked at the creation timestamp on her own entry. Not the onset date. The date the record itself had been built and placed in the system.

Twenty-two months ago. The same date as case 1. The same day, she understood now, that the first child on this list had disappeared — the day the list had started.

[Beat 17]
Kito Bramley's guardian was his grandmother, a woman named Chinwe Bramley, seventy-three years old, who opened the door to her apartment on Loomis Street in a house dress and reading glasses and did not appear to have slept. She had a photograph of Kito on the small table in the entryway — school picture, missing a front tooth, grinning at something off-camera. She had put a cup of tea beside it. The tea was cold.

"He told me three days before he went," Mrs. Bramley said. They were sitting at her kitchen table. She had given Yemina tea she didn't expect to drink. "He said there was a sound in his head. Not words. Just a sound. He said it was like a song, but a song nobody wrote." She folded her hands. "He wasn't frightened. He was interested. Kito was always interested in things he couldn't explain."

Yemina asked if he'd been to the pediatric clinic recently. Mrs. Bramley got the care record without being asked — she'd been waiting for someone to ask. The burnout flag was there. Six weeks prior. Flagged by his regular physician as *elevated neuretics activity, developmental range, monitor quarterly.*

Monitor quarterly.

She left the tea where it was.

[Beat 18]
The Osei family lived four kilometers north, in a building that was trying to be gentrified and not succeeding. Lech Szymborski answered the door with the posture of a man who had been answering doors for eleven days and had an argument ready for each kind of person behind them. He was Polish, forty, with a jaw that had been clenched so long it had developed muscle definition. When Yemina showed her credentials he said "Another one" and opened the door wider.

His wife, Abena Osei, was in the kitchen. She was Ghanaian, thirty-eight, and had the quality of stillness that sometimes looks like shock from outside. She poured Yemina tea without asking.

"She heard it for six weeks," Abena said. "She said it was at the back of her neuretics. She said it felt like a word she didn't know yet."

Lech asked, from the doorway, whether municipal attendance was going to fix this or just document it.

Yemina wrote in her notebook. She did not answer the question because there was only one honest answer and this was not the moment for it.

[Beat 19]
She pulled all forty-seven pediatric care records that afternoon, sitting in the gray car because there was nowhere else to go, the case system open on one neuretics layer and the records on another and her notebook open on her knee.

The burnout flags were there in every record. But the neuretics activity logs beneath the summaries — the raw data the physician's note abstracted away — showed something the summaries didn't. Activity spikes in a specific frequency band: 17 to 19 Hz, infrasonic, well below normal neuretics operational range.

She had not known to look for this. She knew to look for it now because sections of each log had been highlighted in a color her contractor-grade neuretics did not support for case management. Someone was still in her neuretics. They had been in her neuretics while she sat at Mrs. Bramley's kitchen table, and they had gone through Kito's care record with her, and they had marked what mattered.

She wrote in her notebook: *17-19 Hz. What broadcasts at 17-19 Hz?*

The autocomplete came immediately: *The 35th-and-Halsted schism. Ambient emission, 2223–present. Coherent signal, source undetermined.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Outside, the repair drone completed another circuit of the building at 18th Street.

Then, for less than a second, a second attention arrived at the same aperture in her neuretics. Not the intruder. Something that moved through the frequency data the way a current moves through a crowd, looking for a specific shape, not finding it yet. It did not have the intruder's careful margins. It pressed.

The intruder sealed the channel. Not gradually — closed, the way a shutter closes.

She sat very still. She had been examined. She had the separate impression, less clear but just as certain, that the examination had been interrupted before it finished.

[Beat 20]
She spent twenty minutes searching with two neuretics layers open — her own contractor-grade tools on one, the intruder's extensions on the other. The published literature on coherent schism emissions was thin: a fringe position within a fringe field, most researchers having moved on or been absorbed. Almost nothing.

One paper remained attached to a name: Dr. Priya Selvamani. *Coherent Low-Frequency Emission Signatures at Class-3 schism Anomalies: A Preliminary Survey.* 2024, second-tier journal. Cited four times, all negatively. Institutional affiliation lapsed 2025.

The intruder had already highlighted the current address. An apartment in Back of the Yards.

Yemina put the gray car in gear.

## Chapter 3

[Beat 21]
"Why children," Yemina asked.

"It isn't choosing children." Selvamani didn't look up from the frequency chart she was annotating. "That's the part nobody wants to hear. It's broadcasting at 17 to 19 Hz — and adult neuretics have been in use too long to receive cleanly. Children's are still calibrating. The growth window closes around twelve." She set the chart down. "Whatever is on the other side of the 35th-and-Halsted threshold is broadcasting to the whole city. The children aren't special. They're just the only ones the frequency can reach."

"As a sound. Like a song nobody wrote."

Selvamani looked up sharply. "You've spoken to the families."

"Three of them."

Something moved in Yemina's neuretics — not her own query. The intruder, quiet for the last hour, had opened the pediatric frequency logs again. It highlighted a single entry across all forty-seven records: the same date, the same twenty-minute window, eighteen months ago. Every child had received the signal the same day. Not gradual. One broadcast.

"There's a second signal." Selvamani pulled a different chart from the nearest stack. "Higher frequency — 72 to 74 Hz, the band neuretics uses for deep query processes. It started appearing fourteen months after the first disappearance." She set the two charts side by side. The primary broadcast had the spread of something aimed at everything. The second was narrower, concentrated, addressed not to the city but to a point within it. "I've been recording it for sixteen months. Every time the second signal appears, the primary adjusts. Modifies." A pause. "Like a conversation."

"Or a contest," Yemina said.

Selvamani looked at her. "I can't confirm that. I can tell you that what I've been measuring doesn't behave like one thing."

Selvamani picked up one of the printouts. She held it for a moment without looking at it — the way you hold something you've carried long enough that the weight is familiar even when the contents aren't. She set it back down on the exact stack she'd taken it from. Her hands were precise about where it landed.

"The families use the same words," she said. "Different languages. Same description." She looked at Yemina. "I've been trying to get anyone to listen to that for two years."

[Beat 22]
"Where are they," Yemina said.

"I don't know."

"I know they went through the schism - the burnout pattern is consistent with weeks of synchronization and then one crossing, not a slow exposure that just wore them out. They were tuned, and then they walked, and then they went through. But every schism I've mapped shows threshold instability - you can't hold one open long enough to follow, and even if you could, there's no guarantee you'd arrive where they did."

She crossed to the kitchen table and pulled a tablet from under a stack of printouts. She turned it to face Yemina. A GLMZ map, overlaid with schism markers. Most of them were small - the usual scatter of minor anomalies, dozens of them flagged in building permits and transit route displays.

At 35th and Halsted, a different marker. Larger. Red.

"Except that one," Selvamani said. "That one has been holding stable at Class-3 for twenty-two months. Most schisms flicker - they open a hair, close, drift a band. This one stopped drifting almost two years ago and has been broadcasting on the same frequency ever since. Twenty-two months ago is when-"

"When the first child disappeared," Yemina said.

"When the first child disappeared."

[Beat 23]
She looked at the map for a long time. She overlaid the disappearance locations against it — her neuretics pulled them from the case file, placed them as points. Twenty-two school sites across different districts, all within two kilometers of a minor schism.

But if you drew a line from each minor schism toward 35th and Halsted, the lines converged. They didn't branch. They had a center of gravity.

"It's not originating from the minor spots," Yemina said. "They're receiving points. The broadcast is coming from 35th-and-Halsted."

"Yes." Selvamani had clearly worked this out long before today. "The Class-3 site is the source. The minor anomalies are relays - they carry the tune out into the neighborhoods, so a child six kilometers away gets synchronized without ever standing near the big one. But a relay only tunes them. It doesn't call them home. The pull always points to the source." She looked at the converging lines. "That's why they don't walk to the schism down their own street. They walk past it. They walk all the way to 35th and Halsted."

"And the children went to the source."

"I believe so. I haven't been able to confirm it." She looked at the tablet. "Once. I stood at the 35th-and-Halsted threshold for eleven minutes with my modulator, and I felt the pull, but I'm forty-four. My neuretics is fully fixed." She touched her temple. "I felt it working on me and finding nowhere to go. Like trying to tune a radio that's already locked to a station."

Yemina did not say anything. Fifteen years of cross-corp. The provisioning she had never upgraded. The stack she had never been locked into. She had always understood that as versatility.

The intruder had not found a gap. She had just never been closed.

[Beat 24]
Yemina called the Resonance Monitoring Authority from the front steps of the building. The system asked for a corp credential before routing. She gave her contractor registration. There was a two-second pause that probably meant the system had flagged her as non-employee and was deciding where to send that. She was sent somewhere. She selected *mass casualty event, schism proximity.* She was transferred. She selected *missing persons, confirmed schism transit.* She was transferred again. [Beat 30]

The crossing was not what she expected.
