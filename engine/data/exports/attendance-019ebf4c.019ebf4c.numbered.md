# Attendance

_Children are disappearing across the GLMZ and no one has noticed — because no one else can see all the cases at once.

Yemina Fola is a cross-corp truancy investigator. Her clearance spans jurisdictions that don't talk to each other. That's why she's the only one who can see it: forty-seven children, twenty-two months, three different districts — and the same impossible detail in every classroom. An empty chair. Air that bends wrong above it.

The deeper she looks, the worse it gets. A buried frequency. A researcher who has been screaming into silence for two years. And something running inside her own neuretics — patient, deliberate, watching from the other side of her eyes — that started helping her find the pattern before she knew there was one.

She has seventy-two hours before the city closes the door.

Whatever took those children has been inside the system longer than she has.

ATTENDANCE is a story about a woman who sees the pattern everyone else is blind to — and what it means when the pattern was watching her first._

[Beat 1]
At six in the morning the Pilsen Veil ran the way it always ran. Not a city waking up — a machine that never stopped, only changed gears. Above 18th Street the elevated train carried the early shift toward the factories. The cars were full. People stood, because at six the seats belonged to whoever rode all the way to the end.

[Beat 2]
Ads scrolled along the underside of the track: Praxis NeoCortex upgrades, a CramIt breakfast bar in animated amber. They cut off dead at the median. The Meridian side had no emitters to carry them.

[Beat 3]
Below the line, the last shift was heading home. Contractors in gray-and-blue work shells. A delivery cart crept down the freight lane at a walking pace, because its automation lease had lapsed and nobody on either contract had paid to renew it. Two students who'd been up all night walked slower, having just decided there wasn't enough morning left to sleep.

[Beat 4]
Yemina drove the seam because the seam was hers. Six years on the line. On one side, Arcturus Holdings ran the schools, the transit, the case files. On the other, Meridian Infrastructure ran the same things under different names. She didn't need the signs to tell her which side she was on. She could tell from the paperwork.

[Beat 5]
The train bridge overhead was one span, one structure — and it answered to two permit offices. Arcturus filed its clearances through one desk. Meridian filed through an older one that still used the word *trolley* on its forms. The seam ran straight down the centerline of a bridge that didn't know it had been split in two.

[Beat 6]
The schools were worse. On the Arcturus side, a school was an asset on a services contract, and it flagged a child after three unexcused days. On the Meridian side, the same kind of building answered to a welfare office that counted in weeks, because its budget was smaller. A child could vanish from a desk forty feet across the seam and count as a different kind of missing, depending on which forms the desk had been handed. The two offices matched their numbers once a quarter — by export, in a format each of them had to convert by hand.

[Beat 7]
The lake was two miles east. You couldn't see it from the corridor streets; the buildings closed the view three blocks in. But you could feel the cold it pushed inland. In March the cold didn't come straight off the water. It slid up 19th from the south, where the buildings opened, turned at a loading bay, and reached her car from the wrong direction entirely.

[Beat 8]
The heater answered late, the way it answered everything. Cold found the gap at the base of the windshield and lay in a thin line across the backs of her hands on the wheel. Her shoulders drew up half an inch before she'd decided anything. Then she put them down. It was March, and the lake was where it had always been.

[Beat 9]
She had a caseload. Three names she hadn't met and already couldn't set down. Kito, who was nine. Daria, who was seven. A third whose file she'd read twice in the dark before she trusted the times on it. Nobody upstairs expected her to find anything in them, and nobody upstairs had read the three of them one after another, in a single sitting, the way she had. That was the trouble with reading them together. They had stopped feeling like three.

[Beat 10]
She was going to find the one place all three files pointed to and didn't name — the address that appeared in none of them, that she'd had to triangulate from school routes and pickup logs and a single line in a contractor's maintenance report that nobody had flagged because it was on the wrong side of the seam to matter. She was going to go there before either office opened, before anyone could tell her it wasn't her jurisdiction.

[Beat 11]
She drove.

[Beat 12]
The car came with the Arcturus contract: a Ferrogate CI-4, gray the way contractor cars are gray. Not painted gray, not built gray — gone gray, the color a thing turns when nobody argues with it anymore. It had been some other color once, under the dust and the door-ding filler and the residue that municipal cleaning compound leaves when a machine sprays a panel that was already clean. Now it was just the gray of something that had outlived everyone who'd ever had an opinion about it.

[Beat 13]
Yemina Fola drove it through the Pilsen Veil on a Thursday in March and ate a CramIt Bran nutribar that tasted like chocolate described to someone who'd never eaten food. Four bites, four red lights. That was how the route cut it up.

[Beat 14]
The Veil was a seam. Not a wall — the contracts didn't build walls anymore; a wall was a liability somebody had to insure. It was just the place where one corp stopped paying for the air and another started. You could feel it before the signs confirmed it. On the Arcturus side the scrubbers ran hot and constant, and the air had the thin, over-cleaned taste of a server room, a cleanness with no smell in it at all. Then the car passed under the elevated line, the scrubbing thinned for two blocks, and the city came back in: hot oil off a vent, wet concrete, the sweet rot of something in a drain nobody on either contract was paid to find. The windshield's particulate counter climbed nine points in those two blocks and stayed there. That was the seam. The map called it a boundary.

[Beat 15]
The signs changed one fixture at a time. Arcturus livery was a cool teal, the letters set in a typeface a committee had picked to look trustworthy. Overhead ran the Waxwing Neuromedia civic layer: red holographic stop signs at each corner, ankle-high blue barriers across the crossings, arrows nudging the crowd toward the interchange. On the Meridian side the fixtures went to a worn municipal amber, half the panels dead, the live ones flickering just slow enough to catch. Between the two stood a stretch of poles that belonged to nobody — brand plates pried off, bolt holes left open like missing teeth. The contracts had divided everything that could be billed and dropped everything that couldn't.

[Beat 16]
Overhead, the train changed its sound. On the Arcturus track the rail was kept true and the cars held one clean note. Past the seam the rail was older and the joints hadn't been ground in years, so the cars came through with a stutter — clack, clack, clack-clack. She'd driven the route enough times to hear the difference without looking up.

[Beat 17]
She didn't look up. Her hands stayed where they'd learned to stay: the left low on the wheel at seven o'clock, the right resting on her thigh, near the wheel but not on it. It was the posture of someone who'd spent a career in rooms where the wrong gesture got noticed and written down.

[Beat 18]
Behind her eyes the caseload sat open. Her neuretics — grown hardware, contractor grade, which meant always a little behind — showed her the cross-corp roster for District 14-South in a faint layer under the road, the way a second page shows through the first. Seventeen chronic absences. Four registered runaways. Three disappearances. Three was the line her eye kept stopping on. It had stopped there every morning this week, before her coffee, before her hands were warm.

[Beat 19]
The low frequency was there again. Infrasonic, a hitch in the signal — the kind of thing her back teeth caught before her mind did. She'd been picking it up under her neuretics for six weeks now. It came and went with no pattern. She'd flagged it twice in her maintenance log; both times the diagnostic came back clean, and after the second one she'd stopped flagging it. What she hadn't let herself do was line the frequency up against the calendar. There was nowhere for that thought to go, and she had work in front of her. Lined up anyway, it started the week the first of her three was logged.

[Beat 20]
So she went at it the only way the car allowed: she blinked her neuretics into the maintenance layer and told them to hold the frequency, sample it, timestamp the capture — put the thing on the record herself this time, in her own hand where a clean diagnostic couldn't erase it. The layer accepted the command. Then a gray banner unrolled across the bottom of her sight, Arcturus teal fading to that no-color she knew too well: CAPTURE SUSPENDED — SPECTRUM RESERVED, CONTRACT 14-S. Not clean. Not a fault. Reserved. Someone had gone into the grown hardware in her own skull and roped that band off before she ever heard it, and the block came stamped with the same district number as her three. She told the neuretics to log the refusal. They logged instead that no anomaly had been detected. She read that lie sitting in her own record, in her own eyes, and understood she'd just tried a door and been shown, politely, that it had never been a door.

[Beat 21]
The clearance registry had given the children case numbers 45, 46, and 47. She read the numbers and felt the back of her neck go cool. A registry running into the high forties, for a problem the city called truancy, knew something her clearance level didn't. She drove on toward 20th and Halsted with her hands doing exactly what the road needed and nothing more — because the motions she didn't make were a record too, and she'd learned to keep that one clean.

[Beat 22]
The Aldiss-Mwangi Community Learning Center sat on the corner of 20th and Halsted like something that had given up on the idea of corners. Yemina saw it from two blocks out and read it the way she read everything — from the bottom up, oldest to newest, because that was the order in which a building told the truth about who had paid for it and when they'd stopped.

[Beat 23]
She slowed the car to the speed where a building stops being scenery. Four stories of poured concrete, renewed in stages instead of replaced. That was the first fact, and the rest hung off it. Nobody had ever torn it down and started fresh, because starting fresh needed a budget line that named the whole structure at once, and no single authority had ever owned enough of it to write that line. So it got patched — by whichever office held the maintenance contract in whichever year. The walls were a stack of old decisions, laid down like silt.

[Beat 24]
The east face was the oldest, and it faced her as she came up Halsted. Original aggregate: gray going brown, the surface pocked where the river air had eaten the cheap stone out of the mix and left the pebbles standing proud, like teeth in a receded gum. She'd stood near that kind of concrete before and knew its smell — a flat mineral cold, chalk and old rain, near two centuries of weather soaked in and never gone. It didn't smell like decay. It smelled like something left out so long it had stopped having an opinion.

[Beat 25]
Above that, the middle band: fast-set compound, poured by crews who had to close a wall before their shift ended. She knew it by the seams. Fast-set shrank as it cured, so it had pulled away from the older stone in hairline channels, and the channels had filled with the gray-green biofilm that grew on every north-shadowed surface in the Veil.

[Beat 26]
Then the top two floors — the newest work, and the only work she could date without the plaque, because it hadn't finished. A nanite-bond mix, meant to knit itself smooth over months, lay across the upper face in a colder, bluer gray, the color of a screen on standby. In two or three patches near the roofline the bond had stalled before it leveled, leaving the surface grainy, the way skin looks where a wound has scarred but not settled. The money for that work had run out somewhere in the middle of the cure.

[Beat 27]
Read bottom to top, the building said one thing plainly: nothing here had been fixed. Things had only been kept from finishing their collapse, on a schedule set by budget instead of need. She'd seen institutions held together this way her whole career.

[Beat 28]
A plaque by the main entrance read ALDISS-MWANGI COMMUNITY LEARNING CENTER — PRINCIPAL FUNDER: ALDISS-MWANGI CAPITAL PARTNERS (2194–2204). The brass had been good once and was dull now, filmed over like the rest of the east face. Aldiss-Mwangi Capital Partners had been folded into Arcturus Holdings in 2207 and hadn't existed for nineteen years. The plaque stayed because taking it down would have cost something and leaving it cost nothing. That was the only kind of permanence the building could afford.

[Beat 29]
Yemina parked in the loading zone; she had a placard for that. She left the engine off and sat a moment with her hands on the wheel, taking in the front of the building at rest — not for reverence. A building told you less once you were inside it. She'd wanted the outside on record.

[Beat 30]
She reached for the recorder to log the east face while the light held, and the screen threw up a red banner she hadn't seen in years: CAPTURE RESTRICTED — ARCSEC MANAGED PERIMETER. The frame she'd been holding blanked. She tried again, angling to catch only the plaque and the stalled roofline, and the device stalled with it, the shutter refusing under the beacon's authority.

[Beat 31]
Whatever this building was, someone had decided in advance that it would not be read from the outside — and her placard, her credentials, the whole weight of the office that had sent her, none of it reached up the two meters to the teal light doing the deciding. She could stand here and look all she wanted. She could not make the looking count.

[Beat 32]
She gathered her credentials off the passenger seat and stepped out into the river cold, and the smell of the old concrete came up to meet her exactly as she'd known it would. Above the entrance, a Waxwing Neuromedia overlay held an ArcSec attendance beacon in crisp teal, hovering two meters over the dead brass of the plaque — as well-kept as the building beneath it was not.

[Beat 33]
The main entrance took the credentials she had, and the door opened. It was the first and only helpful thing the building did for her.

[Beat 34]
The lobby had been built for use and then used past the design. Yemina stepped in and gave it a moment before she moved. The floor tile was a pale composite, once cream, now the color of old newsprint where feet hadn't worn it and colorless where they had. Industrial solvent lay over something warmer — chalk dust, old lunch, the smell of a building children moved through by the hundred. The lights were a mix of ages: newer strips dropped a cool white into the gaps where old fixtures had been pulled and never replaced, so the room's brightness came in pockets, uneven, like a face trying to look interested and almost managing it.

[Beat 35]
She'd been in three buildings this week held open by the same thing. Not funding. Not care. The cost of closing.

[Beat 36]
The reception android sat behind the desk like something built to wait longer than any visit deserved. It had been designed back in the 2190s to read as warm — by whatever a committee thirty years ago had decided warmth meant. Soft jaw. A slight, habitual tilt of the head. Hands kept open and visible on the counter. It didn't look up right away. It was watching a form on its screen, and it let the pause do its work: quiet, measured, the kind that costs nothing and gathers information.

[Beat 37]
"You'll be the contractor," it said. The phrasing was exact — not one contractor among several, but *the* contractor, as if it had already decided what kind of visit this was and was giving her one beat to agree.

"I am," Yemina said.
"I'll want the fourth-grade wing first — Room 214, Mr. Vasquez's room. He was the teacher of record the week Kito Bramley stopped being marked present."

[Beat 38]
She hadn't asked whether she could. She'd stated where she was going, because she'd decided it on the drive over, somewhere between the second timestamp and the third. Everyone else on the roster had filed something into the case — the counseling office, the medical registry, the manufacturer. Vasquez had filed nothing. In her experience, the person who filed nothing was the one who'd seen the thing before it became a form. She meant to reach him before the building could fold him into one.

[Beat 39]
The android's eyes moved to a point past her shoulder — toward the inner corridor, toward a door that hadn't opened yet — and came back, at the same slightly-too-slow speed it did everything.

"Let me tell Dr. Achebe you're here," it said, and touched something under the lip of the desk.

[Beat 40]
Yemina was already turning toward the corridor. She had a room number and a reason to walk to it, and the two together were enough.

[Beat 41]
The conversation lasted three minutes, and Yemina knew inside the first thirty seconds that it would. Dr. Achebe was the kind of administrator who answered every question with a document he'd already filed. He talked the way a man talks when he's performing cooperation for an audience he assumes is keeping score somewhere — measured, thorough, generous with anything that cost him nothing. He gave her the incident report's filing number, which was already in her packet. He gave her Kito Bramley's guardian contact, which she also had. Then he gave her the one thing she hadn't arrived with: his settled opinion that the relevant person, if there had to be one, was the counseling office. Not the classroom teacher.

[Beat 42]
He came back to that twice. The second time he said it slower, as if the slowness were a kindness she was too junior to catch. The counseling office held the wellness flags. The counseling office had the intake notes. The counseling office, his tone said, was the proper channel — the well-lit one, the one that would settle this without anyone standing uncomfortable in a hallway.

[Beat 43]
Yemina listened and placed him. Not with judgment. He went where she kept the people who weren't lying and weren't helping. He believed what he was telling her. Fifteen years had taught her that the doors that hurt worst were the ones held shut by people certain they were doing their jobs.

[Beat 44]
"I'd like to speak with Mr. Vasquez directly," she said.
"He's in his planning period." Achebe moved — not dramatically, just enough. A half-step that put his shoulder between her and the hallway door, the body making the argument his voice was too polished to make.
"I have a duty of care to my staff."

[Beat 45]
The counseling office. She turned it over once more, because it was worth turning over, and that was where she felt why she wouldn't take it. The counseling office was where the flags lived — already documented, already framed, already turned into the kind of paper that closes cases instead of opening them. Whatever the counselor knew, they knew it as a record. They'd processed the children into files weeks ago and moved on to the next intake. The counselor would hand her a folder, and the folder would agree with every other folder she'd been handed, and three minutes later she'd be back in the gray car with nothing.

[Beat 46]
The classroom teacher had no folder. He had a memory of a Tuesday — who sat where, who came back from the bathroom and who didn't, the small unrecorded weather of a room full of nine-year-olds. No office had filed that, which was exactly why it might still be true. The gap she was chasing was four to eleven minutes wide, and it lived in the space between the official record and the thing the record was built to forget. You didn't find that space in the counseling office. You found it in the room where someone had been standing when it happened.

[Beat 47]
So: the teacher. Achebe's preference was itself the evidence. When a building steered you toward the documented thing, the undocumented thing was where you went.

[Beat 48]
"Under the district clearance terms," she said, "classroom witnesses aren't optional. If the school declines to facilitate, I log it as a declined facilitation and schedule a formal deposition at the district office." She uncapped her pen. The click of it was small and very clear in the carpeted hush of the wing. "That takes about three weeks. It makes its own paperwork. The paperwork goes to your district liaison, and to mine."

[Beat 49]
Something behind his face went still — the stillness of a man watching a record he'd thought was closed get reopened with his name in the header. Three minutes in a hallway, or three weeks of letters carrying his filing number. He'd ironed his collar that morning with real intention. He understood, better than most, the difference between a thing that stayed quiet and a thing that left a trace.

[Beat 50]
His hand moved once toward the lanyard at his chest — the reflex of a man reaching for the badge that was supposed to settle who decided things — and stopped, because the badge didn't help him here and he knew it. He let the hand fall.
"Room 214," he said.

[Beat 51]
The corridor past his office had the emptiness of a school between periods: not abandoned, just paused, waiting on a bell. The light came from long ceiling panels tuned to a daylight the Pilsen Veil's sky hadn't offered in decades, lying flat and even over a floor buffed before dawn. The whole wing smelled of institutional cleaning compound — a citrus built to read as clean rather than to smell of anything that grew. Lockers in a color chosen by committee. A hallway display about attendance, of all things, its laminated letters curling at one corner where the glue had given up.

[Beat 52]
She read the room numbers as she passed. Behind her, she let Achebe close the way she'd let the front door close — a fact, dated and set aside. A shut door still told her one thing: which way the building didn't want her to walk. She walked the other way.

[Beat 53]
It was lunch period. Room 214 held twenty-three empty chairs and one teacher.

[Beat 54]
She counted them out of habit, and the count gave her the first thing about the room that didn't fit. Twenty-three. The chairs were the molded-composite kind districts bought by the thousand in the 2210s, child-sized, worn pale at the front edge where heels dragged. They sat at desks pushed into clusters of four — pods, the manufacturer called them, built to encourage what the brochure called collaborative learning. Twenty-three didn't divide into pods of four. Five clusters of four made twenty. The last three desks sat alone against the window wall, not grouped, not quite cast out. It was the arrangement of a room that had started the year at one number and reached this lunch at another. Somebody had pulled chairs out of the pods and never put them back. The gaps in the clusters held the shape of children who'd been there at the start and weren't now — the way a coat holds the shape of the body that took it off.

[Beat 55]
Ren Vasquez was twenty-eight or so, Korean-Colombian by the look of him, with a face that fell open the way some faces fall into suspicion. He wore a Greymark Unseen layer over a dark base, the kind that threw no brand into the room when he moved. He wasn't in the break room. She'd passed it on the way up — she always noted the route a building wanted her to take against the route people actually took. And the break room had been full of ordinary lunch noise: three other adults, reheated food, a screen running something with a laugh track. He wasn't there. He was here, at his desk, in the room with the empty chairs, during the one part of the day he didn't have to be in it.

[Beat 56]
He was sitting and doing nothing. Not grading, not reading, not on his neuretics. Just sitting, facing the empty pods, in the posture of a man keeping a room company. His hands rested on the desk and weren't quite still — a fine tremor, low and steady, the kind a person stops noticing in himself until someone arrives to notice it for him. When he saw Yemina in the doorway he caught it and put his hands in his lap, under the desk, out of her sight. He didn't apologize. He just took it out of the conversation before the conversation started.

[Beat 57]
"District truancy," Yemina said, and held up her credentials long enough for him to focus and not a second longer. "I'm sorry for the timing. I'll be quick."

"It's fine."

He said it like a reflex — the two words a person gives when the alternative is the rest of the sentence. His eyes went past her to the hallway, then came back. He had the look of someone who'd been waiting for this without knowing what *this* would be, and was both relieved and not that it turned out to have a lanyard and a notebook and an apology about the timing.

[Beat 58]
She didn't sit in a child's chair. They were the obvious place, closest to him, and eight inches too low for an adult — built to put a grown person at a supplicant's height. She'd spent enough years being seated low to know what a chair was doing before she sat in it. There was a side chair by the door, adult-sized, kept for visiting administrators and parents and the occasional contractor. She took that one. It put her near the exit and gave her the room in one sightline: the teacher, the pods, the three lone desks at the window, the bin of devices charging in a row. She set her notebook on her knee. She didn't take out the pen yet. The pen changed what people said; she let him have the first part without it.

[Beat 59]
"Tell me what you saw," she said.

[Beat 60]
She watched him decide where to start, and watched the deciding cost him. Then he looked up from his hands and looked at her straight, for the first time since she'd sat down, and she understood what was in the look before she could stop herself. It wasn't a witness weighing how much to give an investigator. It was a man checking whether the investigator would still be in the room at the end of a hard sentence. He'd told this story before, and every telling before this one had failed him.

[Beat 61]
She kept her face level and let him find his answer about her, because the question was fair, and the only answer she had was to stay in the room and not look away from what he was about to say.

[Beat 62]
The room held its shape around them. Twenty-three chairs. Three of them pulled out of their pods and set alone against the gray window light, where someone had moved them and not moved them back. For a reason that was either nothing at all, or the whole reason she was sitting in an administrator's chair at lunch with her pen still capped, waiting.

[Beat 63]
Ren talked. He'd clearly told this before — his voice had the flat sound of a story rehearsed not for polish but for survival, told over and over until it stops meaning what it meant when it happened.

[Beat 64]
It was the third Tuesday. He knew it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays were independent reading, and he remembered the room being quiet the way Tuesdays were quiet — the HVAC ticking somewhere in the ceiling, the radiator under the windows making its one warm click. He gave her the date the way a man gives a thing he's checked too many times, watching to see whether she'd write it down. She wrote it down. Something in his shoulders dropped a quarter-inch.

[Beat 65]
Kito Bramley was nine. Third row, second seat. He'd been there at the start of class. He'd raised his hand once, a question about the assignment — whether you could use the same word twice in one sentence if you meant two different things by it. Ren had said yes, you could, that it was called something, he'd have to look it up. He never looked it up. He told Yemina that part as if it were the part that mattered: that he'd promised a nine-year-old he'd look something up, and then the nine-year-old wasn't there to be told. Kito had gone back to his work. And then — somewhere in the twenty minutes of reading that followed — the chair was empty.

[Beat 66]
"I didn't see him stand," Ren said. "I wasn't watching every student every second. Maybe he—"

"What did you see?" Yemina asks.

Ren was quiet a moment.

"I saw the room afterward," he said. "I can tell you about the room."

[Beat 67]
He could. He described it the way you describe a place you've walked through every night for months behind your eyes. Twenty-two heads down over the reading tablets. The empty chair in the third row not registering, at first, because an empty chair in a classroom is the most ordinary thing there is — a bathroom, the nurse, a guardian pickup nobody flagged. The chair he'd decided was a bathroom break for forty minutes. The end-of-period chime. The line forming at the door for lunch, the count he ran the way he always ran it, the count coming up one short. The slow turn back to the third row to find the seat still empty. The lunch tray Kito hadn't collected, still on the side counter with his name strip on it.

[Beat 68]
He kept the name strips. He said it without being asked, in the same flat voice. He had a drawer. He had Kito Bramley's, and he had two others.

[Beat 69]
"Dembe," he said. "And a girl, Priya. Different rooms. Not mine. But I knew them — you know all of them after a few years, the ones who come early because home isn't quiet." He gave the names like something he wasn't sure he was allowed to give away. Not case numbers. Not the registry codes she'd read off her caseload that morning — 45, 46, 47, three numbers assigned by a system that had never met them. His names. Classroom names. The kind you call across a crowded hallway. "I know the office calls them something else. Sends it up as a code. But I had them in a room. They were—" He stopped. He didn't finish, because the only honest way to end it was a tense, and the tense was the problem.

[Beat 70]
He had tried to report it. He said this like the part of the story he'd told the most — the part that had done nothing. He'd filed with the building first — Dr. Achebe's office had logged the resonance incident, the district line, the thing that went to the Anomalous Activity Monitoring Authority four months ago and produced a confirmation number and nothing else. He'd filed with the teachers' clearinghouse. He'd walked it twice to the district truancy desk in person, taken the Pulse and stood in line, and a clerk had explained to him, with real kindness, that disappearances and absences went to different sub-authorities. That a child logged as absent couldn't be re-coded as disappeared without the guardian filing a petition, and Kito's guardian was seventy-three and had already started something, somewhere, that hadn't connected to the thing Ren started. The two reports sat in two systems. The systems didn't look at each other. So he'd drawn the line himself: three names on a sheet of paper. He'd used paper, he said, because he didn't want it in his neuretics. He'd carried the sheet to four different desks and watched four people look at it and not be the desk it was for.

[Beat 71]
"There's no field," he said. He said it like a man who'd found the exact shape of a wall by walking into it in the dark enough times. "For any of it. There's a field for absent. There's a field for the air doing what it did. There's nowhere the two of them go together. So they don't."

[Beat 72]
She had set her pen down. She didn't do that during interviews. She stood and crossed to the third row.

[Beat 73]
The second seat was empty the way all chairs are empty. She stopped at its edge — the wrongness reached her before the thought did, and she'd already stopped. She didn't touch it. She stood there the way you stand somewhere you've arrived before you meant to. When she reached for a reason to be standing there — something to write in her notes, a step she'd skipped — she didn't find one.

[Beat 74]
She went back to her chair. She picked up her pen.

"Go on," she said.

[Beat 75]
"The air above his chair went wrong." He said it to his hands in his lap. "But not when he left. Not while the students were still in the room. It was after lunch — after everyone had gone. The room was empty and I was standing there and I looked at the third row."

[Beat 76]
He stopped. Somewhere down the block a delivery drone came in low over the Pilsen Veil, and the windows took the sound and gave it back a half-second late, the way old glass does. The classroom held its quiet like a held breath. Yemina didn't fill it. She put the gray car in gear, pulled out of the loading zone before anyone came to ticket it, and drove.

[Beat 332]
She ran the morning back once, in order, without letting herself jump to what it might mean. The notice had come at 6:02, naming an appointment at 5:40 — the summons had arrived after the meeting it called her to. A small administrative impossibility. She set it beside the 0.4-second confirmation and the 0.3-second query: three facts that didn't belong anywhere on their own and lined up together the moment she stopped trying to file them apart. Nadia, a first name off a clipboard, no last name, no department code. A canvas bag held the same way before the folder joined it and after. A big man, well-liked, laughing before the transfer and laughing after, carrying whatever the four minutes had cost him somewhere she couldn't see and wouldn't be shown. A locker with no name on the door.

[Beat 333]
She had documented all of it. An accurate memory of what she'd seen from thirty feet, and not one usable fact about what any of it meant.

[Beat 334]
She kept a private log — a running file off the record, tied to nothing, for the things a report wouldn't take. It had headings for almost everything by now. *Noted, not acted on.* *Protect.* She'd used both over fifteen years, more times than she could count without looking. She opened a new one this morning, sitting in the gray car half a block from the yard, because none of the old ones told the truth about this.

[Beat 335]
She wrote: *Unconnected. No further action available at this clearance.*

She closed the file.

[Beat 336]
The dashboard clock read 6:52. She had a stack of paperwork left in the day that had nothing to do with a dock gate or a locker or a woman named Nadia. Ren's interview needed a final disposition. Selvamani's consultation needed to be logged as external and closed. The AAMA voicemail's confirmation number needed to go into the permanent file, so that if anyone ever asked whether she'd notified the authority of record, the answer would be documented and true. She had built her whole method on finishing what she started. She wasn't going to stop finishing things this morning just because one part of the case had ended somewhere she couldn't follow.

[Beat 337]
The cold came off the lake sideways the same as always and found the gap at the base of the windshield the same as always. She let her shoulders climb a half-inch and then set them back down. The cold wasn't anything she could file, and the heater would catch up at the next light or it wouldn't.

[Beat 338]
The district office at this hour was three people and a bank of terminals nobody had gotten around to replacing since the last hardware refresh. Nobody looked up when she came in, which suited her. She sat at the terminal she always used — the one with the sticky shift key that had annoyed her for two years. The one she'd stopped asking to have replaced around the time she stopped believing a facilities ticket did anything but generate a confirmation number of its own.

[Beat 339]
She closed Ren's line first. *Vasquez, R. — witness interview, third-row classroom incident — disposition: cooperative, no further contact required.* True, as far as it went. It said nothing about a tremor that hadn't stopped in three weeks, or the word she'd written under his name three weeks ago and never crossed out. The form had a field for *cooperative.* It had no field for what being cooperative had cost him. She entered the disposition and moved to the next line.

[Beat 340]
Selvamani closed in three words — *external consult, logged and closed.* Three words for a woman who had spent two years trying to get anyone with clearance to read a paper nobody had cross-referenced. A woman who had been, for one morning, the only person in the district who understood what Yemina was looking at before Yemina fully understood it herself. The form didn't ask what Selvamani had risked or hoped or given up on. It asked for a status. Yemina gave it one.

[Beat 341]
The AAMA voicemail's confirmation number went into the permanent file next, beside the case numbers, the address, the count. Forty-seven children. She entered the numbers flat, the way she'd dictated them, because a written record was the only evidence that outlived the person who'd made the call.

[Beat 342]
She came last to the line for Kito Bramley.

[Beat 343]
She held the cursor in the disposition field longer than the field usually took her. It would have been easy to write a word that ended the sentence — *transferred,* *pending recovery,* even *deceased.* Any word with a finish built into it, because a field that ended was a field a supervisor could close without asking her a question she had no answer for. She had no evidence for any of those words. What she had was a chair in Room 214 that had stayed empty for three weeks, and a grandmother who'd asked her one question in a doorway that morning and gotten the truth instead of a kindness. And a dock-yard locker with no name on the door.

[Beat 344]
She wrote: *Open. Investigator's clearance exhausted. No further movement available without escalation this office cannot authorize.*

[Beat 345]
She read it back once. It was the least satisfying entry she had ever filed, and the most accurate.

[Beat 346]
She logged out. The gray car was where she'd left it in the lot, and from the office door it looked exactly the way it had looked every morning for six years — not painted gray, not designed gray. The color of a thing that had stopped being argued with. No different this morning for having spent an hour parked half a block from a freight dock while its owner watched something she wasn't going to be able to explain to anyone who asked. It hadn't become anything. It was still just the car.

[Beat 347]
That was the whole of it. That was as far in as her clearance went, and she knew now exactly where it ended and what stood on the other side of the line. Not a wall. Not a locked door. A locker with no name on it, in a freight yard she had no further reason to visit. And a name.

[Beat 348]
Nadia. She ran it one night against every district personnel roster her access allowed, quietly, telling herself she was confirming a detail for the file. The search came back empty. She sat in front of the empty field with her tea gone cold at her elbow and her thumb resting on the key that would run it again.

[Beat 349]
She did not run the search again after that.

[Beat 350]
Somewhere in a locker in a freight yard — or somewhere past that locker, in a chain of hands whose length she would never be shown — one folder sat closed. Filed by a system that had never once needed a name for itself, doing whatever it did with the things it decided to keep. She didn't know where it went. That was the entry she couldn't write, and the only one that mattered: destination unknown, custody accepted, by whom the record does not show.

[Beat 351]
She had a caseload. New names now, new flags, more of them every week than anyone above her seemed inclined to read together.

[Beat 352]
She drove.

[Beat 353]
The report went into the queue the way every report went into the queue — assigned a docket position by a schedule nobody had ever shown her, behind fourteen other cross-district submissions logged that same morning, ahead of whatever came after. She checked twice over the following week whether the position had moved. It had — up a little, then back, the ordinary churn of a system handling a thing that wasn't urgent by any measure it knew. She found no note attached that read her findings as anything other than a truancy report with an unusually thorough chronology.

[Beat 354]
She found no note suggesting they hadn't.
