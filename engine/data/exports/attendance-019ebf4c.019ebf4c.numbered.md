# Attendance

_Children are disappearing across the GLMZ and no one has noticed — because no one else can see all the cases at once.

Yemina Fola is a cross-corp truancy investigator. Her clearance spans jurisdictions that don't talk to each other. That's why she's the only one who can see it: forty-seven children, twenty-two months, three different districts — and the same impossible detail in every classroom. An empty chair. Air that bends wrong above it.

The deeper she looks, the worse it gets. A buried frequency. A researcher who has been screaming into silence for two years. And something running inside her own neuretics — patient, deliberate, watching from the other side of her eyes — that started helping her find the pattern before she knew there was one.

She has seventy-two hours before the city closes the door.

Whatever took those children has been inside the system longer than she has.

ATTENDANCE is a story about a woman who sees the pattern everyone else is blind to — and what it means when the pattern was watching her first._

[Beat 1]
At six in the morning the Pilsen Veil ran the way it always ran. Not a city waking up — a machine that never stopped, only changed gears. Above 18th Street the elevated carried the early shift toward the factories, the cars full, everyone standing, because at six the seats belonged to whoever rode all the way to the end.

Ads scrolled along the underside of the track — Praxis NeoCortex upgrades, a CramIt bar in animated amber — and cut off dead at the median. The Meridian side had no emitters to carry them.

[Beat 2]
Yemina drove the seam because the seam was hers. Six years on the line. Arcturus Holdings ran the schools, the transit and the case files on one side; Meridian Infrastructure ran the same things under different names on the other.

She didn't need the signs. She could tell from the paperwork.

The bridge overhead was one span, one structure, and it answered to two permit offices. Meridian's still used the word *trolley* on its forms.

[Beat 3]
The schools were worse. On the Arcturus side a school was an asset on a services contract and flagged a child after three unexcused days. Forty feet across the seam, the same kind of building answered to a welfare office that counted in weeks, because its budget was smaller.

A child could vanish from either desk and count as a different kind of missing, depending on which forms the desk had been handed. The two offices matched their numbers once a quarter, by export, in a format each of them converted by hand.

[Beat 4]
The lake was two miles east and you couldn't see it from the corridor streets, but you could feel what it pushed inland.

The heater answered late, the way it answered everything. Cold found the gap at the base of the windshield and lay in a thin line across the backs of her hands. Her shoulders drew up half an inch before she'd decided anything. Then she put them down. It was March. The lake was where it had always been.

[Beat 5]
She had a caseload. Three names she hadn't met and already couldn't set down. Kito, who was nine. Daria, who was seven. A third whose file she'd read twice in the dark before she trusted the times on it. Each of the three closed on the same line of scanner shorthand — *calibration print, residual, low intensity, long duration* — and she had copied it into her own notes three times without once asking what an instrument was being calibrated for. Nobody upstairs expected her to find anything in them, and nobody upstairs had read the three of them one after another, in a single sitting, the way she had.

That was the trouble with reading them together. They had stopped feeling like three.

[Beat 6]
She was going to find the one place all three files pointed to and didn't name — the address that appeared in none of them, that she'd had to triangulate from school routes and pickup logs and a single line in a contractor's maintenance report — *special handling, per instruction*, no requestor, no cost code — that nobody had flagged because it was on the wrong side of the seam to matter.

She was going to go there before either office opened, before anyone could tell her it wasn't her jurisdiction.

[Beat 7]
She drove.

[Beat 8]
The car came with the Arcturus contract: a Ferrogate CI-4, gray the way contractor cars are gray. Not painted gray. Gone gray — the color a thing turns when nobody argues with it anymore.

Yemina Fola drove it through the Pilsen Veil on a Thursday in March and ate a CramIt Bran bar that tasted like chocolate described to someone who'd never eaten food. Four bites, four red lights. That was how the route cut it up.

[Beat 9]
The car was the second-cheapest thing the contract gave her. The cheapest was in her head.

Her neuretics were contractor-grade: district-issue mesh, grown in a public clinic eleven years ago by a technician working a queue, seeded shallow because shallow was what the tier paid for. A proper corp installation ran Φ18,000 and came folded into its parent stack — closed at every layer, audited quarterly by people whose whole job was making sure nothing else got in. Arcturus did not install those in contractors.

Arcturus leased.

She paid Φ90 a month against the lease and had been paying it since she was twenty-nine. In eleven years the balance had come down by a little less than half. She had done that arithmetic once, at a kitchen table, and never again.

[Beat 10]
It worked. That was the honest thing to say about it. It ran her caseload, held her font, kept her records, and lagged half a second behind her on anything the tier hadn't paid for.

What it did not do was close. A leased mesh belongs to nobody enough to be defended. She had known that for eleven years the way you know a lock is cheap — as a fact about the door, not a fact about the night.

[Beat 11]
You could feel the seam before the signs confirmed it. On the Arcturus side the scrubbers ran hot and constant and the air had the thin over-cleaned taste of a server room. Then the car passed under the elevated, the scrubbing thinned, and the city came back in — hot oil off a vent, wet concrete, the sweet rot of something in a drain nobody on either contract was paid to find. The particulate counter climbed nine points and stayed there.

The fixtures changed one at a time. Arcturus teal, its typeface picked by committee to look trustworthy. Then a stretch of poles that belonged to nobody, brand plates pried off, bolt holes open and empty. Then Meridian's worn municipal amber with half the panels dead. Overhead the rail joints went unground and the cars came through with a stutter.

That was the seam. The map called it a boundary.

[Beat 12]
Behind her eyes the caseload sat open. Her neuretics — grown hardware, contractor grade, which meant always a little behind — showed her the cross-corp roster for District 14-South in a faint layer under the road, the way a second page shows through the first.

Seventeen chronic absences. Four registered runaways. Three disappearances. Three was the line her eye kept stopping on. It had stopped there every morning this week, before her coffee, before her hands were warm.

[Beat 13]
The low frequency was there again. Infrasonic, a hitch in the signal — the kind of thing her back teeth caught before her mind did. She'd been picking it up under her neuretics for six weeks now. It came and went with no pattern. She'd flagged it twice in her maintenance log; both times the diagnostic came back clean, and after the second one she'd stopped flagging it. What she hadn't let herself do was line the frequency up against the calendar. There was nowhere for that thought to go, and she had work in front of her.

Lined up anyway, it didn't fit. Her first case was twenty-two days old. The hum was six weeks. It had started three weeks before there was anything for it to start with.

[Beat 14]
So she went at it the only way the car allowed. She blinked into the maintenance layer and told the mesh to hold the frequency, sample it, timestamp the capture — put the thing on the record in her own hand, where a clean diagnostic couldn't erase it.

The layer accepted the command.

Then a banner unrolled across the bottom of her sight, Arcturus teal fading to that no-color she knew too well: CAPTURE SUSPENDED — SPECTRUM RESERVED, CONTRACT 14-S. Under it, in the small type the system used for things it did not expect anyone to read, the reserved range.

17–19 Hz.

Not clean. Not a fault. Reserved. Somebody had gone into the leased hardware in her own skull and roped off a two-hertz strip of it before she ever heard what was in there, and the block came stamped with the same district number as her three.

[Beat 15]
The clearance registry had given the children case numbers 45, 46, and 47. She read the numbers and felt the back of her neck go cool. A registry running into the high forties, for a problem the city called truancy, knew something her clearance level didn't.

She drove on toward 20th and Halsted with her hands doing exactly what the road needed and nothing more — because the motions she didn't make were a record too, and she'd learned to keep that one clean.

Halsted ran south past her turn and kept going — out of the school districts, under the seam, down into the freight yards. She had never had a reason to follow it that far. Nothing down there filed anything a truancy contractor would ever be handed.

[Beat 16]
The Aldiss-Mwangi Community Learning Center sat on the corner of 20th and Halsted like something that had given up on the idea of corners. Yemina saw it from two blocks out and started at the footing, working up. Oldest to newest. That was the order in which a building told the truth about who had paid for it and when they'd stopped.

[Beat 17]
She slowed to the speed where a building stops being scenery.

Four stories of poured concrete, renewed in stages instead of replaced. Nobody had ever torn it down and started fresh, because starting fresh needed a budget line that named the whole structure at once, and no single authority had ever owned enough of it to write that line. So it got patched by whichever office held the contract in whichever year, and the patches stacked. Original aggregate at the footing, gray going brown, pocked where the river air had eaten the cheap stone out of the mix. A middle band of fast-set poured by crews closing a wall before shift end. Two top floors of nanite-bond that had run out of money mid-cure and stalled there, grainy, the way skin looks where a wound scarred but never settled.

Read bottom to top it said one thing. Nothing here had been fixed. Things had only been kept from finishing their collapse, on a schedule set by budget instead of need.

[Beat 18]
A plaque by the main entrance read ALDISS-MWANGI COMMUNITY LEARNING CENTER — PRINCIPAL FUNDER: ALDISS-MWANGI CAPITAL PARTNERS (2194–2204). The brass had been good once. Aldiss-Mwangi Capital Partners had been folded into Arcturus Holdings in 2207 and hadn't existed for nineteen years.

The plaque stayed because taking it down would have cost something and leaving it cost nothing. That was the only kind of permanence the building could afford.

[Beat 19]
She reached for the recorder to log the east face while the light held, and the screen threw up a red banner she hadn't seen in years: CAPTURE RESTRICTED — ARCSEC MANAGED PERIMETER. The frame she'd been holding blanked. She tried again, angling to catch only the plaque and the stalled roofline, and the device stalled with it, the shutter refusing under the beacon's authority.

[Beat 20]
She gathered her credentials off the passenger seat and stepped out into the river cold, and the smell of the old concrete came up to meet her exactly as she'd known it would.

Above the entrance, a Waxwing Neuromedia overlay held an ArcSec attendance beacon — Arcturus Civil Security's own, answering to no office but its parent corp — in crisp teal, hovering two meters over the dead brass of the plaque, as well-kept as the building beneath it was not.

[Beat 21]
The main entrance took the credentials she had, and the door opened. It was the first and only helpful thing the building did for her.

[Beat 22]
The lobby had been built for use and then used past the design. Pale composite tile, once cream, now the color of old newsprint where feet hadn't worn it and colorless where they had. Industrial solvent over something warmer — chalk dust, old lunch, the smell of a building children moved through by the hundred. The lights were a mix of ages, so the brightness came in pockets, uneven, like a face trying to look interested and almost managing it.

She'd been in three buildings this week held open by the same thing. Not funding. Not care. The cost of closing.

[Beat 23]
The reception android sat behind the desk like something built to wait longer than any visit deserved. It had been designed back in the 2190s to read as warm — by whatever a committee thirty years ago had decided warmth meant. Soft jaw. A slight, habitual tilt of the head. Hands kept open and visible on the counter.

It didn't look up right away. It was watching a form on its screen, and it let the pause do its work: quiet, measured, the kind that costs nothing and gathers information.

[Beat 24]
"You'll be the contractor," it said. The phrasing was exact — not one contractor among several, but *the* contractor, as if it had already decided what kind of visit this was and was giving her one beat to agree.

"I am," Yemina said.

"I'll want the fourth-grade wing first — Room 214, Mr. Vasquez's room. He was the teacher of record the week Kito Bramley stopped being marked present."

[Beat 25]
She hadn't asked whether she could. She'd stated where she was going, because she'd decided it on the drive over, somewhere between the second timestamp and the third.

Everyone else on the roster had filed something into the case — the counseling office, the medical registry, the manufacturer. Vasquez had filed nothing. In her experience, the person who filed nothing was the one who'd seen the thing before it became a form.

She meant to reach him before the building could fold him into one.

[Beat 26]
The android's eyes moved to a point past her shoulder — toward the inner corridor, toward a door that hadn't opened yet — and came back, at the same slightly-too-slow speed it did everything.

"Let me tell Dr. Achebe you're here," it said, and touched something under the lip of the desk.

[Beat 27]
Yemina was already turning toward the corridor. She had a room number and a reason to walk to it, and the two together were enough.

[Beat 28]
The conversation lasted three minutes, and Yemina knew inside the first thirty seconds that it would.

Dr. Achebe was the kind of administrator who answered every question with a document he'd already filed. He talked the way a man talks when he's performing cooperation for an audience he assumes is keeping score somewhere — measured, thorough, generous with anything that cost him nothing.

He gave her the incident report's filing number, which was already in her packet. He gave her Kito Bramley's guardian contact, which she also had. Then he gave her the one thing she hadn't arrived with: his settled opinion that the relevant person, if there had to be one, was the counseling office. Not the classroom teacher.

[Beat 29]
He came back to that twice. The second time he said it slower, as if the slowness were a kindness she was too junior to catch.

The counseling office held the wellness flags. The counseling office had the intake notes. The counseling office, his tone said, was the proper channel — the well-lit one, the one that would settle this without anyone standing uncomfortable in a hallway.

[Beat 30]
Yemina listened and placed him. Not with judgment. He went where she kept the people who weren't lying and weren't helping. He believed what he was telling her.

Fifteen years had taught her that the doors that hurt worst were the ones held shut by people certain they were doing their jobs.

[Beat 31]
"I'd like to speak with Mr. Vasquez directly," she said.

"He's in his planning period." Achebe moved — not dramatically, just enough. A half-step that put his shoulder between her and the hallway door, the body making the argument his voice was too polished to make.

"I have a duty of care to my staff."

[Beat 32]
The classroom teacher had no folder. He had a memory of a Tuesday — who sat where, who came back from the bathroom and who didn't, the small unrecorded weather of a room full of nine-year-olds. No office had filed that, which was exactly why it might still be true.

The gap she was chasing was four to eleven minutes wide, and it lived in the space between the official record and the thing the record was built to forget. You didn't find that space in the counseling office. You found it in the room where someone had been standing when it happened.

[Beat 33]
So: the teacher. Achebe's preference was itself the evidence.

[Beat 34]
"Under the district clearance terms," she said, "classroom witnesses aren't optional. If the school declines to facilitate, I log it as a declined facilitation and schedule a formal deposition at the district office."

She uncapped her pen. The click of it was small and very clear in the carpeted hush of the wing.

"That takes about three weeks. It makes its own paperwork. The paperwork goes to your district liaison, and to mine."

[Beat 35]
Something behind his face went still — the stillness of a man watching a record he'd thought was closed get reopened with his name in the header. Three minutes in a hallway, or three weeks of letters carrying his filing number.

He'd ironed his collar that morning with real intention. He understood, better than most, the difference between a thing that stayed quiet and a thing that left a trace.

[Beat 36]
His hand moved once toward the lanyard at his chest — the reflex of a man reaching for the badge that was supposed to settle who decided things — and stopped, because the badge didn't help him here and he knew it. He let the hand fall.

"Room 214," he said.

[Beat 37]
The corridor past his office had the emptiness of a school between periods — not abandoned, just paused, waiting on a bell. Ceiling panels tuned to a daylight the Veil's sky hadn't offered in decades. Lockers in a color chosen by committee. A hallway display about attendance, of all things, its laminated letters curling at one corner where the glue had given up.

[Beat 38]
It was lunch period. Room 214 held twenty-three empty chairs and one teacher.

[Beat 39]
She counted them out of habit, and the count gave her the first thing about the room that didn't fit. Twenty-three.

The chairs were the molded-composite kind districts bought by the thousand, child-sized, worn pale at the front edge where heels dragged. They sat at desks pushed into clusters of four — pods, the manufacturer called them.

Twenty-three doesn't divide into pods of four. Five clusters made twenty. The last three desks sat alone against the window wall, not grouped, not quite cast out. It was the arrangement of a room that had started the year at one number and reached this lunch at another. Somebody had pulled chairs out of the pods and never put them back, and the gaps in the clusters held the shape of children who'd been there at the start and weren't now — the way a coat holds the shape of the body that took it off.

[Beat 40]
Ren Vasquez was twenty-eight or so, Korean-Colombian by the look of him, with a face that fell open the way some faces fall into suspicion. He wore a Greymark Unseen layer over a dark base, the kind that threw no brand into the room when he moved.

He wasn't in the break room. She'd passed it on the way up — she always noted the route a building wanted her to take against the route people actually took — and it had been full of ordinary lunch noise. He wasn't there. He was here, at his desk, in the room with the empty chairs, during the one part of the day he didn't have to be in it.

[Beat 41]
He was sitting and doing nothing. Not grading, not reading, not on his neuretics. Just sitting, facing the empty pods, in the posture of a man keeping a room company. His hands rested on the desk and weren't quite still — a fine tremor, low and steady, the kind a person stops noticing in himself until someone arrives to notice it for him.

When he saw Yemina in the doorway he caught it and put his hands in his lap, under the desk, out of her sight. He didn't apologize. He just took it out of the conversation before the conversation started.

[Beat 42]
"District truancy," Yemina said, and held up her credentials long enough for him to focus and not a second longer. "I'm sorry for the timing. I'll be quick."

"It's fine."

He said it like a reflex — the two words a person gives when the alternative is the rest of the sentence. His eyes went past her to the hallway, then came back. He had the look of someone who'd been waiting for this without knowing what *this* would be, and was both relieved and not that it turned out to have a lanyard and a notebook and an apology about the timing.

[Beat 43]
She didn't sit in a child's chair. They were the obvious place, closest to him, and eight inches too low for an adult — built to put a grown person at a supplicant's height. She'd spent enough years being seated low to know what a chair was doing before she sat in it.

There was a side chair by the door, adult-sized, kept for visiting administrators and parents and the occasional contractor. She took that one. It put her near the exit and gave her the room in one sightline: the teacher, the pods, the three lone desks at the window.

She set her notebook on her knee. She didn't take out the pen yet. The pen changed what people said; she let him have the first part without it.

[Beat 44]
"Tell me what you saw," she said.

[Beat 45]
She watched him decide where to start, and watched the deciding cost him. Then he looked up from his hands and looked at her straight, for the first time since she'd sat down, and she understood what was in the look before she could stop herself. It wasn't a witness weighing how much to give an investigator. It was a man checking whether the investigator would still be in the room at the end of a hard sentence. He'd told this story before, and every telling before this one had failed him.

[Beat 46]
Ren talked. He'd clearly told this before — his voice had the flat sound of a story rehearsed not for polish but for survival, told over and over until it stops meaning what it meant when it happened.

[Beat 47]
It was the third Tuesday. He knew it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays were independent reading, and he remembered the room being quiet the way Tuesdays were quiet — the HVAC ticking somewhere in the ceiling, the radiator under the windows making its one warm click. He gave her the date the way a man gives a thing he's checked too many times, watching to see whether she'd write it down.

She wrote it down. Something in his shoulders dropped a quarter-inch.

[Beat 48]
Kito Bramley was nine. Third row, second seat. He'd been there at the start of class. He'd raised his hand once, a question about the assignment — whether you could use the same word twice in one sentence if you meant two different things by it. Ren had said yes, you could, that it was called something, he'd have to look it up. He never looked it up.

He told Yemina that part as if it were the part that mattered: that he'd promised a nine-year-old he'd look something up, and then the nine-year-old wasn't there to be told. Kito had gone back to his work. And then — somewhere in the twenty minutes of reading that followed — the chair was empty.

[Beat 49]
"I didn't see him stand," Ren said. "I wasn't watching every student every second. Maybe he-"

"What did you see?" Yemina asked.

Ren was quiet a moment.

"I saw the room afterward," he said. "I can tell you about the room."

[Beat 50]
He could. He described it the way you describe a place you've walked through every night for months behind your eyes. Twenty-two heads down over the reading tablets. The empty chair in the third row not registering, at first, because an empty chair in a classroom is the most ordinary thing there is — a bathroom, the nurse, a guardian pickup nobody flagged. The chair he'd decided was a bathroom break for forty minutes. The end-of-period chime. The line forming at the door for lunch, the count he ran the way he always ran it, the count coming up one short. The slow turn back to the third row to find the seat still empty. The lunch tray Kito hadn't collected, still on the side counter with his name strip on it.

[Beat 51]
He kept the name strips. He said it without being asked, in the same flat voice. He had a drawer. He had Kito Bramley's, and he had two others.

[Beat 52]
"Dembe," he said. "And a girl, Priya. Different rooms. Not mine. But I knew them — you know all of them after a few years, the ones who come early because home isn't quiet." He gave the names like something he wasn't sure he was allowed to give away. Not case numbers. Not the registry codes she'd read off her caseload that morning — 45, 46, 47, three numbers assigned by a system that had never met them. His names. Classroom names. The kind you call across a crowded hallway.

"I know the office calls them something else. Sends it up as a code. But I had them in a room. They were—" He stopped. He didn't finish, because the only honest way to end it was a tense, and the tense was the problem.

[Beat 53]
He had tried to report it. He said this like the part of the story he'd told the most — the part that had done nothing.

He'd filed with the building first: Dr. Achebe's office had logged the resonance incident, the district line, the thing that went to the Anomalous Activity Monitoring Authority three weeks ago and produced a confirmation number and nothing else. He'd filed with the teachers' clearinghouse. He'd walked it twice to the district truancy desk in person, taken the Pulse and stood in line, and a clerk had explained to him, with real kindness, that disappearances and absences went to different sub-authorities. That a child logged as absent couldn't be re-coded as disappeared without a guardian petition, and Kito's guardian was seventy-three and had already started something, somewhere, that hadn't connected to the thing Ren started.

The two reports sat in two systems. The systems didn't look at each other.

So he'd drawn the line himself: three names on a sheet of paper. He'd used paper, he said, because he didn't want it in his neuretics. He'd carried the sheet to four different desks and watched four people look at it and not be the desk it was for.

[Beat 54]
"There's no field," he said. He said it like a man who'd found the exact shape of a wall by walking into it in the dark enough times. "For any of it. There's a field for absent. There's a field for the air doing what it did. There's nowhere the two of them go together. So they don't."

[Beat 55]
She had set her pen down. She didn't do that during interviews. She stood and crossed to the third row.

[Beat 56]
The second seat was empty the way all chairs are empty. She stopped at its edge — the wrongness reached her before the thought did, and she'd already stopped. She didn't touch it. She stood there the way you stand somewhere you've arrived before you meant to.

When she reached for a reason to be standing there — something to write in her notes, a step she'd skipped — she didn't find one.

[Beat 57]
She went back to her chair. She picked up her pen.

"Go on," she said.

[Beat 58]
"The air above his chair went wrong." He said it to his hands in his lap. "But not when he left. Not while the students were still in the room. It was after lunch — after everyone had gone. The room was empty and I was standing there and I looked at the third row."

[Beat 59]
"Above his seat. Not a shimmer. Not heat-shimmer." Ren's mouth worked around it, hunting. "More like — if sound could make a shape. A frequency you don't hear. Something behind the sternum catches it first. A pressure in the air that isn't coming from the air. It just hung there above the seat. Like something he'd left behind that I didn't have words for. I looked for where it was coming from." He stopped. His hands turned over in his lap. "That was the thing. When I turned toward it, there was nothing to look at. Not the window. Not the wall behind the seat. It came from that direction — from all of it at once — but there was no point to it. Nothing you could walk to. I moved toward it and it didn't get closer."

He looked up. "I know how that sounds."

"I've heard it before," Yemina said.

[Beat 60]
She had. And she felt it now while he spoke — the same place he'd named, a pressure that had nothing to do with this room. Her own body answered his words before her training could decide what to do with them. She kept her face level. The face was the last instrument she had full control of, and she held it steady the way you hold a hand steady over a cut you don't want a child to be frightened by.

[Beat 61]
Witnesses always found different words for the same thing, and the words were never the evidence. The agreement underneath the words was. A wrongness. A note the room played. A maintenance worker in Back of the Yards had rubbed his thumb across his knuckles the whole interview, as if checking they were still there.

A woman in West Lawn had watched her husband walk out of the frame of their kitchen and not come back into it.

[Beat 62]
She'd run that last one herself, three years ago. The woman had played the kitchen recording for her — cheap ceiling unit, fish-eye lens, the husband at the counter and then a doorway where the husband had been — and played it again, and again, on no system but her own grief.

Afterward Yemina had sat in the gray car in the West Lawn cold and hadn't started the engine for a while. She'd never filed what she understood on the fortieth replay: that a man can simply stop being one of the directions a room has.

There was nowhere to file it. Fifteen years, and that was the one thing she'd kept instead of logged.

[Beat 63]
She wrote: *Atmospheric anomaly consistent with prior cases. Echo at seat, not transit. Transition point unknown.*

Three sentences. The first was true and would survive any review. The second was the part that mattered and the part that would mean nothing to whoever read it next, because the difference between an echo — where the thing had found the boy — and a shadow, where he'd stepped through, lived in a vocabulary the form didn't contain and her superiors didn't want to learn.

The third was the honest one. *Transition point unknown.* It meant he'd crossed somewhere this room couldn't show her.

She didn't write the fourth sentence, the one she'd already decided on: that she was not going to find it by standing in this classroom. That one went where the kitchen recording went.

[Beat 64]
What she didn't write was the boy's name in any field that would generate a follow-up to Ren.

What she didn't write was the word she had for the look on his face — a man who'd decided to keep teaching in a room that had shown him the edge of the world. There was no protocol step for that, and she didn't invent one.

[Beat 65]
She looked at him. She had a close for this — the case number, the district line, the boilerplate about follow-up contact. She'd given it thirty or forty times this year alone. Giving it was usually the moment her attention had already left for the car and the next address.

She gave him the case number. She gave him the district line.

Her attention didn't leave the room. That was the thing she noticed about herself, the way she'd notice a timestamp that didn't fit: she was still here. The case number was a door she usually walked through. This time she heard herself say it and stay.

[Beat 66]
She closed her notebook. She didn't stand yet. The empty chair in the third row sat at the edge of her vision, second seat, and she didn't look at it straight, because she'd already learned in this room that looking straight brought the wrongness forward.

Three names. Three timestamps that missed three flags by four to eleven minutes. And now an echo at a seat, and a crossing point that wasn't in this room.

The next right thing wasn't to file and drive to the next address. It was the other school — the second name, the one whose timestamps she'd only read and never stood inside — and she was going to stand inside it before she let anyone close this case as truancy.

[Beat 67]
"Did you file a resonance incident report?" Yemina asked.

[Beat 68]
It was a closing question. She asked it the way she asked all of them, in the flat after-tone that meant the interview was nearly over and there'd be a form. She had her pen against the page before he answered, because there was always a number to write after this question - a confirmation code, a district filing reference - and she wanted to be ready to write it down and leave.

[Beat 69]
Ren was quiet for the wrong length of time.

[Beat 70]

[Beat 181]

[Beat 183]

[Beat 184]

In the first log, a band of the spectrum carried a spike the summary hadn't mentioned, because the summary had no field for it: a narrow run of activity low on the scale, down where neuretics don't work, infrasonic, below the floor of normal operation. *17–19 Hz.*

[Beat 185]

She underlined the line she'd already written.

[Beat 187]

[Beat 188]

*17–19 Hz. What broadcasts at 17–19 Hz?*

[Beat 192]

[Beat 193]
Outside, the repair drone finished another circuit of the building at 18th, the same slow arc it had run since she parked.

[Beat 195]

[Beat 197]

[Beat 198]

[Beat 210]

She pulled out of the loading zone before anyone came to ticket the car, and drove.

[Beat 305]
She ran the morning back once, in order, without letting herself jump to what it might mean. The notice had come at 6:02 naming an 0540 that was already twenty-two minutes gone. She had guessed the next morning. She had guessed right — and a woman had been standing inside the gate with a bag against her chest, waiting on that guess. An administrative error would have left nobody there.

She set that beside the 0.4-second confirmation and the 0.3-second query. Three facts that didn't belong anywhere on their own, and lined up together the moment she stopped trying to file them apart.

[Beat 306]
She kept a private log — a running file off the record, tied to nothing, for the things a report wouldn't take. It had headings for almost everything by now. *Noted, not acted on.* *Protect.* She'd used both over fifteen years, more times than she could count without looking.

She opened a new one this morning, sitting in the gray car half a block from the yard, because none of the old ones told the truth about this.

[Beat 307]
She wrote: *Unconnected. No further action available at this clearance.*

She closed the file.

[Beat 308]
The dashboard clock read 6:52. She had a stack of paperwork left in the day that had nothing to do with a dock gate or a locker or a woman named Nadia.

She had built her whole method on finishing what she started. She wasn't going to stop finishing things this morning just because one part of the case had ended somewhere she couldn't follow.

[Beat 309]
The cold came off the lake sideways, the same as always, and found the gap at the base of the windshield. She let her shoulders climb a half-inch and set them back down.

[Beat 310]
The district office at this hour was three people and a bank of terminals nobody had gotten around to replacing since the last hardware refresh. Nobody looked up when she came in, which suited her.

She sat at the terminal she always used — the one with the sticky shift key that had annoyed her for two years. The one she'd stopped asking to have replaced around the time she stopped believing a facilities ticket did anything but generate a confirmation number of its own.

[Beat 311]
She closed Ren's line first. *Vasquez, R. — witness interview, third-row classroom incident — disposition: cooperative, no further contact required.* True, as far as it went. It said nothing about a tremor that hadn't stopped in three weeks, or the word she'd written under his name three weeks ago and never crossed out. The form had a field for *cooperative.* It had no field for what being cooperative had cost him. She entered the disposition and moved to the next line.

[Beat 312]
Selvamani closed in four words — *external consult, logged and closed.* Four words for a woman who had spent two years trying to get anyone with clearance to read a paper nobody had cross-referenced, and who had been, for one morning, the only person in the district who could see what Yemina was looking at.

The form asked for a status. Yemina gave it one.

[Beat 313]
The AAMA voicemail's confirmation number went into the permanent file next, beside the case numbers, the address, the count.

Forty-seven children.

She entered the numbers flat, the way she'd dictated them, because a written record was the only evidence that outlived the person who'd made the call.

[Beat 314]
She came last to the line for Kito Bramley.

[Beat 315]
She held the cursor in the disposition field longer than the field usually took her.

It would have been easy to write a word that ended the sentence — *transferred,* *pending recovery,* even *deceased.* Any word with a finish built into it, because a field that ended was a field a supervisor could close without asking her a question she had no answer for.

She had no evidence for any of those words. What she had was a chair in Room 214 that had stayed empty for three weeks, a grandmother who'd asked her one question in a doorway that morning and gotten the truth instead of a kindness, and a dock-yard locker with no name on the door.

[Beat 316]
She wrote: *Open. Investigator's clearance exhausted. No further movement available without escalation this office cannot authorize.*

[Beat 317]
She read it back once. It was the least satisfying entry she had ever filed, and the most accurate.

[Beat 318]
She logged out. The car was where she'd left it in the lot, no different this morning for having spent an hour parked half a block from a freight dock while its owner watched something she was not going to be able to explain to anyone who asked. It hadn't become anything. It was still just the car.

[Beat 319]
That was the whole of it. That was as far in as her clearance went, and she knew now exactly where it ended and what stood on the other side of the line. Not a wall. Not a locked door. A locker with no name on it, in a freight yard she had no further reason to visit. And a name.

[Beat 320]
Nadia. She ran it one night against every district personnel roster her access allowed, quietly, telling herself she was confirming a detail for the file. The search came back empty.

She sat in front of the empty field with her tea gone cold at her elbow and her thumb resting on the key that would run it again.

[Beat 321]
She did not run the search again after that.

[Beat 322]
Somewhere in a locker in a freight yard - or somewhere past that locker, in a chain of hands whose length she would never be shown - one folder sat closed. Filed by a system that had never once needed a name for itself, doing whatever it did with the things it decided to keep.

She didn't know where it went. That was the entry she couldn't write, and the only one that mattered: destination unknown, custody accepted, by whom the record does not show.

[Beat 323]
The report went into the queue and behaved like a report. She checked twice over the following week whether its docket position had moved. It had — up a little, then back, the ordinary churn of a system handling a thing that wasn't urgent by any measure it knew.

The second time she checked, one submission sat two positions above hers with no subject line and no originating office. She had seen that blank field once before, at 10:14 on the morning before Kito Bramley's case existed. It wasn't a new thread. It was the same hand, still filing, still a little ahead of her — and there was no field on any form she had access to in which to ask it anything at all.

[Beat 324]
There was one record left that she had standing to pull, and she had been not-pulling it since the first morning.

The spectrum reservation was on her own equipment. That made it hers to request — not the reason for it, not the requestor, but the reservation itself. Band, contract, date. A leased mesh is still a device on an inventory, and a device on an inventory generates a maintenance history, and a maintenance history is a form.

She filed the request from the terminal with the sticky shift key. It came back in eleven seconds, which was exactly as long as it should have taken.

[Beat 325]
CONTRACT 14-S. RESERVED BAND: 17–19 HZ. RESERVED FOR: SPECTRUM MANAGEMENT, DISTRICT. REQUESTOR: —

Blank. Not redacted. Blank.

She put the reservation date beside the date of her own first maintenance flag — the morning she'd woken with a pressure in her back teeth and put it down to the cold. She checked it twice, because it was the kind of thing a person checks twice.

The same day.

Somebody had roped off two hertz inside her skull on the morning something started using them. Not after. That same morning. Whoever did it hadn't been covering a track. They had been closing a shutter over a window they were about to stand in.

[Beat 326]
There was a form for that. She knew its number without looking.

DEVICE INTEGRITY — SUSPECTED UNAUTHORIZED ACCESS, IMPLANTED. Contractor-initiated. It went to Arcturus asset forensics and not to a corp security desk, because a leased mesh was equipment before it was a person, and equipment got imaged.

She knew what imaging meant. She had read the reports from the other end of it for fifteen years. They pull the stack. They read every layer down to substrate. They find what is in there, or they find the shape of where it has been, and either way they write it down.

She also knew the second-order consequence, because it was in the contract she'd signed six years ago in a room with bad chairs. A mesh under integrity review is a mesh under review. Clearance suspends for the duration. The cross-corp endorsement suspends with it, and an endorsement due in nine weeks does not survive a forensic hold. Nobody would have to decline it. It would simply run out while the paperwork was open.

[Beat 327]
She sat in front of the form with her hands flat on either side of the terminal and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since a woman in Back of the Yards told her the schism didn't want anything and the thing in her did.

If she filed it, the thing came out of her head. Whatever it was, whatever it wanted, it stopped riding her clearance and stopped reading her records and stopped standing behind her eyes at eleven o'clock at night. She got her own skull back. She had it in writing, in a contract, that she was allowed to want that.

And Nkechi Vandermolen had twenty-three days when Yemina counted them, and had fewer now, and there was no field for her — and the only instrument in the GLMZ that had ever produced a list with forty-seven names on it was a thing in Yemina's head that could finish her sentences.

She had asked once, in a car on 18th Street, whether finding them was the point or whether she was only the instrument that made them findable, and whether those were the same thing, and whether it mattered.

It mattered. She had the answer now, and it was worse than the question.

[Beat 328]
She did not file the form.

She wanted it on a record — her own, the private log with no heading left that fit — that she had not filed it, and why. So she wrote it out in full, in her own hand, in the layer the thing could read and could not edit, because there was no longer any point in pretending it wasn't reading.

*Device integrity form not filed. Not deferred. Not pending. Not filed.*

*Reason: the access is producing results the investigator's clearance cannot produce. Investigator is aware that this is the reason. Investigator is aware that continued use of an unauthorized access constitutes acceptance of it.*

*Investigator is aware that she does not know what it is for.*

She read it back once and did not soften it. Fifteen years of writing the sentence the form would take, and the one time she wrote the true one it was a confession — with nobody to file it with, and the only other reader already in the room.

[Beat 329]
The new assignment came in at 11:04 that night, while the log was still open.

Sender field blank. No originating office. She had seen it twice before and both times had gone looking for who. This time she went straight to the body of it, because she already knew the answer to who and had decided what she was going to do about it, which was nothing.

*Case 48.*

Forty-seven had been the whole count. Twenty-two months, every corp contract in the city, a single number issued per child, and forty-seven had been complete.

It was not complete anymore.

*Vandermolen, Nkechi. Age 7. Meridian primary, District 11-North. Pediatric neuretics flag on file.*

Not absent. Not missing. There was no incident, because nothing had happened yet. The case had been opened on a girl who was, at four minutes past eleven at night, in a bed eleven blocks from a school, with something under two weeks of ordinary left in her.

[Beat 330]
The thing in her mesh had assigned her a child before the child was gone. It had done that three times already, and three times she had taken it as a fact she could work from without needing its origin.

This time she understood what she was being handed. It was the same offer it had been making since the morning it put Kito Bramley in her queue four hours before his case existed. It had never once been hidden. She had never once refused it.

She could reach Nkechi Vandermolen. She had an address, and a count that was already short, and a clearance that would be hers for nine more weeks, and a thing behind her eyes that would get her there faster than her own hands could — and afterward there would be no way to say, not to Achterberg, not to a hearing room, not to a grandmother in a doorway, whether she had saved the girl or delivered her.

She accepted the case at 11:06.

Then she sat in the dark with the log open and her tea gone cold at her elbow, and let herself understand, for the length of one honest minute, that she had not spent those two minutes in doubt. She had spent them wanting to be the kind of person who doubted.

[Beat 331]
In the morning she drove the seam, because the seam was hers.

She had a caseload. She had nine weeks. She had a name, and an address, and a count that was running, and behind her eyes something patient that wanted the same answer she wanted and had never once told her why.

She did not check the queue again. There was nothing in it she hadn't already agreed to.
