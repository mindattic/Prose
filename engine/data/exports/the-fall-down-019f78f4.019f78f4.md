# The Fall Down

## Up-Altitude

The Ashgrave Spire's forty-third floor smelled like ozone and new carpet — that specific chemical sweetness of a space that had never been lived in, only maintained. Sixty meters below, a lake caught the light and threw it back too blue, and above it the sky went a gold no sky had ever managed on its own, the city's upper strata floating between the two in discrete lit clusters like something spilled and caught before it hit the floor. It was the Volume doing that — full-saturation Hyper Reality, not the municipal gray-static the Gray Zone got. Reza stood at the corridor junction and ran a palm across the security panel — a reflex now, not a thought, his Tier 3 chrome pulling the floor schematic into peripheral overlay without being asked. Forty-three was clear. It had been clear every morning for eleven days.

Onwe found him at 0800, and he was already three moves into a conversation Reza hadn't started yet — no lead-in, no easing toward the point, just the ask.

"The asset on forty-seven requires a dedicated perimeter," Onwe said. "You'll manage it personally. Rotate your two best off their current assignments."

"Forty-seven's been empty."

"It won't be as of tonight."

Reza looked at the schematic in his overlay, the hollow rectangle of forty-seven's floor plan, and did not ask what was going in there. The arrangement was the arrangement. He pulled two names from his roster — Ekwe and Saldanha, both solid, both the kind of people who held a floor and didn't look at what was on it — and confirmed them with a gesture. Onwe nodded once, the way a man nods when a machine performs correctly, and left.

The Volume feeds shifted through their morning cycle — the lake going silver as the light angle changed, the Hyper Reality overlay compensating, the blue deepening to something no lake had ever actually been. Reza watched it for a moment. Somewhere below the Spire's mooring altitude, below the Air Tax threshold, there was the Gray Zone: a street level where the Substrate ran thin and the feeds ran gray and a nineteen-year-old nobody had once stood in an alley at dawn with a garbage bag and a body that hadn't yet learned to be useful. He did not think about that. He thought about the roster, the rotation, the clean perimeter Onwe expected by tonight.

He had not thought about Tavi in — he did not finish the calculation.

Auda Vane did not do in-person if she could avoid it, which meant she had chosen this café — mid-altitude, Bloom Quarter adjacent, the kind of place with a functioning Substrate seam overhead and a menu written in four languages nobody in the room actually spoke — because she wanted Tavi to understand, before she opened her mouth, that she was paying for the inconvenience of his attention. He was already seated when she arrived. He had been there long enough to know the server's rotation and the sight line to the door, and he had ordered nothing because ordering left a record.

She sat. She did not greet him. She set a credstick flat on the table, turned face-down, and said, "Forty-seven. Up-altitude holding site. Corporate annex dressed as empty floor. Asset is a courier — soft, no combat profile, held involuntary. Ghost extraction. No contact, no trace, no incident report filed in any system after."

"Source," Tavi said.

"Three cutouts. I'm the third." She did not say who was first or second — genuinely didn't know, which told him the money was old, careful, and patient enough to have built a chain that long. "Payment is on the stick. Half now. Half on return of asset to the handoff point I'll send you."

He turned the credstick over. Did not pick it up.

He picked it up. He had been doing solo work for fourteen months — the same fourteen months it had taken him to stop cataloguing the gaps in a two-person approach and start designing around one. The Ankle-Biters rode in a double shoulder rig he'd had fitted to his exact geometry, no slack, no tell, nothing that printed through a jacket. He was very good alone. He had become very good alone on purpose. "Handoff point and timing," he said, and Vane slid him a chip across the table with two fingers, the way you'd push a coin to a vending slot, and stood up.

She was already moving when she said, "Don't leave a ghost."

Tavi rode the Ferrogate Metro Bus to the Spire's base-level commercial concourse — the bus being the kind of transport that logged nothing more specific than a zone transit, a ghost's natural medium — and spent the forty-minute crawl through the Gray Zone's industrial haze reading the chip's contents once, then wiping it. The chip told him what the credstick already implied: floor forty-seven, holding configuration, one non-combatant, midnight window. It told him the asset's name: Rafi Sarkissian.

He looked out the bus window at the refinery haze pressing against the glass, yellow-orange in the low light, and found himself doing the arithmetic he had not intended to do. Twenty years old. Gray-Zone-born. Courier background — the kind of work that paid in immediacy and spent you in the same currency, the kind you took at nineteen without knowing the come-up had a ceiling you wouldn't see until you'd already hit it. It was also the work that put a slug in your hand that you probably should have deleted and absolutely did not.

---

Rafi Sarkissian had copied the slug four days ago in a relay station bathroom in the lower Bloom Quarter, hands steadier than they should have been for someone who understood what he was copying. He was twenty years old and he understood exactly what he was copying. That was the problem. The slug contained seventeen months of Ashgrave Materials' internal logistics data — routing patterns, synthesis corridor schedules, quantities that did not correspond to any permit on file — and Rafi had been paid to move it once, unopened, and he had opened it, and then he had copied it, because he had been nineteen once and learned that information was the only thing a Gray Zone kid could carry that couldn't be taken by someone bigger. He had not yet learned that some information takes you with it when it goes.

Ashgrave wanted the copy buried. The Axiom-funded broker three cutouts removed from a mid-altitude café wanted it as leverage. Rafi was on the forty-seventh floor of the Ashgrave Spire with his copy and no exit, and outside the Spire, from opposite directions, two operators who had once stood exactly where he was standing were moving toward the same door — one to open it, one to hold it closed — and neither of them knew about the other, and both of them had been twenty once, and neither of them was twenty anymore.

## Approach and Defense

The sixty-third floor of the Spire had one working camera cluster on the east stairwell and Tavi killed it the way he killed all of them — not with a spike, not with a burn, just a packet that told the cluster it was already recording from four seconds ago, looped, happy, please stop asking. He moved through the silence he'd made ahead of himself like a man walking into his own wake. The air up here was different. Corpo-cold, twelve degrees below the street, the kind of cold that costs Φ and knows it.

He crouched at the T-junction, listening. Three floors of coverage above the executive suite and he'd crossed two of them without meeting a pattern — no sweep, no handoff, no overlapping sight-lines. What he'd met instead was positioning. A man held the north stairwell door without moving. A woman at the elevator bank hadn't shifted her weight in the time it took him to clear two floors. This wasn't patrol. It was vigil — corporations covered ground; whoever held this pattern was covering a person.

---

Reza had the door.

He wasn't thinking about the door. He was thinking about the node that had just gone quiet — not dark, quiet, which was worse — the one covering the service corridor two floors down. Nodes went dark when you burned them. They went quiet when someone very politely asked them to look somewhere else. He'd seen a system die that way exactly once, in a training scenario a contractor ran for Bear's crew three years ago. The contractor had been a small man who moved like he was apologizing for taking up space and who had put seventeen nodes to sleep in eleven minutes without touching a single one physically.

Reza put his shoulder against the wall beside the door frame, not in front of it, and he did not go looking for whatever was coming up through the building. That was the mistake — the chase, the narrow corridor, the invitation. He held the one threshold that mattered and he let the quiet spread toward him, and the thing he filed and kept was this: whoever was doing the nodes had done it before, and had done it for someone they were trying very hard not to lose.

Tavi reached the doorway on the sixty-fourth floor and stopped. Not because of the man in it — the man wasn't moving, hadn't moved, and wouldn't move unless Tavi moved first. He stopped because of the way the man was standing. Weight back on the right heel. Left shoulder dropped three degrees. Hands at the seam of the hips, not crossed, not pocketed — resting. He'd seen that geometry in a basement in Pilsen, in a drill with a mop handle subbing for a baton, in a man who'd spent two hours teaching a crew how to hold a doorway without advertising it.

He didn't move. The man in the doorway didn't move. The air between them stayed still, corpo-cold, and Tavi took inventory of what he wasn't doing — reaching for the spike in his jacket, stepping back into the stairwell, running any of the three exits he'd mapped on the way up — and did none of it. He was reading the silhouette the way you read a signature on a piece of work — not looking for the name, looking for the hand.

---

Two floors down, the node covering the junction corridor went quiet. Reza felt it before the panel told him — a pressure drop in the feed, like the moment before a sneeze. He logged it and kept his shoulder against the wall and did not go looking. The sequence was clean. Too clean. Nodes peeling in order, not by position but by priority, which meant whoever was running the ghost had a map of the building's sight-lines and had ranked them the way Bear ranked them: cover the egress last, let the approach route go first, protect the thing you're carrying.

He knew one person who called that sequencing *working from the inside out.*

He did not say it. He held the door and felt the quiet come floor by floor and he let the thing that wanted to be a thought stay filed under *later*, under *not during*, under the professional discipline that had kept him whole in six buildings worse than this one. The node covering his corridor went dark — not quiet, dark — and that was different. That was someone telling him they'd arrived.

Tavi's comms were dark — had been since the thirty-eighth floor, a precaution that cost him nothing up here where the building's own signal scrubbers ate anything civilian. He pulled the secondary channel — Bear's, not the crew's, set up three years ago for work that wasn't supposed to exist afterward: no log, a rotating key Bear changed every ninety days with the same password he'd used since Pilsen.

The channel opened.

One click. Not a word, not an ID ping — just a click, the old signal, the one that meant *I see you, hold your position.*

Reza's hand tightened on the door frame before his brain finished the math. One click. The rotating key. The channel that didn't log. He knew one other person who had that channel's current key, and that person was supposed to be in Rotterdam, and that person had taught him the click-signal in a stairwell in Pilsen while they were both bleeding from a job that had gone sideways in ways neither of them had been paid for.

He didn't click back. He stood with the wall at his shoulder and the door at his left hand and the corridor ahead of him empty and dark, and he felt the floor tilt — not metaphor, something physical, the specific vertigo of a situation reclassifying itself faster than his body could track. The person on the other side of the dark corridor had a name he didn't say, not even inside his own head, because naming it would make it a problem he had to solve instead of a threshold he had to hold.

A click came back through the channel — the other direction this time, not his, the only language they'd ever shared that didn't require words. It said the only thing it could: I know.

Tavi moved through the dark on old training — no light, no sound, one hand trailing the wall at knee height where the cameras never covered. The executive suite door was unlocked. That was wrong enough to stop him for a half-second, one hand on the frame, reading the room before he entered it: a desk, a chair turned toward the window, the Lake spread out sixty-four floors below like something that had been there before the building and would be there after. Someone sitting in the chair. Small. Young.

The kid turned around before Tavi made a sound.

"Cutout," the kid said. Not a question. Seventeen, maybe, with the close-cropped hair and the secondhand jacket and the specific quality of stillness that Gray Zone children learned before they learned to read — the stillness that meant *I have already counted the exits.* His eyes were doing the math on Tavi the way Tavi had done math on a hundred rooms, quick and involuntary and trying not to show it. "You're actually Cutout. Rampart said — " He stopped himself. Swallowed. "Rampart said you were in Rotterdam."

Tavi crossed the room without answering and crouched down in front of the chair, eye level, careful to make it read as anything but a threat. The kid — Rafi, the package, the reason there were six static guards on three floors and a man Tavi loved like a brother standing between him and the exit — had a secondhand Tier 1 rig etched behind his left ear, the neuretic trace still pink at the edges, recently grown. Someone had paid for that. Someone had paid for the jacket, too, and the room, and the six guards, and Tavi had a very clear picture of what that payment looked like from the inside: it looked like safety, right up until it looked like a door that only opened one way.

"You grew up on the story," Tavi said, and it came out quieter than he meant it to.

"Everyone in the Yards grew up on the story." Rafi's hands were in his lap, loose — Bear's crew had drilled that into him: don't reach, don't grip, don't give the room a reason. Twenty years old and already trained out of his instincts. "Rampart and Cutout. The two who made it out of Pilsen without a corpo contract, without a Lotus seat, without — " He looked at Tavi's hands, at the jacket, at the spike in Tavi's breast pocket that Tavi hadn't bothered hiding because hiding it would have been a lie neither of them needed. "You're here to take me somewhere."

"Yeah."

"Is it better than here?"

Tavi looked at the kid's face — the specific math happening behind it, the same math he'd run at nineteen in a different room with a different set of bad options. The contract had a name on it and a figure and a client he'd never met, and the client had six guards and a static vigil and a neuretic upgrade for a Gray Zone kid, and none of that was free, and Tavi had been doing this long enough to know what it looked like when the cage had good furniture.

"I don't know yet," he said.

Rafi looked at him for a long moment. Then he stood up, because he was twenty and he'd grown up on the story and the story said Cutout didn't lie, and that was the worst thing Tavi had ever been handed in a room.

## Without Finishing the Other

The Render's service corridor ran east-west, twelve meters, a single overhead strip throwing amber light the color of old nicotine. Tavi had walked it twice in his head before he walked it with his feet, and the real thing matched: drain grates every three meters, a junction box at the midpoint, one camera above the west door with a forty-degree arc and a two-second sweep lag. He'd clocked the lag from the maintenance manifest Rafi had pulled three days ago. The manifest was four months old and he'd told himself that was close enough.

He didn't slow down. He kept his shoulders loose and his footsteps even, the kind of walk that belongs to someone whose job is boring, and he counted the grates by feel through the soles of his boots — one, two, three, junction box — and at the fourth grate he stopped and crouched and the drain cover came up with the specific reluctance of a thing that had been seated a long time, a low metallic complaint that lasted less than a second and still felt like a shout. Below: a maintenance shaft, vertical, three meters down to the Package's floor. The smell that came up was condensate and something ferric, a sweetness under it that didn't belong to metal. He noted it and set it aside.

On the floor above him, at this same moment — he knew this the way he knew the camera lag, as a number held flat in his chest — Reza was thickening the north corridor. He'd watched the guard rotation shift an hour ago from a gutted storefront's window across the street, and the north corridor was where he'd seen Reza's silhouette pass twice against the service window, unhurried, deliberate, the particular unhurriedness of someone deciding where not to be. The south approach was thin. The east was thinner. Tavi had mapped the corridor pressure and found a single path that kept him off every floor Reza had weight on, a route so specific it almost looked like luck.

The shaft was cold and the rungs were cold and Tavi went down into the dark with the cover back in place above him, and the ferric sweetness got stronger with each rung, and somewhere beneath the smell and the darkness and the precise count of his own breathing was a fact he'd been holding at arm's length since Rafi had briefed the job: there was one door between the shaft and the Package, and the door was on Reza's floor, and no amount of routing fixed that.

The door opened inward. Tavi knew this. He'd noted it from the hinge placement in the manifest photograph, and he used the knowledge now — came through low and left, keeping the door's sweep between him and the corridor's center, and Reza was already there.

Not waiting. Moving. The first round came while Tavi was still in his crouch, punched through the door panel above his right shoulder and let daylight in from somewhere, and Tavi's return was low and fast, two rounds at ankle height, because Reza's first instinct under pressure was always to plant and Tavi had watched him plant a thousand times in practice corridors that smelled nothing like this one.

Reza didn't plant. He dropped left and the rounds caught nothing but floor.

*He stopped planting. When did he stop planting.*

The Ankle-Biter was a cheap gun with a cheap voice — loud, flat, no suppression, a bark that bounced off the corridor walls and came back changed. Reza's return fire was quieter and faster and the rounds hit differently, a corporate weight behind them that Tavi felt through the junction box he'd put between himself and the muzzle. The box shredded. Tavi moved before it finished shredding, right, toward the camera, the short direction Reza would discount because it was also the lit direction, and the round that should have found his center mass found his left arm instead, just below the elbow — a graze, not a puncture, but it burned like one — and he got behind the camera housing and the camera housing was not cover, it was concealment, and he knew the difference and moved again.

Three seconds. Maybe four.

Reza came forward instead of back. That was wrong — wrong by everything they'd ever run together, every corridor drill, every argument about angles — and it meant Reza had done the same math Tavi had and arrived at the same answer, which was that the corridor was twelve meters and at twelve meters the Ankle-Biter's cheapness started to tell, and so Reza closed, because Reza's hardware was built for close and Reza's body had always been built for close and this was the thing Tavi had routed around for three days and could not route around now.

The Ankle-Biter ran dry. Tavi heard his own trigger click on empty, heard Reza's slide lock back a half-second later — simultaneous, near enough — and then they were the same distance from each other and there were no more rounds between them, only twelve meters of corridor and the specific knowledge of each other's hands.

Reza came in at the hip. Tavi caught the wrist with his right hand, the graze-arm folding wrong under the load, and used the turn to drive his elbow into Reza's jaw — glancing, not the clean hit he wanted, but Reza's head snapped back, and that was half a second, enough to reload, except the Ankle-Biter was at his hip and his left hand wasn't working right and Reza recovered faster than the half-second should have allowed, recovered the way someone recovers when getting hit is something they've practiced absorbing, and the return came as a palm strike to Tavi's sternum that put him into the wall.

The wall was concrete. It said so.

*You taught him that. That specific palm angle. You taught him that.*

Tavi hit the wall and didn't stay there — stayed meant finished — and he got his feet under him and his right hand found the Ankle-Biter's grip and he reloaded by feel in the dark of his own body, the magazine seated with a click that felt like the only certain thing left in the corridor, and across three meters Reza was doing the same thing, both of them breathing, both of them bleeding somewhere, the guns coming up at the same moment like a mirror that hated itself.

The guns stayed up.

Reza's chest was moving, big visible pulls, and his jaw was already swelling where the elbow had caught it, and Tavi watched him do the math — watched the exact moment Reza's eyes moved left, toward the Package door, and came back — and understood that Reza had counted the same things he had: two guns, two people, one door, and behind the door a Package that one of them was paid to deliver and one of them was paid to stop. Clean arithmetic — a job with its own ending already written in.

*He's going to let me through.*

Tavi knew it before Reza moved. Knew it the way he'd known the camera lag and the hinge placement and every other fact he'd filed and held flat — knew it because Reza's gun arm was dropping three degrees, losing the certainty that makes a gun a gun, more than simply lowering, and the drop was costing him something, costing him visibly, his jaw tight and his eyes doing something that sat somewhere between forgiveness and defeat without landing on either. Tavi's own arm didn't move. He held the sight line and held it and held it, and then he let the Ankle-Biter fall to his side, because spending Reza was not the same as winning, and he had known that since before he mapped the route, since before Rafi pulled the manifest, and he had been lying to himself about what the job required every hour since.

Neither of them spoke. Reza stepped back. One step, and then his shoulder turned — not a full turn, not a presentation of his back, just a degree of rotation that opened the corridor — and Tavi walked through it, past the swelling jaw and the blood on Reza's collar and the gun that wasn't raised, and he did not look at Reza's face because looking would have been a transaction and this was not a transaction, this was something that would cost them both in a currency that didn't have a symbol, and the Package door was ten meters ahead, and behind him he heard Reza's breathing, and then he heard it stop — not stop, recede — Reza moving away down the corridor, choosing the direction that had nothing at the end of it, and the ferric sweetness was everywhere now, thick enough to taste, and Tavi's left arm was burning and the corridor was empty and he was alone with the door.

## The Package Walks

The slug went public at 04:17 — Rafi saw it happen, or heard it happen, or simply felt the pressure change before the rain arrives, a ripple through the Substrate, Axiom's containment flag tripping something in the atmospheric nano-cloud above the Render, and then every display surface in a four-block radius flickering with the package's header data, the routing manifest, the chain of custody that named three Ashgrave shell accounts and one Axiom-adjacent broker whose name Rafi had never been told and would never need to know. Someone had copied the slug before he ever carried it, and had been waiting for exactly this kind of pressure to release it.

He moved while the displays were still cycling. The chemical fire had taken the northwest face of the building eighteen years ago and left a stairwell that no official map acknowledged, three flights down to a sub-basement that smelled of burned polymer and old water, and from there a utility corridor that opened eventually onto a maintenance crawlspace above the condemned street. Rafi knew this because Reza had shown him this, four months ago, walking the Render slow and deliberate, like a man making sure it would stick. *In case*, Reza had said, and hadn't finished the sentence, and Rafi had filed it the way Gray Zone kids file everything useful: without ceremony, without asking why.

He came out on the far side of a collapsed retaining wall with his knees wet and his palms bleeding from the ferrocrete edge and the Render behind him still flickering, Ashgrave's contracted crew pulling back from a fight that was no longer worth having because the asset they'd come to hold was already everywhere and therefore worth nothing. The broker's leverage was gone. The slug was spent. Rafi stood in the pre-dawn dark of a street the city had already decided didn't exist, and he was twenty years old, and he had nothing in his hands, and the next rung of the ladder was somewhere above him in the haze, and he started walking toward it the way you walk toward something you can't quite see yet — not running, not certain, just moving, because the alternative was to stop.

Onwe's message came through at 04:31 — not a call, not a Volume burst, just text on Reza's display, two lines, the timestamp precise the way Onwe did everything:

*Retainer closed. Ashgrave won't seat this.*

Reza read it twice in the doorway of Ozzie's building with the pre-dawn cold coming off the Lake in long flat waves and the Render's residual flicker still visible four blocks south, the Substrate above it cycling through containment protocols that were fourteen minutes too late. The second read didn't change the words. He put the display away and stood there with his hands at his sides, six-foot-three of paid-for muscle and nowhere to stand, and felt the altitude drain out of him — not a crash, just a slow, complete emptying, the specific quiet of a room gone cold once nobody's tending it.

Coeli didn't know yet. She would soon enough. Onwe moved clean and Ashgrave moved fast, and the broker whose name he'd never been told would already be reframing the loss — with Reza's name surfacing as the variable that hadn't held, not Tavi's. Tavi was the ghost; ghosts didn't exist on paper. It was Reza's name on the coordination layer, Reza who'd put himself between the package and the up-altitude world he'd been trying to enter — the same Reza who, two weeks ago, had stood in Coeli's kitchen drinking coffee that cost more than his monthly UBC and believed, briefly, completely, that he was the kind of person that kitchen was built for.

---

Tavi heard it from a Ringo kiosk, which was worse than hearing it from a person — the kiosk's market-feed had already indexed the routing manifest, already filed the chain of custody under *circuit breach, GLMZ-04, unresolved*, and the little scrolling summary at the bottom of the feed said *package status: public domain* in the same register it used for weather and transit delays. He stood in front of it on a street that smelled of burned polymer and wet ferrocrete and read his own failure summarized in fourteen words by a machine that charged Φ0.40 for the privilege. He paid. He always paid. He noted the amount without meaning to, already flinching at a cost he hadn't let himself feel yet.

He thought about Reza — specifically about the moment two hours ago when Reza had said *I've got the egress* and Tavi had let him, had handed off the piece of the job that should have stayed in his own hands, because Reza was there and the timeline was tight and the word *together* had felt, briefly, like a load-bearing wall instead of a door that opened onto nothing. The kiosk cycled to the next story. Tavi turned away from it and started walking north, into the haze, into the pre-dawn gray that made every building look like a thing that was already in the process of becoming rubble, and he did not run, because running was a confession, and he had not yet decided what he was confessing to.

The coffee counter at the Bloom Quarter's edge was standing-room only because there were no chairs — just a poured ferrocrete ledge at chest height and a woman behind it who had been making the same three drinks since before either of them was born. Tavi arrived first. He ordered the cheaper one and put his hands around the cup and watched the Substrate coverage degrade in real-time above the Quarter's boundary, the nano-cloud thinning to something almost visible, a shimmer in the air where the city's basic maintenance just — stopped. He had been here before. He had been here with Reza, the morning after the job that first got them booked as Rampart and Cutout, two years ago, when the world had felt like a thing that was opening rather than closing.

Reza came from the south. Tavi saw him at forty meters and did not straighten up or turn to face him — just kept his hands around the cup and let Reza walk the full distance under his own weight, six-foot-three of it, all that corporate chrome catching no light, face unreadable at twenty meters and at ten and at two. Reza stopped on the other side of the ledge. He did not order anything.

"The egress held," Tavi said.

"I know it held."

"Rafi got out."

"I know." Reza's jaw moved once, a small adjustment, like he was deciding between two words and picking neither. His right forearm was on the ledge. Where his twin should have ridden — the cheap Meridian Ankle-Biter he'd carried the first two years and upgraded away from before he ever went corporate — there was a heavier corporate piece in a proper rig, and above it a strip of medical nano-patch, clean white, already doing its work. Tavi looked at it for one second and looked away. His own twin still rode the inside of his left forearm, the same cheap Ankle-Biter it had always been, the one thing in his inventory the night hadn't touched.

"They drew on you," Tavi said.

"Onwe's crew. Yeah."

The woman behind the counter moved to the far end. The Substrate shimmer above the Quarter pulsed once, some feral swarm testing the seam, and then settled. Reza picked up Tavi's cup, looked at it, set it back down without drinking. The gesture cost nothing and said everything — not aggression, not warmth, just the ease of someone who had always been allowed to touch what was Tavi's, and the weight of knowing that was over.

"You're going to Coeli," Tavi said.

"No."

Tavi looked at him then.

"She already knows," Reza said. "Onwe moves fast." He pushed off the ledge. Not leaving yet — just redistributing the weight, like he was deciding whether the next thing needed to be said standing up. "You should have kept the egress."

"Yeah."

"You knew I'd take it if you handed it off."

"Yeah."

Reza nodded once, slow, and it wasn't a nod of agreement. It was the nod of someone closing out an account he knew was accurate. He turned. Tavi watched him go, south again, back into the haze, and did not call after him, and did not move. Then he unclipped the twin from the inside of his left forearm — the same cheap Ankle-Biter it had always been, the one thing in his inventory the night hadn't touched — and set it on the ledge, and left it there, and the cup in his hands went cold in the way things go cold when you stop noticing them.

Tavi stood at the ledge until the cup was genuinely cold, not just cooling — until the ceramic had equalized with the air and there was no more pretending it was still a thing you'd drink. He set it down beside the Ankle-Biter and did not pick either of them back up. The woman behind the counter did not look at him. The Substrate shimmer above the Quarter had settled back into its baseline degradation, that thin iridescent nothing at the edge of coverage, and the pre-dawn gray had started its slow, bureaucratic negotiation with light.

He walked north because north was where he'd been going before Reza arrived and there was no reason to change it. The Bloom Quarter thinned around him — the body-mod clinics dark at this hour, their window signage cycling through procedures in the Volume's ambient glow, the nano-patch dispensaries already open because there was always someone who needed a nano-patch at five in the morning. He passed the corner where Ozzie's dead storefront was visible three blocks east, the squat's lights still on — Ozzie hadn't slept, which meant Ozzie already knew, and by now everybody did.

The augment booth was at the Bloom Quarter's north edge, exactly where it had been for the two years Tavi had been working this corridor — a half-collapsed transit shelter with the public-feed display long since stripped for parts, and a man sitting inside it at a folding table with a credstick reader and a stack of slips. Two kids stood in front of the table. Seventeen, maybe eighteen, one of them with the new-chrome nervousness of someone who'd just taken their first augment and hadn't finished growing into what it did to a room. The fixer handed a slip across the table. The kid took it with both hands. Tavi watched the kid's face change — that first flush of relief before the weight of what came with it settled in — and kept walking, and did not look back.
