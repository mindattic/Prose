# The Ghost Period

_A wealthy Evanston family hires Kyle to find their missing daughter, last seen researching cat-ear genemods. The father is convinced it's organ harvesting. Kyle follows that thread — because it's there — and finds a real operation. It's not connected to the girl. The girl is in a voluntary 72-hour integration stay at an unlicensed Pilsen shop, offline by design, healthy, exactly where she chose to be. The case becomes a guided tour of the body-mod subculture 200 years forward: SNT bonding, the ghost period, cosmetic vs. structural mods, the unlicensed scene, and what it means to choose who your body is. A corporate investigator (Aleksei) is also on the case, working the wrong angle. Pixel is Kyle's way in. The story ends quietly: a mother and daughter closing a distance on a sidewalk in Pilsen, and Kyle riding north wondering about the difference between modification done to you and modification done by you._

[Beat 1]
The contact request came in at 11:40 on a Tuesday, Evanston prefix, residential channel, clean encryption. Not how work usually found Kyle. Work usually found him through a referral chain long enough that by the time it reached him the original source was three people removed and the job description had accumulated all the creative liberties that passed through six hands tend to acquire. This was direct. The NeoCortex flagged the source address automatically, no prompt required: Tier 4, North Shore, residential block, registered to a property with a tax assessment in the range where people heated their floors and called it practical.

He sat with it for forty seconds, which for him was a long time. The channel was clean, the tone in the opening message was professional without being practiced — the kind of professional that comes from actually having done professional things for a long time, not from learning what professional sounds like. The message said: we need to locate a family member, we were given your name by a reliable source, please advise on availability.

No one who runs an organ operation opens with please advise on availability.

He accepted the contact and sent back a time.

[Beat 2]
He took Milwaukee north, which was the long way, because the long way had fewer checkpoints and he was not in the mood for Arcturus's toll architecture that morning. The city changed in the way it always changed going north: not dramatically, not all at once, but incrementally, the way a conversation changes when someone becomes aware they are being recorded. The buildings got newer. The transit infrastructure got quieter — fewer jury-rigged additions to the Pulse access points, fewer hand-painted schedules posted at street level because the displays were working. The commerce changed character. Storefronts replaced by curated storefronts, which were the same category of thing but were lit differently and stocked with a different class of impermanence.

By Evanston it felt like a different negotiation with the same century. The same pressures had resolved into different-looking decisions. He noted the building from half a block out: fourteen stories, glass and poured concrete, the kind of construction that did not have to apologize for itself. There was a human doorman in the lobby. Kyle parked and sat with the engine cooling and looked at the doorman.

A human doorman meant either old money or theater. The distinction mattered in different ways depending on which it was. Old money meant a client who had resources and was used to having them handled. Theater meant a client who wanted to be seen as having resources, which meant someone who needed to win something. He put on his jacket and crossed the street. He supposed he'd find out.

[Beat 3]
The apartment was on twelve. The elevator was fast and quiet and smelled like nothing, which was its own kind of information.

Douglas Hartley had prepared a briefing on actual paper — printed, collated, paper-clipped at the upper left corner. Kyle hadn't held a physical briefing document in four years. The last one had been from a Corponation legal team that communicated exclusively in hard copy for security theater reasons, which was at least honest about what it was doing. Douglas's paper read differently: the pages were from a home printer, the content was carefully organized, and the act of preparing it had the quality of someone who believed that the format demonstrated the seriousness of the problem. Kyle accepted it when it was slid across the table.

Douglas had the posture of a man who had decided the meeting's outcome before it started. Not arrogant — Kyle had been in rooms with arrogant clients, and arrogance was a different shape — but settled in his conclusions in the way a structure is settled on its foundation. The conclusions had been poured and cured. The meeting was about confirming them.

Mei-Lin Hartley stood at the window with her arms crossed. Not the arms crossed of cold. The arms crossed of someone containing something with physical effort. She had looked at Kyle when he came in, and the look was the look of someone reading a person, not appraising one. Kyle noted the distinction.

In thirty seconds he had what he needed. Douglas had conclusions. Mei-Lin had information. These were very different things to carry into the same room, and the room was small enough that neither of them seemed to have fully registered the difference.

[Beat 4]
The daughter's name was Celeste. Twenty-two. She had come to them six days ago and said she wanted cosmetic genework — functional cat ears, a melanin shift, some expression mods. Nothing structural, Douglas said, as though this were relevant. Nothing that would compromise her function. Kyle waited to see if he would finish the sentence with a subject — her function as what — but he didn't. The word sat there by itself like a load-bearing wall someone had forgotten to connect to anything.

Kyle asked where she'd gone.

Douglas said that was what he'd hired Kyle to determine.

Kyle asked when she was last in contact.

Four days, Douglas said.

Kyle asked if there had been any attempt to reach her through her neuretics — a missing ping, a location request, anything.

The expression that crossed Douglas's face was brief and specific. It was the expression of a man for whom the question had implied something he found unpleasant. He said: she's not responding.

Kyle said: that's different from no attempt. He said it without inflection. He said: can you send me her neuretics ID?

Douglas wrote it on a piece of paper — actual paper, consistent with the briefing — and slid it across. Kyle looked at it. The NeoCortex flagged it, queued it. He said: I'll need an image and any last known locations.

Douglas opened his tablet. He'd prepared those too.

[Beat 5]
Douglas had a tablet. He slid it across without asking whether Kyle wanted it — the gesture of a man presenting evidence to a subordinate, not a contractor he'd hired that morning.

The screen held a curated collection. News items, carefully chosen: body-mod shops that had fronted for organ operations, three of them, two with documented arrests, one with a Meridian 88 investigative segment that had run eight months back. A Pulse Network report on missing-persons cases where the last known activity touched the unlicensed corridor. A Pilsen community alert, six months old. Each item saved with care.

Kyle read through them. He gave each the time it deserved, which was enough to read it and not enough to perform thoroughness. He kept his face in the register that read as thoughtful. The NeoCortex was doing something else underneath: logging the shape of the selection, which was not random.

The articles were real. He had no quarrel with the facts inside them. But the selection had been assembled to support a conclusion reached before the assembling started — which meant the evidence hadn't produced the conclusion. Something else had. Fear, most likely; the particular fear of a parent who'd read the wrong story about where his child might have gone. The articles came after. You don't go looking for kindling until you've already decided to be afraid of the dark.

He held that for later. He handed the tablet back and said: I'll look into it.

[Beat 6]
Douglas took a call in the other room. The moment the door closed Mei-Lin was already moving — not urgently, not with the performance of someone who had been waiting for permission, but with the directness of someone who had been waiting for the obstacle to clear.

She showed him a social feed on her own device, held out flat so he could read it. Not Celeste's public profile. A semi-private one, accessible only if you knew it was there and had a social link through a mutual acquaintance. She said: I found it through a friend of a friend of hers. Eight months of posts.

Kyle read. He took his time.

The posts were not what a kid looks like when they discover something new. There was no breathless enthusiasm, no overclaiming, no the world has been hiding this from me energy that tends to come with a fresh conversion. What he was reading was a research log. Careful, specific, incrementally building. The language was precise in a way that took months to develop: baseline drift, willing flesh, integration stays, the unlicensed corridor, motor map formation, ghost period. The terminology of someone who had spent a long time learning how to talk accurately about something they'd been thinking about for longer.

Eight months of documented research. Which meant the thinking had come before the documentation.

Mei-Lin said: she knew what she was doing.

Kyle said: yes.

She said: find her.

He said: I will. He meant it both as a professional statement and as a fact. He said it the same way for both.

[Beat 7]
Douglas came back from his call and sat down and, almost in passing — the way you mention something you assume everyone already knows — said that he had also retained Meridian Consulting. Corporate investigative firm, he said. Comprehensive service. Two days ahead on the case, working the same file, full team deployment. He said this the way people mention insurance: coverage, redundancy, the responsible thing to do when the stakes are high enough.

Kyle didn't react. He kept his face in the same register and his posture in the same configuration and let Douglas finish the sentence and let the pause after it exist without filling it.

Mei-Lin, across the room, watched him not react.

That was the thing. She watched him not react, and something in her expression changed — not toward relief exactly, but toward the particular recognition of someone who has just confirmed a hypothesis they'd been running quietly. Her husband had hired a corporate investigative firm two days ago and had retained Kyle this morning, and the sequence meant something about what he actually wanted from each arrangement. She had figured out the difference and she knew that Kyle had just figured out that she had, and for one moment there were three people in that room and two of them understood what was happening.

Douglas said: I assume that's not a problem.

Kyle said: no problem. He was looking at Douglas when he said it. He was aware of Mei-Lin when he said it.

[Beat 8]
He took the stairs down. Twelve floors, because the elevator felt wrong for the math he needed to run.

By the fourth floor it was already clear. Douglas had hired Meridian Consulting to confirm a conclusion: that Celeste had been taken by someone in the unlicensed modification corridor, harmed, and needed to be recovered by force or negotiation. Meridian would execute that brief competently and expensively and without ever asking whether the conclusion was correct, because that was not what they were paid to do.

Douglas had retained Kyle — two days late, as a hedge he wouldn't have called a hedge — because some part of him wanted coverage on a different outcome without admitting there was a different outcome to cover.

Mei-Lin had told Kyle to find her daughter. No conditions. No conclusion pre-loaded. She had shown him the feed. She had said: she knew what she was doing. She had said: find her.

Two different jobs in one contract. The invoice would say the same thing. The work would not.

He pushed through the lobby door. The human doorman held it without comment, without eye contact. Old money, then. Theater would have wanted to be thanked.

He crossed to his bike. A girl to find, and a community he'd lived beside his entire adult life without once turning to look at it directly. He started the engine and went south.

[Beat 9]
He called Pixel from the bike, still parked at the curb outside the Hartley building.

First contact since the night in her workshop. He'd thought about what to say for most of the elevator ride down and had the answer before the third floor: keep it flat, keep it professional. The alternative was a conversation that needed more room than a parking spot in Evanston.

He said: I need someone with inside knowledge of the unlicensed mod scene. The parts of it that don't register as addresses.

A pause. One beat longer than thinking. He knew the difference. He let it sit.

She said: parts shop on Damen. One hour.

He said: thanks.

She was already gone. She ended things the second she'd decided them, no ceremony, because the ceremony was wasted motion — he'd told himself that for months, and most days he believed it. Today the beat she'd held before answering was still sitting in his ear.

He put the bike in gear and headed south, taking his time. He had an hour, and Damen was twenty minutes off, and the route through Wicker Park gave him room to think. Riding was sometimes the same as thinking and sometimes a way of not. He wasn't sure yet which one he'd come for.

[Beat 10]
Milwaukee going south was a core sample, if you knew how to read it. The city had been laid down in layers — not by time, exactly, but by the accumulation of arguments about what the city was for and who it was for, each argument settling into the strata when nobody had the authority to enforce a winner.

The North Shore's glass and order gave way at an altitude that had nothing to do with elevation. The buildings stopped apologizing for ambition and started showing their maintenance — the hand-patched infrastructure, the Pulse access points wearing their repair histories openly, the communal decisions about which problem got solved this week with the money that existed. The commerce moved laterally. Φ that didn't climb the chain but went sideways, block to block, in exchanges that didn't need a corponation's processing fee to be real.

By the time he crossed into Pilsen the city felt like itself. Not lesser. The North Shore was the trim. This was the framing.

He noted the feeling and didn't trust it — a man who'd spent the morning in a building that heated its floors should be careful about deciding which part of the city was honest. The Hartley apartment had left something on him, a film he could taste. He parked on Damen and went to find Pixel.

[Beat 11]
Pixel was already at the counter when he arrived, one hand inside a disassembled something he couldn't identify from the door — could be a junction box, could be part of a neuretic calibration rig, could be something that had no official product category and lived in the space between what was built and what she'd made. She didn't look up when the bell over the door announced him.

He came to the counter. He laid out what he knew in the order he'd arranged it on the bike: Celeste, 22, Evanston, functional cat ears, went offline four days ago, parents haven't heard from her, father has the organ-harvesting theory and has assembled evidence for it, mother thinks the daughter knew what she was doing.

Pixel pulled the component free from whatever it was part of. Set it down on the counter. Turned to give him her full attention, which meant she stopped doing anything else entirely, the way she was capable of stopping and most people weren't.

She asked three questions: Was the feed activity consistent or recent-spike? Did the parents have the neuretics ID? Did Kyle know the specific modification she'd requested, meaning the actual structural scope, not the parent's version of it?

He answered: consistent, eight months, yes he had the ID but it was cold, and functional ears with independent movement.

She said: she's not harvested.

She said it the way she said things that were obvious to her and she suspected were not obvious to him — without condescension, but without softening it either.

[Beat 12]
Pixel picked up a stylus and sketched directly on the counter's surface — the counter had a reactive coating she used as a working surface, covered in the palimpsest of previous diagrams. She drew an ear. Then she labeled the parts with the economy of someone who had explained this before and knew where people's comprehension usually fell off.

Functional cat ears — not decorative, not fixed-position, but independently mobile, tracking sound — required: auricular cartilage reshaping at the structure level, which meant the existing ear framework had to be rebuilt, not added to. Tragus repositioning to allow the new pinna range of motion. Follicle transplant with growth programming for the fur, which was a dermal procedure with its own integration timeline. And — she tapped the last item — neural re-routing of the pinna muscles, so the ears actually moved in response to sound rather than just sitting there doing cosmetic work.

That last part, she said, was the hard one.

The brain did not have a pre-existing motor map for ears that moved independently. It had the vestigial map it had inherited — humans had the muscles, they just didn't do anything — but activating and expanding that map to allow genuine directed movement required the brain to build new pathways from the neuretics up. That took time. That took stillness. Neuretics suppressed, external stimulation minimized, because stress during the integration window could spike rejection. 72 hours minimum. Some people needed 96.

She said: she's in an integration stay. She's not missing. She went offline on purpose, because she had to.

Kyle looked at the diagram. He said: I didn't know any of this.

Pixel said: I know.

[Beat 13]
Kyle asked how he'd spent his entire adult life in the modification world and never once seen it.

Pixel gave him the look. He'd learned to read it as the one she made when she was deciding how direct to be — not whether, because with Pixel the answer to whether was always yes, eventually, but how fast and with how much cushioning for the landing.

She said: you have military-grade SNT bonding, full exoskeletal integration, an Atlas NeoCortex that costs more than most buildings in this corridor, and a brace doing load-bearing work on your leg. You've been living in the modification world your whole adult life. You just never called it a world. You called it maintenance.

He considered this. The NeoCortex ran its periodic inventory underneath the thought, the way it always did — brace status, cortical load, SNT bridge calibration, the same read every six hours, numbers he'd stopped registering years ago. Brace at 94%. Cortical load at a level that would be a red flag on any standard installation and was routine for Atlas-grade. The bridges adapting. The negotiation that never closed.

He'd been paying attention to whether the hardware worked. He had never once paid attention to whose it was.

He said: point taken.

She turned back to the counter. She said: I'll ask around. Give me an hour.

[Beat 14]
Pixel drew two circles on the counter surface beside the ear diagram.

The first circle: cosmetic layer. Melanin shifts — the pigmentation algorithms, the ones that could take you from baseline to a new skin tone over eight to twelve weeks, or do it in three days if you wanted the inflammatory response. Hair follicle programming. Eye color. Bioluminescent patches, scale patterns, ear shapes on the cosmetic end — the ones that didn't move, the ones that sat there looking like what they were and changed over time as the immune system reasserted baseline. The body's default position on anything foreign was: we'll tolerate this for now. The cosmetic layer was in ongoing negotiation with that position. Keep up the suppression protocol and the change held. Drop it and you drifted back. Replaceable if you changed your mind. Identity on the surface.

The second circle: structural layer. Functional extra limbs. Sensory expansion — additional photoreceptor arrays, echolocation implants, the kind of input that required the brain to grow new processing infrastructure. Joint replacement with SNT-bonded synthetic. Neural rewiring for new motor pathways, which was the category Celeste's ears belonged to. Longer integration, real medical risk in unlicensed settings, but once the brain built the map, the map was permanent. You could remove the hardware. You could not remove the map. The brain did not unlearn a pathway it had built and used for two years.

She said: Celeste's feed reads like someone who started in the first circle and figured out she was actually interested in the question the second circle asks.

Kyle said: what's the question?

Pixel said: what does my body want to be.

She capped the stylus.

[Beat 15]
Pixel named three operators. She named them in the way she named things she knew from inside — no hesitation, no qualifier, no I think it might be, just the names and the geography attached to them. Pilsen, Bridgeport, Back of the Yards. The corridor that ran south of the gray zone, where the unlicensed work happened in the spaces left by the licensed industry's routing choices: where the licensed supply chain didn't run, where the regulatory enforcement was inconsistent enough to breathe in, where the community density provided the kind of distributed social accountability that worked better than most oversight boards she'd encountered.

One of the three did cat ears, she said. She knew them from inside because she had done unlicensed calibration work for some of them — the SNT bridges that the licensed supply chain wouldn't service, because the hardware was installed by someone without a current corponation contract, which was a category distinction that had nothing to do with the quality of the installation or the health of the patient. She had calibrated SNT bridges in unlicensed settings for two years. The adverse event rate was something she could speak to personally.

She said she would make calls. She would ask about a client matching the description — not the name, she said, not the name — and let him know.

Kyle said he would canvas the first two addresses while she worked her connections. He said it as a division of labor. It was also the more dangerous half of the work, and he was aware of that, and he didn't mention it.

[Beat 16]
The first address was a recessed door off Ashland, behind a building that had been a pharmacy before the local pharmaceutical distribution model had been absorbed by Arcturus's Wellness Division — one of the quieter acquisitions of the early 2210s, a corponation deciding that the gap between what people needed and what licensed channels provided was large enough to qualify as infrastructure, and infrastructure could be owned. The door was the kind that didn't announce itself.

A Meridian Consulting van was already parked on the block.

Clean. Wrong for the neighborhood the way a new installation is wrong when it hasn't been weathered yet. The van was the correct color and the correct size for this street and had managed, through some quality of newness and precision maintenance, to stand out anyway. Kyle read it from the corner before he committed to the block, the NeoCortex logging it without prompting.

The man stepping out was Aleksei. Kyle had crossed his path twice on contract adjacencies — not working together, working in the same operational space, which was a different thing. The kind of professional you nodded at in lobbies. Tier equivalent, no discernible ego about the equivalence.

Aleksei saw Kyle. He produced no visible reaction. He said: Hartley girl.

Kyle said: Hartley girl.

Aleksei said: I'm two days ahead of you.

Kyle said: I know.

They looked at each other for a moment. Neither of them had anything additional to add to this accounting. Aleksei's expression said he found this reasonable. Kyle's expression said the same thing.

[Beat 17]
Neither of them wanted to pool resources. Not unfriendliness — operational clarity. Pooling meant sharing contact networks, which meant burning contacts through redundant approach, which meant neither of them could work this corridor again with the same ease. The unlicensed community kept a long memory for investigators who treated it as a seam to mine. Pixel had told him this; he'd understood it as information. He was understanding it now as cost, which was a different kind of understanding.

They stood on the sidewalk and arrived at the minimum viable arrangement in about forty-five seconds, neither of them having to say it. Negative findings: shared. If one cleared a location — not Celeste, not the integration stay — he flagged the other. No further coordination. No report, no check-in, no touching base. Just: not here, clean, move on.

Aleksei said: reasonable.

Kyle said: reasonable.

He said: you take this block, I'll take Bridgeport.

Aleksei said: I was going to say the same thing. He said it without staking a claim on having thought of it first. Kyle went south. Aleksei went to the door. Kyle didn't look back. The arrangement would hold or it wouldn't — but Aleksei was the kind who made the arrangements hold, and Kyle had stopped checking over his shoulder for that kind a long time ago.

[Beat 18]
The first operator ran out of the back of what looked like a textile repair business. The front room smelled of heat-bonding compounds and recycled fiber and the particular quality of air that builds up in a room where focused work happens over many years. The woman at the front desk did not ask what he needed. She looked at him for three seconds and said: back room, knock first.

He knocked. The woman inside was mid-fifties, with the precise hands of someone who worked small — the kind of precision that comes from doing detailed work for so long that the hands develop their own calibration, a steadiness that didn't require conscious attention. She had the manner of someone who had possessed a license once and had lost it in the specific kind of dispute that occurs when a corporation's definition of whose body an installation belongs to and a practitioner's definition of the same thing arrive at different conclusions and neither one has the political capital to make the other one move.

She did limb attachment. Sensory expansion. She did not do cat ears — she was clear about this, without hedging, without the defensive quality that comes from expecting accusation. She simply did not do it. Different specialty.

She was willing to talk because she had looked at Kyle and determined he was not Arcturus and not a journalist, two things she said were equally dangerous to her business in different ways. She said this without anger. She showed him the front room of the operation without asking why he wanted to see it, which was either trust or a demonstration of confidence, and Kyle couldn't tell which and suspected it was both.

[Beat 19]
In the back room, a young man slept in a reclined chair with neural dampeners over his eyes. The dampeners were the low-profile kind — not the blunt instrument that full neuretics suppression required in older hardware, but the calibrated version, which worked by narrowing the input bandwidth rather than shutting it off, the way you dim a light rather than kill the circuit. His breathing was even. His face was still.

His new arm rested on a foam support beside him.

It was fully formed and fully attached, joining the body at a point just below the left shoulder where the junction site showed the calibration markers the SNT bridge required during initial bonding — small printed indicators the color of fresh bruising, temporary, they'd fade when the body stopped needing the external reference points. The arm was indistinguishable from the other two in construction. It was not indistinguishable in one quality: it was still. Not relaxed in the way of sleep. Still in the way of a thing that was present in the body but not yet claimed by it.

The operator said: he'll be in ghost period for two to four weeks. The arm won't feel like his yet. The brain knows it's there but hasn't built the map that makes it feel like it belongs. Some people panic in ghost period, she said. Some cry. She paused. She said: some people feel complete for the first time.

She said the last thing the way she might say the sun comes up in the east. As a fact that happened to be good.

[Beat 20]
Kyle asked her to explain the SNT difference. He said: I have military-grade. I know the maintenance cycle. I've never known the alternative.

She walked him through it without performing surprise that he'd ask. She had the quality of a person who spent very little energy managing other people's comfort with their own ignorance.

Military-grade SNT, she said, uses a bandwidth-brute protocol. The synthetic pathway is forced into connection with the organic nerve endings. The immune system identifies it as foreign and fights it perpetually. Monthly calibration keeps the bridge from degrading — because without calibration the immune response would win, and the bridge would fail, and the hardware would go dark. The body tolerates the installation. It does not accept it.

Consumer-grade SNT works differently. The synthetic tissue is seeded rather than forced — it's introduced in contact with the organic nerve endings and left to grow. Over six months it integrates. The nerve endings extend toward it. The body stops reading it as foreign and starts reading it as self. Once accepted, no maintenance. No monthly calibration. The prosthetic reports to the brain the way flesh does: as something that was always there.

Better than flesh, in some configurations, she said. No involuntary pain response unless the client programs one.

Kyle asked why the military didn't use it.

She said: the military doesn't want its hardware to feel like your body. If it feels like your body, you might start making decisions about it the way you make decisions about your body. It wants you to know the hardware belongs to them.

She said it without particular heat. As another fact.

[Beat 21]
Kyle asked directly. He said: there's an operation in this corridor that the community knows about. Not a modification shop.

She did not pretend not to know what he meant. She did not check his face for evidence of a setup. She had already decided what he was and the decision held.

She said: yes. And she said: I won't name it. The community has been working to push it out for two years. The problem — she used the word problem the way you use it for something that is accurate and insufficient at the same time — is that every time something like that surfaces, it becomes the story. The unlicensed modification corridor, the organ operation, the harvested client — that becomes the story that people tell about this work. The community's two years of trying to push it out does not become the story. The journalists don't cover the community's enforcement of its own code. They cover the violation.

She said: it poisons every conversation about unlicensed modification with the word they all dread.

Kyle didn't ask what word. He knew what word.

She said: Ashland, near Cermak. Former clinic. The signage is gone but you'll read the building.

He said: thank you.

She said: find what you're looking for and leave the rest of us alone.

He said: that's the plan.

[Beat 22]
The building was a former licensed clinic. The signage was gone but the ghost of it stayed — the mounting hardware, the shadow lines on the brick where the letters had blocked weather for years, the anchor points set at a height that read medical-professional, not retail. Someone had taken the sign down and left the evidence of the sign. An operational decision that made sense in the short term.

From the outside it read body-mod. The window display had the right vocabulary — the curated visual language the community used, the same photographs, the same aesthetic, done correctly. Correctly was its own kind of information.

From the outside it also had a loading dock on the side street taking traffic at hours no cosmetic practice kept. Kyle clocked two deliveries in six minutes, neither one the scale of consumables. The refrigeration behind a ground-floor grate ran larger than a mod shop needed — cold storage sized for quantity, not for patients.

And the absence. A working body-mod operation had a patient rhythm: arrivals, appointments, departures, people in the before-state and people in the after. This building had traffic and no rhythm. People went in. The street never got them back in any shape worth photographing.

He read it for six minutes. He did not go in. He had enough.

He called Aleksei.

[Beat 23]
He told Aleksei: organ operation, Ashland near Cermak, former clinic, loading dock live on the south side, refrigeration at scale. He said: not the Hartley girl. He said: clean negative on the locate. Different kind of problem.

Aleksei was quiet a moment. Not the quiet of surprise — the quiet of a man re-filing something into the right drawer.

He said: I can route this to Arcturus's organized-crime unit. They'll move. The synthetic-organ manufacturers have been lobbying enforcement for two years; this is the exact operation that hands them the narrative they want about unlicensed modification. They'll jump.

Kyle said: I know.

Aleksei said: you cleared it anyway.

Kyle said: I cleared it.

He'd spent six minutes reading a building and the byproduct was a thing enforcement could use. He'd routed it because not routing it would have meant deciding why not, and he didn't have an answer to that he could stand behind. The community had been trying to push this operation out for two years. Handing it to Arcturus wasn't the community's method. But it was a method that worked, and a method that worked argued for itself, and he'd learned to be suspicious of every time it did.

Aleksei said: I'll make the call.

Kyle said: all right.

He put the bike in gear and didn't feel clean about it.

[Beat 24]
Pixel's message came through while he was still on Ashland, the device buzzing once against his chest pocket.

Storefront on 18th in Pilsen. Operator named Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo — she spelled it out, which meant she expected him to say it right when it came time to introduce himself. Known in the community, she wrote. Good reputation. Cosmetic and light structural. Runs integration stays. Six years at it.

Then: I called ahead. She knows you're coming.

Then: she won't confirm or deny clients. That's standard. Just talk to her. Don't be a cop about it.

Kyle read the last line twice. A note on something she'd seen in him, or a thing she'd tell anyone walking into that room — it was both, and she'd known it was both when she wrote it, and she'd sent it anyway. He put the device away.

He went south on Ashland, turned east on 18th, looking for the storefront.
