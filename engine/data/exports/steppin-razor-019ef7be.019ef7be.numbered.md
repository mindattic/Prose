# Steppin' Razor

_She has been hearing the schism since she was small and cannot make it stop.

Sasha Võ is nineteen, gray-zone freelance, no registered identity — born and grown in the only places quiet enough to manage the noise. Her neuretics were never locked to a band. That means the membrane between the world and whatever leans against it from the other side has been audible her entire life: a low hum under everything, a wall she can feel in every room she walks into. The one thing loud enough to drown it is violence. So she picks fights for thirty seconds of silence, and does cold, terrible things without heat, the way you'd crank a radio to kill a noise.

When the fight ends, the hum comes back.

The membrane is thinnest where the towers are heaviest. The rot was never at the edge.

STEPPIN' RAZOR is a story about a woman who has been hearing the end of the world since she was small — and what it costs to be the one who carries the warning in._

[Beat 1]
The yard smelled of diesel and rust water. She paid Φ3 at the gate — a transit slug fed into the reader while the dispatcher watched his screen instead of her face. Neither of them wanted a look at the other. She crossed without checking her back and pushed through the lit door into the noise.

[Beat 2]
The Pulse hub at Kankakee ran all night. Freight didn't keep hours. Eighteen bays cycled their pressure down the line, the big compressors kicking over in sequence, so the floor never quite stopped shaking under a boot. Cargo slugs dropped off the north feed onto the cradles. The cradles walked them to the outbound bays. Forty-three workers in gray coveralls moved between them with hooks and manifests and strong opinions about how to stack a pallet. The junction had done this for sixty years and did not care who watched.

[Beat 3]
She was the thing that watched.

[Beat 4]
Corner position. That was the term she'd set with the fixer. Not a question.

"Corner position. I don't touch anyone unless the deal goes sideways. I leave when I say."

The fixer had said something about a quiet presence. A cargo-clearance negotiation. Stand in the corner, look at the wall, be the thing that doesn't have to happen because it's there.

[Beat 5]
The deal was forty slugs of gray-market neuretics being repapered as regulated medical gear. Neuretics: the mesh grown into your skull before you turn ten, meant to seal at twelve and go quiet for life. Everyone's did. Hers hadn't. That was why she could stand in the corner and hear the low thing running under the floor that none of the forty-three could. Everyone in the room knew what the slugs were. The paper said otherwise.

[Beat 6]
She stood in the corner. She looked at the wall. She listened to the thing under the floor.

[Beat 7]
It had been there since she was nine. A weight with no source she could point to — not a sound, exactly, something down in the gap between what the ear takes and what the brain does with the rest. It came up through this floor the way it came up through every floor, heavier where the ground was thick with people and machines. It got louder when she was still and quieter when she worked. In the corner of a sorting office, doing nothing, it sat at full weight. That was one of the three reasons she'd taken a job she should have turned down.

[Beat 8]
The man who noticed her was on the wrong side of the table. Not the client — the other one, the documentation man, with a face built for sizing people up. He'd been sizing her since she walked in. She was nineteen and small and the answer he was reaching was never going to help him.

"How old is she?" he asked. Not to her.

The fixer said something that meant nothing. The documentation man tilted his head, patient as a man who'd been right for twenty years.

"She's what, eighteen? Nineteen? What's she supposed to do if this goes sideways?" he asked.

[Beat 9]
She crossed the room in six steps and hit him with the bottle off the table on the way past — clean at the temple, the way you hit a thing when you want it to stop, not when you want to make a point. Glass met bone with a dull, definite jolt that ran up into her palm, the sound arriving after the motion was done. His body took it in the order bodies always take it: one staggered step before the decision. He went down.

[Beat 10]
For four seconds the room hadn't caught up. Forty-three workers, briefly, did not matter. The weight under the floor fell back just far enough that the outside came up level with the inside — as loud out there as it always was in here, the two of them equal, one clean interval where she was not the only loud thing in the world. Four seconds. Then the outside went quiet and the inside didn't, and the two came apart the way they always came apart.

[Beat 11]
She set the bottle on the table. The man was breathing. She'd checked.

[Beat 12]
"I need to go," the fixer said, to no one.

It meant the deal was dead. It meant the forty slugs were someone else's repapered problem. It meant she should be out of this building before the documentation man woke up with a name to give.

[Beat 13]
Outside was a freight yard and a road going south. She took the road. The fixer caught her at the perimeter, where asphalt turned to gravel, past the last ring of yard lights.

"The client's out," the fixer said.

"I know."

"I told them you were discreet."

"He had a question." She kept walking. "I answered it."

"That's not the answer clients pay for."

"It answered the part that mattered."

[Beat 14]
The fixer went quiet — the particular quiet of a person deciding whether a tool has turned into a liability. She'd worked with this one three times. She could hear the decision being made in the gravel-crunch that didn't come after her. That version of tonight — Võ as the reason it went wrong — would reach whoever needed it. That was how a gray-zone name moved: not announced, just left lying around as the obvious explanation.

[Beat 15]
A dog fell in with her around the second field. Gray, ribs showing, the kind that follows a moving thing because it's out of reasons to stop. She walked faster. It walked faster. She stopped. It sat and looked at her like it had reached a conclusion.

"No," she said.

[Beat 16]
It wasn't listening, or it was listening and had decided the word didn't apply to it. She couldn't tell which. The towers stood off to the northeast where they always stood, their glow smeared low on the sky like a sun that had quit setting. She kept the road, the dog at her left heel, and did not turn her head toward the light.

[Beat 17]
The gravel ran south another half-kilometer before the fields gave to a thin scrub of birch, the trunks catching the far corporate glow and throwing pale strips of it across the road.

She heard the transport before she saw it — electric, running with its lights off. It slowed to hold her pace thirty meters back.

The dog stopped. She didn't.

[Beat 18]
The fixer couldn't have turned something around this fast. So this had been waiting before she walked out the door. Two facts, and she had them both before the side door opened.

[Beat 19]
It opened while the transport was still moving. A man stepped onto the running board and dropped to the road behind her — landed easy, no stumble, which told her about his legs and about how many times he'd done it.

The vehicle rolled ahead and stopped sideways across the gravel, closing south.

[Beat 20]
She read the ground the way she read everything, fast and without looking like it. One on foot behind her. At least one still in the cab. No cover she could use — the birch was decoration, not timber. The windows on the transport were tinted past anything the Gray Zone allowed, which told her nobody in it was worried about Gray Zone rules. The dog was already gone, a gray nothing between the trunks, keeping itself alive the way it kept itself alive: by knowing the exact second to stop being a dog.

[Beat 21]
"Documentation man's brother," the man behind her said. Flat, even, a cadence read off a card. Nervous or professional. She'd know which in thirty seconds.

"He wants to know who sent you? Before he decides what to do about it."

[Beat 22]
Nobody had sent her.

That was going to be the hard part.

[Beat 23]
Beecher had a grain elevator, a Pulse freight stop, and a two-person fixer shop that ran out of what was, on paper, a hardware repair store.

She found it because Kankakee was finished, she needed a job, and this was what was open.

[Beat 24]
The job read clean. A man in Beecher had been promising the same grain-transport contracts to more than one party at once, and one of those parties wanted the promises to stop. Find the man, apply enough pressure to make them stop, leave. Not a kill — the word was discourage, and out here that word meant something exact: visible, lasting, functional. He could still talk afterward. He just wouldn't want to.

[Beat 25]
She'd have turned it down any other month. Any other month, the sixty seconds of quiet a corner job bought her wouldn't have been worth crossing three counties for. She caught the thought before it finished and put it away unlooked-at, the way she put away everything she didn't have room to carry.

The problem was Kankakee. The documentation man's network had run bigger than she'd read off his face, and in six days it had reached people she hadn't seen coming. The word moving through the channels south of the city was that someone had broken a deal badly and there was interest in learning who. She didn't have the money to move fast, and Kankakee hadn't paid. Beecher would put enough in her pocket to get further south before the interest sharpened into a name.

[Beat 26]
She took it.

[Beat 27]
The grain man wasn't hard. One office in the elevator building, the same hours, in view of the street. She watched him six hours across two days and came in on the third morning through a door that was supposed to be locked and wasn't.

She knew where to put a hand — the nerve shelf under the floating rib, the high spot on the arm where pressure builds to a fast white heat and breaks nothing.

The first contact took the air out of him before his mind caught up to what his body had already worked out: knees going, hands off the desk, a short sound he didn't choose to make. She felt the lesson land through her own palms and eased off with exactly enough left in him. He was sitting upright when she left. The door locked again behind her. To spec.

[Beat 28]
She was at the south edge of town before she understood what she'd missed.

[Beat 29]
The grain man had a mother. Not a wife, not kids — a mother, in a town of four hundred, where the whole network was just the town and a mother outran any fixer shop in it.

The mother had watched her come out of the elevator building. The mother had a description. And a description, in Beecher, was at the hardware store that was really a fixer shop before the afternoon was out.

[Beat 30]
She walked faster. It didn't help. The two of them found her two hours down the farm road — one she knew from the shop, one she didn't, which meant either more reach than the shop let on or a favor called in. The second man had a hand under his coat, working hard at making it look like nothing.

[Beat 31]
They stopped. She stopped.

[Beat 32]
"The mother is upset," the hardware man said.

"The mother's son was promising grain contracts to three parties at once. That's what upsets mothers."

"She sees it different."

"I was hired for an outcome. I delivered it to spec."

[Beat 33]
He looked at her the way they all looked at her once the plan they'd built around her size met the actual thing. The plan had assumed a conversation. She was not a conversation.

The second man's hand tightened on whatever was under the coat.

[Beat 34]
"Don't," she said.

He did.

[Beat 35]
Problem Solver came off her right hip in the same motion that put her inside his reach, and it ended the way it ended when it went this way — faster than she'd planned, slower than she'd have liked, because it made noise, and noise made witnesses, and a witness was one more thing to carry south.

The hardware man ran. She let him. Running meant he'd read the situation and taken himself out of it, and she wasn't in the business of punishing the right call.

[Beat 36]
She stood in the farm road in the after, Problem Solver still warm in her hand. She'd done the discourage clean, to the letter of the job — and the letter of the job had said nothing about a mother in a town of four hundred, which was exactly how a clean job left a network to the north of her and a fresh one to the south, a description, an incident, and a dead man to hang the whole thing on.

Two towns now. Both behind her. Both hers.

[Beat 37]
She put Problem Solver away.

[Beat 38]
The dog was at the next junction, where she'd left it with her pack before the elevator — a dog didn't help a discourage go quiet. It had stayed, which was more than she'd asked of it.

It fell in at her heel and asked her for nothing. First thing all day that hadn't.

[Beat 39]
The road south ran flat through cut fields, stalks at ankle height, the soil that dark gray-brown of ground rained on and not yet dried. The Pulse freight corridor ran parallel four hundred meters west, and she could feel it before she could hear it — the low shudder the maglev put into the earth each time a load ran north toward the city.

That shudder wasn't the thing she carried in her back teeth. That one had no source and no schedule; it had been there since she was small and it never came in waves. The freight came and went. Next to the other thing, it was almost company.

[Beat 40]
She crouched and set her palm flat to the ground to read the freight the way she read everything, through the part of her that took pressure before it took sound. Cold. Wet. The way the ground was this month.

[Beat 41]
Then, for less than a second, the patch under her hand went warm.

[Beat 42]
Blood-warm. A heat with no business in a cut field in the dark — no engine near it, no body. She spread her fingers into it and it was gone: cold wet dirt, the way it should have been the whole time, the way it swore it had been all along.

[Beat 43]
She stayed crouched a moment longer than the ground deserved. Then she stood and wiped the hand on her coat.

[Beat 44]
South on the horizon the Thread's pods barely moved, lit against nothing, climbing toward somewhere she wasn't going. She had four hours, maybe. The Beecher fixer had run before he keyed his handset, but he'd had one, and the mother would have reached the freight stop by now — the one place in a four-hundred-person town with an outside line and a log, and the log had her on visual from the morning she'd walked in not hiding, because that morning she'd had no reason to. Four hours, then, and a list of open roads getting shorter behind her as she walked it.

[Beat 45]
None of that was the thing she kept turning over.

[Beat 46]
She looked back once at the patch of field where the ground had gone warm under her hand for no reason the world would ever admit to. Then she put it with the other things she'd stopped trying to explain, and left it out there in the dark with everything else she wasn't going back for.

[Beat 47]
The Wilmington hub was a mid-size freight transfer point built around a Pulse maintenance depot, and it was the first place in three months where the hum dropped below its normal weight.

[Beat 48]
Not silence. She'd given up on silence. But something under the baseline — still there, carrying less. She noticed it the afternoon she arrived, said nothing about it, and kept noticing it for three weeks.

[Beat 49]
The hub had a crew. Not affiliated in any formal way — no corporate registry, no faction charter, no shared handle. What they had was a fixer named Adisa Montoya, who ran legitimate cargo out of the depot and less legitimate consulting out of the same office, and around him a set of people who'd come through for one reason or another and found the hub more useful than wherever they'd been headed.

A rail mechanic named Fen. A woman called Oshun who moved documents for people who couldn't move them for themselves. An older man the others called the Professor, because he'd once been one.

Three nights a week they ate dinner together in the break room, because Adisa had decided that was a reasonable use of time and nobody had managed to overrule him.

[Beat 50]
She came for the work and stayed because the weight was lower — and because she noticed, for the first time in ten years, that the lower weight here wasn't the same thing as the pull.

[Beat 51]
The pull was northeast. The quiet in Wilmington was just quieter. Not pointed. Not the towers reaching harder. Just a pocket where the signal ran thinner, for reasons she couldn't establish. She'd stayed in places before because they were there. This was the first time she'd stayed in a place because it was actually better.

[Beat 52]
Adisa gave her work. Transit verification — checking cargo-slug contents against manifests for clients who couldn't check for themselves. She was good at it, because noticing what wasn't supposed to be there was a thing she'd been running on for years, and it carried over.

She did the work, got paid, and came back each evening to the lower pressure and the three-nights-a-week dinners. She didn't go at first. Then she went, because Fen had left food outside her door. Fen had decided, without asking, that she was one of theirs now, and kept deciding it.

[Beat 53]
The dog had made itself at home with a nerve she could only call rude. It slept at the base of the depot heater. The Professor fed it scraps. Oshun called it something untranslatable and clearly fond. The dog found the arrangement acceptable.

[Beat 54]
At the end of the fourth week, Adisa sat across from her in the office.

"You're planning to move on," he said.

"I haven't said that."

"You don't need to say a thing for it to be true." He folded his hands on the desk and waited, unhurried, a man who'd built his shop by understanding what he was working with. "We could use the skill. I won't pretend otherwise. But I need to know the situation."

She looked at him. He looked back. He had the calm of someone who'd been the steadiest person in enough rooms that he no longer had to act steady.

"There's a standing order," she said. "North. Two incidents combined. Not corporate."

"How patient?" he asked.

"Patient enough that it doesn't need to hurry."

He nodded slowly. She could see him weighing it — whether her being here would eventually pull the order's eyes onto the hub, whether the hub could take that, what it would cost the people who ate dinner together three nights a week. He was too careful to say it out loud. Careful enough that he didn't have to.

"I'm not asking you to leave," he said. "I'm asking you to tell me what we're dealing with."

[Beat 55]
She stayed.

It wasn't a decision she looked at while she made it. She simply didn't make the decision to go. The verification work went on. The dinners went on. The dog stayed at the base of the heater, and the Professor kept feeding it, and the pressure held its slightly lighter weight — not silence, not comfort, but less than it had been anywhere in ten years.

[Beat 56]
She told herself she was staying until the order showed signs of moving south.

She told herself the false trail she was laying northeast would buy enough time to make staying reasonable.

These were reasons for a decision that didn't need reasons, and she knew the difference, and she stayed anyway.

[Beat 57]
The towers were northeast. She looked at them from the depot roof some evenings and felt the pull at its usual slight angle, and admitted, as a fact and not a feeling, that she was somewhere she could have stayed.

[Beat 58]
She thought about the week after this one, and then the week after that, and let the week after that be a thing she didn't look at too closely.

[Beat 59]
She burned it on a Tuesday, over nothing.

[Beat 60]
Not nothing. There was a man, and a thing he said, and the mechanism that had been running since she was nine. But the cause was the small, avoidable kind: a man who sized her up wrong, thirty seconds she wanted, and a bill that was Wilmington's to pay now.

[Beat 61]
His name didn't matter. A transit broker passing through, doing the kind of business Adisa tolerated because it moved cargo and cargo was the point. He'd been watching her since morning the way men with sizing-up faces watched her — height, age, the cross-draw rig, arriving at the wrong answer in the same order every time.

She'd watched him watch her, and let the waiting decide whether it was going to be an incident.

[Beat 62]
What he said, he said to Fen.

"Does she do the checking, or just stand there looking unavailable?"

Fen said something to bend it away.

The broker looked at her straight.

"What are you, nineteen? What are you supposed to be doing here?"

[Beat 63]
She crossed the room in five steps.

[Beat 64]
Three steps in, she already knew — not after, the way she usually took stock, but mid-stride — that this one cost more than thirty seconds. Not because of the broker. Because of the quality of Fen's stillness: the particular way a person holds themselves in the half-second before they learn a thing about you that changes what you are to them. She knew, two steps out, exactly what Fen was about to learn. She finished crossing anyway. That was the part she'd carry — that she'd seen it coming and gone through with it.

[Beat 65]
Eleven seconds. Quick, specific. The broker on the floor, sore for a week, cured of the sentence for good. Clean work.

[Beat 66]
Fen was looking at her. Fen had never looked at her quite like that.

[Beat 67]
She put her hands in her pockets.

"He was going to—"

Fen's hand came up, half a motion toward her arm, and stopped short of it.

"I know what he was going to do."

Not angry. More careful than she had ever heard Fen be, and Fen was careful when things counted.

"I was handling it."

[Beat 68]
She looked at the broker. She looked at Fen. Fen's handling would have taken longer and settled less, and both of those were true, and neither was the thing Fen was telling her, and she understood the difference in the second it took to not say a word of it out loud.

[Beat 69]
Adisa was in the office doorway. He looked at the broker. He looked at her. He didn't ask what had happened, and that was how she knew he already had the shape of it — not the facts, which Fen would give him, but the shape: a thing she'd done the way she always did it, for the reason she always did it, in a place she'd had no right to do it in.

[Beat 70]
He didn't ask her to leave.

He said, "Take a few days. Let the broker settle."

She understood what he meant, and she took her pack and last payday's transit provision and walked out to the south road without letting herself look at the heater, or the break-room table, or the roof where she'd stood some evenings and admitted she could have stayed.

[Beat 71]
The dog was already at the junction.

[Beat 72]
She had nothing to say to it that would have made either of them feel better, so she said nothing. Behind her the pocket of lighter air she'd found in Wilmington was closing over the place she'd been, the way water closes over a stone — not cruelly, not even slowly. Just completely, and as though she had never once displaced a thing.

[Beat 73]
Six weeks out of Wilmington, the standing order found a hunter.

[Beat 74]
She knew it was a hunter and not a routine location pass because the man who turned up in Braidwood didn't ask anyone where she was. He already knew.

He came into the transit shelter she was using for the night the way you enter a room you've already mapped — not exploring, orienting. He clocked the dog first. Then her. Gray-zone, her age or a little past, with the spare movement of a man who knows he's the most dangerous thing in the room and has no feelings about it.

The order had found money. This was the kind you get by pooling two networks with a winter coming and nothing better to spend it on.

[Beat 75]
She put her hand on Problem Solver's grip and didn't draw.

[Beat 76]
"The documentation man's network," she said.

"Among others." He stopped six feet off, hands where she could see them — courtesy, and a way of telling her he didn't need to hurry. "You've been active."

"Not looking for the job."

"Doesn't look that way from the order's side." He studied her, doing the thing the documentation man had done, and the broker, and the Beecher man — weighing her size, her age, the rig. But where the others had gone wrong somewhere in the weighing, this one wasn't. She could watch him revise the number down in real time. "How much of Beecher was you?"

"All of it."

He nodded, like that settled something. "The order says alive."

"The order says identified."

"The order says identified first. Alive's the preferred outcome. It's not the only one." He shifted his weight forward an inch.

[Beat 77]
She'd been in this exact conversation enough times to know its floor. He was deciding whether the gap between her and his picture of her was wide enough to change how he came in. The answer would be yes or no inside thirty seconds, and yes was a problem.

[Beat 78]
She drew both.

[Beat 79]
Problem Solver right, Trouble Maker left, cross-draw, and she was inside his reach before he'd finished reading the motion.

That was the whole of the cross-draw — not a trick, a logic: both hands at once, because she didn't think in sequences.

He'd been watching her right hand. He hadn't been watching her left.

Trouble Maker met the side of his head before Problem Solver was all the way clear.

[Beat 80]
He didn't go down.

[Beat 81]
It was the first time since she was sixteen that someone hadn't gone down.

[Beat 82]
He caught her left forearm on the rebound and held it, turned it, pinned it against his own body where Trouble Maker couldn't finish — and her right was still coming up with nothing to pair it, because the cross-draw was two hands or it was nothing and he'd just taken one.

She'd committed to the finish before she'd read that he could stop it. Nobody had ever stopped it mid-motion. He put his shoulder into her and shoved, and she hit the shelter wall hard enough to send her vision to static for a second.

[Beat 83]
She came back with both feet under her and both pieces in hand and the man between her and the door, breathing hard, blood at his temple, still up.

She pulled Trouble Maker to single-shot.

[Beat 84]
He stopped moving.

[Beat 85]
"Alive is what the order says," he said.

"I know." She had both on him, the line between them clean, and she watched him run his own count. "I don't work for the order."

[Beat 86]
He was still for three seconds, working out whether the situation could be saved. She watched him decide it couldn't. Right call.

[Beat 87]
"Walk out," she said.

[Beat 88]
He walked out.

[Beat 89]
No clean interval came after. The fight had ended on someone else's terms — upright, negotiated, walked out of — and it left her nothing but the ordinary after: the ache where the wall had met her back, the dog pressed trembling against her left leg at something that wasn't quite a sound.

And the one fact that mattered was walking north on two good legs. A man who now had her rig, her technique, and a corrected read of exactly what she could do, and every intention of writing it down.

[Beat 90]
The order upgraded the price two weeks later. She had it from the same supply woman in Peotone who'd told her about the standing order three months back — a thorough woman, with what she called a professional interest in current pricing.

"It's up," the woman said. "A lot."

"How much?"

The woman named a number. It was a lot more than the first one.

"Same terms? Alive first?"

"Alive preferred. The wording changed — it's 'alive strongly preferred' now." A pause. "The hunter from Braidwood filed a report. Took notes, apparently."

She bought what she'd come for and left.

[Beat 91]
They came two days out of Peotone, in the flat country between freight stops where the only buildings were farm monitoring stations and the road ran straight enough to show her a man at four hundred meters.

He had someone with him this time.

That was the corrected read in practice: if one wasn't enough, bring two.

Not amateurs, either — the higher price bought operators who read the ground before it changed and stayed off the open sightlines, people who moved through country the way she did.

[Beat 92]
She didn't wait for them to close. That was the one thing about the engine she ran on that had ever made plain sense to her — not the quiet, not the after, but the part that knew staying still was always worse than moving.

She went at them across the flat ground with both guns, quick and sure, because the only gap that ever mattered was the one between them deciding to close and her deciding first.

[Beat 93]
Nine minutes.

[Beat 94]
When it was done she stood over both of them, breathing. Her forearms had gone thick and slow from the grip. A split at her lower lip she hadn't felt open. A hot line down the outside of her right thigh where something had landed hard enough, somewhere in the middle of it, that the leg had briefly thought about quitting. Damage present, function intact, nothing that changed the next move. Problem Solver had run dry and Trouble Maker was close behind.

She reloaded, field-stripped, cleared, fed them both fresh. Then she went through the nearest man and took the frame off him — bigger than hers, chambered in something that wouldn't share her rounds, but loaded, and a loaded piece she didn't know beat an empty one she did. She'd worked that out at sixteen, from nobody. Left coat pocket. She checked it and stood up into the wind.

[Beat 95]
The quiet that came after was the longest she'd gotten in three years.

[Beat 96]
She stood in it and waited for the weight to come back the way it always came back. It didn't. The interval stretched — past the four seconds, past the thirty, past anything a fight had ever bought her — the outside gone flat and silent and staying that way, the inside not returning to fill it. She'd spent six months burning every quiet place she found. She'd made a standing order, a hunter, a higher price, and two people breathing on the ground, all of it to stand in a quiet exactly this size. She stood in the middle of it and reached for what she'd been buying her whole life, and there was nothing there to take hold of. It was empty. It had always, it turned out, been empty. She'd just never once managed to buy enough of it at a time to notice.

[Beat 97]
She kept the map in her head, because paper comes off a body easily and a head does not. It wasn't a picture. It was a list — place, status, reason — three years of moving through country and learning which parts of it were still open to her.

She walked it the way other people walk a set of worry beads, gravel underfoot, dog at her heel, nowhere in particular to be.

[Beat 98]
She said it out loud. There was no one to hear but the dog, and the dog had never once acted on a thing she said.

[Beat 99]
"Kankakee. Closed." A field of cut stubble slid by on the right. "Documentation man's network."

[Beat 100]
The dog kept pace.

[Beat 101]
"Beecher. Closed. Grain man's family had reach I didn't check for." She pulled the frame she'd taken off the flat ground and checked the action by feel — wrong caliber, six rounds, no way to feed it out here — and put it back.

"Everything between here and Wilmington, watched. The order buys the hubs to add me to their list, one after the next, south as fast as I walk it." She'd watched it happen from the inside: the order sitting patient and funded up north, buying the next hub, and the next, until its reach and hers were nearly the same shape.

"Wilmington. Closed." Her voice did something small there. "That one's mine. I did that one myself."

[Beat 102]
