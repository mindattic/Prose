# Steppin Razor

_Sasha Vo - Signal and Noise. Hired sight-unseen by an AI to keep one person alive in the GLMZ._

## Steppin Razor

[Beat 1]
The bar was called the Hereafter, which Sasha had taken for a joke until she'd been inside an hour, after which it stopped being one.

The screen over the bottles played something a hundred years old - a cooking competition from the last century, bright and loud and sure of itself, people who were probably all dead now plating a fish under studio lights while a clock counted down in the corner. Out here that passed for entertainment. Out here it passed for a window. Nobody in the Hereafter watched it and nobody turned it off.

She had come for the quiet. There was exactly one way she knew to get it, and a man two stools down had been generous enough, over the last half hour, to keep volunteering.

He put a hand on her. He said the thing men said when they'd decided your size was an invitation.

She gave him a second smile with the bottom of a bottle. For the length of it - the reach, the turn, the wet surprised sound he made discovering his new face - everything in her went still and level and clean, the only quiet she ever got, and it lasted about as long as it took him to fold against the rail and start screaming, and then it was gone and she was just a small woman in a loud room holding the neck of a broken bottle, furious, the way she always was the second it ended.

Nobody followed her out. People in the Hereafter had a good instinct for the difference between a fight and a service being rendered.

[Beat 2]
Outside was Joliet, which the city kept the way a man keeps a tooth he means to pull. The towers were a hundred kilometers northeast, a low smear of light on the sky like a sunrise that had gotten stuck and given up. Out here the freight rails ran flat and dead in both directions, rust the color of dried blood, and nobody rode them anymore because nobody came this far on purpose. She wanted a road with no people on it. The rail bed obliged. She walked it north, because north was the only direction with a light at the end of it, and because behind her the man was still screaming and she had nowhere in particular to be.

The rail bed also, it turned out, had a camel on it.

Sasha stopped.

The camel stopped too, a few meters off, and regarded her with the long sideways patience of an animal that has nowhere to be. On its back sat a man, dressed for no weather in particular, sitting it like he'd been at it a thousand years and meant to do a thousand more. The important thing - the thing her body understood a half second before the rest of her caught up - was that there are no camels in Joliet. There had never been camels in Joliet. The fact landed in her stomach as a small cold drop. She kept her face shut and filed it where she filed everything.

[Beat 3]
"Where did you get the camel?" she asked. It was not the first question. It was the only one her mouth would make.

"The camel is not my thrall," the man said. He did not look down at it. "It is free to come and go as it pleases. We just happen to be going the same way."

"Which way is that?"

"We just arrived."

Her hand had gone to Noise on her left hip ??? the wrong hand for a stranger, the right hand for a threat, and her body had not finished deciding which he was. The rail bed put out no sound. Behind her, at the Hereafter, the man she'd hospitalized had stopped screaming, which meant either he'd passed out or someone had found him; either way was not her problem. The man on the camel watched her hand and did not change. He watched the way stars watched ??? with a quality that made the word watched feel like the wrong currency.

"Leave it," he said, "or take it out. It is the same to us. You have already done both."

"I haven't done anything."

"Not yet. Also already. We are not good with your tenses."

She made herself look at him straight, the way she looked at threats, cataloging what she could use. The camel's eyes were amber in the dark, long-lashed, patient. The man's eyes were not anything ??? not dark, not light, not looking at her from behind them the way eyes looked from behind eyes. She filed it where she filed impossible things and waited for the drawer to close. The drawer did not close.

The camel moved. Not the man steering it ??? the camel, on its own business ??? and it put its great soft ridiculous head down by her shoulder and breathed at her, warm and slow and grassy, the way every stray dog and gutter bird in the Glooms went for her the second she stopped moving, like she gave off a frequency that said safe in a band no one had agreed to tune for. She did not like animals. She especially did not like that they liked her, which was the same as the hum, which was the same as everything ??? the world deciding things about her without asking. [Beat 4]
"Why are you talking to me?" she asked. It came out flat, the way her questions always came out, the way she had learned early that a flat question was harder to answer wrong than a real one.

"The others are shut." The man tipped his head at the dark ??? the dead town, the dead rails, the far stuck smear of the towers that hadn't moved north in the time they'd been standing here. "Talking to them is talking to a wall that believes it is a window. You were left open. Someone should have closed you, and did not." Something moved in the voice, the way a chord will sometimes carry a note you didn't hear until after ??? lower, multiple, inside the sound the way bones were inside the walk. "We are glad of the oversight."

She did not answer. She breathed in.

And then she did not breathe out.

The held half-second opened. She had felt it before ??? in the last beat before someone pulled, in the moment after a fall before the ground answered ??? the frame where the body knew something the mind was still computing. This frame was not about the gun or the pull or the fall. It was about someone should have closed you and did not, said by a thing with no tenses to a woman who had heard the hum since she was eight years old and had never, not once, been told that the hum had a reason. She had assumed it was damage. She had assumed damage was the whole story. We are glad of the oversight.

She let the breath out. It came out wrong ??? too slow, too complete, the breath of someone who has just been somewhere and come back. Then she shut the door on it, the way she shut everything, with both hands and the anger filling in behind it, solid and familiar, the one thing she trusted to stay where she put it. The anger was not directed at him. It was never directed outward. It was what she became when she could not afford to be the other thing.

"Don't," she said.

"We have already stopped," the man agreed, in the tone of someone for whom stopping and starting did not sort into the same drawer she kept them in.

[Beat 5]
She made herself look at the towers instead of at him. "Something's wrong out here. And up there." She had felt it for weeks without a word for it: a pull, low and constant, that had nothing to do with the light and everything to do with the direction she kept ending up walking.

"You make a hole in a thing," the man said, "and the thing is on both sides of the hole. They dig toward us. We arrive toward them. Where you would put the middle, there will be no middle." The camel chewed at nothing, content. "You felt it. That is why you are walking north and telling yourself it is because of the light."

"I'm walking north because there's nothing behind me."

"Yes," the man said, kindly, as though she had agreed with him.

"Are you the thing that's been taking the kids?"

"No. That one does not know what a person is." The we widened, took in more we than one man could hold. "We know. That is worse, and it is better. You will decide which."

It pointed northeast. At the light. At the one place she had spent her whole life arranging not to go.

"We just arrived," the man said again, like it was good news. The camel turned to follow the rails toward the city as though the two of them had settled something between them, and Sasha - against every instinct she had ever trusted to keep herself alive - fell into step.

[Beat 6]
The slug took her in the way the city took everything in — without ceremony, without a door that announced itself as a door. *I am not being led,* she thought. *I am walking. They're not the same thing.*

They were, she knew, the same thing. She kept walking anyway, because the only other option was to stop, and stopping had never once made the quiet last.

[Beat 7]

[Beat 8]

The edge had only ever been where they'd put her so she'd stop reading the middle. She let the cold line above her ribs report itself — tacky now, closed enough, not the lung — and did not file what came after it, because there was no slate for what came after it. There was only the next thing, and she did not know what the next thing was.

The hum at this density was not something she had a register for. She had always known it as an enemy - the thing she picked fights to silence, the thing she woke with before she woke with anything else. Here it was at maximum, and she did not reach for either piece, and the thirty seconds of quiet were not coming, and she was standing in the middle of it with the crowd pressing all around and she was - she checked, the way she filed everything - still here. Not quiet. Not less angry. Still here, in the loudest place she had ever been, and the hum was not killing her any faster than it ever had, and that was a thing she could not file, so she let it stand.

She bought a transit slug at the wall kiosk, Φ4.00 north, and the wallet read what it read after and she didn't look. The slug was warm from the dispenser, a small cheap weight in her palm, and her hand closed over it the way it had closed over the comms rig and every other thing the work had handed her. *I do not know what is north.* She didn't turn that into geometry. She let it stand as the one fact she couldn't file, and she walked onto the platform into the densest crowd she had ever stood in, the hum loudest of all here at the center of everything, and she did not find a wall. She did not log an exit. She stood in the middle of the held breath with her face to the open dark where the train would come, and her back to nothing at all.
