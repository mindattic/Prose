# Steppin' Razor

[Beat 1]
She paid Φ3 at the perimeter gate — a transit-access slug she fed into the gate reader while the freight dispatcher on the stool looked at his screen instead of her face, which was the professional courtesy running in both directions. The yard beyond smelled of diesel and rust water. She crossed it without checking her back and pushed through the hub building's lit door into the noise.

The Pulse hub at Kankakee ran all night, because freight didn't keep hours. Eighteen bays of pressurization cycled in sequence, the big compressors kicking over one after another down the line so the floor never quite stopped shaking under a boot. Cargo slugs came off the north feed and dropped onto the sorting cradles, and the cradles walked them down to the outbound bays, and forty-three workers in gray coveralls moved between the cradles with hooks and manifests and strong opinions about the correct way to stack a pallet. The dispatch office smelled of machine oil and recycled air. Somebody's radio played a station that was mostly static. The junction did its work the way it had done it for sixty years, which was without caring who watched.

She was the thing that watched.

Corner position.

That was the term she had given the fixer. Not a question. 'Corner position. I don't touch anyone unless the negotiation goes sideways. I leave when I say.'

The fixer had said something about a quiet presence. A cargo-clearance negotiation. She had listened and agreed. Stand in the corner. Look at the wall. Be the thing that doesn't need to happen because it's there.

The negotiation was about forty slugs of gray-market neuretics being repapered as regulated medical equipment. Neuretics: the mesh they grew into your skull before you were ten, the compute that was supposed to seal at twelve and go quiet for the rest of your operational life. Everyone's did. Hers hadn't — which was the whole reason she could stand in a corner of a sorting office and hear the low thing running under the floor that none of the forty-three workers could, a thing that did not go on any manifest. Everybody in the room knew what the slugs were. The paperwork said something else.

She stood in the corner. She looked at the wall. She listened to the hum.

The hum had been there since she was nine and she had never once been without it. A low pressure with no source she could point to - not a sound, precisely, because she had established at eleven, in a soundproof booth at a school, that it held in absolute silence - but something down in the register between what the ear processed and what the rest of the brain did with what was left over. It came up through the floor here, the way it came up through every floor, a little heavier where the ground was thick with people and machines. It varied only in one direction: it got louder when she was still. When she was moving, when she was working, when she was occupied enough that the brain had something else to run on, it fell to a manageable background frequency. At rest, it filled up the available space.

She was at rest in the corner of the Kankakee sorting office, watching two men negotiate forty slugs of illegal components, and the hum was at its normal volume. That was one of the three reasons she had taken a job she would have been better off declining.

The man who noticed her was on the wrong side of the table. Not the fixer's client - the other one, the documentation specialist, the one with a face built for making assessments. He'd been making them since she walked in. She was nineteen and small and the assessment wasn't going to land anywhere useful.

"How old is she?" he said. Not to her.

The fixer said something noncommittal. The documentation man looked at her again. He had the patience of someone who had been right about things for twenty years.

"She's what, eighteen? Nineteen?" He tilted his head. "What is she supposed to do if this goes sideways?"

She crossed the room in six steps. She hit him with the bottle from the table on her way past - clean at the temple, the way you hit something when you want it to stop, not when you want to make a point about it. He went down and she stood there for four seconds in the only quiet she was going to get, the room frozen behind its own processing speed, forty-three workers momentarily irrelevant, the hum receding just enough that she could feel the edges of a world that was as loud outside as it was inside her. Then the outside world quieted. The inside world didn't. Equal for four seconds, then diverging in the same direction they always diverged, the hum returning to its baseline volume, patient and permanent.

She put the bottle on the table.

The man was breathing. She'd checked.

"I need to go," the fixer said, to no one specifically. It meant the negotiation was finished. It meant the forty slugs were someone else's repapered problem. It meant she needed to not be in this building when the documentation man finished running his own assessment.

Outside was a freight yard and then a road that went south. She took the road.

---

The fixer found her at the perimeter where the asphalt became gravel, standing in the dark past the last ring of freight-yard lights.

"The client's out," the fixer said.

"I know."

"I told them you were discreet."

She looked at the gravel road going south. The hum held its volume exactly. It always held its volume exactly. That was the thing about it - it didn't rise with her pulse or drop with the temperature. It just was, at the frequency it was, like gravity.

"He had a question," she said. "I answered it."

"That's not the kind of answer clients pay for."

"It answered the part that mattered."

The fixer was quiet for a moment the way someone goes quiet when deciding whether a tool had become a liability. She'd worked with this one three times before. She could see what was happening in the silence. The read was simple enough: she was nineteen years old, she'd been in the city for fourteen months, and she cost jobs. It only ran one way.

"I'm going to have to tell them something," the fixer said.

"Tell them the asset neutralized a risk to the negotiation." She started walking. "That's what I was hired to do."

The fixer didn't call after her. That meant the conversation the fixer was already composing in their head was about her, not to her. The version where Võ was a problem would reach whoever needed to hear it. These things always did. That was how a gray-zone reputation moved - not by being announced but by being the unsurprising explanation for why a thing had gone wrong.

She walked south. The freight yard lights fell away. The hum kept pace.

A dog fell into step with her somewhere around the second field - gray, ribs showing, the kind that follows something that's moving because it ran out of reasons to stop. She walked faster. It walked faster. She stopped. It stopped. She looked at it. It sat down and looked back with the focused attention of something that had reached a conclusion.

She walked on.

It came with her.

"No," she said.

It wasn't listening, or it was listening and had decided the answer didn't apply to it. She couldn't tell which. She walked south with the dog at her left heel and the hum at its baseline frequency and the knowledge that whatever the fixer told the client, the client would tell someone else, and that someone else would remember her face.

The quiet she had bought had already cost more than it was worth. It always did. She had known this going in. She had taken the job anyway because the hum had been loud for six days running and she had needed the thirty seconds badly enough to pay whatever the bill turned out to be.

She kept walking. The dog kept pace. The towers were northeast, as they always were, their glow on the horizon like a sun that had stopped setting — the towers, the core, the densest signal on the continent, the one direction the hum always leaned and she never went. The hum leaned that way too, the way it always leaned, and she didn't look at them.

[Beat 2]
The gravel road ran south for another half-kilometer before the fields on either side gave way to a thin scrub of secondary-growth birch, their trunks catching the distant corporate glow and throwing pale strips of it across the road. She heard the vehicle before she saw it - a low ground transport, electric, running with its lights off, which was the kind of choice that announced itself. It slowed to match her pace from thirty meters back. The dog stopped walking. She didn't.

*The fixer wouldn't. Too quick. This was already waiting.*

The transport's side door opened without the vehicle stopping. A man stepped out onto the running board while it was still moving, which told her something about his legs and something about his patience. He dropped to the road behind her and the vehicle rolled on ahead and stopped sideways across the gravel, blocking south. She read the ground: one on foot behind her, at least one in the vehicle, no cover within useful distance except the birch on her left, which was decorative rather than real. The dog had vanished into the dark between the trees with the specific silence of something that had survived by knowing when not to be a dog.

"Documentation man's brother," the man behind her said. He had the flat cadence of someone reading off a card, which meant he was nervous or professional, and she'd find out which in the next thirty seconds. "He wants to know who sent you before he decides what he wants to do about it."

*Nobody sent me. That's going to be the part that's hard to explain.*

She kept her hands visible and her weight slightly forward and looked at the transport sitting sideways on the gravel road, its windows tinted past the legal threshold for the Gray Zone, which meant nobody inside it was worried about Gray Zone ordinance. The hum held its frequency. It always held its frequency. It came up out of the ground under the gravel the way it came up out of every ground she had ever stood on, a low weight in the register nobody around her had ever once complained about hearing, because nobody around her heard it. It leaned northeast, toward the towers — a direction that was being asked for rather than arrived at, a thing under the world with a heading. Somewhere in the birch the dog was making a decision about whether it had followed the wrong thing south, and she couldn't blame it for whatever it decided.

[Beat 3]
The town of Beecher had a grain elevator, a Pulse freight stop, and a two-person gray-zone fixer operation that ran out of what was technically a hardware repair shop. She found it because she needed a job and the Kankakee option was finished and this was what was available.

The job they gave her was clean enough in its description. A man in Beecher had been making representations about grain transport contracts to multiple parties simultaneously, and one of the parties had decided that the representations needed to stop. The work was: find the man, apply sufficient incentive for the representations to cease, leave. Not a kill contract — the word used was discourage, which was a word with a specific meaning in this context and she understood the meaning. Discourage meant visible, lasting, functional. It meant he could still talk, he just wouldn't want to.

She would have declined this contract in any month other than this one. The problem with the current month was that she had the Kankakee debt accumulating in the form of the documentation man's network, which she had learned in six days was larger than she'd estimated from looking at him. The man had reached people she hadn't predicted. The word moving through the gray-zone channels south of the city was that someone had broken a negotiation badly and there was interest in knowing who.

She didn't have money to move fast. The Kankakee job had not paid out. The Beecher contract would pay enough to get her further south before the Kankakee network's interest in her identity became specific enough to be dangerous.

She took it.

The grain transport man wasn't difficult to find. He worked from a single office in the elevator building, same hours every day, visible from the street. She watched him for six hours across two days, established his patterns, and came in on the third morning through a door that was supposed to be locked and wasn't.

The discouragement went as planned. He would remember the conversation. He would make his representations to one party at a time going forward, with appropriate clarity about the terms. She had been efficient about it — not brutal, functional, nothing that would require a hospital. He was sitting upright when she left. The door re-locked behind her.

She was at the south edge of Beecher before she understood that she had miscalculated.

The grain transport man had family in Beecher. Not a wife, not children — a mother, which was a different category, because a mother in a town of four hundred people where everybody's network was the town was a woman with more reach than any gray-zone fixer operation. The mother had seen her come out of the elevator building. The mother had a description. The mother's description would be at the hardware store that was technically a fixer operation before the afternoon was out.

She walked faster.

The Beecher fixers found her two hours south on the farm road, which told her the description had moved faster than she'd expected. One of them was a man she recognized from the hardware shop — the other was someone she didn't know, which meant they had more network than the hardware shop suggested or they'd called someone in from outside. The second one was carrying something under his coat that he was trying to make look like nothing.

She stopped walking. They stopped walking.

"The mother is upset," the hardware shop man said.

"The mother's son was misrepresenting grain contracts to multiple parties," she said. "That's what generates upset."

"The mother sees it differently."

"I was hired for a specific outcome. The outcome was delivered within specification."

The hardware shop man looked at her the way everyone looked at her when they had made a plan that assumed her cooperation and were encountering the actual situation. She was nineteen. She was small. The plan had assumed a conversation. She was not a conversation.

The second man had his hand on the thing under his coat.

"Don't," she said.

He did.

Problem Solver came out of the right-side holster in the same motion she was already inside his reach, and the conversation ended the way conversations ending this way always ended, which was faster than he had anticipated and slower than she would have preferred because it generated sound and sound generated witnesses and witnesses generated more entries in the ledger she was already carrying from Kankakee.

The hardware shop man ran. She let him. Running meant he had understood the situation and was extracting himself from it, which was the correct decision and which she wasn't going to punish because punishing correct decisions was bad incentive design.

She stood in the farm road in the aftermath quiet with Problem Solver in her hand and the hum at its baseline and the documentation man's growing network to her north and now a new problem to her south, which was the Beecher fixer network, which would now have a description and a specific incident and a dead man to attach to it.

She had delivered the discourage correctly. The fallout had come from something she hadn't been hired to account for. This was technically not her fault and also completely her problem.

She put Problem Solver away and kept walking.

The dog was waiting at the next road junction. It had been waiting, apparently, since she'd left it with her pack at the junction before the elevator building — she'd told it to stay because a dog doesn't help a discourage go quietly, and it had stayed, which was more than she had expected from it. She reached the junction. It fell into step at her left heel.

She said nothing to it. It seemed to consider this adequate.

Beecher was behind her. The farm road went south. She walked south with the dog at her heel and the new debt in the ledger and the Kankakee debt in the same ledger and the knowledge that the available territory was shrinking from the north faster than she was moving south.

[Beat 4]
The road south ran flat through harvested fields, the stalks cut to ankle height and the soil the dark gray-brown of something that had been rained on and not yet dried. The Pulse freight corridor ran parallel about four hundred meters west — she could hear it, or feel it more accurately, the low-frequency vibration the maglev rails put into the ground with every load moving north toward the city. The hum of it wasn't the hum she carried in her back teeth, the one that had no external source, the one that had been there since she was small. That hum was internal and constant. The freight hum came and went in waves and was, by comparison, a comfort.

She crouched once and set her palm flat to the ground, reading the freight vibration the way she read everything — through the part of her that took pressure before it took sound. The soil was cold and wet, the way soil was this month. Then, for less than a second, the patch under her hand went warm — blood-warm, wrong, a heat with no business in a cut field in the dark — and when she spread her fingers it was cold wet dirt again, the way it should have been. She stood and wiped her hand on her coat. She had four hours and a shrinking list of open directions; a patch of ground that ran warm and then didn't was not on it, so she left it there and walked.

*The Beecher fixer had a handset. He ran before he used it, but he had it.*

She had maybe four hours before whoever the hardware shop man reported to had a name or a face or a direction. Less if the mother had already talked to the Pulse freight stop, which was the kind of thing a mother in a four-hundred-person town would do because the freight stop was the only place in Beecher with regular outside contact and a logging system. The freight stop would have her on visual from when she'd come in. She hadn't been hiding coming in — she'd had no reason to hide coming in.

The dog walked at her heel and put its nose down briefly at something dead in the drainage ditch and then came back to position. She didn't look at what it had found. The ledger had enough in it without additions.

[Beat 5]
The Wilmington hub station was a mid-size freight transfer point built around a Pulse maintenance depot, and it was the first place in three months where the hum dropped below what she thought of as its normal operating volume.

Not silence. She had given up on silence. But something under the baseline — a frequency that was still present but carrying less weight, the way a sound carries across a wall without pressing on the wall the way it does from the same side. She noticed it the afternoon she arrived and didn't say anything about it and kept noticing it for the three weeks that followed.

The hub station had a crew.

They weren't affiliated in the formal sense — no corporate registry, no faction charter, no shared handle. What they had was a fixer named Adisa Montoya who ran legitimate cargo operations at the maintenance depot and less legitimate consulting work out of the same office, and around him a collection of people who had come through for one reason or another and found the hub more useful than wherever they'd been going. A rail mechanic named Fen who had opinions about everything and was usually right. A woman who went by Oshun who did the kind of document work that was only available if you knew the right people. An older man the others called the Professor because he had once been a professor, which he maintained was irrelevant to anything, which meant it was relevant to everything. They ate dinner together three nights a week in the depot break room because Adisa had decided this was a reasonable use of time and nobody had successfully overruled him.

She came for the work and she stayed because the hum was lower and she noticed, for the first time in ten years, that the lower frequency here wasn't the same as the pull.

The pull was northeast. The reduced frequency in Wilmington was just — quieter. Not pointed. Not the towers reaching harder. Just a pocket of reduced pressure where the ambient signal was thinner for reasons she had no way of establishing. She had stayed in places before because they were available. This was the first time she had stayed in a place because it was genuinely better.

Adisa gave her work. Transit verification — confirming cargo slug contents against manifests for clients who couldn't verify themselves. She was good at it because noticing things that weren't supposed to be there was a skill she'd been running on for years, and it translated. She got through the work and got paid and came back to the depot each evening to the lower frequency and the three-nights-a-week dinners, which she didn't initially attend and then attended because Fen had left food outside her door, which was an invitation in the only language she had consistently found difficult to decline.

The dog had made itself at home with an ease that she could only describe as impertinent. It slept at the base of the depot heater. The Professor fed it scraps. Oshun called it something untranslatable but clearly affectionate. The dog seemed to consider this a reasonable arrangement.

At the end of the fourth week, Adisa sat across from her in the depot office and said: "You're planning to move on."

"I haven't said that."

"You don't need to say things for them to be true." He folded his hands on the desk. He was a careful man, Adisa — the kind who had built his operation by understanding what he was working with. "We could use the skill. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But I need to know the situation."

She looked at him. He looked back. He had the patience of someone who had been the steadiest person in enough rooms that he no longer needed to perform steadiness.

"There's a standing order," she said. "North. Two incidents combined. Not corporate."

"How patient?"

"Patient enough that it doesn't need to hurry."

He nodded slowly, doing the calculation she'd already run — whether her presence would eventually attract the order's attention to the hub, and whether the hub could sustain that, and what it would cost the people who ate dinner together three nights a week. He was too careful to say it directly. He was careful enough that he didn't have to.

"I'm not asking you to leave," he said. "I'm asking you to tell me what we're working with."

She stayed.

This wasn't a decision she examined while making it. She simply didn't make the decision to go. The transit verification work continued. The three-nights-a-week dinners continued. The dog stayed at the base of the depot heater and the Professor continued to feed it and the hum continued its slightly reduced frequency, which wasn't silence and wasn't comfort but was less than it had been anywhere else in ten years.

She told herself she was staying until the standing order showed signs of moving south. She told herself the false trail she was planning northeast would buy enough time to make staying reasonable. These were explanations for a decision that didn't actually require explanation, and she knew the difference, and she stayed anyway.

The towers were northeast. She looked at them from the depot roof some evenings and felt the pull at its usual slight angle and acknowledged, as a fact and not a feeling, that she was somewhere she could have remained.

She thought about the week after this one, and then the week after that, and let the thought of the week after that be something she didn't examine too closely.

[Beat 6]
She burned it on a Tuesday, over nothing.

Not nothing — there was a man, and there was a thing he said, and there was the mechanism that had been running since she was nine, and nothing about any of that was nothing. But in the accounting of causes, the cause was exactly the kind of petty, easily-avoided thing that made a clean ledger impossible. A man who made an assessment. Thirty seconds of quiet. A tab that was now Wilmington's problem.

His name was irrelevant. He was a transit broker passing through the depot, doing the kind of business Adisa tolerated because it moved cargo and cargo was the point. He had been watching her since the morning, the way men with assessment faces watched her — calculating size, age, the cross-draw rig, arriving at the wrong conclusion in the same order every time. She had been watching him watch her and deciding whether it was going to be an incident, which was a decision she made by waiting to see what he said.

What he said was: "Does she do the checking or just stand there looking unavailable?"

He said it to Fen. Fen said something deflecting. The broker looked at her directly and said: "What are you, nineteen? What are you supposed to be doing here?"

She crossed the room in five steps. Three steps into the crossing, she already knew — not after, the way she usually took stock, but mid-motion — that this one was going to cost more than thirty seconds. Not because of what the broker had said. Because of the quality of Fen's stillness: the particular way someone stands when they are about to learn something about a person that changes what the person is to them. She knew, three steps out, what Fen was about to learn. She finished the crossing anyway.

The thing she did next was efficient and specific and took eleven seconds, and in those eleven seconds the hum receded the way it only receded during violence — the outside world catching up to the inside world, equal for a moment, the low pressure releasing into something that felt briefly like the absence of frequency. Then the broker was on the floor and the outside world went quiet again and the hum returned to its slightly-reduced Wilmington baseline, and she stood there in the aftermath with the feeling of the thing that had just happened being exactly the kind of thing she had been doing since sixteen, for exactly the same reason, in exactly the same way.

The broker wasn't badly hurt. He would be sore for a week. He would remember the conversation clearly and he would not repeat it to another gray-zone asset. These were the measurable outcomes. She had delivered them efficiently.

Fen was looking at her. Fen had never looked at her quite like that before, which was the other measurable outcome — the one she hadn't been measuring.

She put her hands in her pockets.

"He was going to—" she started.

"IFen's hand came up — half a motion, toward her arm — and then stopped. “I know what he was going to do," Fen said. Not angrily. More carefully than she'd ever heard Fen say anything, and Fen was careful about things when they mattered. "I was handling it."

She looked at the broker on the floor. She looked at Fen. She didn't say that Fen's handling of it would have taken longer and produced less certain results, because this was true and also not the point, which she understood the instant she thought it.

Adisa appeared in the depot office doorway. He looked at the broker. He looked at her. He didn't ask what had happened, which was how she knew that he already understood what had happened — not the facts, which would come from Fen, but the structure of it, which was visible in the shape of a thing she had done the way she always did it, for the reason she always did it, in a place she had no right to do it.

She left that afternoon.

Not because she was asked to — Adisa didn't ask her to leave. He said: "Take a few days. Let the broker settle." He meant something different, and she understood what he meant, and she took her pack from the depot and the transit provision she'd been given last payday and walked to the south road without looking at the heater or the break room table or the roof where she had sometimes stood evenings and watched the towers.

The dog was already at the road junction.

She didn't say anything to it. She had no words that improved the situation. She walked south with the dog at her left heel and the Wilmington frequency already rising back toward baseline behind her, the slightly-reduced hum becoming the ordinary hum, the pocket of reduction closing over her absence the way a place closes over an absence once the absence has made itself permanent.

The thirty seconds were already gone. The hum was back.

The flaw had eaten exactly the thing it had wanted. She had known it would. She walked south without looking back, which didn't make any of it better and was the only thing she could do.

[Beat 7]
Six weeks out of Wilmington, the standing order found a hunter.

She knew it was a hunter and not the order's network doing a routine location pass because the man who showed up in Braidwood didn't ask anyone where she was. He already knew. He came into the transit shelter she was using for the night the way someone comes into a room they have already mapped — not exploring, orienting. He clocked the dog first. Then her.

He was gray-zone, her age or a few years over, with the economy of movement that comes from knowing you are the most dangerous thing in a room and not having any feelings about it. The standing order had found money. This was the kind of money a motivated injured party got from combining two networks' resources with a winter season ahead and nothing better to do with the surplus.

She put her hand on Problem Solver's grip and didn't draw.

"The documentation man's network," she said.

"Among others." He stopped six feet away. He had his hands visible. This was professional courtesy and also a way of showing her he didn't need to rush. "You've been active."

"Not looking for the job."

"Doesn't look that way from the order." He studied her. He was doing the same calculation she'd seen the documentation man do, the broker in Wilmington, the Beecher network's second man — sizing, age, rig configuration — but where the others had gotten it wrong somewhere in the process, this one wasn't getting it wrong. She could see the adjustment happening in real time: he had arrived with an expectation and was revising it downward. "How much of the Beecher situation was you?"

"All of it."

He nodded, like this confirmed something. "The order says alive."

"The order says identified."

"The order says identified first." He moved his weight forward an inch. "Alive is the preferred outcome. It's not the only outcome."

She had been in this conversation enough times to know the shape of it. The shape of it was: the man across from her was deciding whether the gap between her and his model of her was large enough to change his approach, and the answer was going to be yes or no in the next thirty seconds, and the yes would be a problem.

She drew both.

Problem Solver right, Trouble Maker left, cross-draw — she was inside his reach before he'd finished reading the motion, which was the whole point of the cross-draw, which was not a technique but a logic: both hands at once, because she didn't think in sequences. He'd been watching her right hand. He hadn't been watching her left. Trouble Maker's barrel connected with the side of his head before Problem Solver was all the way clear of the holster.

He didn't go down.

This was the first time since she was sixteen that someone hadn't gone down.

He caught her left forearm on the rebound, turned it, and got his shoulder into her and pushed, and she went into the wall of the transit shelter hard enough that her vision went to static for one second. She came back with both feet on the ground and both pieces still in hand and the man between her and the door — breathing hard, blood at his temple, not going down — and the thirty seconds of quiet she'd been expecting nowhere yet in evidence because the fight was still going and the outside world had not yet caught up to the inside world.

She pulled Trouble Maker to single-shot.

He stopped moving.

"Alive is what the order says," he said.

"I know." She had both pieces on him and the line between them was clear and he was running his own calculation. "I don't work for the order."

He was very still for three seconds. He was deciding whether the situation was recoverable. She watched him decide that it wasn't. That was the right decision.

"Walk out," she said.

He walked out.

She stood in the empty transit shelter with the dog pressed against her left leg, vibrating with a frequency that wasn't quite a sound — it matched nothing she could identify — and let the outside quiet close in. No thirty seconds. The fight had run too long for the clean interval. What she got instead was the ordinary aftermath, which was just the hum at its normal volume and the soreness of having hit a wall and the knowledge that the standing order now had a hunter who had her rig configuration, her technique, and a revised assessment of her capability.

She moved on before first light.

The standing order wasn't going to cancel because a hunter had walked out of a transit shelter. The hunter hadn't walked out because he was afraid of her — he had walked out because the odds had changed and he was professional enough to update his read. Professional enough to update his read meant he would go back to the order, report the revised assessment, and let the order decide whether the upgraded price was worth paying.

She walked south through the dark with Problem Solver on her right hip and Trouble Maker on her left and the standing order a patient fact behind her, and the map of available territory was now smaller by the amount that Braidwood had just become a place she couldn't be again.

The dog walked at her left heel and didn't seem troubled by any of this. She didn't find this comforting. She found it accurate — there was no reason a dog would be troubled by the collapsing available territory of a nineteen-year-old gray-zone operator whose ledger was accruing faster than she was moving.

She kept walking.

[Beat 8]
The order upgraded the price two weeks later. She found this out from the same supply woman in Peotone who had told her about the standing order three months ago, because the supply woman was thorough and had what she called a professional interest in current pricing.

"It's up," the woman said. "Significantly."

"How significantly."

The woman named a number. It was significantly more than the original number.

She thought about this.

"Same conditions? Alive first?"

"Alive preferred. The language changed. It's 'alive strongly preferred' now." The woman paused. "The hunter in Braidwood filed a report. Apparently he had notes."

She bought the transit provisions she'd come for and left.

The hunter came for her again two days outside Wilmington, in the flat territory between freight stops where the only structures were agricultural monitoring stations and the road ran straight enough that she saw him from four hundred meters. He had someone with him this time, which was the order's revised assessment in practical form — if one wasn't enough, bring two.

The two of them weren't amateurs. This was the problem with upgraded prices: they bought upgraded operators. The standing order had been patient and funded and it had apparently reached the level of funding that bought people who moved through territory the way she moved through territory — reading the ground before the ground changed, staying off the sightlines that were available.

She didn't wait for them to close the distance. This was the first thing that had ever made sense to her about the engine she ran on — not the thirty seconds of quiet, not the aftermath, but the part where staying still was always worse than moving. She went at them across the flat ground with Problem Solver in her right hand and Trouble Maker in her left and the efficiency of a person who had been doing this since sixteen and had learned, over three years of accumulating ledger debt, that the interval between them deciding to close and her deciding to close was the only interval that mattered.

The fight took nine minutes. What it bought her was approximately forty-five seconds across three separate intervals — the time she spent completely engaged, where the outside world ran as loud as the inside world — and between those intervals the hum came back at full volume and she ran on the logic of not stopping, which was the only alternative to the quiet and also the only thing that had ever worked.

When it was done, she was standing in the flat ground between freight stops with both of them down, breathing, and with Problem Solver empty and Trouble Maker down to three rounds, and the hum at its full baseline.

She ran a count. Problem Solver: slide back, chamber empty, six brass on the ground. She field-stripped it, cleared it, re-seated the slide. Then she went through the man nearest her and took what he was carrying — a different frame than hers, larger, chambered in something that wouldn't share her round, but loaded. She took it anyway.

This was the practice she had learned at sixteen, not from anyone who taught her but from the logic of the thing itself: you take what is available, because the alternative is to be choosy and then be empty. Problem Solver and Trouble Maker were hers in the way that a good tool became yours — not because she'd picked them from a catalog but because she'd carried them long enough that she knew every variance in their pull weight and every way her hands fit theirs. But a loaded frame she didn't know was better than an unloaded frame she did.

She counted the rounds in the new piece. She checked the function. She put it in her left coat pocket, Trouble Maker back in the left holster, and stood in the field with the wind coming off the flat ground and the two men breathing behind her.

The quiet that came in after this fight was the longest she had gotten in three years.

She stood in it and waited for the hum to come back, because the hum always came back, and it came back in its own time, and what she was standing in was the interval between the outside world returning to silence and the inside world returning to its normal volume, and the interval was longer than it had ever been before.

It was also emptier than it had ever been before.

She was standing in the longest quiet she'd ever produced in three years of buying quiet, and all she could do with it was notice that it was empty — that there was nothing in the absence of the hum except absence, that the thing she had been working toward her whole life was, in its actual experience, nothing at all. She had spent six months burning every quiet place she'd found and generating a standing order and a hunter and a revised price and two people breathing on the ground behind her, and the thing she had bought was empty.

The hum came back.

She walked south. The dog walked with her, its breathing the same as it always was — slow, even, already oriented to the next thing. She carried Problem Solver and Trouble Maker and a loaded frame whose name she didn't know in her left coat pocket, and the standing order behind her at its upgraded price, and the empty interval fading back into the baseline frequency.

She said nothing. There was nothing to say that improved it.

Somewhere north, the hunter was writing a second report. The order would read it and upgrade the price again or decide the asset was not worth the escalating cost. Either way was information. Either way she would be further south by the time the decision was made.

She kept walking.

[Beat 9]
She kept the map in her head, because paper came off a body easily and a head did not. It wasn't a picture. It was a list - place, status, reason for status - the accumulation of three years of moving through territory and learning which parts of it were still open to her. She ran it the way other people ran a set of worry beads, walking the gravel with the dog at her heel and nowhere in particular to be.

She said it out loud. There was no one to hear it but the dog, and the dog had never once acted on anything she said, which made it the ideal audience for a thing she only needed to say to know it was true.

"Kankakee. Closed." A field of cut stubble went by on the right, the soil dark gray-brown where it had been rained on and not dried. "Documentation man's network."

The dog kept pace.

"Beecher. Closed. Grain man's family had reach I didn't check for." She pulled the frame she'd taken off the flat ground out of her left coat pocket and checked the action by feel — different caliber than hers, six rounds left, no way to resupply the type out here. She put it back. "Everything between here and Wilmington - monitored. The order pays the hubs to add me to their watch list, one after another, south as I go." She had watched it happen from the inside: the standing order sitting patient and funded in the northern network, growing its distribution range the way a funded order always grew, buying the next hub south and the next after that until the order's reach and her own were nearly the same shape. It hadn't been fast. It hadn't needed to be. "Wilmington. Closed. That one's mine. I did that one myself."

The dog looked up at the change in her voice, then away.

"Braidwood. Closed. The hunter filed a report before I put him down. Thorough of him." She kept walking. "The flat ground east of the freight road - both operators down, so, active, and the order's about to upgrade the price on the strength of it."

She ran the list to the bottom and checked it against what she knew of the order's distribution and found that the number of places still open to her had shrunk to what she could cross at a walk in a single morning.

"You want to know how many roads are left," she told the dog. "One."

She had enough money for two weeks of provisions. She had Problem Solver and Trouble Maker and a frame she'd taken off the flat ground whose action was acceptable and whose round she couldn't resupply. She had the dog, which wasn't an asset and wasn't a liability and was simply present. And she had the list, and the list said every direction but one had closed.

The one direction was southwest. The dead end of the freight line.

Joliet was the last major hub before the line terminated. Past Joliet the tracks thinned; past the thinned tracks the salvage crews had stripped the infrastructure, the freight line going gravel going grass going nothing. Nobody went further southwest than Joliet needed them to. Nobody had funded an order out that far because nobody valuable went out that far. The order's southern reach had stopped at Wilmington because past Wilmington was territory not worth paying for.

She stood on the last open road south and had two options: the direction the order wasn't watching, or the directions it was.

The direction the order wasn't watching was southwest, toward the dead end.

The towers were the other way. Northeast, the densest concentration of people and signal on the continent, the thing she'd spent her whole life keeping at her back - and the hum ran toward them, slightly, consistently, the way a direction gets asked for rather than arrived at. Three years of walking away from it, and all the walking had done was compress the open ground until the only road left ran southwest, toward nothing, away from the pull. That was where you ended up when you kept moving off a thing that leaned on you without ever picking a direction to replace it: the dead end. She'd been walking to it since she was sixteen without saying so.

"Southwest," she said, to no one in particular.

The dog was already facing southwest.

She picked up her pack and walked toward Joliet, which was also toward nowhere, which was also toward the one open box left on the list, and the hum ran its frequency at its baseline volume and leaned northeast, and the towers stood on the horizon.

*Médecin Moreau was the medic's name. She had paid him Φ40 for a shoulder she couldn't explain and he had not asked her to explain it.*

She lay back on the bunkhouse cot with her boots still on and ran the list one more time, the way she always did before sleep took whatever it was going to take. Kankakee, closed. Beecher, closed. Wilmington, closed. Braidwood, closed and paying for a hunter's silence. The order's price still climbing somewhere north of her, patient and funded and gray-zone all the way down — money moving hand to hand outside any ledger, hunters bought on reputation and a folding table's word. That was what had let it reach as far as it had: it never had to answer to anyone. It also meant it stopped exactly where that kind of money stopped being useful. North of Joliet the freight interchange ran a Consensus credential check for anyone continuing into the city proper — a checkpoint that took a registered identity, not a rumor and a price. She had neither. The order's hunters didn't either, which meant that for the first time in three years the thing chasing her and the thing she was walking toward wanted two different kinds of proof of who she was, and neither of them had it. It wasn't safety. It was just the first fence in three years built out of something other than money. She let that be enough to sleep on.

## Steppin Razor

[Beat 11]

[Beat 12]
Outside was Joliet, which the city kept the way a man keeps a tooth he means to pull. The towers were sixty miles northeast, a low smear of light on the sky like a sunrise that had gotten stuck and given up. Out here the freight rails ran flat and dead in both directions, rust the color of dried blood, and nobody rode them anymore because nobody came this far on purpose.

She had walked toward the quiet for three weeks. The trouble was the quiet kept thinning out ahead of her, always one town further, and the only direction it hadn't thinned was northeast, toward the light, toward the exact place the hum had always been loudest. She had been too tired to notice the contradiction until tonight: she was hunting silence by walking away from the towers, and the towers were where the silence ran out. She turned that over and put it down. She walked the rail bed north because north was the only direction with a light at the end of it, and because behind her the man was still screaming and she had nowhere in particular to be.

The dog wasn't at her heel. She looked back: it had stopped six paces behind her in the gravel, sitting, ears forward, not following. She'd never seen it do that before.

She turned forward.

The rail bed had a camel on it.

Not a rendering - an animal. She could smell it: warm, biological, faintly grassy, the unmistakable signature of something that ate and breathed and had traveled far enough to work up a smell. It stood with its feet on the dead gravel and regarded her with the long sideways patience of an animal that has nowhere to be.

Sasha stopped.

The camel stopped too, a few feet off. On its back sat a man, dressed for no weather in particular, sitting it like he'd been at it a thousand years and meant to do a thousand more. The important thing - the thing her body understood a half second before the rest of her caught up - was that there are no camels in Joliet. There had never been camels in Joliet. The fact dropped into her stomach cold. She kept her face shut.

She had the paper cone in her jacket pocket still - three gooseberries, the ones she'd set down before the man folded. She pulled it out. The camel reached, without ceremony and without being invited, and took what was left in one patient sweep of lip built for deserts and not, she would have thought, for paper cones from Joliet. She watched it chew, filed the transaction under things that told her nothing about range, weapons, or exits, and let it go.

The hum, which had been quiet all the way out here, was not quiet now. It had come up the second the camel did, low and forward and pointed, like a hand laid flat against her sternum from the inside. She didn't like the timing.

On the far rail, a gray cat sat with its paws folded under its chest and watched her. Not the camel. Her. The dog from the bar was nowhere in it, and she didn't go looking for where it had got to. She filed the trade in the same place she filed everything nobody was ever going to pay her to explain.

[Beat 13]
The man on the camel spoke without preamble, the way a fact speaks.

"We have been watching your progress."

"Which one of you," Sasha asked.

"We just arrived."

She pulled Problem Solver off her right hip and held it at her side, muzzle down, not aimed at anything, because you didn't aim at a neuretic call. She aimed at things she could hurt. The man on the camel registered the gun the way a window registers weather.

*Nobody had taught her a word for this. Out on the road you learned what worked and named it after, if you bothered naming it at all.*

"The camel," she said, because she needed to establish something factual, even if the fact was embarrassing. "It's yours."

"The camel," the man said, "is not our thrall. We happen to be going the same direction."

"Which direction is that?"

"We just arrived."

She breathed through her nose. The hum was not in her teeth yet, which meant whatever this was, it was not doing it at full power. She filed that. The cat on the far rail had not moved. It was watching her with the patience of something that had been waiting longer than she had been alive, and she did not like that either.

The camel put its head down and breathed at her — a long, warm gust from somewhere deep in its chest, the smell of hay and distance and something she couldn't name that was older than either. It was the same thing the strays did, every stray she'd ever walked past: the immediate, unreserved decision that she was the one. Not the person next to her, not the safer-seeming target. Her. She had never once invited it. She had never once managed to stop it.

*They always found her. She had never figured out if that was a comfort or a diagnosis.*

"What do you want," she said.

"Seventeen past two," the man said. "Three days ago. You felt it."

She had. She had been standing at the Glooms perimeter and she had felt something fire through the platform steel into the arches of her feet, before the nearest seismograph had recorded anything. She had not told anyone.

She holstered Problem Solver.

[Beat 14]
The man on the camel said, "Someone should have closed you. We are glad of the oversight."

Not a question. Not a threat. Just two facts delivered the way you'd note a door left open on a cold night — and then the additional one: the gladness.

The breath she didn't take was the tell — that half-second where her chest didn't move and her hand didn't move and the rail bed didn't move and the hum leaned in through the gap like it had been waiting for exactly this width of silence. She felt it arrive. Not through her ears. Through the place behind her sternum where the mesh sat flush against bone, the place that registered wrongness as pressure before the mind had words for it. *The mesh had been running open a long time. She'd been managing it. She'd been managing it fine.*

The anger came back. It always came back this way — like it had only stepped out for air.

She hadn't asked yet. She had been building toward it, waiting for the right seam in the exchange.

She pulled that apart and put it back together. *She had spent her whole life making sure she was never in a room with a corpo credential reader in it. The towers were nothing but rooms like that, stacked to the sky.*

This was the 72, patient and unresigned, pushing up through the steel deck and into the bones of her feet and her ankles and the long column of her, asking nothing, expecting nothing, still here. The cold line above her ribs answered it on the exhale. Two frequencies. Neither one stopping.

She found a position on the platform and stood facing the tube mouth, the crowd thick at her back, the dark throat of the vacuum line open in front of her. The door behind her was not locked. She hadn't put her back to it. These were small things and she was counting them anyway, because they were new, and she had learned early that new things required inventory before they could be trusted. Deep in the line a pod's approach lights bloomed — a spreading circle of white in a long throat of black — and the crowd shifted forward with the pull of people who have somewhere to be.

She still had the comms rig's frequency. One channel, one silence, one dead-drop she could not confirm. A thing she could keep, the way she'd kept everything — a list in her head, private, hers, deniable.

She keyed it out instead.

Not to the dead-drop this time. She opened it into the platform itself — into the standing wash of ten thousand neuretics and arrays, the densest signal field on the continent — and let the frequency go. The mesh, for the first time, opened in the other direction. Thirty years of signal admitted without her permission; now one sent out on hers. It left the same way everything always arrived — through the seam, through the bones, not pain, just presence — seeded into the bleed where it would ride out on every rig in range and could not be pulled back to one silenced channel again. The pod slid into the berth. The doors breathed open. The crowd moved forward. She didn't.

She stood on the platform and let the press sort itself out around her — something she hadn't done since sixteen, staying put while a crowd moved, not reading an exit, not threading ahead — and when the last body had cleared the apron and the doors were narrowing on empty air, she stepped through. Not first. Last. The dog came in at her heel.

She was already changed — not quiet, not less angry, still here — and the frequency was out now, riding on ten thousand rigs she would never be able to trace, her name on nothing she could recall.
