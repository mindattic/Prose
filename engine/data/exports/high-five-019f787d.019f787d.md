# The Come Up

## Kicked Out

The door hit Tavi's garbage bag on the way out, not his back, which was the only mercy Ozzie had left in him.

"She's coming at eight," Ozzie said from the threshold, retreating by inches into the squat's orange glow — the boarded storefront's one working bulb, wired straight into whatever the building still had left to give. "Eight, Tavi. You know what eight means."

"It means seven fifty-five," Tavi said.

The door sealed with a magnetic click, then the heavier thunk of the dead-bolt Ozzie had welded on himself two years back. Reza thumped down onto the stoop beside him a half-second later, his own bag over one shoulder and a foam pillow tucked under his arm that he had absolutely stolen from Ozzie's couch.

The Gray Zone opened around them, the way it always did at this hour — gray because the sun hadn't decided about it yet, gray because nothing here had ever been painted a color on purpose. The street ran east into a row of dead storefronts just like this one, plywood over the display glass, and west into the alley mouth where Ozzie's building backed up against three others exactly like it, close enough to touch both walls with your arms out. The city's nanocoverage did its civic minimum out here, maybe fifteen percent on a good morning, and thinner the farther you got from anything wearing a badge. No patrol came through before nine. Nobody's problem this early.

Reza looked up and down the empty street with the expression he wore when he was pretending not to be delighted. His diploma was in his bag — Tavi knew this because his own was in his bag, both of them the same laminated nothing from the same basic education cohort, signed by the same administrator who had spelled Tavi's name wrong and not corrected it. No chrome at either of their temples, no etch at the jaw, no augment, no tier that bought you anything — just the civic neuretics everyone got seeded with before they could walk, the baseline lattice and nothing on top of it. Out here the overlay ran as thin as the nanocoverage: a civic marker, a dead ad-channel, then the bare substrate showing through.

South, past the rooftops, the Thread went up. Orbital tether, anchored somewhere near Crestwood, rising until it stopped being visible and then rising further into a sky that didn't care. Most residents stopped noticing it after a year. Tavi had been on this block for three months and he had not stopped noticing it once.

Reza raised his hand. Tavi looked at it for one full second, then slapped it anyway. The crack of the high-five bounced off the boarded-up window behind them and disappeared down the empty street.

"Okay," Reza said, grinning now that Ozzie couldn't see him do it. "So. Up's a direction."

"Up's one direction," Tavi said.

The block south of Ozzie's squat opened onto Skillet Row by way of an alley that smelled like hot oil and condensation and, underneath that, something sweet from the row of stalls ahead. Skillet Row ran the length of a dead loading dock nobody had bothered to seal off — a hundred booths wedged into a space built for delivery trucks that hadn't run in a decade, each one a fold-out counter and a burner and whoever was willing to stand behind it. The smell hit them at the mouth of the alley. Tavi's stomach answered before he did.

They ate standing at a counter whose proprietor had not looked at them once, the bowls in front of them the kind of cheap that meant the broth was real because nothing else was. Reza ate with the focused attention of someone who had skipped breakfast. Between them, they had Φ38 on a single credstick, which was Reza's, because Tavi's had read Φ0.00 since Tuesday.

"We could do courier work," Reza said.

"Courier work pays eleven quanta a day."

"We could do a lot of courier work."

Tavi looked at him. Reza kept eating.

The booth two down had no sign. That was the first thing. The second thing was the woman behind it — broad, unhurried, wearing an apron that had been white once and had arrived at its current color through honest labor. She was watching them the way a person watches weather: not interested, just tracking. When Tavi met her eyes she did not look away. She ladled broth into a bowl for someone else without breaking the look.

"That one," Tavi said.

Reza glanced up. "The lady with the ladle?"

"The lady with the ladle."

They left their bowls half-finished — the only currency either of them had left to spend on dignity — and moved down the Row. Up close the booth smelled like pork fat and ginger and something chemical underneath that Tavi couldn't name. The woman — Scraps, though nobody introduced her; a name just accumulated, the fifth or sixth time someone said it in her hearing — set the ladle down. She looked at their temples — no etch — then their hands — no chrome — and last their faces, with the expression of someone reading a document they had already read many times and finding it unchanged.

"You're not here for noodles," she said.

"We're looking for work," Tavi said.

"What kind."

"The kind that exists."

She picked up a cloth and wiped the counter. It didn't need it. "You ever hurt anyone?"

Reza said, "Not professionally."

Something moved in her face, some thin flicker that wasn't quite amusement. She reached under the counter and set a folded slip of paper on the surface. An address, a name, a number. The number was Φ200 split two ways — Φ100 each. Tavi had not seen that much in one place in six weeks. His face did not move.

"Remind him," she said. "Don't break anything you can't explain. Come back with confirmation and I'll have your hundred each. No advance. No exceptions. No sympathy if you don't come back, because I'm not your mother and this isn't that kind of booth."

Tavi picked up the slip. Reza was already grinning. Tavi did not ask what confirmation meant, which was the part he should have been thinking about, and wasn't.

They left thrilled, walking fast, the slip of paper folded flat in Tavi's shirt pocket. Behind them, Scraps picked up her ladle. She did not watch them go. She was on to the next bowl.

The address on the slip was a walk-up three blocks over and around the corner, which they found on the second try after the first door turned out to be a laundry operation whose proprietor threw it open mid-tirade about something that had nothing to do with them. Wrong door — except it wasn't, not exactly: Tavi had read the number correctly, and the building had numbered its units incorrectly. Two different problems.

The debtor's name was Pellucid, which was not a name, and the door that bore it — stenciled in flaking yellow — was answered by a man who was not Pellucid but who was very large and standing close enough that Tavi could smell the protein supplement on his breath. Behind him, a Volume feed played something with a lot of red in it. The unit was the size of Ozzie's squat except fuller, hotter, and occupied by three additional people who all stopped what they were doing to look.

"We're here about the money," Tavi said, and knew it was wrong the instant it left his mouth — the room's temperature changed before he finished the sentence.

The large man's hand came off the door frame fast enough that it wasn't a punch — it was a grab, closing on Reza's collar, because Reza was the one who'd stepped forward to look friendly, which had seemed like a good idea eight seconds ago. Reza went sideways into the door frame and then the large man was pulling him in and Tavi had exactly one thought before his body made the decision for him: *the slip said remind, not ask*, and then he was inside too and the door sealed behind them with a magnetic click.

What happened next took eleven seconds. Tavi counted because counting was something he could do while his brain was trying to catch up with the situation. Reza hit the floor once, found his footing, and came up at the wrong angle — too close, no room, the large man moving again before Reza had reset his feet — and Reza threw his whole weight into the man, all mass and no technique, his elbow catching the nose at a geometry that nobody had planned. The sound it made was specific. The large man's head snapped back and he sat down on a folding chair that buckled under him and deposited him on the floor, and he stayed there, and the room went quiet except for the Volume feed, which kept going.

*He's not moving like someone who made a mistake. He's standing over him and he's breathing and his hands are at his sides and his hands are completely still.*

The three additional people were looking at Reza. Reza was looking at the man on the floor. His face was not the grinning face. It was something else, something that had no name yet in either of them, and it lasted three seconds before he turned around and looked at Tavi with an expression that said *now what* in a register that sounded almost calm.

Tavi found the panel. Every squat like this one had a panel — maintenance access, utility routing, easy to overlook until the day you needed it. He found it behind a crate of something, pried it open with the edge of his credstick, looked at the board inside and understood approximately forty percent of it, which turned out to be the correct forty percent. He pulled the right wire on the second try. The lights in the unit dropped to emergency orange. In the confusion — and there was confusion, because the three additional people were now moving toward the door — Reza said, very clearly, like he already knew someone would ask him to repeat it, "He says next Tuesday," and then they were out the door and moving, running south because south was away.

They were four blocks and two turns before they stopped. Reza bent forward with his hands on his knees. Tavi's lungs were doing something he would not describe as breathing. Around them the Gray Zone churned on, indifferent, the haze thick and brown and full of things that weren't their problem. Reza made a sound that was either a laugh or the thing that comes just before one. Tavi looked at his hands — both of them, his own — and looked at Reza's, which were still completely still.

"Confirmation," Reza said, still bent over. "What's confirmation?"

"We go back and tell her what he said," Tavi said. "That's confirmation. Proof we were in the room."

Reza straightened up. The grin was back but it was sitting on top of something that hadn't been there this morning, and Tavi saw it and did not say so, and filed it in the column where he put things that mattered and could not yet be named.

Scraps took the credstick without looking up from the ladle. She tapped it against a reader mounted under the counter lip and handed it back to Tavi and that was it — no ceremony, no acknowledgment that anything had happened, no version of *good work* that he would ever receive from her. He looked at the display.

Φ200.00.

He stared at it, waiting for the zero count to correct itself. He looked at Reza. Reza looked at the display. The number sat there, patient and actual, and then Reza made a sound that was not a word and grabbed Tavi's arm with both hands and the credstick nearly slipped from his hand into the gutter running alongside the Row and Tavi snatched it back against his chest like it was a child.

"Don't," Tavi said, but Reza was laughing, turning a full circle in the middle of the Row, and a woman with a bag of vegetables stepped around him without breaking stride because the Gray Zone had seen worse.

They walked fast, talking over each other, drawing up tonight's plan with the reckless arithmetic of nineteen-year-olds holding the biggest number either of them had ever personally owned: drinks first, something real, not the floor-sweepings Ozzie bought in bulk. Then something to smoke or swallow or inhale, whatever Reza's guy in Bridgeport was moving this week. Then girls — or whoever, Tavi said, and Reza said *obviously, whoever* — and the rest could sort itself out by morning. Every quanta of it was getting spent, and spent tonight — that part was never in question.

Reza put his hand up and Tavi hit it, hard, palm to palm, the crack of it swallowed by the Row's noise, and they were both grinning now, the same grin, the one that didn't know yet what it was standing on top of. The credstick was warm in Tavi's pocket. His hands had stopped shaking somewhere between the squat and here, and he hadn't noticed when.

## Or We Do It Again

The credstick sat on the bar between them, balance strip lit, and Reza was already doing the math out loud — same as he did everything else, at volume, with his hands — dividing by two and naming what his half could buy, a room with a window, a meal that came on a plate instead of in a bag, maybe the Cermak Tier-2 consult he'd been putting off, get the basic mesh started, stop being the guy in every room who processed slowest.

Tavi looked at the number on the strip.

*Enough to stop.*

"Or we do it again," he said.

Reza stopped mid-sentence. Turned. The look on his face was the look of a man who had heard a language he almost spoke.

"Do it — " he said. "The same job?"

"Different job. Same decision."

Reza picked up the credstick, turned it over in his fingers, and let it drop back to the bar. It was loud around them — someone's Volume feed bleeding sports commentary into the air above the corner booth, the bartender running a rag across chrome that didn't need it — and for a moment neither of them said anything, which for Reza was almost physiologically unusual.

"Brother," he said finally, "I have been sleeping in a stairwell for six weeks."

"I know."

"I have been eating — " he paused, searching for the word. "I don't know what I've been eating. Gray things."

"I know."

"And you want to put the number back in the machine."

"I want to put half the number back in the machine," Tavi said. "Take the room. Eat something with a color. Just — " He stopped. He was not good at finishing this kind of sentence and he didn't pretend otherwise. He looked at the credstick instead of at Reza. "Keep the other half working."

Reza stared at him for a long moment, a stare doing real computation behind it, and then he laughed. Not the loud one he used when something was actually funny — the shorter one, the sound that came out when something landed somewhere unexpected and true.

"You've been wanting something," Reza said. Not an accusation. Something quieter than that.

Tavi didn't answer, which was also an answer.

Reza picked up his glass, drank, set it down, and looked at the strip one more time. The number glowed. He pushed the credstick across the bar toward Tavi — not all the way, just far enough that it was Tavi's problem now — and said, "Split it clean. You run the reinvest. I don't want to know the details until there are details worth knowing."

"That's not how it works."

"That's exactly how it works," Reza said. "I got the door. You got the plan. That's the whole us."

The job was a runner intercept — lift a data package from a mid-level Axiom logistics courier before he made his drop at the Cermak border market. Ozzie pulled the brief from a contact he wouldn't name and took his fifteen percent off the top before he handed it to them, which was fair enough given that Ozzie had a room and a contact list and they had neither.

Tavi spent two days on the approach geometry. The courier's route had three soft points; he mapped all three, costed each one in time and exposure, and then sat with the result until only one remained. The handoff window was four minutes, the market was loud, and the courier wore his package on a lanyard under his jacket — which meant Reza would need to get close. Close meant the courier's attention had to be somewhere other than his chest.

"That's me," Reza said, when Tavi walked him through it.

"That's you."

"What do I do?"

"You knock something over."

Reza looked at him.

"You knock something over," Tavi said again, "and when he turns to look, I'm already past him."

The thing Reza knocked over was a three-tier display of vacuum-sealed protein blocks at the market's west entrance, which went down with the specific catastrophic sound of something that had been stacked by someone who cared about it. The courier turned, and so did every vendor in a ten-meter radius. Tavi went through the crowd at an angle that wasn't quite a run — too deliberate for panic, too fast for browsing — and when he came out the other side the lanyard was in his jacket pocket and the courier was still apologizing to the vendor about the protein blocks, because Reza was helping restack them, one block at a time, with enormous solemnity, and it was very hard to be angry at a man that large who was being that careful.

Afterward, in the freight corridor behind the market, Tavi handed the package to Ozzie's contact and received a credstick that read higher than the last one. His hands were steady. He checked them, specifically, because he wanted to know — and they were steady.

The clinic was called Biter, which Ozzie said was either a recommendation or a warning, and the sign above the door was a tooth rendered in chrome-flake paint that had started to peel at the canine. Bloom Quarter fringe — not inside the Substrate seam, but near enough that the nano coverage ran thin and the air tasted faintly of something the body knew wasn't right. The woman who ran the front desk had eyes that tracked half a second behind her head, which meant Tier-1 mesh at minimum, probably installed here, probably by whoever was in the back room with the bone saw.

Reza went first because he was always going to go first.

The subdermal work took three hours. They laid him on a table that smelled of isopropyl and something organic underneath it, and the technician — small hands, efficient, no wasted motion — worked the composite sheeting in through four incisions along the lateral torso, two more at the shoulder caps, and used a thermal press that made a sound like a staple gun amplified into something you felt in your molars. The reflex splice was faster — a cluster of six thread-anchors seated at the C6 vertebra — and it required him to be awake for the second half of it, lying facedown on the table, not moving. The technician told him not to move. He didn't move. His hands were flat against the table and he focused on them.

Tavi watched from the waiting chair until they told him not to watch, then he looked at the wall.

The jack behind Tavi's ear was forty minutes, local anesthetic, and a pressure he could feel through his skull but couldn't name as pain — something more like the world insisting on itself in a place it hadn't been before. Afterward the technician handed him a small mirror and he looked at the port: flush-set, matte gray, barely larger than the nail on his smallest finger. He touched it once. The edge was smooth. His face in the mirror looked the same as it had before, which felt like a small, specific lie.

They bought the pistols from a counter at the back of the same building — Ozzie had arranged that too, because Ozzie arranged everything and took fifteen percent of everything — two identical matte-black holdouts, compact enough to sit in a waistband without printing, simple enough to field-strip without a manual. No branding visible. The man behind the counter called them Ankle-Biters, which was either a model name or a joke about their range. Tavi took his and turned it in his hands twice, checking the action, and then looked at Reza, who had taken up the identical grip without seeming to think about it — same angle, same hold — and something moved across Reza's face that wasn't quite a smile.

"Same," Reza said.

"Same," Tavi said.

Reza holstered his and winced when the motion pulled at the incision sites, and for the next three days the hardware argued with him — low fever, the composite sheeting hot to the touch through the skin, a bone-deep ache at the shoulder caps that woke him twice in the night. He lay in the room he'd rented — window, as promised, overlooking a ventilation stack — and catalogued the wrongness like weather: present, specific, temporary. On the fourth day it stopped arguing and started being part of him, and he pressed two fingers against the shoulder cap and felt the resistance there, something that hadn't been there before, something that would stop what it was designed to stop.

Tavi's jack itched for a week. Not at the surface — underneath, where the anchor threads had seated into tissue. He didn't touch it. He checked in the mirror each morning and it looked the same as the day before: flush, matte, unremarkable. The first time he plugged a test line into it and felt the data arrive as something other than sound or sight — a temperature, a quality of the air — he sat very still for a long moment in Ozzie's Squat with Ozzie's soldering equipment on the table in front of him.

He unplugged the line. He looked at his hands. Steady.

## The Angels

Three flights down from street level, the Substrate coverage thinned to something you could feel — a faint absence in the air, like pressure dropping before weather. Reza paid at the front desk, the credstick chirping twice for both their tabs, and a woman with neuretic etching running silver from her jaw to her ear showed them down a hall that smelled of cedar oil and recycled breath and something underneath both of those things, something sweet and slightly wrong, like fruit going. The rooms were separated by a curtain of weighted beads. Tavi pulled his aside and looked at his bed and the two women already on it, one sitting cross-legged and one lying with her arm over her eyes, and he looked back at Reza with an expression that had nothing in it except the fact of the moment, which was enough.

Sunday was the one sitting. She smiled when Tavi came in — not the professional smile, or not only that, something warmer underneath it, the way a person smiles when they have decided to be present. She held up the jack lead between two fingers, nails short, wrists small. Tavi sat on the edge of the bed and she leaned forward and seated the lead behind his ear at the port and the connection opened and the Honeycomb's Glass architecture flooded in and the ceiling went away.

What the Glass did was not hallucination. Hallucination is the brain inventing. This was the brain receiving, every sensory channel opened past its governor, the world remixed into something that kept its physical grammar — weight, warmth, the specific grain of another person's skin under a hand — but dialed every parameter past the range the body knew how to contextualize. Sunday's collarbone under his mouth was real and more than real, a texture that registered in his chest before it registered in his lips, heat that moved through him in a slow wave that had no ceiling, and he heard her breath change and felt her hand press flat against his back and pull, and the pull was an answer to something he hadn't known he'd asked.

Across the bead curtain — close enough that the sound carried, far enough that it was its own world — he heard Ivet say something low to Reza, a single sentence, and then Reza's laugh, and then silence that wasn't empty. Tavi's hand found Sunday's hip and she shifted her weight into it, a small deliberate motion, her eyes open and on him, and somewhere in the architecture of the Glass her presence was amplified until she felt enormous, the only fixed point in a room that kept softening at the edges. He raised his palm from the mattress and held it out sideways, through the gap in the curtain, fingers spread. A beat. Then Reza's palm met his from the other side, the slap of it loud and stupid and perfect, and Tavi laughed into Sunday's shoulder, and she laughed too — actually laughed, the real kind, her ribs moving under his arm — and he took that as proof of something he would not examine until morning, when the Glass was gone and the proof was all he had left.

The Glass never eased them down; it simply let go — no ceremony, no gradient, just a threshold crossed, and the world was ordinary again, worse for the comparison. Tavi lay on his back and the ceiling was a ceiling, water-stained foam tile, one corner pulling away from its adhesive. Sunday was on her side facing him, awake, the jack lead coiled on the pillow between them. Her eye makeup had tracked a little. She looked at him with the expression of someone who had been watching him come back to himself and had not looked away, and there was nothing in her face that was trying to be anything.

"You've got money left," she said. "For the morning. If you want."

Tavi said nothing.

The bead curtain shifted. Ivet came through dressed and ready to leave, credstick in her jacket pocket, one earbead in. She looked at Reza, who was sitting up with the sheet around his waist and his hair wrong, blinking at the room like it owed him an explanation, and said nothing. Her bag was still in the corner where she'd set it the night before — set it, not dropped it, the strap looped neatly over the chair — and she picked it up and was gone, the curtain settling behind her, the sound of her footsteps on the stairs fading before the curtain stopped moving.

Sunday watched the curtain. Then she looked back at Tavi and something moved across her face, fast and practical, and she reached up and unclipped her second earbead from the shelf above the bed and seated it, and in that one motion she was somewhere else entirely, three transactions further down the day, and Tavi saw it happen and felt something cold settle in his chest that had nothing to do with her and everything to do with the ceiling and the foam tile and the specific smell of this room in the morning. He sat up. Reza, across the curtain, was very quiet.

"We're not doing this again," Tavi said. Not to Sunday. To Reza, through the gap.

A pause. Then: "No," Reza said. No joke in it, no warmth, no exclamation point. Just the word. Sunday pulled her jacket from the foot of the bed and did not look at either of them, and Tavi understood that she had heard boys say it before, and she was already somewhere the sentence couldn't reach her, and he did not blame her for that, and he did not look away.

The next eight months had a shape that was only visible in retrospect: a narrowing. Reza found a trainer named Breckenridge who ran a gym out of a gutted auto shop two blocks from Ozzie's Squat, ex-Arcturus, a man whose opinions about footwork carried the weight other men reserved for religion. He charged Φ40 a session and Reza paid it every time, and what he got back was a body that already knew how to be large learning how to be precise about it — where the threshold was between a presence and a problem, how to fill a doorway so that the filling was itself a communication. Tavi watched him run the drill once, near the end of those eight months — three of Breckenridge's other students taking turns rushing the frame while Reza gave nothing back, weight sunk low, breath even, until the count ran out and he was still standing in the doorway like he'd grown there. Tavi, across those same months, found a different kind of narrow. He had always been fast at reading a room. He got faster. He started auditing security architecture compulsively, for pleasure — pulling at a thread because the pulling felt good and he wanted to know how far it went.

Ozzie's board ran two kinds of listings, mostly: solo work and paired work, and paired work paid less per head, because a fixer selling a client two bodies was selling insurance, not skill, and insurance was a discount item. A ghost booked alone got a solo rate. A wall booked alone got a different solo rate. A package deal — ghost and wall, guaranteed together — bought the client peace of mind and cost Reza and Tavi money apiece, which neither of them minded yet, because neither of them had needed to be apart.

The job was a courier intercept in the upper Yards, a Lotus-adjacent contract through Ozzie's board, the target a data package moving through a mid-tier logistics node that had better security than it deserved. The client wanted the package and wanted no noise. They went in through the service corridor on Tavi's count and he was at the terminal in forty seconds and his fingers were already moving and the node's architecture opened for him the way a combination lock opens when someone who has been listening to it for years finally hears the right click. Alarms, cameras, the desk ident panel, the access log, the automated flag protocol — he took them in sequence, not fast, exact, each one clean, and when he was done the room was dark in a way that was different from the lights being off. This wasn't lights-off dark. It was erased-from-the-record dark, the room struck from the conversation entirely. Somewhere above them, in the logistics node's incident queue, there was nothing. He pulled the package and stood up. A runner named Vasquez, waiting at the corridor end, said *what the fuck* under her breath, and then said, quieter, *cutout*, and Tavi heard it and did not respond and filed it.

Reza's part came twenty minutes later, on the way out, because there is always a part on the way out. Three of the node's private security detail rounded the stairwell corner and found Reza in the doorway of the only exit that mattered, and Reza did what Breckenridge had taught him to do, which was nothing complicated: he held the door. The first one came in low with a shock baton and Reza caught the wrist and redirected it into the second man's jaw and the jaw made a sound and the second man went down and did not get up, and then it was two, and then it was one, and the one looked at Reza in the doorway and made the calculation that the doorway was not going to become available, not through any effort available to him, and that calculation was correct. Vasquez went past with the package. Tavi went past. Reza stepped back and let the door close and the one remaining security man did not follow. On the street outside, in the dark, Vasquez looked at Reza for a moment and said *rampart* like she was testing whether the word fit, and it did, and she didn't say anything else.

Tavi felt it on the walk back to Ozzie's Squat — not as a problem, not yet, just a fact he turned over like a stone. Reza had stood in a doorway and made his body into the only door that mattered. Tavi had stood at a terminal and made himself into something the room's cameras couldn't find a reason to remember. Nobody had trained them to be opposite. It had just come out that way, and the job had used both halves of it without asking. They were on Ozzie's board by the end of the week as a package, Rampart/Cutout, and the board was a good board and their rate started, slowly, to climb.

## The First Job Without Him

The job was a pull-and-carry: one data slug from a dead-drop inside the Ashgrave perimeter, no contact with anyone who worked there, no trace on the way out. Auda Vane had said *precision* the way other people say *please*, like it was a courtesy and a threat at the same time.

Tavi had asked, at her counter, whether it was a two-hand job. He hadn't said Reza's name; he hadn't had to. Vane didn't look up from her annex. "The client bought a ghost," she said. "Bring a second and the contract voids and you eat the kill fee." She slid the brief across the counter with two fingers. "You want to carry a wall around, carry it on your own time." She had looked at Tavi when she said it. She had not looked at Reza, who was not in the room, because there had been no reason to bring him — and that was the new thing, and neither of them would have said it out loud.

Tavi moved through the Corridor's service margin at the hour when the haze thickened enough to cut visibility to fifteen meters and the perimeter lights went amber-yellow and useless. The smell was chemical and wet — sulphur compounds and something sweeter underneath, the Substrate's remediation nano losing its argument with whatever Ashgrave was cooking in the secondary refinery. He kept to the drainage channel, ankle-deep in runoff that left an iridescent film on his boots, and did not think about Reza sitting in the rented room above the noodle counter in the Yards, waiting for a ping that would not come until it was over.

Twice on the approach his hand drifted toward the channel to loop Reza in — reflex, two years deep, the wall goes where the ghost goes — and twice Vane's terms stopped it before the thought finished. There was no wall on this one. No second body, no noise, no door to hold; a wall in a place like this was just mass that got seen. The client had bought a ghost, and a ghost works alone. That was the whole of it, and it was not personal and it was not cruel — it was just the shape the trade had grown into, and the shape did not have Reza in it. The friendship was the thing standing in the doorway now, and the job wanted the doorway empty. He took his hand off the channel. What he could not stop looking at, later, was how little it had cost him to do it.

The dead-drop took four minutes to find — a maintenance panel with a loose lower-left fastener, a tell Vane had described in twelve words. The slug was there. He pocketed it without breaking stride, came back out through the drainage channel, and was two blocks into the buffer zone before Ashgrave's perimeter sweep completed its next cycle — clean and quiet, an exit that left nothing behind to explain. For a moment he stood in the gray space between the Corridor's glow and the dark of the Gray Zone proper, feeling — briefly, specifically, without permission — the particular lightness of a job that had only one weight to carry.

He pinged Vane. One character. She responded with the transfer confirmation before he'd closed the channel. Somewhere behind him, in the rented room, Reza's screen would still be dark.

The perimeter sweep had a twelve-second gap between the eastern sensor array and the secondary thermal net — Tavi had clocked it on the way in, filed it without thinking much about it, the same way you file a step-count or a barometric reading. What he had not filed was the variable. The gap ran twelve seconds when the sweep ran clean. When a secondary trigger fired — a stray heat signature, a Substrate bloom, anything — it compressed to four.

The guard came out of the haze at the three-second mark.

Tavi went left and low, got under the swing, and the baton caught him across the ribs instead of the back of the skull. The crack was specific and internal, a sound that lived inside his chest rather than outside it. He kept moving. The guard was big and trained and had four inches on him, and none of that mattered as much as the fact that Tavi had both hands free and the guard had one occupied with the baton. His elbow found the man's trachea at the angle Ozzie had shown him in the kitchen at Ozzie's Squat two years ago, using a mop handle and a lot of patience, and the guard sat down in the runoff and stayed there.

*Reza takes the right side. Always. You don't even look anymore. You just go left.*

He went left. There was no one there.

The remaining four blocks to the buffer zone he walked in a controlled way — not fast enough to register as flight, not slow enough to register as anything — and only when he was in the dark properly, the Corridor's amber glow behind him and nothing ahead but the Gray Zone's ambient noise, did he put his hand against the wall of a shuttered freight intake and press until the pressure told him what he was working with: two ribs, maybe one cracked clean and one just flexed, both kinds he'd felt before. He breathed carefully through his nose and felt the Substrate's nano begin its argument with the swelling, the familiar low warmth that meant the city was at least paying attention, even out here where coverage ran thin and slow.

Reza did not get a ping.

Three weeks out from the Ashgrave job, Tavi met Reza for dinner at a noodle place in the Yards that wasn't the one above their old room — that one had closed, the counter sealed with a strip of amber tape and no explanation. They found a booth in the back and ordered the same thing, the lamb broth with the soft egg, and Reza talked about his new contract and Tavi listened, and at some point Tavi noticed he was tracking the exits. Not because he needed to. Just because there was nothing else to do with his hands.

Reza had a new piece on his right forearm — full-sleeve haptic array, Helix-grade, the etching running clean from wrist to elbow in a pattern that caught the light when he moved. He didn't mention it. He didn't have to. Tavi had the twin in his jacket pocket, the original Ankle-Biter, the one they'd bought from a gray-market Splicer in the Bloom Quarter when they were nineteen and thought hardware would solve whatever they were. Reza's was in a drawer somewhere. Tavi still carried his because he hadn't decided yet what it meant to stop.

"You remember Ozzie's mop handle," Reza said, at some point, and Tavi said "yes," and they both smiled, and the smile was real, and then it was over and there was nothing to put after it. Reza reached across the table with his palm up — the old signal, the one that meant *we did a thing*, the one that had never needed to be explained — and Tavi brought his hand up a half-second late, and the slap landed slightly wrong, fingers instead of palm, and neither of them said anything about it.

*The mop handle was yours. The left side was mine. That was the whole system.*

They finished the broth. Reza picked up the tab, which was new, and Tavi let him — also new — and they stood outside in the Yards' amber light while the Substrate nano moved slow and warm through the thin coverage and Tavi's ribs ached at the seam where the cracked one had knit. He thought about saying something true and did not. Reza said "same time next week" and meant it, and Tavi said "yeah" and meant something slightly different, and they went in separate directions, Reza up toward the float layer where his new contract had an address, Tavi back into the street-level dark with his hand in his pocket around a piece of hardware that no longer matched anything he was doing.

Auda Vane sent two messages. That was how it happened.

The first came at 0740 on a Tuesday, a job brief addressed to CUTOUT/RAMPART — the paired handle they'd used since Nena had written it on a napkin and slid it across the counter like credentials. The brief was for a courier intercept in the Bloom Quarter, mid-complexity, Φ800 split. The second came eleven minutes later, a correction: *Disregard previous. CUTOUT booked solo. RAMPART: see attached, separate rate, separate brief.* The attached brief was for a different job, different district, same day. No explanation. The market had done the math and the math had come back clean.

They met anyway — Vane suggested it, which surprised Tavi, because Vane did not suggest things without a reason. The place was a standing-room coffee counter at the edge of the Bloom Quarter, the kind with no chairs and the Substrate coverage thin enough that the nano-warmth in his ribs ran slow, and Vane arrived exactly on time, ordered a black coffee, and told them both that they were good and that she had clients who needed specific things and that she was booking accordingly. She said it the way she said everything: with the courtesy that was also the whole sentence. Reza nodded. Tavi nodded. Vane finished her coffee, said *good luck to both of you*, and left. The whole thing took twelve minutes.

Outside, the Quarter's amber haze sat low and the eigenlift terminal was half a block north, its column of soft white light rising through the coverage gap. Reza looked at it and then at Tavi, and his face did the thing it did — the grin that arrived before the thought — except this time the grin didn't fully make it. He put his hand out, palm up. Tavi brought his up and they met it clean this time, fingers to fingers, palm to palm, the slap landing right. Reza said "same time next week" and didn't mean it, and Tavi said "yeah" and they both knew, and Reza walked north toward the terminal and stepped into the light and rose, the float layer pulling him up into the tier where his name was starting to mean something bright and loud, and Tavi watched him go until the column closed behind him.

He turned south. Skillet Row ran along the block under the old elevated freight line, second-level pilings holding up rail nobody had run a train on in years, the smell arriving before the stalls did — pork fat, ginger, the chemical thread underneath that he still couldn't name after two years of trying. Scraps had moved up in the world: a proper stall now, a real sign, a girl half her age working the second burner. She still wore the same apron, or one exactly like it. Beside her, taking a folded slip of paper with the particular stillness of people being told something they're afraid to ask her to repeat, were two kids who couldn't have been older than nineteen. The thin one looked up as Tavi passed, and their eyes held a beat longer than a stranger's should. Tavi's hand found the grip of the Ankle-Biter in his pocket, worn to the shape of his palm and no one else's, and he left it there and kept walking. He had a job in the mid-levels, a courier intercept, Φ800 all of it, and Scraps didn't look up, and neither did he.
