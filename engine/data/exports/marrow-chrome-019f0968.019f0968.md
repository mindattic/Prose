# Crimson & Chrome

_Three jobs. One plan. Rook has been a contractor to her own ending the whole time.

The first job got Soraya out of Axiom and put her research in the open. The second cracked a partition and found what was inside it: twenty-one registered Reads, hidden, alive, and now surfaced. The third job is the one the Nano Triumvirate has been waiting for — because they needed the first two done before they could move, and they needed someone they could not be seen hiring to do them.

The twenty-one people on that list can testify. They are the only ones who can. And a facility called the Marrow — built to process registered Reads into compatible neural substrate at industrial scale — is already operational.

She understood. She came back.

CRIMSON & CHROME is a story about a crew who thought they were doing three separate jobs — and what it costs to discover they were doing one._

Mrs. Chen set the bowl down in front of Soraya before she'd ordered. That was how Mrs. Chen said she'd seen her coming a block off. Steam came off the broth and fogged the old privacy glass at the edge of the booth.

Across the table Rook ate with her right hand. Her left rested on the table edge, three fingers tapping a slow pattern that wasn't nerves and wasn't music — the count she ran under everything, the way other people hummed. Her coat hung open enough to show the Reibo riding her hip. Coat and gun were both matte, cut to drink the red light off Mrs. Chen's sign instead of throwing it back. That was what you paid for when you didn't want to be seen. Out past the fogged glass a queue barrier ran its pale blue line along the curb, the only clean shape on the block.

"Don't tell me the number yet," Rook said. "Tell me the shape."

Soraya set her chopsticks down with a care that had nothing to do with the chopsticks.

"Twenty-one," she said. "Off Old Harbor. The ones the registry wrote off when the barge's manifest went dark. They're alive, Rook. Scattered. Hunted. Somebody on a registry desk finally put the number together and started selling it."

The three fingers stopped.

Twenty-one. She had carried a number like this once before. Twenty-one people walked onto a boat off Tidewell, eleven years ago, and fourteen came home. The gap had always been seven — a wound small enough to carry without letting it change how she counted for everyone else. Now the whole thing was walking back through the door, and a fourth finger came down on the table edge, uninvited.

"Yes," she said.

The word was gone before she'd decided to spend it. Four names came up behind it without her asking — Vox, Lace, Boiler, Scout. That was the habit. She'd built it after Tidewell: say yes fast, then trust the people you'd say yes with. Behind Soraya the sign bled slow red through the glass and laid a wash of it across the table.

There was a name at the bottom of Tidewell she didn't say out loud. Wennick. He'd taught her why the number mattered — that a runner who got sloppy about the count was a runner who'd already decided some of them could be lost. She'd promised herself, at a different window over this same lake, never to be sloppy again.

The three fingers started up again. She let them.

"You said yes before I told you where they are," Soraya said quietly. "Before I told you who's hunting them."

"I know." Rook picked the chopsticks back up. "Tell me anyway."

Soraya named them in the order they'd say yes. Boiler first, because Boiler always answered a wall before he answered a person.

"He'll want the building before the job," she said, and Rook nodded, because that was Boiler exactly. Four years ago that read had put her on the right side of a collapsing floor. Half a second — his hand flat on a pillar, his chin tipping toward the east stair, no words — while two men whose names she never got went down into the dust with the slab. She could still feel the floor go in her knees if she let it. The lurch, then the drop, then the silence a building makes in the quarter-second before it admits it's done. Boiler had felt it coming through his palm a breath before the floor knew. That breath was everything.

Under the booth the Pulse came through the slab as a low, even hum — the vacuum rail that ran GLMZ's spine at Mach six, slugs seating and burning. You stopped hearing it until the night you needed it gone.

"Scout's still in the Gray Zone," Soraya went on, "walking it on foot like the maps owe her money. She's got Gerald."

Rook almost smiled. Gerald was a salvaged six-legged crawler the crew had named two jobs back. Scout had never used the name and never corrected them, and the name did quiet work — a thing called Gerald didn't read as a weapon on a checkpoint scan. It read as scrap nobody had thrown out yet. Scout didn't trust any way in she couldn't reach on her own two feet. She'd turned down clean Pulse berths to walk six hours through the gray between zones, because — her words — "the train tells you where the doors are. The walk tells you where they put the ones they don't mention."

"Lace will come because Lace wants to see who breaks first," Soraya said. "And Vox —"

"Vox will say no, then show up two hours late with coffee and pretend she was always coming." Rook set the chopsticks down.

On Tidewell, Lace had called which of the hired hands would fold under pressure three days before the pressure came, and she'd been right about every one.

And Vox would grumble down the line that she was retired, that she had a life now, that Rook had some nerve — and then the glass would fog and she'd be standing in it with two cups going cold in her hands and a look that dared anyone to say she'd come. Four years gone, and Rook could already taste the coffee. Always terrible. Vox always brought it anyway.

"That's five." She breathed out slow. "Five I can carry."

She let a moment go by before she said the rest, because the rest had weight.

"Stave won't answer his line. He left it on the table the last time — the whole rig, the comm, everything. Said he was done being the person people called when it got bad." She'd been the bad, once. She was the call he'd meant. "But Stave's the only one who can get us into the registry vault clean, and he knows it, and he knows I know it. So somebody's going to have to knock on a door that's been shut on purpose for four years."

Soraya had been quiet through all of it — not the quiet of a woman done with her list, but the quiet of one watching the list turn into a weight and deciding whether to say so. She set her spoon down.

"I asked you first," she said. "Before I asked any of them. I wanted to know if you would come."

Rook looked at her. Soraya's face was the face of a woman who'd done her own counting for four years and been very careful about which column things went in.

"You already knew I'd come."

"I knew you'd say yes." She picked the spoon up, turned it over, set it down. The motion had nothing to do with the soup. "That's not the same thing. I needed to know if you'd come because you wanted to — or because this is the kind of debt you can't leave unpaid." She looked up, level, the scientist's eyes that never gave you the kind reading when the exact one was there. "Because if it's the debt, I brought you a job. And if it's the other thing, I brought you the one I should have brought you four years ago — when I had the count and didn't trust it enough to say out loud."

Steam rose between them, cleared, rose again.

"I should have told you," Soraya said, and the words came out smaller than anything else she'd said. "About the twenty-one. When I first had the count. Before that registry desk started selling it. I had it, and I held it, and I filed it under protecting them — a thing kept safe from any ledger that might hear it."

Rook held still. She didn't fill it in.

"You were protecting yourself," she said.

Soraya's jaw set. She didn't deny it.

The three fingers stopped against the table edge.

"Yes," Soraya said. A breath. "And I'm asking you to come anyway. Both things are true and I'm asking anyway, because you're the only one I trust to carry more than they were rated for."

The Pulse came up through the slab again, steadier, a slug settling into its berth somewhere down the line. Rook reached for the slate.

"Then we're both asking for something we should have said earlier," she said. She wrote Scout's address into the slate, then held the booth door open with her shoulder while Soraya pulled her coat on. Outside Mrs. Chen's the night was the same as it always was — cold, indifferent, lit from below, the Thread on the southern horizon dropping its pods against the dark. They stood in it a moment before they split off toward doors that had been shut on purpose.

Three days after the knock, Stave came through the door.

He didn't look like a man who'd made a decision. He looked like a man the decision had finished with. His coat was wrong for the season — too heavy, sleeves shoved back — and he didn't scan the room when he came in, which meant he'd already had that fight with himself on the walk over. He found the booth. Found Rook in it. Stood there half a second with the manifest in both hands.

He set it on the table folded to the wrong page — the page no one at the registry was meant to read in order, the one that only made sense if you turned it around and read it backward against itself. He put it down and stepped back from it, the way you set down a thing you don't want to be caught holding.

Soraya sat at the far end of the booth. She hadn't looked up.

"Three registry seals to get that," Stave said. He sat, not because he wanted to, but because the debt needed a table. "Burned a key I'm never getting back on the second one. This clears the gray-market balance from the NRST crack. We're square."

"We're square when I can count them," Rook said.

Stave didn't answer. He looked at the window instead.

Rook spread the manifest flat and read it the way she'd learned to read anything official — past the parts built to be believed. Past the letterhead. Past the Voluntary Relocation header and the stamped department seals. She looked for the weight underneath. Twenty-one names. One barge out of Old Harbor, departure logged to the minute, arrival never entered. And the names didn't scatter to twenty-one places the way relocated people are supposed to. They funneled to one.

On the public rolls, these twenty-one had never carried a delivery mark — clean white space where forty other names squared against a receipt, the kind of gap a tired clerk learns to read past. Here, on the page nobody was meant to read in sequence, the gap had a word. Down the column, stamped beside every one of the twenty-one where the delivery mark should have been: *processed.*

Below that, smaller, was a routing code — a chain-of-custody tag, the kind a corp stamps on freight it expects to be asked about later.

She'd seen it before. Four years ago, burned into the side of a tank while the tank was trying to kill her. She'd read it then as a serial number and carried it out of Axiom without meaning to. Now it sat beside the word *processed*, beside twenty-one names that had walked onto a boat, and the two facts found each other in the dark and clicked.

Not a transfer order. A bill of lading. Freight gets a routing code, a count, a destination. People get a story. Somebody had worked out that you could pay for the one and file it as the other.

The code was Helix's — the same stamp the corpo ran through every link of its supply chain, from buying to shipping to intake, on anything it planned to audit. Axiom had been running transfers for Helix four years back. And the Marrow — Helix's body-bank, the facility no registry would admit existed — was where people went when Helix stopped calling them people.

"The Reclamation ran the barge," Soraya said, and let it sit a moment, the way she always did with the bad ones. "Registered Reads, all of them — neuretics-capable people the corps log and want, the ones any facility would pay to have alive and cooperative. They walked aboard at Old Harbor under their own names, in daylight, because the order said sunrise. Said sky. Said a place the processors can't send you to and back from." She turned the page the right way then, slow, smoothing the fold flat with two fingers, gentle, the way you'd draw a sheet up over a face. "One of them came back. She's the reason I have the count at all."

The woman came in behind the steam.

Mrs. Chen set a bowl down for her without being asked — at the empty place at the end of the booth, with the small ease of a hand that had done it many nights running. That, more than anything Soraya could have said, told Rook how long this woman had been folded into the back of this room, fed and waiting. Her left hand didn't fully close. At the wrist the chrome and the flesh argued, a seam where something had been done and then undone, gone in and come back out wrong. She carried the hand like a thing that used to be hers.

She set a paper photograph on the table, face-up.

Twenty-one people on a barge deck, squinting into a morning.

Rook looked at it and didn't pick it up. They stood the way people stand when they've been told to gather for a picture and half-believe it's good news — shoulders not yet braced, one of them caught mid-laugh, a man at the rail with his hand up against the low sun, a kid turned the wrong way to watch the water. Morning light coming in flat and clean off the lake. They didn't know yet. You could see in every face that they didn't know yet.

Her hand moved toward the edge of the photograph and stopped, palm flat on the table, a centimeter short of the gloss.

"Sefi Okonkwo-Reyes," the woman said. "There were twenty-one of us when we walked on. I can give you nine." She didn't look at the photo. She'd had four years not to look at it. "The other twelve I watched go through a door. I can tell you the door. I can tell you it locked behind them. And I can tell you nobody on that registry desk wrote down the sound it made — because the sound wasn't on the manifest, and if it's not on the manifest, it didn't happen."

She slid the photo across the table and left it there.

"You've carried a count like this before," she said. "Fourteen out of twenty-one, off Tidewell. Soraya told me there's a name at the bottom of it you don't say out loud. So don't sit there and tell me the number's too big. Tell me when we leave."

The booth went quiet. In that quiet Rook looked at Soraya — not to ask anything, just to look, the way you look at the person who was standing next to you when the floor went. Soraya was looking at the photograph, at the morning light on twenty-one faces that didn't know yet, her hands very still in her lap. She felt Rook's eyes and didn't look up, and that was its own answer. She'd known for four years, and she was sorry it had taken this long to put the count in a room where something could be done about it.

Rook picked up the photograph. She turned it face-down.

"Get me the quarter's full relocation rolls," she said. "All forty names on it besides these twenty-one. I want to know who isn't on either list."

"The buyer was Helix," Rook said. "That job got Soraya out clean for them — proof the harvest could be pulled off a live person. The offer felt too easy. It came through three brokers and cleared every checkpoint like someone had walked the route ahead of us and left the doors open. I called it luck. They lost the survivor list in the move — the way corponations lose what they don't want anyone auditing. Then Soraya's partition cracks open four years later and the count surfaces, and the same registry desk that misfiled it starts selling it back at a markup. And the competitor running the public Class V trials — they weren't competing with anyone. They were mapping. Finding out who had it, before anyone else did, and dressing the search up as science." Her voice went flat, the voice she kept for bad news in front of a crew. "We didn't stumble onto an audit trail. We are the audit trail."

The three fingers had stopped somewhere in the middle of that and she'd let them. She looked at Sefi's left hand, the seam where the chrome and the flesh still argued. Then she said the older thing.

"Not Axiom. Tidewell." Flat, the same voice, a number set on the table and left there. "Seven years before Soraya. Twenty-one names on a contract that called itself a rescue. Paid by the head. I ran the count. Cashed it, called it a good outcome, went home." A breath. Across the booth Sefi laid the chrome hand flat on the laminate — no sound, just the placement of it, something acknowledged. Her jaw worked once. "The seven weren't the cost," Rook said. "They were the product."

The booth held the line after she said it. The broth over Soraya's bowl was still steaming faintly, going the way food goes when nobody touches it. At the edge of Rook's sight, Vox hadn't moved since she sat down.

She'd believed the word on the contract because the word was a door and she'd needed a door out of something and had gone through it with her hands full. There wasn't a method for that part. She moved her right hand off the table into her lap and closed it around nothing and held it there.

Across the table Soraya had gone very still. She wasn't looking at Rook. She was looking at the routing code, the way you look at a sentence that changes the meaning of the whole page, and keeping her breathing even on purpose, which was the only tell she ever gave.

"They needed me willing," Soraya said, in the data-reader's voice, precise, the one she used when the number was worse than she'd modeled. "I believed it because I wanted to. Someone handed me a door that opened the right direction, and I didn't check whose hand was on the latch." She looked up. Her eyes found Rook's and stayed. "You opened that door. And I walked through it, and told myself I was lucky."

Sefi didn't flinch. She'd had four years and a door that locked behind twelve people to teach her how not to.

"Then you know the work better than they think you do," she said. "When do we leave?"

The question held the room a second. Rook knew what it was. She did the same thing herself.

She reached for the chopsticks and ate.

The door had been shut three days, and Sven Goldberg opened it himself — which Rook noticed before she noticed his face. A man who'd spent four years routing other people through doors had come to this one and put his own hand on it. The booth's privacy seal was rated to open from a slate, from a desk, from anywhere but the threshold itself.

She let the door close behind him before she let him speak. That was the pause — the dam swallowing the booth's noise back down to the low red nothing it kept for paying tables, the broth going from steam to grease as it cooled. He didn't sit. He set a partition slate on the table beside Sefi's photograph, squared its long edge to the table's the way a clerk squares a stack, and left it there. "I'm the desk that misfiled the survivor list," he said. "I want that on the table before the food gets cold. I didn't lose it. I was paid to lose it. Helix routed the QUANTA through a relocation contingency line, and I signed it because the line existed and the signature was already half-printed. A missing list isn't a body. That's what a missing list is, when you're the one filing it."

He turned the slate so the column faced Rook — the same *processed* she'd been reading sideways for three days. "There were twenty-one on that barge, and the manifest I cleared listed forty names across the quarter. Forty I audited. I never read a forty-first." He moved one finger down the column and stopped it in the white space past the words. "I built the count clean enough that nobody above me had to know there was a count at all. That's why it surfaces now as something they can sell instead of something somebody can prove. So whatever you think you're owed off me — I owe more, and I know the exact figure, which is more than they ever wanted me to."

She didn't answer.

She let it sit there the way he'd set the slate down — squared, waiting. There's a thing people do when a man confesses clean: they reach for him, they make the room warm again, because a cold room with a confession in it is hard to sit in. She'd watched fixers do it her whole life and never once seen the reach cost the man anything. So she let the room stay cold. He stood there with what he knew and no one to take it off his hands. The broth ticked as it cooled. Somewhere past the dam a slug hit the Pulse tube and the building took it in its molars and let it go.

He had the look of a man who'd already counted his own figure and picked the next thing before he crossed the threshold. She'd done the same three days ago with Soraya, fast enough that no one saw. That was useful. A man already past his own denial has nothing left to protect but the work.

So she made her choice in the cold, where it would hold. Not to warm him, not to send him back out the door he'd opened himself — but to spend him, the way he'd come here to be spent, on the one thing his four years of clean counting had left unpriced.

"Then we're not allies," she said. "We're two people who finally read the same book the same direction. You bring what PEREGRINE saw" — Helix's private muscle, ex-military chassis with the serial numbers scrubbed off — "and I bring the seven I left behind and the fourteen who'll move for me. Stave opens the vault, if I can knock soft enough. First thing you do for me: find me the forty-first name. The one you never read. Somebody on that barge was never on your manifest, and that's the head nobody got paid for, and that's the one they'll have left somewhere they think nobody's still counting."

He gave a single nod, the clerk taking the assignment. And Rook did the thing she hadn't planned to. She lifted her hand off the photograph — felt the gloss let go of her fingertips, Sefi's face still turned down under where her palm had been — and set it on the cold glass of his slate, beside his, the column of *processed* running up between them, and stopped a centimeter short. Close enough to feel the warmth come off his fingers. Not close enough to touch. She left it there a moment, then took it back, because a centimeter was the exact amount of warmth the room could carry and still do the work.

Sefi turned her left hand over on the table, palm up, and the chrome at her wrist caught the red light and held it — a seam where flesh stopped being flesh, put there by a Splicer whose name wasn't on any manifest either, on a date nobody logged. She didn't show it like a wound. She showed it like a fact she'd checked twice and wanted entered into the record before the next thing she said could be argued with.

"You want to know what a door that locks is for," she said. "I'll tell you the room. They walk you down a corridor that smells like nothing — that's the first wrong thing, a place that processes bodies and has no smell, because the air's been scrubbed to spec. The walls are that white that isn't a color. It's an absence somebody paid for. And there's a hum. Not the floor. Lower. You feel it in your back teeth before you hear it, and you think it's the machines, and it is — but it's also us. The ones already in the racks, the Reads they pulled and kept warm. Class V doesn't run on dead tissue. It runs on tissue that still half-remembers it was a person."

She paused, and her tongue moved against the inside of her teeth, like she was checking whether the hum had ever fully left them.

"And then you hear the doors," she said. "All down the corridor, the same sound, exactly the same, like one door learned to be every door. A soft seat and a click. No air in it. The good doors in GLMZ wheeze — you know the sound a thing makes when it's been opened ten thousand times and nobody's oiled the second hinge. These don't. These got maintained. You stand in that white and count the clicks and understand that in this whole stack of us, the one thing they kept running right is the part that closes."

She closed the chrome hand. The metal argued with the flesh and the flesh lost, the way it had lost on the barge. "They don't call it killing. There's no word in the manifest for it. They call it intake, and conditioning, and substrate yield — every one with a QUANTA figure that drops the longer you sit in the rack. We're stock with a shelf life. A registered Read costs them more fresh than spoiled, so they tune the throughput to keep us useful, which is a worse word than dead. I watched twelve go through that door, and I want you to understand: they didn't die in there. They got priced."

Rook didn't answer.

Her jaw had set somewhere in the middle of it, and she made herself unset it, because a locked jaw was a thing a crew read and she wasn't going to hand them that yet. She'd walked in here to plan a crew and a vault and a cost. What Sefi was describing had gotten into the room instead — not the sound of it, the shape of it — and her right hand had gone flat on the table without her telling it to.

Across the table Goldberg hadn't moved. His clerk's hands stayed flat and still on the slate, and he looked at them like a man reading a figure he'd checked a hundred times and still couldn't make come out clean. Whatever it cost him it cost him in the neck and the shoulders, not the hands; the hands he kept still on purpose, the way he'd kept everything still for four years.

Soraya hadn't spoken since Sefi began. She was looking at the bare laminate — not the manifest, not the photograph — the thing you look at when a model you built to stay theoretical stops being theoretical. Her breathing was even. She was keeping it even on purpose.

The red light held on Sefi's chrome and on the dead photograph, and the booth went quiet, nobody willing to be first to lift the thing sitting in it.

"Helix didn't build a vault," Rook said, and her voice came out level, because level was the only thing holding. "They built the one place in GLMZ where the bill comes due from the inside — where the asset pays it down by falling apart. Everything else in this city they let rot. This they maintained. They tuned the doors to close right so nothing they could still sell got out early. And they used me to prove the racks could be stocked with live people." She let that stand. "So when we knock soft on that vault, we're not cracking a bank. We're walking into the only room in GLMZ they kept the lights on for. That's the part I owe."

Goldberg cleared Sefi's column and brought up a different one, and Rook watched him do it the way she'd watch a clerk she didn't trust yet — the economy of it, no wasted motion, a man who'd squared too many stacks to fumble the one that mattered. Vox was where she'd been the whole sit, at the edge of the booth where the red light went thin against the partition, turned three-quarters away with her shoulder to the table and her good ear toward the door. She hadn't said a word in an hour.

The Marrow ran sealed. Its own feeds, its own air, its headcount kept inside and out of reach from the street. You couldn't cut its power from outside. You couldn't blind the perimeter crew. You couldn't bleed off the outer ring and leave the center deaf. Stave's substation had been the same idea at a tenth the scale, and the answer there had been Stave himself. This was that problem, grown.

"Before you spend the fourteen," Goldberg said, "you spend this."

A face came up on the partition. Vox's face, rendered in Cinderblock gray, the flat resolution of an asset that had been bought once and filed away. The live woman three feet from it didn't turn to look. She only lowered her chin a degree, the way you tilt your head off a thing you can already feel on the side of your neck.

"Stave paid for this in the same currency you all pay each other in — a face, traded for a vault crack he hadn't done yet. He logged it. The trade exists. And Helix doesn't pull threads, Rook. Helix pulls the one thread the whole thing hangs from, then bills you for the unraveling." He moved his finger to the corner, where a routing code blinked. "This is the thread. They ID Vox, they follow the trade back to Stave, and Stave is standing in the only door you've got."

Rook let the slate be. The face on the wall and the woman at the edge of the booth were the same person, and Helix would only ever come for one of them, and it wouldn't be the one with a pulse. The air handler held its one low note. Vox shifted her weight off one foot and didn't turn, and the small sound of it — boot leather on the grated floor — was louder in Rook's ear than the code blinking on the wall.

That was the trouble with Helix. It built the straightest run it could from the asset to the person who'd handled it, then sent a clerk down that run to settle up. Vox's face, filed. The trade, logged. The thread, blinking. Stave, in the door. A clean straight line — and a clean straight line is the one thing you can always see coming.

"Then the way in isn't the way in," she said, and her voice came out flat and held. "It's a way Vox doesn't surface until after. And a way Stave's standing somewhere that isn't the door when they walk the trade back. I build two cracks. The soft knock, and the one that opens if the soft knock costs me Stave. I don't put a person on a number I can't carry twice."

At the edge of the frame Vox let a breath go through her nose, slow.

Then she turned around. Not all the way. Half-facing the table, the wall with her own face on it still over her left shoulder, not looking at it, the way you stand in a room with a mirror you don't want to check. She had two cups of coffee in her hands. She'd gone and got them while Goldberg was pulling up the slate, and nobody had noticed. She set one in front of Rook. The knock of the cup on the table was the first sound she'd made since she sat down.

"It's bad," she said, about the coffee, meaning the coffee, not meeting anyone's eyes.

Rook picked it up. It was bad. Vox always brought it anyway.

"Here's the thing nobody wanted me to know either," Rook said, and made herself say it to Vox, not to the wall. "The fourteen who'll move for me — they move because I count right. The day I count wrong, I'm the desk that misfiled them, and I'll have done it for the same reason you did, Goldberg. Because the line existed." She didn't reach for the wall. "So tell me what it costs to make sure the thread they pull comes up in my hand instead of theirs. Because it's coming up in somebody's, and I'd rather it be the one already holding the count."

Vox looked at her own face on the wall — one glance, flat and quick, a woman checking a balance she already knew. Then at Rook.

"You're going to need someone on the east core," she said. "Not to run it. To be loud on it, if the soft knock turns hard." She picked up her own coffee. "I know what loud looks like. I've spent my whole career not doing it."

Rook didn't say you don't have to. Vox didn't need telling.

"Then you're on the east core," Rook said. "Not in the door. Behind it, until I need the loud."

Vox nodded once, looked out the fogged glass at the neon bleeding through, and drank the bad coffee. Rook drank hers too.

Goldberg's hand stilled over the slate. He didn't clear Vox's face. He looked across the table at Rook, and something in the line of his shoulders gave — the discipline he wore like a clean cuff, loosening a notch at the wrist.

"You said find you the forty-first name like I'd have to dig for it," he said. "I don't. I've had it since the day I logged out of Helix the way you log out of a thing you're stealing from — quiet, and with more than you came in for." He pushed his sleeve back and let the seam at his own wrist show — chrome and flesh, the same unlogged date as Sefi's, except his was worn smooth at the edge by a thumb that had run it a thousand times in the dark. A coin he'd kept in case he ever had to spend it. He didn't turn the hand over. He just let the cuff sit back off it. "I didn't only walk out with my body. I walked out with the substrate ledger. Names, intake codes, rack assignments. The live stock and the deferred kind. I've kept it dark four years, because the day I opened it I'd stop being a man with a useful past and become a man with a debt he couldn't pay twice."

He found the seat of the junction with the meat over his collarbone and held, and the load came onto him all at once. Not a number. A pressure. The whole downward pull of the manifold finding the one place in the city that had agreed to stand under it. Twenty-one. He'd held the boiler seam for Scout; he could hold this. The first surge went through his left arm like a cable pulled past its rating, and he heard the sound his own joint made — not a crack, lower than that, a wet give, the exact note a fitting makes the instant before it stops being a fitting. His body did what bodies do: it told him in one white flood to set the weight down and step off the line. He kept lifting. He counted instead of listening to it. He'd spent a life trusting what load told him, and this once he made himself the wall that lies — the wall that carries what it was never rated for and says nothing — and he held the give in his arm shut by main force, the way you hold a door. Somewhere four levels up the red light hitched, dimmed, and the draw on the working floor went slack. The count Gerald was holding reached the rooms it was meant to reach, and the rooms held.

Goldberg's crossing finished on the bay-C feed, which Rook had left open on purpose so the building would log its own witness. He was three levels up at the compliance junction, still in the gray Helix hadn't thought to take off him yet, his credential doing the last piece of work it had left in it. Scout saw him find the count in the frame — his eyes going to where she stood with twenty-one and not twenty in her arms — and hold there. On the feed it was the smallest motion a body could make and still mean something: a slow half-turn of the head, against the pull of the loud channel that had run this floor for four years. Helix's voice was still coming through every speaker in the building, still asking for the count back. Goldberg didn't answer it. He turned to the panel in front of him and finished rerouting the alert his own hand had built the door for. That was the whole of the crossing — not a rescue, not a speech, just a man who'd spent four years deferring people finally putting his weight on the other side of it, before anyone could take back the credential that let him.

Helix did not go dark. Its voice came back up the open channel thinned, the clerk-softness gone ragged at the edge where the audit had failed under it — "Manifold's down. We have a structural casualty in the sub-basement and an audit that is now, regrettably, public" — and that was the corp's own speakers saying public into every ear on every level, the building putting on record the thing it had spent thirty years built not to say. Boiler heard it from under the dead junction with his left arm wrong. Not numb — he knew numb, and this wasn't it. This was the arm reporting in from a long way off, the way a far span reports the wind. His cheer had burned out of him, and twenty-one beds were out of reach behind a door he'd just shouldered shut from the wrong side. He didn't whistle. He counted, once, under his breath, careful, the way you read a gauge you don't trust your own eyes on — to make sure the number that had cost him the arm was the number it was supposed to be. It was. Twenty-one, not twenty. The only clean thing in the room.

Lace came through the sub-basement door thirty seconds later at a dead walk, not running, because running down a ramp with a casualty is how you become two casualties. She'd swept the secondary staging block on her way down — ten more racks nobody's manifest had ever carried — and left them stable on Stave's open line before she doubled back for Boiler. She took one look at the arm and didn't say anything. She crouched, got her shoulder under his good side, and pulled him up. He let her. The calibration wheel was still in his right hand, shut in his fist, and she didn't ask him to open it. She walked him up toward the light without a word.

The Waterline sat where Cinderblock's tier met the Gray Zone seam, a long counter and a long window, and the window gave you the lake the way only the cheap places did — nobody on this tier paid for a view of water that had been here longer than their lease. Rook took the stool at the far end, where the glass met the wall, set the roll of receipt paper on the counter beside a coffee she wouldn't finish, and looked at the lake while it did the thing it always did, which was nothing. The window gave her back to herself over the water — olive and tired, dark hair come loose from the tie, the pale eyes, the scar high on her right cheekbone she'd carried longer than any name she was about to write. For once she didn't read the face for what it gave away. She let it sit there over the lake, a person and not an operator.

She unrolled the paper. She had a pen, the analog kind, the kind that didn't log to anything and couldn't be edited after the fact. She wrote *Palesa* first, and kept going down the rows — not the rows that priced, the rows that had come out — bay C, one bed at a time, most of them still just a face and a rack number until somebody could sit with them and ask.

She wrote until the names ran out at twenty-one, and then she counted them, once, the way Boiler had counted under the dead junction to be sure the arm had bought the right number. Twenty-one, whole. Every bed in that room accounted for on the page.

Below the twenty-one, on its own line, she wrote *Goldberg* — not counted, she told herself, and wrote it anyway. He wasn't one who'd come out of a rack; he was the one who'd opened the door, and that debt went on a different page, one she'd settle with him herself. Under that she wrote *the woman at forty with the clean cabling — name pending, get the name*, and set it apart too: the landing, two levels up, not bay C, the one Goldberg's own forty-name manifest had never carried and nobody had billed. She left a gap after that, room for the ten who'd come out of the secondary block and whose names she didn't have yet, and below the gap she wrote *32 confirmed*. Then she didn't close the roll. She left it open on the counter, facing the window.

Outside, the lake held the color of old concrete — the gray of a city that had been raining for weeks and was thinking about stopping but hadn't decided. She'd grown up without water this size. The first time she'd seen the lake she'd been twenty-two, running a courier job through Z11, and she'd come out of a drainage corridor at the wrong exit and found herself at the lakeshore with the water running out farther than she could measure. She'd looked at it for about four seconds. Then she'd gone back into the corridor and finished the job, because she didn't have time for horizons.

That had been before the counting. Before the Rook who had a method, and a crew, and a gun with a registered name, and a stool at the end of a counter where the water was there without her having to stand at the edge of it.

The Reibo sat in the holster, chrome that swallowed the dawn light and gave nothing back. She put her hand flat on the counter — the heel of it still faintly pink where the tablet glass had opened it going down into the Marrow, left to close on its own instead of under a synth-patch, because some lines she'd decided were worth keeping. She wasn't studying it. She was just sitting.

The stool beside her had been empty since she sat down. It stopped being empty when Soraya took it — no announcement, no asking, the quiet arrival of a woman who'd spent four years not saying the thing out loud and had nothing left to announce. She had her own coffee, also bad. She set it on the counter and looked at the lake too. The roll of paper sat between them, twenty-one names in analog ink above the gap, *32 confirmed* at the bottom, the kind you couldn't edit after the fact.

Soraya read the list the way she read data — all the way, from the top, every entry. When she reached *the woman at forty with the clean cabling — name pending, get the name*, she stopped on it a moment, and her finger stopped there on the paper, and then she kept reading. She finished the list and set her finger at the bottom, below the last name, the way you point to where a signature should go.

"I know her name," she said. "The woman at forty. She was in the Axiom lab three floors below mine. Emeka. She was teaching herself Mandarin from a program she'd borrowed off a colleague, and she kept the earpiece in one ear the whole shift, even on breaks." She lifted her finger off the paper. "She had good recall. The corps would have noticed that on the intake scan."

Rook picked up the pen. She crossed out *name pending, get the name* and wrote *Emeka*, and under it, smaller, *Mandarin, one earpiece*. She wasn't sure why she wrote the second part. It seemed like the right kind of fact to have on the paper. The kind that didn't price.

"There was one more," Soraya said. "In the secondary block. The one Lace found — the ten. I knew one of them from the lab. A kid named Bram, early twenties. He'd been an apprentice surveyor before the intake. Mapping elevation data for a dam project in Z8." She was looking at the lake, not the paper. "He was good at estimating distances from reference points. Better than the engineers who supervised him."

"What block?"

"Secondary substrate staging. They moved the lab residuals into that block when the main facility filled." She picked up her coffee. "He was lucky. Or unlucky. Depending on when you start counting."

"We start counting when someone's on the list," Rook said.

"Yes." Soraya drank her coffee, bad as it was. "I know."

Rook wrote *Bram — apprentice surveyor, Z8 dam project, secondary block* in the gap after the twenty-one. The gap was filling.

They sat with the lake a while. The coffee went cold between them at the same rate.

"It came out whole," Soraya said. She wasn't asking.

"Thirty-two," Rook said. "More than it came in with."

Soraya almost smiled at that. The almost was there, at the corner of her mouth, for three seconds before the lake took it back.

"I need to tell you something," Soraya said. She set her coffee down and turned to face Rook directly, which was unusual for her at this kind of table. "The Marrow wasn't the only one. The program ran at three facilities — the Marrow was the one closest to the delivery cycle. The other two are further along." She paused. "I didn't tell you before the run because I needed you focused on the twenty-one, and because the other two aren't problems this table solves tonight." She held Rook's eyes steady. "But I'm telling you now, because you should hear it from me and not from a registry desk."

Rook looked at the list. Twenty-one names and the gap, filling. *32 confirmed.*

She set the pen down without writing anything else.

"It won't be tonight," she said. "And it won't be this list. Whatever's running at the other two gets its own count, its own crew, its own morning — not this one." She looked at Soraya, then at the lake, and didn't reach for the roll again. "The GLMZ doesn't stop being what it is because one building came down. I've known that since the bridge in Z5. But the number I'm keeping tonight is thirty-two, and I'm not letting a number I haven't earned yet crowd the one I have."

Outside, the morning was the same morning it always was over the lake — gray and cold and paying no attention. A barge was crossing in the middle distance, slow and heavy, the kind that carried substrate without caring what it carried or where it went when the transit was done. She watched it a moment, and then she didn't, because there was a list on the counter that was actually hers to finish, and she turned back to it instead.

Soraya watched the barge a moment longer. Then she reached over and rolled the paper closed herself — gently, the way she'd smoothed the original manifest, except now the smoothing wasn't covering anything. The paper said what it meant, all the way to the last name, and she was done adding to it tonight.

"Thirty-two," Rook said again, quieter, the way you say a thing you mean to keep saying.

They sat with the lake a while longer, and didn't talk about the other two facilities again that morning, and the coffee finished going cold, and the lake did the thing it always did, which was nothing, and for once that was enough.
