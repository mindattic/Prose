# A Restless Mind

_The fixer he has known only as a voice through a cracked terminal never comes in person. Tonight she stands in the rain at the noodle stall, hood up, and says his name. Kyle says yes to a shielded room before he knows whose room it is — and spends the walk home deciding how much the yes is going to cost._

[Beat 1]
The noodles were getting cold. Kyle was watching a woman pretend not to watch him.

[Beat 2]
Not loud. Not for Mrs. Chen. For him, across six feet of rain. *"Kyle."* Just the name — in the same steady, modulated voice he had been getting contracts from through a cracked terminal for two months, and had never once heard attached to a body.

[Beat 3]
He set the chopsticks down across the bowl. He did not stand. He did not move his hands away from the counter — both of them already living where the draws began. He let her see him not move and let the not-moving be the answer to the question she had not asked yet. *Nobody stands still in the rain unless the rain doesn't bother them or the job requires it.*

[Beat 4]
Eleven minutes. Same foot position. The rain came down at the angle the atmospheric processors preferred — steep, efficient, redirected from somewhere that mattered — and it came off her hood in a clean sheet that said the coat was quality, the kind of quality that didn't announce itself. No chrome. No augmentation profile his implant could resolve. His Neuretics kept reaching for a signal and returning empty.

[Beat 5]
Behind him, Mrs. Chen stirred the broth with a long-handled spoon that had lost its lacquer in three places and been re-gripped with electrical tape, the same spoon she had used for the four years he had been eating at this stall, and she said one word into the steam. *Húlijīng.* Low. Mandarin. Not for him. She said it the way a woman who has seen things names them — with the flat certainty of a diagnosis, not a warning. Kyle set his chopsticks down across the bowl. He had heard Mrs. Chen mutter at the rain, at the Circuit boys who walked too close to her signage, at the atmospheric processors when they made the broth steam wrong. He had never heard her name a person.

[Beat 6]
She tilted her head — a fraction, a direction, nothing more.

*If she wanted me dead, the angle from the doorway is already open. She is here because the room she lives in is not safe.*

Kyle's chair scraped back. He set the chip on the counter and said it the way he said it every night, low and level: *Standard rate.* Mrs. Chen's hand covered the chip without looking down, which was how she always took money from him when she thought he was about to do something she didn't approve of and had decided not to say so.

[Beat 7]
He followed the woman into the rain. He fell in six feet behind her — far enough to read her, close enough that she knew he had chosen to come. Silence's weight shifted against his back with each step on the wet ground, a small correction Seo had drilled into him until it stopped requiring effort. She had stood in the rain eleven minutes waiting for him specifically, and she moved like the route belonged to her. A contract or a problem. In his experience, usually both.

[Beat 8]
The mochi cart first. Closed, a chain through the handles, the vendor's stool folded against the wheel. He tracked her coat. The way the hem swung. The fact that she had not once adjusted her stride for the puddles, which meant she knew the route or had walked it enough times that her feet had learned it separately from her attention. *Preparation. Weeks, maybe.*

[Beat 9]
*She has never come in person before. The shape of an ambush.*

[Beat 10]
The recycler vent exhaled hot industrial breath across the walkway — paper-dry and chemical-sweet — and she walked through it without breaking pace. Kyle held his breath for two steps on instinct. Old facility reflex. The security drone hung at the third-block junction, its sensor array cycling through its standard sweep in the standard interval. It did not flag them. She had timed the approach or she had known the interval, and either answer said the same thing about how long she had been thinking about this particular walk. He filed that. His Neuretics ran hot in the rain, the lattice's temperature regulation working harder than it should have been, a low warmth behind his left ear that he had learned to read the way a pilot reads engine noise — not yet a problem, a thing to monitor.

[Beat 11]
The relay station resolved out of the dark at the end of the block. Wartime construction: poured concrete, no ornamentation, a deactivated antenna mast running up into the rain — dead a decade, the city's records said. *It is a relay station. It is empty. There is nothing here worth stopping for.* The kind of building that became invisible through its own explanation.

Halfway up the dead mast, a single status light was burning amber. Steady. Patient. He blinked the rain out of his eyes and it was just wet metal and old paint. He did not mention it to the woman. She did not look up.

She stopped at the door. She did not produce a key. She did not unlock anything. She turned, with the same composed efficiency she had walked the route with, and looked at him from inside the hood.

[Beat 12]
The woman stepped under the awning. The hood came back an inch — not all the way, just enough that the streetlight caught the shape of a face and the dark of red hair beneath the rain hood, and Kyle catalogued *attractive* the way the array catalogues anything: as a fact registered without commentary. Tall. Late thirties. Bone structure that had cost her nothing because she had been born with it. He had not seen her face before tonight and he was not entirely sure he was supposed to be seeing it now.

[Beat 13]
"Tomorrow," she said. "This address, this time, the room behind this door. Shielded. Bring nothing electronic you can leave behind. Your implant comes with you because your implant cannot be detached, but anything else you carry stays in your apartment. The conversation we are going to have cannot happen in a room that can hear it."

[Beat 14]
"Whose room is it?" he asked.

"Mine." A beat. "That used to be a comfort."

The rain worked the awning while he absorbed the grammar of that — a fixer who could afford a Faraday cage, admitting the cage was the only place she still trusted.

"You've run forty-one contracts through me," she said. "Eleven this quarter. How many of them do you think came from clients I could sit across a table from?"

He didn't answer. It had never been his question to ask. That was the whole arrangement: she vetted the clients, he never needed to.

"I used to know," she said. "That's the conversation."

[Beat 15]
He read the wording. *Shielded* did most of the work. The kind of shielding she meant was the kind that cost money she did not casually spend. The hum behind his sternum — the low electromagnetic signature that had lived in the implant's background like a second heartbeat for as long as he'd had the implant — was suddenly relevant in a way it had not been in years. Not louder. Just no longer ignorable. Some part of him had already started asking what, exactly, a room like that was built to keep out.

[Beat 16]
"Why me?" he said. Not the question he wanted to ask, but the question the discipline asked for him. She let it stand in the rain.

[Beat 17]
"Because you are the one this concerns," she said. "The rest tomorrow. In the room. I cannot say more here."

[Beat 18]
He held her gaze through the hood for another beat. He could leave. He could turn around and ride home and pretend the noodle stall had been a noodle stall. He could pretend three blocks east-northeast had been a walk. He could pretend that *Because you are the one this concerns* had not been the sentence it had been, the one that had been sitting in his chest all evening. He could pretend the shielded room would empty without him in it.

[Beat 19]
He was not going to pretend any of those things. The discipline was the discipline. It showed up.

[Beat 20]
"Tomorrow," he said.

[Beat 21]
The hood inclined. Not a nod. An acknowledgment. Then she stepped past him into the dark of the block without unlocking the building behind her. The building was where Kyle was going tomorrow, and Kyle was going to go.

[Beat 22]
She was gone in three steps. The Gray Zone's bad rain swallowed the tan coat the way the Gray Zone swallowed most things at this hour.

[Beat 23]
Kyle stood at the door for a count of ten. The brass-gasketed lock he could see through the window beside the threshold was the tell he had assumed it would be. He did not test the door. He turned. He walked back the way they had come — east-northeast in reverse, through the recycler vent's hot industrial breath and past the third-block security drone on its standard sweep and past the awnings that had not stopped dripping in the eleven minutes the walk had taken in the other direction and the further eleven it would take him in this one. Somewhere up in the ductwork between two buildings an elevator cable thrummed against the wall it ran behind, a long steel note rising and cutting off as the car reached its floor. He did not run the route past the array. He let it sit. He walked it.

[Beat 24]
The bike was where he had left it, two blocks south of the noodle stall, the plate still the one he had ridden down on, the cover still beaded with rain. He started it and rode the long way home, because the long way was where Kyle did his thinking, and tonight had given him something to think about. He did not know who she was. He knew he had said yes. He knew tomorrow was a shielded room. The rest would have to be tomorrow's problem.

[Beat 25]
The bike's headlight crossed the alley wall at 22:14 and Kyle killed the engine before he turned in, coasting the last forty feet on momentum because the engine cutting at the corner had been Seo's lesson and Kyle had not yet found a reason not to do everything Seo had taught him to do. The rain had softened to mist by the time he got to West Town. The towers leaned in the way they did this far up; the canyon between The Pivot and the residential block across from it was just wide enough for a single VTOL to thread through if a VTOL had any business being this far down, which they didn't. The space above the alley was dark. The light came up from the windows, not down from the sky. The last stretch before the building ran long again — it always did — and he let it go.

[Beat 26]
*Sable.*

The match landed before he had time to refuse it — voice plus face. Two months of contracts through a cracked terminal three blocks east. The fixer who paid on time, never haggled, and never once handed him work that smelled wrong, which in the Glooms made her rarer than honest scales. Nobody on the boards had ever seen her face. He had just spent eleven minutes failing not to look at it.

Whatever was waiting in that shielded room tomorrow, it had been worth breaking the habit that had kept her invisible for years. People broke habits like that for exactly two reasons: because something was worth more than the habit, or because something had already cost them more than the habit could cover.

[Beat 27]
Through the walk he had had no idea who she was. A sense of the way she moved, the way she had timed the drone interval, the way the coat had not soaked through. Her voice from twelve seconds at the noodle stall — too few words to identify anyone he had ever spoken to before. The hood was the kind of hood that did not come off accidentally, and he had filed it without prying at it. Now the file had a name on it, and the name changed the weight of everything in it.

[Beat 28]
He kicked the stand down. He locked the wheel hub. He covered the plate. He walked the bike's stillness into the elevator and the elevator into the second floor and the second floor into the hallway. The stairwell wall carried the cable's thrum up beside him the whole climb, the building's pulse running parallel to his own, and the light switch on the landing was worn to bare brass and still warm from the last hand that had thumbed it. His step hit the landing across from 2F at the angle he had not announced and Pixel's door opened anyway.

[Beat 29]
She was in a t-shirt over leggings, no shoes, the hallway's bad amber light catching the rim of two cups. The steam rose off both, something herbal and lemon-bitter he had long ago stopped trying to find in Pixel's tea cabinet. He took the cup she held out. The handle was the side without the chip; she had given him the better cup, which was her version of saying something she did not intend to say aloud.

[Beat 30]
They stood in their doorways across the hall and drank. The hallway smelled faintly of Pixel's apartment behind her — solder flux and bergamot, proportions that should not have worked and somehow did. The cup in Kyle's hand was too hot to drink fast, which suited him fine.

[Beat 31]
She sipped her tea. He sipped his. The mist outside was settling on the alley wall the way mist settles when the air below is colder than the air above. Kyle had stopped checking the forecast around the same week he had stopped trying to keep up with Pixel's tea cabinet. *Hm.* He drank. Something in him eased at the first sip. Something else went watchful at the second.

[Beat 32]
He laughed. Quietly. One short exhalation through the nose, the kind that would not have been a laugh in any room but this hallway. The evening had been: agreeing to walk into a shielded room for a fixer who had never had a face until tonight, then walking home to find a cup of tea waiting in his neighbor's doorway. The contrast was funny. Nobody else needed to know why.

Pixel did not ask what was funny. Pixel never asked what was funny. The not-asking was the punchline.

He did not tell her about the woman in the hood, or the shielded room he had said yes to, or how easily he had said yes.

[Beat 33]
"Bird's still out," she said. She meant the upstairs neighbor's bird, a recurring data point in Pixel's running commentary on the building — a bird Kyle had never actually heard but understood to be a presence in her life the way the rain was a presence in the city.

"Sang twice this evening. New piece. I think it learned from the radio."

*Mm.* He turned the cup.

"Yeah?" The word came back from somewhere. He meant it.

[Beat 34]
She watched him over the rim of her cup. Not with the focus she put on a fragment in his shoulder; with the looser focus she used for the larger question of whether he was, on a given night, all right. He was. She read it, and her shoulders settled by a quarter inch.

"Tomorrow's going to be its own day," he said, eventually.

[Beat 35]
He finished the tea. He handed her back the cup, handle-first, the way she handed him his when the convention ran the other direction. She took it. She bowed, a small bow, the bow of a friend acknowledging the closing of a transaction that had no exchange in it. He bowed back, smaller. That was the shape of the convention they had built over four years across this hallway: she did not ask, he did not tell, and it worked.

[Beat 36]
"Goodnight, Pixel."

[Beat 37]
"Goodnight, samurai."

[Beat 38]
She closed her door. He unlocked his. He sat on the edge of the bed in the dark, the tea still warm in his chest, and listened for the bird he had never heard. The building was quiet.

Three blocks east, a shielded room waited for the morning — a room built so that no machine in the city could hear what got said in it, owned by a woman who had stopped trusting her own client list. He decided he would walk the block at first light and find out whether the antenna was still pretending to be dead.

If this was a trap, it was the most patient trap anyone had ever set for him. That was either flattering or very bad. He slept on which.
