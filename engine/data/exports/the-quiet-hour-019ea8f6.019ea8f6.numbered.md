# The Quiet Hour

_A tutorial-grade corporate-tower extraction shows the freelancer network at its most professional — code names, compartmentalized knowledge, no questions — and then shows it break. Kyle and a small crew lift data off an air-gapped node; the operator codenamed X does it not by jacking in but by reading the on-site Technician's mind and replacing the encounter with a pleasant fiction. The job is too clean. A cleanup contractor, War Dog, is hired to silence X the moment the data is out. Kyle ends up between them. The lesson Kyle files away: a stranger's rate means nothing if the referral is compromised — trust is the only currency, and he is starting to keep his to one fixed handler._

[Beat 1]
The meet was a corner, not an address, which was the first thing Kyle liked about the job. Halsted and a cross-street whose sign had been pried off years ago for the scrap value of the letters, a noodle vendor folding his cart for the night, the smell of fryer oil cooling into the wet. Nobody who worked clean ever gave you a door. A door was a thing that could be watched, recorded, served with paper. A corner was just weather and the people standing in it, and if it went wrong you could be three different streets away before anyone agreed on what you'd looked like.

Stash got there first, because Stash was always first, a wide flat man leaning against a shuttered pharmacy with two cases at his feet that he would not let anyone else carry. He ran logistics for half the freelancers Kyle respected and most of the ones he didn't, and the rule with Stash was that you did not ask what was in the cases until he opened them and you did not ask where they'd been before.

Echo came out of the dark on the other side, hood up, a thin person with a voice like a radio tuned a half-step off the station — recon and comms, the one who'd be in everyone's head once the neuretics synced. No earpieces. Earpieces were a tell, a thing a guard could see and a scanner could find. Everyone on a clean crew ran the channel behind their own eyes, grown into the skull young, and Echo was the one who'd be holding the whole conversation together from somewhere none of them would ever stand.

Three of them, and the client wanted four.

[Beat 2]
The fourth came up the block the way ordinary people came up blocks, which was to say Kyle didn't clock her until she was already inside the loose triangle the three of them made, and that bothered him more than if she'd dropped out of a window. She was medium. Medium height, a coat the color of a coat, hair pulled back the way a person pulls it back when they've stopped thinking about it. The kind of face that a room would round down to nothing. You could share an elevator with her and a week later swear you'd ridden up alone.

"Null," she said, which was the codename and the whole of the introduction, and she said it the way you'd give a barista a name you didn't expect to hear called.

Kyle filed her. Quiet, polite, forgettable on purpose, which was a skill and not an accident, and skills that took that much work to look like nothing were usually pointed at something. He had no idea what she did. The brief hadn't said. On a clean crew the brief never said more than your own piece — Echo knew the building, Stash knew the gear, Kyle knew that his piece was to stand between everyone else and whatever came through a door — and the fact that he didn't know Null's piece meant her piece was the one the client cared about.

"You're the muscle," she said to him. Not a question. She had a read of her own. Then, tilting her head the way someone does when they've already decided to like the answer: "What do I call you?"

Kyle opened his mouth. He got one syllable in — maybe less, the K of his name, a principle of Bushido that he'd never fully stopped applying to strangers who asked — and Echo's voice came into the channel, flat: *No real names.*

He closed his mouth. He looked around. The corner. The rain. The shuttered pharmacy with Stash's weight still denting the doorway. And down near the base of the step, where the gutter had deposited it from somewhere else, a glass soda bottle with a faded orange label.

"Soda Pop," he said.

Null snorted. It was a real snort, unguarded, the first thing she'd done that wasn't careful, and it did something to her ordinary face he hadn't expected. "That won't do."

Kyle picked up the bottle and threw it against the pharmacy wall.

The glass went everywhere. Sharp. Sudden. The small violence of it carrying a weight out of proportion to the sound it made.

"Broken Glass," he said.

She looked at the pieces on the wet concrete. She looked at him. Something moved in her face, there and gone — amusement, maybe, or a reappraisal, or both — and when it settled the almost-smile it left behind meant something different than the earlier ones.

"I can work with that," she said.

He went back to watching the street, and the corner was just weather and the people standing in it, and he did not examine why it mattered to him whether she could.

[Beat 3]
Echo ran the brief once, in the channel, so it lived in four skulls and nowhere a court could find it. A tower in the prestige blocks, close enough to the Loop that the rent was a sentence you said to frighten children. Vant/Meadowlark Trust, a holding company that did nothing you could name and owned things you couldn't afford to be near, the sort of corponation whose business was being the wall other businesses leaned their secrets against. On the thirty-first floor they kept a node that touched no network. Air-gapped — a machine with its hands cut off from the world, deaf to the Pulse, deaf to the Network, a vault you could only open by standing in the room with it. Whatever lived on it was worth more than a thing you could simply steal across a wire, because a thing you could steal across a wire was a thing someone could steal back.

"So we walk in," Stash said, "and we walk out, and the trick is the walking."

"The trick is the elevator," Echo said. "Lobby's contractor-staffed. Arcturus."

Arcturus Civil Security. Private badges who answered to whoever signed the building's services contract and to nobody who lived in the city, which made them honest in the only way that mattered — they would do exactly what they were paid to do and not one motion more. You could not bribe a cop, Kyle thought, because the city had stopped keeping them. You could read an Arcturus shift roster like a tide table.

"And the node," Kyle said. "Who opens the node?"

"Null handles the node," Echo said. "Every door between the lobby and thirty-one, that's hers too." And that was all the channel offered, and Null stood there and let it be all.

## A Waterfall and No Chairs

[Beat 4]
The lobby had a waterfall and no chairs. That was the whole philosophy of the place in one room — a sheet of water two stories tall sliding down black stone for no reason a person could sit and enjoy, because sitting was for people with somewhere they belonged and this lobby was a thing you moved through on your way to being approved. The floor was the kind of pale stone that showed every footprint and was mopped often enough that it never did. Somewhere money was being made by the ton and none of it had ever once been happy in here.

They came in as a delivery. Stash had the cases and the work order, a real one, bought off a real subcontractor who would swear under oath he'd dispatched it because as far as his records knew he had. Kyle had a vendor's jacket and the particular dead patience of a man who carried other men's furniture for a living. Echo did not come in at all; Echo was a parked van and a voice in the back of the skull. Null came in three minutes behind them as a woman who'd gotten off at the wrong floor of the building next door and was very sorry, and the Arcturus guard at the desk waved her toward the directory with the bored grace of a man who had decided forty seconds ago that she was no one.

"Badges," the guard said, not looking up, and Stash laid the work order on the black glass and the guard touched it and the building decided they were furniture.

[Beat 5]
The freight elevator smelled of other people's moving days. They rode it to twenty-two because twenty-two was as high as the work order would carry them, and Kyle watched the floor numbers climb and thought about how the most dangerous part of any job was the part where nothing was happening, where you were a man in a box being lifted toward a thing, and all your training was useless because the correct action was to keep being furniture.

"Camera in the corner's live," Echo said behind his eyes. "Don't look at it. You looked at it."

"I looked at the floor I was looking at the floor that the camera is in," Kyle said, without moving his mouth, which was a thing the neuretics let you do and a thing it had taken him a year to stop feeling insane doing.

Stash hummed, tunelessly, a man waiting for an elevator the way furniture-haulers wait for elevators. Null stood in the corner the camera could see and was, somehow, less interesting than the wall behind her. Kyle had been in a lot of small rooms with a lot of dangerous people and the dangerous ones usually filled the room. Null emptied it. The longer he stood next to her the less sure he was she was there, and he made himself stop testing it, because whatever that was, it was the client's piece and not his.

The doors opened on twenty-two. Nine floors to climb on foot, which was six more than the brief had promised, because the work order had a freight permit that died at twenty-two and the thirty-first was its own world with its own elevator bank that asked questions a work order couldn't answer, and the stairs were the seam between. Every building had a seam. You went up through the part nobody had budgeted to watch, and tonight that part was longer than anybody had wanted it to be.

[Beat 6]
The stairwell was poured concrete and emergency-green light and the particular silence of a place built to be used once, in a fire, by people who would never come. Their footsteps went up ahead of them and came back down changed. Stash carried the cases like they weighed nothing and his breathing said otherwise; he was not a young man and these were not young man's stairs, and nine floors was a long apology for a permit that had quit early.

"You always this quiet on the way up?" Stash asked, to nobody, which meant Kyle.

"Talking's for the way down," Kyle said. "On the way down it means we made it."

They made it as far as the twenty-fourth landing before the seam stopped being empty. The fire door above them opened on its slow hydraulic sigh and two Arcturus Civil Security guards came through it shoulder to shoulder, doing the floor sweep that the brief had said happened on the half-hour and that Kyle's watch said was four minutes early, and being four minutes early was the kind of small dishonesty that got people killed.

He was already moving when the first one's hand went toward his belt. Kyle kept Silence sheathed, because Silence was a conversation you could not take back and these two were just men paid to walk stairs. He took the first guard's wrist away from the belt and put Cacophony's butt into the soft place under the jaw, once, controlled, the revolver staying a club because a gunshot in a concrete tube would carry to thirty-one and end the quiet hour before it began. The man folded. The second one got his mouth open and Kyle was already inside the distance, a forearm across the throat, a sweep, the back of the head meeting his palm instead of the floor because a cracked skull was paperwork and a stunned man was just a man who woke up confused.

It took six seconds. When it was over both guards were down and breathing and Kyle's pulse was exactly where it had been.

He turned to tell Null they had a camera problem and found that they didn't.

She was at the corner of the landing where the stairwell camera sat in its little smoked-glass blister. Her hand was against the casing — not on the lens, not on the bracket, on the flat face of the unit where the processing board sat behind the plastic. Her eyes had gone somewhere else. Not listening. Reading. The expression was the expression of a person parsing text in a language they'd spoken since birth: effortless, fast, the understanding ahead of the symbol. It lasted the length of a breath. Then her eyes came back, and she took her hand off the housing, and she said, "Loop's running. Go."

Kyle looked at the camera, which told him nothing, and at her hands, which were empty. No tablet. No cracking dongle. No dummy plate, no spoofer. His NeoCortex flagged the absence: no RF signature, no magnetic bloom, nothing the building's electromagnetic baseline would read as a tool running in this room. He catalogued it under *expensive* and stepped over the guards.

"You're wondering what I do," Null said, falling in three steps below him, climbing without a sound.

"I'm wondering what floor we're on," Kyle said. It was both true and a door closing.

"You could ask," she said. "Either thing."

"People who know what they're doing tell you before you need to ask," he said, and kept climbing, and did not ask, and the stairwell climbed with him.

[Beat 7]
The checkpoint was waiting on the landing between twenty-six and twenty-seven, where the architecture changed its mind and a half-height security door cut the stairwell in two. A card reader glowed beside it in patient amber. One Arcturus guard stood the post, and he was better than the two below — he saw them clear the lower flight, and his hand went to the radio at his shoulder instead of the weapon at his hip, which was the smart move and the one that put a clock on everything.

He got one word out. "Stairwell—" was as far as he got before Kyle reached him, because Kyle had stopped being a furniture-hauler at the bottom of the run and become the other thing, and the other thing covered a flight of stairs faster than a sentence. Cacophony's butt, the jaw, the fold. The radio swung loose on its coil and said nothing else.

But one word was a word. Somewhere a board had a light on it now that hadn't been on a second ago, and a failed check-in had a response time, and Kyle's read of this building's response time was about sixty seconds before somebody with worse intentions came to see why the man on the stairs had started a thought he didn't finish.

"Sixty seconds," Kyle said.

Null was already down on one knee at the reader. Not at the keypad — beside it, her fingers spread against the steel housing where the unit bolted to the wall, a wider stance than the camera had needed. Her jaw tightened once and smoothed — the only sign that this machine was fighting back, that its resistance was real even if her face was saying otherwise.

"How long?" Kyle asked.

"Thirty seconds," Null said.

He wanted to ask how. Thirty seconds to brute a corporate card reader was a number that didn't exist for the kit he knew; the best cracking suites he'd watched work needed a tap into the data line and a minute of polite lying to the lock before it believed them, and she had her hand on the chassis like she was taking its temperature, nowhere near the line, nowhere near the keys. He let the question sit next to the looped camera and the empty hands from the stairwell, a small growing folder of things he'd ask about on the way down, if the way down happened.

Twenty-eight seconds later the lock cycled. A green tongue of light, a soft mechanical concession, the door drifting off its seat.

She'd touched the frame, not the keys. He'd watched her do it and he could not have said what she did. He opened his mouth, decided it was a technique thing — every specialist had a tell that looked like nothing, a way of standing or breathing that the work hid inside — and closed it again. Specialists earned the not-asking. That was the deal.

"You didn't have to hit him that hard," Null said, stepping over the guard, glancing once at the man's slack jaw.

"I had sixty seconds," Kyle said, and held the door for her.

[Beat 8]
The stairwell ran out at thirty. The last flight gave onto a service vestibule and a bank of elevators that did not go anywhere a work order could follow, because the thirty-first floor answered to its own keyed cars and the panel beside them wanted a credential the brief had no way to forge. It was the only way up that wasn't a rope and a prayer. Nine floors of climbing had bought them a console and one more thing Null would have to talk a machine into.

"Four minutes," Null said, reading the panel the way Kyle read a room.

"I'll take the door," Kyle said, and put his back to the stairwell entrance, because four minutes was a long time to stand in a vestibule on the thirtieth floor of a building that had started missing check-ins, and somebody had to be the wall.

He watched the stairwell. That was the job. But the vestibule was small and the angles were bad and overwatch meant keeping the whole space in his eye, and the whole space included her, and so he watched her too.

She stood at the console with one hand on it — not on the display but below it, against the lower housing where the actual circuitry lived, her fingers not spread but pressed close, the posture of someone listening through bone rather than ear. Her eyes were forward. Aimed at the seam of the closed elevator doors with the patience of someone who had sent something ahead of themselves and was waiting for it to arrive. She did not type. In four minutes she did not type once. Her hand stayed where it was and her eyes stayed where they were and the only thing that moved was the soft unfocused tide that came and went behind her face — the same one from the camera, the same one from the reader — a thing he kept seeing and had not yet let himself line up in a row.

He watched it for longer than overwatch needed. Long enough that the watching became its own thing, a question his eyes were asking that his mouth hadn't agreed to.

"You're staring," Null said, without turning around.

"You're not looking at the controls," Kyle said.

A pause — not a guilty one, not a caught one, just a small considered quiet, the quiet of someone deciding how much of a true thing to hand over and when.

"I'll tell you after," she said. The same words she'd given him on the stairs. They landed differently here, in a sealed vestibule with her hand on a machine she wasn't touching, with nine floors of unconscious men behind them and one floor of quiet ahead. On the stairs it had been a deflection. Here it sounded like a promise he wasn't sure he wanted kept.

Something behind the doors gave way. A chime, soft and expensive. The keyed car answered a call it had no record of receiving, and the doors slid back on a mirrored box and a single button for a single floor, and Null took her hand off the housing and stepped inside like she'd ordered it from a menu.

Kyle followed. The doors closed. The number above them went from thirty to thirty-one, and started climbing the rest of the way alone.

[Beat 9]
The thirty-first floor door was the first real lock of the night and Stash opened it in the time it took Kyle to decide whether he liked the man, which was less time than it should have taken either of them. Stash didn't carry tools so much as he carried the right tool, already chosen, because the cases held a thing for every door in the brief and nothing for any door that wasn't, and that was the whole of his religion — you did not improvise on a job, you packed for it.

Beyond the door the prestige floor was carpet that swallowed sound and lighting that came from nowhere and the kind of quiet you paid for. No people. It was the quiet hour, the dead stretch after the last meeting and before the night cleaning crew, the one window Echo had found in three weeks of watching when the floor held exactly one human heartbeat — the Technician, in the room with the node, doing the maintenance that a machine cut off from the world still needed from a man who could touch it.

"He's in there," Echo said. "Has been for forty minutes. Logs say he runs the diagnostics, he gets a coffee from the floor galley, he comes back. The coffee run is your window. Six minutes, give or take his bladder."

"And the node?" Kyle asked. "Six minutes isn't a node. Six minutes is barely a door."

There was a small silence in the channel, the kind that meant somebody had decided not to answer a question.

"Null has the node," Echo said. "You have the hall."

## The Quiet Hour

[Beat 10]
Kyle took the hall. It was what he was for. He set himself at the bend where the corridor turned toward the elevator bank, a place from which he could see anyone arriving and be seen by no one until he chose it, and he let his hand rest where it rested and did not draw, because drawing was the second-to-last thing and he was a long way from there. Silence stayed sheathed against his back, a plain blade with a plain handle, nothing on it that caught light, a tool that asked nothing of a room until it was asked of. Cacophony rode at his hip, five rounds and no more, a revolver for a man who had decided that if he ever needed a sixth shot the night had already gone wrong in a way that more bullets would not fix.

Down the hall, the galley light clicked on. The Technician, on schedule, going for his coffee.

And Null did not go to the node.

Kyle watched her, because watching was the job, and what he watched did not make sense. She did not move toward the room with the air-gapped machine in it. She drifted toward the galley, toward the man, the way water drifts toward the lowest place in a floor, no hurry in it, and by the time the Technician turned with his cup in his hand she was simply there, a woman who'd gotten off at the wrong floor and was very sorry, and she said something Kyle couldn't hear and the man laughed.

[Beat 11]
It looked like nothing. That was the thing Kyle would keep coming back to. It looked like a man and a woman in a galley at the end of a long shift, the oldest scene in any building, the cup of coffee going cold in his hand while he forgot he was holding it. She stood close but not too close. She tilted her head when he talked. She laughed before he was quite done with the joke, the way you laugh when you've already decided to like someone. The Technician leaned a hip against the counter and got taller in the way men get taller when a woman is paying attention, and Kyle, watching, felt the specific discomfort of seeing something work that he didn't understand the mechanism of.

"Six minutes is up," Kyle said in the channel. "She's not at the node."

"She's at the node," Echo said.

"She's drinking his coffee."

"Hold the hall, Kyle." Echo's half-step-off voice had no give in it. "Hold the hall and don't watch."

But he watched. He had a clear lane and an empty corridor and nothing to do with his eyes, and so he watched the Technician's face, because faces were a thing he could read, and the Technician's face was doing something Kyle had no name for. The man's eyes had gone soft at the edges. Unfocused, the way eyes go in the half-second before sleep, except he was standing and talking and laughing, present and absent at once. And there was a smile starting at the corner of his mouth that had nothing to do with the joke X had just told. A small, private, pleased smile, the smile of a man remembering a good night that had not yet happened.

[Beat 12]
It went on for nine minutes. Kyle counted them, because counting was a thing to do with the cold thing growing in his chest. Nine minutes of a conversation he couldn't hear and a man getting happier and a woman who was, somehow, still less interesting than the wall, and then Null straightened off the counter and touched the Technician's forearm once, briefly, the way you touch someone you're sorry to leave, and walked away down the hall toward Kyle.

The Technician stayed where he was. He looked at the space where she'd been with that private smile, and then he looked at the cold coffee in his hand like it had appeared there, and a small confusion crossed his face and lost. He shrugged it off the way a man shrugs off a thought he can't catch, turned, and went back into the room with the node to finish a shift that had, as far as he would ever know, contained nothing but coffee and the long quiet of a floor at night.

Null reached the bend in the hall where Kyle stood. She had not been near the node. She had not touched a keyboard. She had not, that Kyle had seen, done one single thing.

"We can go," she said.

"You didn't get it," Kyle said.

"I got all of it." She was already moving toward the stairwell, that whole-footed silent walk, and over her shoulder, mild as weather: "He's a very nice man. He wanted to be helpful."

[Beat 13]
They went down the way they'd come up, and Kyle was quiet, but it wasn't the way-up quiet anymore. He was doing arithmetic he didn't like the sum of. A node cut off from every wire in the world, openable only by a man standing in the room with it. A man who knew the passphrases, the maintenance schedule, the things you'd need to know to take what was on it. A woman who walked up to that man and made him happy and walked away with everything.

"You read him," Kyle said. He kept his voice flat. It came out flat anyway. "You're a Read."

"That's an ugly word for it, the other one," Null said. The stairwell light made her face green and ordinary. "Psyko. People say it like it's the spitting that matters and the word's just the runway." She didn't slow down. "Yes. I read him."

"You took the passphrases out of his head."

"I took everything I needed out of his head. Passphrases. The node layout. The fact that he writes the rotation down somewhere he shouldn't because he can never remember it." She said it the way Stash talked about the cases — flat inventory, a professional describing a clean job. "He didn't fight me. They almost never fight, the nice ones. They've spent their whole lives being helpful. You just have to be a thing it feels good to be helpful to."

Kyle thought about the soft eyes and the private smile. "And after. The smile."

"That's the part that matters," Null said. "Anyone can take. The trick is putting something back."

[Beat 14]
"Put what back," Kyle said.

"A nicer night than the one he had." She took the stairs at the same unhurried pace, like they were discussing the weather, because to her they were. "He won't remember a woman pulling things out of his head, because that isn't what happened to him. What happened to him is he met somebody — his brain fills in the where, I just give it the shape — and she was funny, and she liked him, and he told her a few things he probably shouldn't have because she was easy to talk to. And he went home feeling thirty years old again. That's what's in there now where I was."

The cold thing in Kyle's chest had a shape now and he didn't care for it.

"He gave you the keys to his employer's vault," Kyle said, "and he's going to spend the next week feeling good about it."

"He's going to spend the next week wondering if he should've gotten her number." Null reached the landing and turned to look up at him, mild, that almost-smile, and for the first time Kyle understood that the forgettable face was the most honest thing about her — because what she did left no mark, and a person who leaves no mark should look like no one. "I don't hurt them. That's not a kindness I'm claiming. It's just cleaner. A man who feels robbed makes calls. A man who had a nice night makes coffee."

[Beat 15]
The extraction point was the loading dock two levels under the lobby, where the building breathed in its deliveries and breathed out its trash and nobody looked at either, and Stash was already there with the cases closed and a panel van backed up to the bay. It had gone clean. That was the problem. Kyle had been on enough jobs to know the texture of a clean one and this had too much of it, the way a wound that doesn't hurt is the one you worry about. No alarm. No guard who got curious. No node that fought back, because the node had never known it was being robbed — it had simply been told the right words by a man who had every right to say them.

"Six minutes of work and a coffee," Stash said, reading Kyle's face, because Stash read faces too, it was half his job. "What's eating you?"

"Nothing's eating me. That's what's eating me."

Echo's voice came into the channel and there was a new thing in it, a tightness, the sound of someone watching a number change that shouldn't be changing. "Hold. I've got a vehicle inbound on the dock ramp. Wasn't there ten seconds ago. No transponder, no work order, it's just — it's just on the ramp."

"Ours?" Stash asked.

"Nobody's ours," Echo said. "Everybody's here."

[Beat 16]
The thing that came up the ramp had been a man once and had spent a great deal of money disagreeing with the fact. Kyle's read was instant and complete, the way it always was when it mattered, the part of him that catalogued a threat before the rest of him had finished being afraid. Four arms. Three of them ended in shotguns — not arms holding shotguns, arms that were shotguns, the barrels grown into the wrist where a hand used to make decisions, short and wide-bored and built for a hallway. The fourth arm was wrong in a way the other three weren't. It ended in a launcher tube, a stubby rack of cells where a rocket battery should sit, and the rack was dark. Empty. A dead arm on a body that had sold everything else it owned to be deadly.

He wore his money like scar tissue. Whoever fronted the augmentation had not paid for the part that made it look like anything but what it was, and so he came up the ramp as a wide low silhouette of barrels and intent, unhurried, a man who had done this enough times to know that the running started later.

Kyle put the dock's geometry in his head in the second it took the shape to clear the top of the ramp: concrete pillar to his left, the steel stairwell behind and right, Stash and the cases between him and the bay door, Null at his shoulder.

"War Dog," he said, by way of telling them his name was none of their business. The codename, the whole introduction, the way you'd give a barista a name you didn't expect to hear called. "Only here for one of you."

He looked past Kyle, past Stash, past the cases, to the woman in the coat, who had gone very still.

"The Read," War Dog said. "Client wants the head that holds the file. Rest of you can walk."

[Beat 17]
And there it was, the math finishing itself. The job had been clean because the job had never been the data — the data was the bait, the thing that made a Read worth hiring, and a Read who'd just pulled a corponation vault out of a man's skull was a Read who now carried that vault behind her eyes where no firewall could reach it and no contract could compel her to forget. Whoever had hired them had hired a key and a lock and a door, and then hired a second contractor to break the key the moment the door was open. The smile she'd left in the Technician's head had a sibling. Somewhere a client was already filing tonight under *done*, and War Dog's fee was the same whether he killed her in this dock or three streets from here — paid for the attempt, the way cleanup always was.

Kyle did not draw. He moved one step, an ordinary step, the kind of step a man takes to ease a stiff knee, and it put him — *almost* — between the barrels and the coat.

The lead arm was already moving. Not at Kyle. Past him. War Dog had done this before, had read the exact step and fired through it before it could close, and the blast went past Kyle's shoulder at the range of a handshake and found what it had always been aimed at.

Null went down without a sound.

There was a half-second — smaller than a breath — in which the name he'd smashed a bottle to earn meant something entirely different than it had at a corner in the rain.

Kyle heard the concrete. He heard Stash say something in the channel that was a single clipped syllable and then nothing, and then the channel had three voices where there had been four. He stood between the empty barrels and the coat that was now on the floor — the way she went down, the specific way she had been there and then was somewhere else, and the sound the blast had made, and the fact that the lead arm was already cycling to chamber another round and had three others behind it and War Dog was looking at him now with the patient professional eyes of a man who had already completed the job he was paid for and was taking stock of what remained.

"She's done," War Dog said. It was not cruel. It was simply an accounting.

A stillness came into Kyle that had nothing to do with calm. He drew Silence.

## Three Barrels

[Beat 18]
The first shotgun spoke and the loading dock answered in dust. Kyle was not where the dust was. He had read the lead arm's settle a quarter-second before the wrist committed — the way the shoulder loaded, the way a man with three barrels still aims with his eyes — and he'd gone low and wide, off the line, toward the concrete pillar that held up the level above and would hold up against buckshot, which a freelancer would not. The blast took a bite out of the bay door behind where he'd been and the noise of it was a flat hand against both ears.

The NeoCortex was running. He was aware of it running the way you're aware of a machine in a room you live in — steady, in the background, doing its arithmetic. He was also aware of the other thing underneath the arithmetic, the thing that was not cold or steady and had no word for itself, only a direction. He let the NeoCortex handle the math. He let the other thing handle the rest.

Three barrels was a math problem. One barrel you closed distance on between shots. Two you played the gap. Three meant there was no gap — War Dog could walk a wall of fire across a room and never stop to reckon with an empty chamber, and Cacophony held five rounds against that and Kyle was not going to win this by spending them. He didn't draw the revolver. He kept Silence in hand, because Silence asked for distance closed and War Dog's whole architecture was built to deny it, and the only way through a wall was to be already past it before the wall knew you'd moved.

[Beat 19]
He used the dock. That was the only way to use it. The bay was a stacked geometry of crates and the lift platform and the steel stairwell that climbed the wall to the loading office, and Kyle put the crates between himself and the barrels and made War Dog spend his fire on plywood, working the angles the way you work a building — not running from the guns, running the room around them, so that every shot the man took was a shot at where Kyle had been and every reload, every half-second the three arms cycled their breeches in sequence, was a step closer.

War Dog turned with him. He was not slow. The augmentation had bought him a torso that rotated on a ring, the upper body coming around independent of the legs, and Kyle felt the lead arm track him through a stack of cartons and threw himself off the angle a breath before the boards became air.

"Pillar's chewed," Echo said, fast, in his ear. "You can't trade. He's got a belt-feed on the left arm, he doesn't run dry—"

"I know what he doesn't do," Kyle said, and went up the stairwell.

It was the wrong direction. That was the point. A man fighting three shotguns ran for the exit; Kyle ran for the high steel, two flights up the bolted stair, because the stair was a corridor and a corridor was a thing that made three barrels into one — War Dog could only bring so many arms to bear up a narrow flight, and the geometry that had been Kyle's enemy on the open floor became, on the stairs, the only thing keeping him alive.

[Beat 20]
War Dog came up after her, after the file, after the thing the client had paid for, and that was his mistake and Kyle had built it for him. The loading office at the top of the stair was a glass box, a supervisor's perch with a long window that looked down over the whole bay, and Kyle let himself be seen going into it because a man who'd run himself into a glass dead-end was a man you took at your leisure. War Dog came through the door sideways, the only way the wide architecture fit, the lead arm first — and that was the half-second, the one barrel committed and the other two still turning the corner, the wall made into a doorway, the doorway into a single point.

Kyle did not cut the arm. The arm was the strong part. He stepped inside the swing of it, past the muzzle, into the place where a shotgun is just a heavy stick, and Silence found the seam where the augmentation met what was left of the man — the cabling at the shoulder ring, the soft junction no plate covered because no engineer expected anything to get that close. The blade went through hydraulic line and signal bundle and the lead arm went dead mid-rotation, dropped, hung. One barrel down. Not a killing cut. A disarming one, in the oldest sense of the word.

War Dog screamed, and it was a man's scream, and Kyle filed that too — there was still enough man in there to feel a thing.

[Beat 21]
The other two arms came around then, both of them, the belt-fed left and the third, and at that range in that box they could not miss and Kyle could not be there to be missed. He went the only direction left, which was down — dropped under the swing, took the floor on his shoulder, and the double blast went through the glass wall above him and turned the supervisor's window into a falling sea of it, a thousand bright edges coming down across both of them. War Dog flinched from the glass the way anything with a face flinches, and Kyle came up out of the roll already moving, Cacophony in his hand now because the distance had closed to the kind where five rounds was plenty, and he put two into the left arm's belt-feed, into the cycling breech where the mechanism was open and stupid for the third of a second it took to chamber.

The arm jammed. It jammed loud, a wet metal cough.

The dead launcher arm swung up — reflex or desperation, something the augmentation had learned before the rack went empty — and found nothing to fire. The rack had been empty when he came up the ramp. It dropped.

Now War Dog had one working gun and a spent launcher and a severed lead and a glass box full of a man who had spent the whole fight refusing to stand still long enough to be killed. He did the math. Kyle watched him do it. The torso ring rotated, the one good barrel coming around, and behind the man's eyes a calculation finished that had nothing to do with the barrel — the calculation of a professional who'd been paid for the attempt and was not, it turned out, being paid enough to die in a glass box over a woman he could find again on a slower night.

War Dog threw himself out the window.

Not the glass wall, which was already gone — the outer window, the one that looked off the back of the building over the alley three stories down, and he went through it backward and shoulder-first with the casual violence of a man who had done it before and budgeted for the landing. There was a sound like a side of meat hitting a loading ramp, because that was, more or less, what it was. Kyle made the window in three steps and looked down in time to see the wide low shape gather itself off the alley concrete, one arm dragging, moving wrong and moving anyway, folding itself into the dark between two dumpsters with the particular ugly competence of a thing that had been built past the point where falling killed it.

He'd live. The augmentation that made him a slow target on open ground was the same augmentation that turned three stories into a bruise, and Kyle had known that going in — had known the window was an exit and not a kill, had chosen the disarming cut over the other one because the killing cut had other costs, costs Kyle hadn't finished deciding he was ready to pay. He let it go. Cacophony had two rounds left and an alley was a bad place to spend them on a thing that was already leaving.

It was not a win. War Dog would file a report. *Attempt made. Target acquired. Fee invoiced.* Somewhere a client would read it and mark it closed, and the night that Null had spent nine minutes making a man feel like thirty again would be the last night she'd made anyone feel anything. Kyle stood in the window with the rain coming in and the alley going dark below and the glass from a supervisor's box in his hair and let that sit where it sat.

## Who Vouches for You

[Beat 22]
Stash had pulled the van back from the bay and was standing in the gap between the rear bumper and the dock's concrete wall, and the expression on his face needed no translation. He'd laid his coat over her. The shape under it was the specific small shape of a thing that had already left, and the coat's hem was dark in a way that wouldn't wash out.

Kyle stopped. He put Silence away. He stood for a moment that lasted exactly as long as a moment could last before it became an excuse.

"She was already —" Stash started.

"I know," Kyle said.

The bay door hung open to the wet night. Somewhere below, in the alley, War Dog was moving through the dark — damaged, operational, a professional closing the distance to somewhere his wounds could be addressed. The job was complete from his perspective. The client had purchased a result and received it, and the rest was just weather and a filing fee.

Kyle looked at the coat and the shape under it. She'd given him a name and he'd smashed a bottle to earn it, and she'd said it worked for her, and on the stairs down she had told him what she did after, exactly as promised, and the nine minutes in the galley were in his skull now with different edges than they'd had before. He did not examine the logic of it. It was done and the coat was on the floor.

"We need to move," Stash said. "Echo's already clear."

"I know," Kyle said again. He didn't move yet. He owed her at least that much — one beat, unhurried, standing in it before the professional machinery of *after* took over and she became a thing that had happened on a job.

Then he got in the van.

[Beat 23]
Stash drove and said nothing.

In the back of the van, under Stash's coat, Null was whatever was left after you'd been paid to close a loop.

She'd said she'd tell him after. She had. He was still thinking about it.

He watched the wet streets unspool.
