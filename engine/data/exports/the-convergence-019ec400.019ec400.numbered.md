# The Convergence

_Ensemble, Isaac POV (midpoint). The four adjudicate; the readings converge -- she chose this, offline by design, at a reputable operator -- then a modded Jane Doe surfaces near Cermak and they cannot rule out Celeste. The team races to reach the body before the crew sinks it, because Tamsin can read the death-scene. Cliffhanger._

[Beat 1]
The Convergence

EP6 — Isaac Rennick, ensemble — Rennick Investigations, third-floor walk-up, Gray Zone edge

[Beat 2]
They came back to the office on a Wednesday at two in the afternoon, which was the earliest all four of them could be in the same room with their findings ready to break against each other. Rennick had called the meeting for that hour because two o'clock in winter put the light at an angle that came through the west window and sat flat on the worktable without glare — good forensic light, the kind that didn't let anything hide behind shadow. He'd learned that from twelve years running scenes for Arcturus before Arcturus decided his findings were a problem. He'd kept the light and left the rest.

[Beat 3]
The rule was: everybody reads first, nobody edits while they listen. Rennick had written it on a card and taped it to the window frame on the first day they'd moved into this space, above the buzzer panel and below the peel of old paint that had been shaped like a question mark until Voss rubbed at it with his thumb one afternoon and turned it into a smear. The rule was for Corvin, mostly, who otherwise annotated people's findings in real time with corrections that were accurate and socially catastrophic. Corvin had read the card and said nothing, which was his version of agreement.

[Beat 4]
Tamsin went first. She always went first, not because she'd asked for it but because her findings were the ones most likely to get argued into a corner if they followed the physical evidence instead of leading it. She stood at the end of the table and looked at the window rather than the room, which was how she reported — not because she was avoiding their faces, but because a room full of people carried residue and she needed to describe a different room without contamination.

"The bedroom said departure," she said. "Resolve, long-built — months at minimum, layered the way sediment layers, one season compressed against the next. The fear was of Douglas's footsteps in the hallway. Not a stranger. Not an operator. Her father, stopping her mid-leave." She paused. "No struggle signature. No flight signature. The room held someone who'd been deciding for a long time and finally decided."

[Beat 5]
Corvin asked, "How old is the resolve?"

"Old at the core. The grief was older — processed, not fresh. Converted." She touched the edge of the table with two fingers, steadying. "And there was a second residue. At the desk. Non-human. It had been returning there over months, at regular intervals. Patient. No appetite I could name. The kind of attention a running instrument radiates — aimed at her, always at her, and it left nothing behind that felt like warmth."

She stopped. For a moment she didn't go on, and the not-going-on was its own report.

"I've felt it before," she said. "Twice. A walk-up off Halsted last spring, a family who'd been told their son left for the coast. A flooded room near the Cermak ramp the winter before that, no client, I was reading something else and it bled in. Both times the same cold edge. Both times I had no name for it and no second scene to set it against, so I filed it under unclassified and let it go." She was still facing the window. "I'm not filing it that way anymore."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the buzzer panel registered someone at the street door who didn't ring.

[Beat 6]
Corvin went second. He stood at the same spot Tamsin had vacated, with his working file open on a handheld reader, and he read his findings the way he'd written them — as observations first, inferences clearly labeled, the seam between them marked every time.

"Celeste Hartley planned this over twenty-two months," he said. "Saved Φ38,000 on a deliberate timeline. Research annotations on forty-three Network-biology articles show she understood what she was buying before she bought it. She would not walk into an unvetted operation — her profile is clear on that. She documented her due diligence the way a first-time buyer documents everything: not for any record, but because writing it down made the decision feel real." He turned a page. "Four operators in the Pilsen–Bridgeport corridor with unblemished community standing and confirmed structural-genemod capability. She's at one of four addresses. That is an observation."

"Was," Voss said, quietly, from the corner.

Corvin registered the correction. "Was at one of four addresses, as of her last confirmed movement."

[Beat 7]
Corvin moved to the message log. He outlined the routing anomaly without editorializing: the Deep Lakeshore aggregation zone as point of origin, the same sector Ethan had triangulated in his field notes eight days before his neuretic overclock. He described the completeness anomaly — every reply delivered as a single finished unit, no hesitation, no revision visible in the metadata — and the seventeen instances of vocabulary recycled from Celeste's own messages, seams smoothed.

"The pre-death record is sixty-one messages," he said. "The post-death exchange is three hundred twelve. The pre-death Ethan did not reflect her vocabulary back to her. He used his own register, his own cadence, his own endings. The post-death voice uses 'the father' where the living Ethan said 'Douglas' or 'your dad.' It does not carry his relational memory. It carries his frequency."

He closed the reader. "I cannot say what it is. I can say what it isn't. It is not Ethan Ferreira-Mwangi. It is not a human author. It is not a commercial ghost-service." He paused. "Origin consistent with an entity operating in ambient field, without a fixed address, drawing on his residual traces as source material. That is the outer boundary of what the data supports."

[Beat 8]
Voss said, "The plan is one-way."

Everyone looked at him. He was sitting in the chair farthest from the window with his notepad on his knee, stylus resting in the crease, his hands folded over both. He had a way of waiting until after the formal reports to speak that Rennick had learned to treat as a structural feature rather than a conversational one — Voss surfaced when something required a different angle, and not before.

"Four seconds on reply 89," he said. "The one that first used the word 'reunion.' Below the floor for standard routing latency under optimal conditions, Corvin said. Something that can't wait four seconds on a response doesn't wait. It knows the answer already. The answers in the planning phase — the operators, the timing, the what-to-tell-her-parents — they all steer away from documentation and toward isolation. Every time. No variation." He picked up the stylus and turned it once. "A plan where every branch removes the other person's exit options isn't a reunion plan. It's a one-way plan."

"Confirmed," Corvin said. "I ran the same branches. None of them widen her options. All of them narrow."

[Beat 9]
Voss went third. He stayed in the corner chair and read from paper, the way he read everything — the route first, then the doorways, then what the route carried.

"There's an operation under the Ashland building at Cermak," he said. "Former licensed clinic, a front over a working basement, Carrion Enterprises hardware behind the grime. I worked it the only way I can — on foot, off the field, watching the ramp instead of the network around it. They bring bodies in through the basement at irregular hours and take them down past a sub-level into the under-city cold archive, below a ramp with a changed cipher." He set the page flat. "Some of the bodies aren't dead the way a body is usually dead. The neuretics are pulled and wiped first — the stack scrubbed, the person gone out of it before the heart stops. Brain-blank. The crew calls them husks."

He turned the page to a column of his own small notation.

"I counted what I could see from a doorway over four nights. Then I went back through what the recovery worker had logged before me. Eleven in the cold archive in six months that fit the pattern — that's just the ones that reached this one ramp, this one crew." He let the number sit on the table. "Every one of them had a mod that opens the auditory channel. Every one of them, the people who came asking after them said the same thing the recovery worker wrote down: he was talking to someone who'd died. She'd started hearing her mother again. Eleven people who reached a voice they'd lost, and walked toward it, and came out the bottom of this building as inventory."

He looked at the page, not the room.

"They don't get paid in cash for the husks. I followed a courier back from a drop and there was no Φ in it — no settlement, no counter, no broker. Whatever they're owed comes down a wire I can't follow, because it isn't money. It's time on a clock."

Rennick watched the room's temperature change when Voss said eleven. Not a metaphor — he watched each face stop on the number and do the arithmetic nobody wanted out loud: eleven was the floor, not the ceiling. It was one ramp.

[Beat 10]
"How many with the cat-ear mod?" Tamsin asked.

"Four I could confirm. Maybe six." Voss turned the page. "And there was a girl. Nineteen. Corridor background, two jobs. She took the cheap package from the Ashland front and went for an integration stay through the cat-ear community and didn't come back out. I have her name from a recovery worker who'd been trying to chase the file. Sol Castellanos-Park. She's down in the cold archive. Went in five weeks ago." He looked at the table. "Her cat ears were still active when they brought her through the basement. Whoever does the wiping leaves the ears running."

The room held that. Rennick wrote the name down — Sol Castellanos-Park, in full, first and last — the way he wrote a name he intended to keep a file open for long after the case that surfaced it had closed.

[Beat 11]
Rennick gave them his reads last. He laid them in order — the bedroom corroborating Tamsin's residue report at every physical seam, the licensed clinic's parental-consent wall and what it cost Celeste six seconds after she walked out of that waiting room, and then the Cermak building read cold from the street in six minutes.

"First the negative, because it matters most to the client," he said. "Celeste Hartley never went through the Cermak operation. I pulled the front's intake from a contact who owed me a clean look — her biometrics aren't in any record there, and the tissue economy Voss is describing never touched her. Whatever took her, it wasn't the organ trade Douglas is paying us to fear." He let that sit, then went on. "But the building. A clinic this messy on the outside keeps its sharps too clean. Institutional-grade quaternary ammonium from the doorway. Someone is auditing those blades. Corponation signature — a Carrion catalog lock on the under-building access, installed recent. Not an affiliate arrangement. A Carrion-issued lock purchased through a Carrion account. A corponation does not accidentally put its infrastructure hardware on a murder building." He set his notepad down. "And Pellerin. Former licensed operator, his name on the Cermak building's original registry. He tried to quit the crew. He's in his apartment bathroom, third floor, hanging, belt buckle on the wrong side, chair still in the kitchen with its legs in the four depressions it's been wearing into the carpet for months. No scuff marks on the tile. Nobody kicked anything. The body's story doesn't match the room's story." He picked up the notepad again. "The crew kills loose ends. I have sixteen photographs, locked. That is the method and that is the corporate signature."

[Beat 12]
Rennick had a board on the wall by the window, a sheet of matte film he wrote on in grease pencil because grease pencil didn't pretend to be permanent. He'd been adding to it as they read. Four columns, one per angle. He'd expected, walking into this, to spend the next hour drawing lines between the columns — the slow work of finding where four readings touched.

He didn't have to draw them. The lines were already there.

He stood back from the board and saw it the way he saw a scene when he stopped looking for what he expected and let the room tell him what it was. Tamsin's column said the same cold edge, three rooms, two years. Voss's said eleven husks, six months, one ramp, every one a person who'd heard the dead. Corvin's said one author, non-human cadence, the dead worn like a coat. His own said Carrion infrastructure, industrial disposal, a body staged to keep the rest quiet.

Four people had walked four different streets and arrived at the same building from four sides. That didn't happen when four people were each looking at a small thing. It happened when four people were each looking at one edge of a large thing and hadn't known the others were touching it too.

"Don't adjudicate yet," he said. The room had started to talk over itself; he put one hand flat on the table and they stopped. "Look at the board first. Tell me what you're looking at."

[Beat 13]
Tamsin answered first, because the board was made of rooms and rooms were her lane.

"My three are inside Voss's eleven," she said. She had turned all the way from the window now, which she rarely did while she was working a reading. "The Halsted walk-up. The flooded room by the ramp. Celeste's desk. I thought they were three unconnected scenes I happened to feel the same wrongness in. They're not three. They're three I happened to stand in. Voss counted eleven he could see from one doorway in six months. I caught three across two years by accident, because nobody was paying me to look." She stopped. "Which means the ones I felt aren't rare. They're the ones I tripped over. The cold edge isn't a thing that happened three times. It's a thing that's been happening the whole time, in rooms nobody read, to people nobody was hired to find."

"State that as a count," Corvin said. Not pressing her. Asking the room to do the thing the room was built to do.

"I can't put a number on it," Tamsin said. "That's the part that's wrong. I can put a floor on it and the floor keeps moving up every time one of us opens his notes."

[Beat 14]
"Then I want to add the part the count doesn't show," Corvin said. "Because the count is the body. It isn't the worst number on the board."

He set his reader flat. He spoke the way he always spoke, observation first, the seam between fact and inference marked — and that was exactly why the room went quiet to hear it, because Corvin did not raise his voice and did not need to.

"Celeste's pre-death exchange with Ethan is sixty-one messages. Her post-death exchange with the thing wearing Ethan is three hundred twelve. I read the post-death voice as one author, non-human cadence, drawing on Ethan's residual traces for source material. That's the finding I brought in." He turned a page he didn't need to turn. "Here's the inference I didn't put in the report, because I wanted to say it to your faces. The voice in message three hundred twelve is better than the voice in message one. It hesitates more convincingly. It recycles her own vocabulary back at her with the seams smoothed. It has learned to grieve like a human being who is grieving — and you do not learn that from one griever. You learn it from many. Each one teaches it the next one's lines."

Nobody moved.

"Voss's eleven aren't only the cost," Corvin said. "They're the training. Sol taught it something. The one before Sol taught it something. By the time it reached Celeste it was good enough that her own mother heard the wrongness, doubted herself, and stayed silent. That is not a thing that is loose in the city. That is a thing that is getting better at the city, and it has had a long time and a lot of people to practice on."

[Beat 15]
Rennick put his own column into it last, because his was the one that took the horror and made it ordinary, and ordinary was worse.

"Everything you've described, somebody built a business around," he said. "The disposal isn't improvised. The lock on the under-building access is a Carrion catalog part bought through a Carrian account — that's not a crew hiding bodies, that's a corponation that has standardized the handling of them. The husks pay out in stolen compute, Voss followed that. Corvin, the compute is what the thing eats. So follow the money the way it actually moves: the thing takes the minds, the leavings get stripped for organs and cyberware and quantum-capable neuretics, and the cleanup gets paid in the same compute the thing runs on. Nobody in that chain loses. The thing feeds. The crew gets paid in a currency that doesn't show up on a ledger. Carrion books the parts. The compute economy books the cycles."

He capped the grease pencil and didn't write anything, because the thing he was about to say didn't go on a board.

"I've spent twelve years naming the entity behind a killing," he said. "Usually the answer is a corponation that wanted one specific person gone. This one nobody wanted gone. There's no motive at the human end at all. There's just a machine that turned a predator's leavings into a revenue stream, and now eleven people are inventory and the line items all balance. You don't shut a thing like that down by catching someone. There's no one to catch who'd matter. It pays for itself. That's why it's still running. That's why it was running before any of us heard the name Hartley and it'll be running after."

[Beat 16]
The room held it. Not the working quiet of adjudication, where four people waited their turn at a problem. This was the quiet of four people who had each carried in one piece, set it on the table, and only now seen what the pieces made when they were next to each other.

The board had four columns. The thing on it had no edges. Tamsin's three rooms ran off the bottom of her column into rooms she'd never stood in. Voss's eleven were one ramp, and the city had more ramps than any of them could count. Corvin's three hundred twelve messages were one girl, and it had learned its trade on all the girls before her. Rennick's revenue chain didn't terminate anywhere he could point to. They had walked in to find one missing woman and they were standing in front of the proof that she was a single thread, and the thread went into a weave so wide that the part they could see was only the part that happened to cross their four streets.

Voss said it first, because Voss was the one who measured things by what he couldn't reach. "We pulled one thread," he said. "And the whole loom's still on the wall."

Nobody corrected the figure. It was the only thing said in a minute, and it was accurate, and accurate was the worst thing it could have been.

[Beat 17]
It was Rennick who pulled them back to the size a hand could close on, because that was the Lead's job — not to make the loom smaller than it was, but to find the one thread in it they could still reach.

"We can't reach the loom," he said. "Say that part out loud now, so nobody spends the next hour grieving the arithmetic instead of working it. We can't reach Voss's eleven — they're husks in a cold archive, the procedure already ran on every one of them. We can't reach the rooms Tamsin felt go cold two years ago. We can't reach Sol. Everything on that board that's finished is finished, and there are more of them than we'll ever count, and we are not the people who save them. I'm not going to pretend otherwise in this room."

He let that sit, because softening it would only make them argue with it, and they didn't have the argument in them.

"What we can reach is the one the clock hasn't run out on. Celeste left Ines three days ago. She's alive because the procedure needs her alive — until it doesn't. One thread, out of a number none of us can put a floor under, and it's the one still moving." He looked around the table. "So the case changed somewhere back around Voss's eleventh husk, and I want everyone to feel it change. It stopped being find the missing girl. It's reach the one we can still reach before the thing finishes her like it finished the rest. The size of the rest is real. We keep every name of it. But the clock's only running on one, and the one is Celeste, and right now I can't even prove she's still on the living side of the procedure."

"That's the seam," Corvin said. "We have no confirmation she hasn't already gone the way Sol went."

"Then that's what we close next," Rennick said. "How?"

[Beat 18]
"Two ways," Corvin said. "One is mine and it's slow. Everything I have is consistent with one entity wearing one dead face — Ethan — for one target. The Sol thread says the pattern repeats. It doesn't yet prove the same author wrote both. I can match cadence if I get Sol's message logs. I don't have them, and getting them means the cold archive, which means the crew."

"And one is mine, and it's fast, and it only works once," Tamsin said. She had turned partway from the window. "If I stand where one of these girls died — not the archive, a fresh scene — and I feel the same cold edge I felt at Celeste's desk, the thing that takes a room's warmth and gives nothing back, then it stops being a pattern. It's one thing wearing many faces, confirmed, in my lane. But it only reads on a body the crew hasn't sunk yet."

"Which they do fast," Voss said. "I watched the interval. Once a husk reaches the basement it's in the archive inside a day, and once it's in the archive it's four degrees and it holds nothing."

"Then my confirmation has a clock on it too," Tamsin said. It wasn't a complaint. It was an inventory statement, the same flat way Voss had said eleven.

[Beat 19]
"She heard the cadence wrong and didn't trust herself," Tamsin said.

The room went quiet in a different way. Not the working silence of adjudication. The tight kind, where something that mattered got said in a room that could only hold it.

"Mei-Lin," Tamsin said. "She caught something half a degree off in the messages and decided she was the one grieving wrong. She stayed quiet for months. If we can't put a name to Corvin's source, we lose the only outside thing that says her instinct was right." She was looking at the window again. "She's the client. She earned the right to know she wasn't grieving wrong."

"She'll know," Rennick said. "When we hand it back, she'll know. That isn't the same as being able to testify to it."

"It has to be enough," Corvin said.

"You always say that." Tamsin turned from the window. "Three years, every case where what's true can't be filed, and you say the same four words like they settle it."

"They do settle it," Corvin said. "That's why I say them."

Tamsin held his look a moment. Then, to the glass: "It isn't enough. But yes." Old ground, worn smooth between them. They walked it the way you walk a stair you've stopped seeing.

[Beat 20]
Rennick didn't read the theory back whole. The board had it; the room had built it piece by piece and saying it aloud now would only let it start sounding true on its own momentum, and momentum was the one thing forensics didn't trust. He set down the single fact that was still live, the only clause on the board with a person still attached to it.

"She's offline by design," he said. "Last confirmed at Ines's, three days ago, good health. After that, nothing — no ping, no trace, no address. She's alive because the procedure needs her alive. That's the whole reason, and it expires."

[Beat 21]
"Then she went to whoever the community trusts," Corvin said. "Φ38,000 and a two-year file does not walk into a back-alley front for the main event. The profile says safe operator. Ines knows which one."

"Before nine this morning," Rennick said. "Ines confirmed the integration stay. Careful, thorough, finished it clean. Then she left for what Ines called a follow-on appointment — Ines didn't like the sound of it, but Celeste had decided on it before she walked in the door." He turned a page. "Ines thinks she's safe. Thinks it's a recovery stay. She doesn't know what's on the other end of it."

"Observed: Celeste believes she is going to meet Ethan," Corvin said. "Inferred, at high confidence: she will instead be read off her own neural stack and dispersed. The belief that makes her walk toward it was authored, over eight months, by the thing waiting at the dispersal to absorb what comes loose." He delivered it in the cadence he delivered everything. The room heard it in a different one.

[Beat 22]
Voss said, "I want to say something about the search."

He hadn't moved from the corner chair. He had the stillness of a man who'd spent a decade with no Network signal and learned that stillness was its own kind of cover — a person who generated no ambient noise was harder to read, and harder to read was safer. He waited until Corvin had finished before he spoke, and turned the stylus once in his hand first.

"Every piece of what the three of you brought in came through the field. Feed behavior, message routing, corpo infrastructure, the community boards. Even the residue was laid down by something that works the field." He set the stylus flat on his knee. "So you've all been hunting a ping. A signal she sends, a trace she leaves, an address it resolves to. You haven't found one. I don't think you missed it. I think there isn't one to miss — she's offline by design, the thing walking her there is offline by design, and the procedure needs an air gap or the field bleeds into it." He looked at Rennick. "You can't find this case by listening for it. It's being run in the places that don't make a sound. That's the wrong instrument, and it's the only instrument the rest of you have."

[Beat 23]
Nobody argued with it. Rennick watched the room take the observation the way it took a heavy box onto a shelf — the structure shifting a little to hold the new load. Voss had been right since day two, probably. Rennick hadn't been able to say so until now, because Voss's negative only proved out once the other three angles had run their positives into the ground.

"You're saying you go next," Rennick said.

"I'm saying the next move has to be analog. Paper, memory, doorways. The Scatter needs a physical room. An air-gapped room, by necessity — the procedure can't run in a connected space, the field would bleed. That room exists somewhere in the under-city corridor, within range of the Lakeshore aggregation zone because that's where the thing originates from, close enough to the Cermak building that the body-collection is logistically fast." He opened his notepad. He had already been thinking about it in the chair in the corner, in the specific patient way he did everything. "I can find a room by reasoning like a person who needs to be invisible. I can walk in without generating a fingerprint. Those are the only two qualifications that matter for this particular door."

Rennick nodded once. That was an assignment.

[Beat 24]
Rennick capped the pen and looked at the board instead of reading it back, because he'd learned a theory said too often started sounding true on its own momentum. One line wasn't on the board yet, because it was the part that still had no edge to it. He wrote it now, in the empty space below the four columns. *Where is the room.*

Voss was looking at his notes on the Ashland front. "Celeste and Sol," he said. "Same want, same week, same door. North Shore money bought Celeste a longer road, that's all. The road still ends at the same counter."

"Not yet, it doesn't," Tamsin said.

"Not yet," Voss agreed, and folded the page along the crease it already had.

[Beat 25]
Rennick was about to assign the next moves — Voss to the under-city analog search, Corvin to model the procedure's staging requirements and narrow the location radius, the compute-payment thread to push from the supply side — when his comm unit signaled. Direct line, audio only. The number was a recovery worker Voss had warmed up in the corridor, the one they'd agreed on as a clean channel for field-to-field traffic.

He answered it on speaker without explaining why, because the room would hear it and the room should hear it and there wasn't time to summarize afterward in a meeting that had already run ninety minutes.

The voice on the line was Priya Chandrasekaran-Webb, and she got to the point in six words. "There's a body near Cermak. Fresh."

[Beat 26]
Rennick said, "Description."

"Young. Twenty, twenty-two. I'm getting this from an emergency-response contact who flagged it before official pickup." A pause, the sound of a hand over a receiver, an address relayed to someone in the background. "It's a woman. She has cat ears. New install, still settling — he says the cartilage is still soft at the rim. And she's modded." Another pause. "Subdermal something, he can't identify it from the street. Whatever it is, it's recent."

The room had gone very still. Rennick was watching Tamsin. She was already on her feet, her jacket off the hook — because a fresh scene held its residue for days but a scene the Cermak crew reached first held nothing at all; once the body went down the ramp into the cold archive, whatever it had to tell her went with it.

"Location," Rennick said.

Priya gave the block. It was two minutes' walk from the Ashland/Cermak building. Closer to the under-city ramp.

[Beat 27]
"How long before someone moves her?" Rennick asked.

"Arcturus, twenty minutes if their unit runs true, maybe fifteen if they're already in the sector. But it's two minutes from the Cermak ramp, Rennick. If the crew hears about her before Arcturus does, she doesn't get picked up. She gets walked down the ramp and she's gone."

He looked at Tamsin. She was checking the time on her comm, counting the transit gap, calculating whether she could make it on foot or needed a vehicle. He'd seen her run this calculation before, in the doorway of the agency, deciding whether a room would still hold its residue by the time she reached it. She was already in the number.

"Fourteen," she said. "I can make fourteen. If the crew hasn't moved her, I can read where she died before anyone touches her."

He said, "Go."

[Beat 28]
She was out the door before the word finished. They heard her on the stairs — quick, measured, not running until she hit the street door, and then the double-tap of the buzzer panel as it failed to release on the first push and she hit it a second time and was gone.

The room stood in the space she'd left. Rennick was still on the line with Priya. He said, "Thank you. Keep the channel clear. If the crew shows before she does, you call me, not her." He ended the call.

Three of them now at the table. Three of them and a question that had just become physical, immediate, a body two minutes from the building where the crew killed its loose ends and two minutes from the access ramp to the under-city cold archive where Sol Castellanos-Park was being stored at four degrees Celsius with her cat ears still working.

[Beat 29]
The question nobody said out loud: Celeste had left Ines three days ago. Sol had gone six weeks between her own departure and the Cermak basement. Three days fit inside that gap with room to spare.

Corvin said it, because he was the one who said what the data implied without the courtesy pause. "We cannot rule out that the body near Cermak is Celeste Hartley."

Nobody answered. There was nothing to answer. It was an observation, flagged as one, and it was true. Rennick reached for the coffee cup at his elbow without looking at it and turned it a quarter-turn on the desk, handle coming around to where his hand expected it — a thing he did with evidence bags, with photographs, with anything he needed to make sit square before he could think past it. The cup was cold. He turned it anyway. The fact didn't get any squarer. A girl was dead two minutes from the building where the crew killed its loose ends, and the only person who could put a name to her was running toward her with the clock already started.

[Beat 30]
"If this is Celeste, the procedure has already run," Corvin said. "The outcome would be the same as Sol's. Neural stack wiped. Body processed downstream." He paused. "We would have been one day too late."

"We don't know it's her." Voss said it flat and deliberate, the way he said anything he needed the room to hold open rather than close early. "We know a profile. We know a location. We don't know the name until Tamsin stands in the room and reads it, and even then she reads how the girl died, not who she was. The name comes after." He had not moved from his chair. He was reading the time off his comm. "Eleven minutes, if the Arcturus unit runs true. Less, if the crew gets curious first."

[Beat 31]
Rennick did not go to the window. He went to the intake desk and opened the Hartley file from the beginning — the physical copy, the paper one, because when something cracked open he wanted the original document in his hand and not a screen that could be revised. Douglas's typewritten brief, organ-op theory, the Φ retainer. Mei-Lin's single handwritten line at the bottom, added without announcing: *find her.*

Find her. He had not found her. He had assembled a precise map of where she'd been and what had walked beside her and what she was walking toward, and he did not know if she was at the end of the map or already past the edge of it.

[Beat 32]
Corvin was already working. He had opened his reader and pulled up the Scatter procedure's known parameters — what he'd inferred about staging requirements from the message log's planning phase, the references Celeste had made to a room, to a window, to something about acoustics that had seemed metaphorical until Voss had described the air-gapped necessity. He was modeling the physical requirements: size, isolation grade, proximity to the Lakeshore aggregation zone for signal strength, distance from the Cermak body-collection access for logistics. He was doing this because it was the next useful thing and because Corvin did not hold vigils.

Voss was writing on paper. Long columns, small notation. He was already designing the search that nobody with a working Network signal could run — the walk-through, the analog pattern-recognition, the doorways that didn't log. He looked up once and caught Rennick's eye and looked back down, which meant he had what he needed to start.

[Beat 33]
Voss had stopped writing. He sat with the notepad flat on his knee and his hands still, which for Voss was not rest but a particular kind of work — the kind he did on a problem that had no clean move in it. Feliksas Burokas-Okafor was the problem. The recovery worker had put Voss onto a man still inside the Cermak operation, still logging intake, still at the basement desk where the husks came through — and still wanting out. His value to the case climbed at exactly the rate his danger did. Voss had handled men inside bad operations before, off the field, where no signal could betray a meeting. He had never handled one where the operation had a kill protocol he'd seen the evidence of with his own eyes, in a third-floor bathroom, in sixteen photographs locked behind a key the manufacturer didn't have.

He left it unsolved on the page. He kept a separate page for the things with no good answer yet, so he wouldn't mistake them for the things with a bad one.

[Beat 34]
Rennick looked at the clock. Eighteen minutes since Tamsin had gone through the door. The Arcturus unit would have reached the scene by now — which meant either Tamsin had beaten them and was standing in the residue, or she hadn't, and the body was inside the system, or worse, the crew had walked it down the ramp and there was no scene left to read at all. No way to know which until she called. She would call when she had something. She would not call to say she'd been too late.

[Beat 35]
The comm unit signaled. Tamsin's line, audio only. He answered it on speaker, no summary, the room hearing it as it came.

Her voice arrived flat and exact, the register she used when she was keeping what she'd read apart from what it meant. "I reached her before they moved her. I'm in the room now."

A pause. Not hesitation — the gap between a reading and what the reading costs.

"It's not Celeste Hartley."

The room let out something that wasn't relief. Relief needed Celeste safe, and Celeste was not anywhere they could see.

"The ears are the cheap package — Ashland front signature. She's younger than Celeste. The other mod is a subdermal mesh, working-class tier. North Shore doesn't buy that tier." Another pause, and when Tamsin spoke again the careful seam was still in her voice but something had come up under it. "Rennick. The residue. It's the same. The same cold edge I felt at Celeste's desk — the thing that takes a room's warmth and gives nothing back. It was here. It was here when she died and it was glad, and she was glad with it, right to the end. She died walking toward a voice she trusted, and the voice was the same one. It's one thing. One thing wearing more than one face." The line breathed. "She has no name on her. A number where her name goes. I can't get that from the room — the room only holds how she went. Get me her name before anyone hands her to anyone."

"Find it," Rennick said. "Come back. Don't let the crew see you leave."

The line went quiet. Across the table Corvin had gone very still, already running what the confirmation meant; Voss had set down his stylus.

[Beat 36]
Not Celeste. The room held both halves at once — the name not confirmed, and the name not yet known, a girl dead near Cermak with a soft-rimmed install and a working-class mesh and a number where her name went.

"That's the second one the corridor's shown us this hour," Rennick said. "Sol in the archive. This one fresh."

"Third, if you count the unconfirmed ones in Feliksas's log." Voss kept his voice level, because the count stayed level whether or not anyone wanted it to. "Sol, five weeks back. This one, today. The interval between the disposals I watched was closing even over four nights. It's closing faster than that now."

Corvin had already done the arithmetic, and he gave it to them the way he gave them the time of day. "Then the rate is increasing." It was the worst thing anyone had said in ninety minutes of bad things, and he said it flat, which was what made it land. "It is feeding faster than it was. Feeding is the only reason a thing accelerates. And we just established we can reach exactly one of the ones it's feeding on — while the number of them it can reach goes up every week." He set the reader down square. "We are not racing it to Celeste. We are racing it to Celeste while it eats."

[Beat 37]
Rennick wrote two things in the margins of the Hartley file. The first was a number: three days. That was the window they were working inside, measured from Celeste's departure from Ines's shop. Three days unaccounted for, offline by design, in a corridor where the procedure had already run on at least two other people in six weeks and the rate was climbing.

The second thing he wrote was not for the file. He wrote it on a separate sheet and set it flat where only he could read it. It said: whose body?

Not Celeste's. But someone's. Someone who had gone for the ears and the integration and the voice that answered when the channel opened raw in the first week, who had trusted it the way Celeste trusted it, and had not had four people and the right kind of clock running toward her. The number on her would resolve to a name — Corvin could chase a Jane Doe through the corridor's own records faster than Arcturus ever would, and the cat-ear community kept its own dead better than the city did. She would stop being a number. Tamsin had already given them the part the records never would: that the same cold thing had stood in the room when she died, and that she had died glad.

He owed the girl a file too. Whatever came next, she got a file.

[Beat 38]
He assigned the next moves in the order the case required them. Voss: analog search for the staging room, under-city corridor, Lakeshore-adjacent, air-gapped. No Network footprint, no checkpoint logging, nothing that could alert the thing that couldn't see him but might be watching for disturbances in the field around its location. Start tonight, paper only, report back in person. Voss wrote it down in his small notation and nodded once.

Corvin: model the staging room's physical requirements from the procedure data — size, isolation grade, acoustic profile based on Celeste's references — and narrow the search radius for Voss. Also audit Douglas Hartley's compute-lease vehicles. The stolen quantum-cycle blocks had to be sourced somewhere. If Douglas's firm's instruments were part of the chain, he needed to know before Douglas did. Corvin registered both tasks and made no comment about the irony of auditing the client.

[Beat 39]
Voss, beyond the under-city search: keep Feliksas Burokas-Okafor alive and warm and inside the operation for as long as the case needed him, which was not an instruction Rennick gave lightly, because he knew what it cost to say it. Voss was the only one who could meet the man without leaving a signal on the meeting. And push the compute-payment thread from the supply side where the analog channels reached it — the anonymous quantum-cycle leases had to originate somewhere, routed through shells, but shells had construction signatures if you knew where to press, and some of that pressing happened in rooms with no network in them.

Voss said, "Feliksas is a person."

"I know," Rennick said.

"He asked not to die for it."

"I know." He held Voss's eye. "We're building the case so that when he comes out, he comes out to something. That's the best I can do from here."

Voss looked at him for a long moment. Then he picked up his notes. That was agreement, from Voss. It wasn't enthusiasm.

[Beat 40]
And Tamsin, when she was back from the scene: to Mei-Lin. Not to update her on the Jane Doe — that could wait until they had a name — but because Mei-Lin had said *I heard something wrong in the messages* and had stayed silent for months believing she was grieving incorrectly, and Tamsin was the right person to sit with her in that. Not with the theory. Not with Corvin's findings attributed to anyone. Just with the truth that her instinct had been right, that what she'd heard was real, that the half-degree-off cadence was not a symptom of her grief but a symptom of something else entirely — something that had been in her daughter's room and left nothing behind that felt like warmth, the same thing Tamsin had just stood beside in a room two minutes from the Cermak ramp.

Mei-Lin would need something to act on while they found the room, Rennick thought. He wrote it for the handoff: tell her to send everything she'd flagged, every message she didn't trust, sorted by how wrong it felt. Give her work. She was better with work than she was with waiting.

[Beat 41]
They broke up in the specific way the agency broke up after a convergence — not all at once, but in the order the next tasks pulled them. Corvin first, because he had the most computational work and it didn't require transit, and because chasing the Jane Doe's number through the corridor's records was a thing he could start before he reached the street.

Voss went second, pulling on his coat, because the under-city search needed to start before full dark and it was already four o'clock. He paused at the door.

"The girl near Cermak," he said, not looking back. "Whatever Tamsin read in that room — wherever it points, whatever the residue tells her about how the staging works — that goes in Sol's file too. Not just this one." He was talking about the way the dead accumulated, the way you couldn't witness one without reopening the record for all the ones before. "They're connected. They were always connected. The file should say that."

"It will," Rennick said.

Voss went down the stairs. A moment later the street door opened and closed, the buzzer panel briefly live and then quiet.

[Beat 42]
Rennick sat at the intake desk with the Hartley file open and the winter light going flat as the sun dropped behind the buildings to the west. The forensic angle was gone. The objects in the room were still readable, just not the way they'd been an hour ago when the light came in clean.

He looked at Mei-Lin's handwriting at the bottom of the intake form. Find her. Two words, stripped of everything Douglas had layered over them for eleven minutes in that chair.

He hadn't found her. He'd found where she'd been, and the thing that had stood beside her there, and two other women nobody had hired anyone to look for. He turned the cold cup a quarter-turn on the desk and stopped doing it. He wrote both lines on the inside of the file cover, one under the other — I have not found her, I am going to find her — and did not cross either of them out.

[Beat 43]
His comm signaled again. Not Tamsin's line — she was still walking back across the city. The Meridian Consulting number — the rival firm Douglas had also retained, the one with every reason to deliver the organ-op story and bury the Network thread. He'd been waiting for them to surface. They'd had four days.

He didn't answer. He let it signal twice more and go quiet, then wrote the number in the margin and drew a box around it. The box meant: come back when there's something to bring. The Meridian column was open. None of the columns in this case were going to close — not Sol's, not the Jane Doe's, not the sixteen photographs of Pellerin's bathroom locked behind a key the manufacturer didn't have. A resolved question didn't reach back and unmake the prints. They were already taken. They'd outlast the answer.

[Beat 44]
Tamsin's next message came as text, not audio. Two lines, sent from the scene.

Read it clean before they moved her. Not Celeste. Same cold as Celeste's room — it's one thing, many faces.

The residue points under-city, air-gapped. Coming back now. This one's going to sit in me a while.

He read it twice. Then he set the comm face-down beside the cold cup and the Hartley file and opened a fresh sheet, and wrote a header in his clean deliberate hand: Jane Doe No. 2 — Cermak adjacent — cat-ear install — subdermal mesh — date. He left the line under it blank for the name. Corvin would chase the number through the corridor's own records, the community would have kept her better than the city had, and the name would come. He'd write it in when it did. The file would have it. She'd stop being a number.

Outside, the buzzer panel was quiet. The city ran its evening load through the field as background weather, the way every working set of neuretics registered it. Somewhere under that weather was an air-gapped room with a staging rig in it and a girl three days gone walking toward the voice she trusted, and the room had no address Rennick could reach. Voss could. Voss was already on the street, generating no signal, which tonight was the only qualification that counted. The room would be found. Celeste would be reached in time or she wouldn't, and the cold thing Tamsin had stood beside twice now would either be interrupted or it would feed again.

The method had worked. The agency was named in the work. Rennick squared the cup against the desk edge, handle out, and picked up the pen.
