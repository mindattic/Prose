# The Harvestman

_A massive Iowan Behemoth — no manufacturer, no CorpoNation transponder, no owner — loses its orientation grid and walks off-course into the Zone-12 outskirts of GLMZ. ArcSec mobilizes. CorpoNations scramble for liability. The machine just walks. A boundary surveyor, an ArcSec lieutenant, a corporate liaison, and a man who spent fifteen years fixing Behemoths in Iowa have to figure out what it wants before someone fires a missile at forty thousand tonnes of confused machine. First contact, told through the lens of a lost thing trying to find its way home._

[Beat 1]
The seismic array had been reading quiet since 0340. Maris Delacroix-Oye noted this in her instrument log with the same shorthand she used for everything out here: time, sensor ID, deviation from baseline. The Zone-12 outer buffer ran flat and empty at this hour, former AgriCore leasehold that hadn't been worked in eleven years, and the ground tended to settle sometime before dawn when the temperature differential stopped torquing the irrigation channels. Quiet was correct. Quiet was what the data should show.

At 0407, sensor cluster J-19 registered an anomaly.

She almost dismissed it. The deviation signature was repetitive — a 1.4-second pulse at 47-second intervals — and her first read was subsurface drainage shift, the kind of thing that happened when a decommissioned pipe finally gave in the cold. She pulled up J-20 to cross-reference. J-20 had the same signature, origin point 210 metres north of J-19. She pulled J-17. J-21. The pulse tracked across the array in a straight line, south-southwest, consistent velocity.

She opened the triangulation overlay and measured.

The origin point was 4.2 kilometres out. The velocity was 1.2 kilometres per hour. The mass load implied by the ground displacement was somewhere above eight hundred tonnes.

*She had been filing anomaly reports about this buffer zone for six weeks. Unusual seismic activity, no source identified. This was the source.*

Maris did not move from her survey post. The post was a reinforced prefab unit mounted on a concrete pad twelve metres above the buffer plain, south-facing windows giving her a clean line of sight to the former field boundaries. Dawn was arriving from the east in stages — first the horizon edge, then the low cloud layer, then enough ambient grey to see the trench lines without enhancement. She activated the long-range optical and pointed it north.

Four kilometres away, in the early light, something moved.

It was not moving fast. That was the first thing she registered. A thing that size moving fast would have been a different set of decisions entirely. It came across the flat ground at a walking pace — which was what it was, she understood now, watching the stride pattern. Six articulated limbs cycling in a forward-motion sequence, hull rising above the tree-break that marked the old property boundary at kilometre 11. An Iowan Behemoth, she thought, agricultural-class, and she was already opening a new log entry before the thought finished forming.

She noted the time: 0411:33.

She noted sensor cluster J-19 as point of first detection, cross-referenced to J-17 through J-22. She noted the stride cycle, the measured velocity, the approach vector: bearing 172 degrees, deviation from straight-line transit less than one degree over the 4.2-kilometre observation window. She noted the absence of any ArcSec transponder signal, any manufacturer registry ping, any corporate livery that the optics could read on that weathered dark hull.

The machine had no markings she could identify. The hull was agricultural steel gone almost black with decades of oxidation and field weathering, the kind of surface that absorbed light rather than reflected it. Even at distance she could see the heat shimmer off it — bioreactor warmth, which meant the machine was running, had been running for some time, had enough fuel load to sustain operation. The ground vibration was settling into her building now, a low frequency she felt more in her sternum than her feet.

*If the ArcSec array hadn't already triggered, it would. They would see no transponder. They would see eight hundred tonnes of unregistered autonomous system crossing the outer buffer toward Zone-11 residential.*

She kept her hands on the console and continued logging. She noted the approach rate. She calculated time to buffer boundary at current velocity: 3.3 hours. She calculated time to the first residential tier of Zone-11, if the machine cleared the buffer: 5.1 hours. She opened a channel to the zone-wide anomaly report stack and filed entry 47, tagging it priority-one for the first time in six weeks.

The Behemoth came on through the grey light. It did not weave or drift. It did not accelerate. It occupied the buffer plain the way weather occupied a forecast — not threatening, not retreating. Certain.

Maris pulled up the map overlay and marked the machine's position at 0411:33. She set it to update every sixty seconds. Then she opened a thermos of water, because she had learned a long time ago that the first hour of any crisis was mostly waiting, and waiting was better done without a dry throat.

She watched. She logged. The distance closed at 1.2 kilometres per hour.

[Beat 2]
The zone-wide ArcSec priority broadcast arrived in her neuretics at 0414, two minutes and forty-seven seconds after she filed her anomaly report. She noted the response time in her log.

The broadcast was short: unregistered autonomous system, outer buffer, bearing 172 south-southwest, estimated mass above threshold for neutralization protocol. All Zone-12 tactical elements to active cordon posture. Two-kilometre exclusion perimeter. Civilians to shelter positions.

Lieutenant Kaden Vu's voice in the broadcast was the same tone she'd been hearing on the zone channels for four years, since he'd rotated in as Zone-12 tactical commander. Even through the neuretics compression it read flat and measured, the voice of someone who had already finished being surprised and was working the problem.

*He hadn't seen it move yet.*

On her perimeter grid, the ArcSec deployment icons began populating. She watched them spread in a clean arc — three ground units from the Zone-12 tactical post at kilometre marker 8, a mounted response vehicle from the eastern checkpoint, two aerial observers she tracked as Eigenlift silhouettes rising into the dawn grey three kilometres to her northeast. The cordon was fast. It was also a doctrine response: unregistered autonomous system, no transponder, no credentials, no registry. The doctrine did not include much space for questions.

She watched the ordnance icons light up on the broadcast feed.

Vu had requested battalion-grade authorization and received it in under forty seconds, which meant the escalation had come from the zone command layer rather than the district level — someone upstairs had been watching the buffer feeds, or the mass-load trigger had auto-escalated. Either way, the authorization chain was closed. The LT now had ordnance capable of stopping something that weighed eight hundred tonnes, and his doctrine told him he was authorized to use it against any unregistered autonomous system that crossed into Zone-12 without declaration.

Maris pulled up the Behemoth's current position on her optical and checked the stride cycle.

The machine had covered another sixty metres in the time since she'd last measured. Still bearing 172. Still 1.2 kilometres per hour. It had not deviated. There were no broadcast signals coming from it — no ping to establish identity, no automated transponder declaring classification or ownership, no emergency stop code being transmitted. It was simply walking. The cordon was positioning in a two-kilometre arc around a machine that had not acknowledged the cordon's existence.

She opened the zone-wide broadcast channel in her neuretics — observer-grade access only, she could monitor but not transmit on the tactical band. She listened to the deployment chatter. The ground units were confirming positions. The aerial observers were calling distance: 3.8 kilometres and closing. Vu was issuing instructions in the flat cadence she recognized: confirm sight lines, confirm fields of fire, confirm fallback positions.

*He was building a solution to a problem he hadn't fully read yet. That was what doctrine was for.*

Her own report — anomaly entry 47, forty-seven entries going back six weeks — had not appeared anywhere in the broadcast feed. She did not expect it to. Priority interrupts in the ArcSec tactical stack did not search the peripheral surveyor logs for context. The system was designed for speed.

The ArcSec mounted vehicle was parking now at what her grid showed as the 2.1-kilometre line, engines cycling to standby. The Behemoth was at 3.6 kilometres from her post and closing. The arithmetic was not complicated: at current velocity the Behemoth would reach the 2-kilometre cordon line in approximately ninety minutes, and it would reach that line without transponder, without registration, without any of the identity signals that would tell Vu what he was dealing with.

Maris noted the ordnance positions on her grid. She noted the Behemoth's position. She noted the distance between them and the rate at which it was decreasing.

She opened a new log section and titled it: TIMELINE UNTIL CONTACT. She began to fill it in.

[Beat 3]
The corporate interrupt hit her survey feed at 0421, and it hit hard — priority-class burst, the kind that overrode the instrument display and demanded acknowledgment before it would reduce to sidebar. She acknowledged it.

AgriCore Eastern Division, automated asset-tracking protocol, triggered by electromagnetic signature match. The message was dense and corporate-formatted, which meant it took her longer to read than it should have because the information was buried under liability language and division headers. She found the relevant line on the third pass:

ASSET ID: CROWFOOT-7, CLASSIFICATION: BEHEMOTH (IOWAN HARVEST SERIES), STATUS: DECOMMISSIONED 2204-08-14, DISPOSITION: SOLD FOR COMPONENT RECOVERY, REGISTRY CLOSURE: CONFIRMED. Below that: SIGNATURE MATCH: 97.3%. CONFIDENCE: HIGH.

She read it again.

The machine outside — 3.4 kilometres from her post and moving at 1.2 kilometres per hour toward Zone-11 residential — had been decommissioned twenty-two years ago. Its registry was closed. It had been sold for parts. According to the legal and operational record of the largest agricultural CorpoNation in the Midwest territories, it no longer existed.

*The record said the machine was not here. The ground said something different.*

Maris pulled the electromagnetic signature data from her own sensors and compared it against the AgriCore confirmation. 97.3 percent match. The remaining 2.7 percent was within normal drift for two decades of field operation — oxidation changes, component replacements, accumulated variation in the bioreactor signature. The match was valid. This was CROWFOOT-7. Or it had been CROWFOOT-7, before the legal fiction of decommissioning had erased it from the operational record.

She opened the CROWFOOT-7 file and read the component recovery record. Component recovery was the industry term for: we are done with this unit, we will strip the usable parts and let the remainder go inert. The record showed partial component recovery — the navigation sensor suite had been flagged for extraction, the secondary harvest limbs listed for refurbishment. But the record showed a recovery work order dated 2204. It did not show confirmation of completion.

The machine had been decommissioned on paper.

She logged this. She cross-referenced the signature match data. She attached both to anomaly entry 47 and filed an updated version with a new priority flag. Then she thought about the corporate scramble that was probably happening right now in AgriCore's Eastern Division offices, because the automated asset-tracking system had just fired a 97.3-percent match alert on a machine that was legally not running, crossing into Zone-12 at 0421 on a July morning. Someone's terminal was lit up. Someone was reading a decommissioning record that was not as clean as it looked.

*They were managing information. They did not know yet what they were managing it toward.*

She noted, in her log, that the AgriCore alert had arrived through her survey feed's corporate priority channel, which meant AgriCore's systems had detected the machine before she'd filed her report — or at minimum simultaneously — and had routed the alert to field operators in Zone-12 rather than to ArcSec tactical. She noted this without editorializing. The log was for facts.

Outside, the Behemoth crossed the 3-kilometre threshold at the same pace it had maintained for the last twenty-one minutes: 1.2 kilometres per hour, bearing 172, no deviation. The dawn light was stronger now, enough to pick out surface detail without enhancement. She ran the optical feed and recorded. The hull was enormous — the scale of it kept arriving in small increments, each pass of the optics adding some new reference point that made the size concrete. Forty-two metres from the base of its articulation structure to the top of the primary harvest rig, the tallest moving object within twenty kilometres.

The AgriCore corporate feed was still open on her sidebar. She watched it for thirty seconds. No follow-up burst. No updated instruction.

She closed the sidebar and went back to her instruments.

[Beat 4]
At 0427, the ordnance prep order went out on the ArcSec tactical broadcast.

Maris had been watching the Behemoth on her optical for six minutes when she heard it — Vu's voice through the zone observer channel, flat as always, issuing the activation sequence for the heavy ordnance units. She had not expected it this soon. She checked her timeline: the Behemoth was still 2.8 kilometres from the cordon line. Doctrine apparently allowed for prep at 2.8, not just at contact.

She was already watching the instrument feed when the Behemoth stopped.

It stopped at the intersection of two drainage lines — decommissioned irrigation channels from the old AgriCore field grid, crossing at roughly forty-five degrees to each other, the concrete lips of both channels visible as low ridges in the flat ground. The machine stopped with two of its articulation limbs straddling the eastern channel. It was not a stumble or a terrain response. It was a deliberate stop.

She watched it pivot.

The hull rotated seventeen degrees clockwise, the articulation limbs adjusting to compensate, the whole mass shifting in a slow deliberate arc. The ground vibration changed with it — she could feel the change in the prefab floor, a slight alteration in the frequency as the load redistributed. The pivot took forty-one seconds. Then the machine stopped again and held position for seventy-three seconds.

Maris opened a new log entry and recorded: CROWFOOT-7, 0427:14, intersection of channels J-9 and J-11, stopped, pivoted 17 degrees CW, held 73 seconds. She noted the duration. Then the machine began moving again — not on its previous bearing but on the adjusted one, approximately 155 degrees. It had recalibrated.

*That was not an attack vector. That was a search pattern.*

She had seen search patterns before, in the context of industrial survey drones running grid-coverage protocols. The characteristics were periodic stops, bearing adjustments, resumption of forward motion, systematic coverage. The Behemoth was doing a version of that. It was not charging. It was not accelerating. It was looking for something.

She switched from observer channel to request channel in her neuretics and opened a connection to Vu's command line.

The connection resolved in under two seconds. He picked up but did not speak first. She could hear the tactical environment in the background — vehicle engines, the compressed chatter of unit comms, someone reading a distance aloud.

"Surveyor Delacroix-Oye," she said. "I have been watching this machine since 0411. I need you to hold the ordnance order."

A pause. Not long. "On what grounds?"

"It stopped twice in the last eight minutes and recalibrated. It's not running a transit vector, it's running a search pattern." She pulled up the timestamp log on her secondary screen and kept talking. "I have forty-seven anomaly reports filed on the outer buffer going back six weeks. This machine has been in the approach corridor for at least that long. It hasn't changed velocity or heading outside of those recalibration pivots."

Another pause. She could hear the transmission artifact that sounded like him breathing.

"What are you asking for?"

"Ninety minutes. I need time to identify the search target. If I can identify what it's looking for, I can tell you whether it's going to stop before it reaches the residential tier."

He said, "Sixty minutes," and the cadence did not change at all, which meant he had already decided before she finished talking, which meant he had been watching the same feed she'd been watching and had come to a similar preliminary read.

"Sixty," she said.

"Ordnance is prepped, not active. Cordon holds at 2 klicks. You have until 0531. If it passes the 2-kilometre line before then, the hold ends."

The channel closed.

Maris noted the time: 0429:17. She had sixty-two minutes. The Behemoth was at 2.75 kilometres and closing. At 1.2 kilometres per hour it would reach the 2-kilometre cordon line in thirty-seven minutes and thirty seconds, which gave her twenty-four and a half minutes of buffer before Vu's hold became academic.

She opened her historical survey database and started with the most recent AgriCore field maps she had access to: 2211, 2215, 2218. She went back further.

[Beat 5]
The ping came through her public observation channel at 0433, which was the channel she kept open on her outer-buffer instruments because anomaly reports required corroboration, and corroboration required that other people could see what she was seeing. She accepted the connection.

"You're watching the gait feed," the voice said. It was not a question.

The connection showed as a civilian access code, category: technical operator, which covered former industrial workers with residual maintenance credentials in the zone. The voice was unhurried. There was a pause before the word gait, the way someone pauses before a technical term they are about to use precisely.

"I am," she said. "Who am I talking to?"

"Tamboli-Nkosi. Yusuf. Former AgriCore maintenance, Iowa Behemoth division." Another pause. "I worked on that machine. Eight years ago, before I rotated out of the crop zones."

Maris stopped what she was doing with the historical maps and gave the voice her full attention. "You recognize it by gait."

"Crowfoot series have a characteristic cycle in the left-forward articulation — there's a 0.3-second lag in the return stroke from the mid-position. Not a malfunction. It's a design feature from the third-generation Crowfoot build, tuned for soft-ground stability in high-moisture soil. Every machine off that line walks with it." He paused. "But CROWFOOT-7 specifically had a rebuild on that limb in 2218. The lag came back different. 0.41 seconds. Slightly longer arc."

She looked at her gait analysis data. She had the cycle timing from her sensor array. She pulled up the left-forward articulation specifically and measured the return stroke lag.

0.41 seconds.

"All right," she said. "Tell me what it's doing."

"Crop-cycle protocol," he said. "Standard harvest sequence for territorial field machines. The protocol is: receive deployment coordinates, transit to the assignment area, locate the field boundary marker, begin harvest pattern from marker origin." He said the words like he was reading from a technical manual, or from memory — the cadence was the same either way. "The field boundary marker is a physical object. Titanium spike, roughly forty centimetres, driven into the soil at the origin corner of the assigned plot. The machine has to locate it physically — optically and by ground-contact sensor — before it will initiate the harvest pattern."

"It's searching for the marker," Maris said.

"Yes."

She looked at the Behemoth's recalibration points on her log — the stop at the irrigation channel intersection, the seventeen-degree pivot, the adjusted bearing. "The stops. The bearing corrections."

"It's triangulating. The crop-cycle protocol has a search subroutine for when the marker isn't at the expected position — degraded GPS signal, seasonal ground shift, anything that puts the machine more than twenty metres from the coordinate. It expands the search in a spiral pattern from the last known point." He paused. "It's been running that subroutine for a while. You can tell by how far the bearing has drifted from the original transit vector."

*The machine was not lost. It knew exactly what it was looking for. It simply could not find it.*

She checked her GPS overlay: the search trajectory implied a target coordinate approximately 1.4 kilometres ahead, somewhere in the approach corridor. She needed the exact point.

"I need the deployment coordinate," she said. "The assigned field marker position. Where is it trying to go?"

"I don't have that off-hand. That would be in the mission assignment log — the data the machine carries internally." He paused. "But I can tell you where CROWFOOT-7's assigned harvest zone was in 2218, when I last worked on it. It was assigned to sector 7-Alpha in the Iowa Central Lease block. GPS coordinate 41.8642 north, 87.6244 west."

She typed the coordinate into her overlay and watched it populate on the map.

The pin landed 14.3 kilometres north of her position. It landed in the middle of what her map showed as Zone-11 mid-residential, the dense residential tier built up over the last fifteen years as GLMZ's boundary had pushed northwest.

Maris looked at the coordinate. She looked at the boundary line. The boundary line was where it had been since 2219, when the zone expansion had absorbed the northern buffer zones and extended Zone-11 through what had been the outer agricultural lease block.

"The boundary moved," she said.

"When?"

"2219. Seven years ago."

A long pause. Long enough that she checked the connection was still open.

"The machine doesn't know that," he said. He said it without any particular slowness, without weight — not as a revelation but as a statement of a fact that had just become undeniable.

*The machine had been running the same route for twenty-two years. The world had moved around it and nobody had told it.*

"I need you on-site," she said. "Can you get to Zone-12 outer buffer?"

"I'm already heading for the transit hub," he said. "I've been watching your feed for ten minutes."

She noted the time: 0436:02. She had fifty-five minutes until Vu's hold expired and thirty-one minutes until the Behemoth reached the 2-kilometre cordon line. She updated her timeline. Then she opened her historical map database and started pulling 2219 boundary survey records, because she needed to understand exactly what had been at 41.8642 north, 87.6244 west before Zone-11 residential had been built over it, and whether any trace of a titanium field marker spike might still be in the soil somewhere beneath twenty-three floors of residential architecture.

[Beat 6]
After Tamboli-Nkosi disconnected, Maris sat with the coordinates for forty-three seconds before she did anything with them.

She had the number: 41.8642°N, 87.6244°W. She had his explanation. She had a working hypothesis that she had not yet proven, and the part of her brain that had been filing boundary reports for nine years did not accept working hypotheses. She opened her survey terminal's archive interface and pulled up the Iowa agricultural territorial registry.

The first access level required her Zone-12 observer credentials. The second required a cross-jurisdiction data request that her survey licence technically covered but rarely invoked — she had used it twice in nine years, both times for contamination spill cases where the plume had originated in former crop territory. The third level required a historical data request, which meant specifying a date range: she typed 2190–2205 and watched the interface process the query for fourteen seconds. The fourth level required jurisdictional confirmation that the data was being accessed for boundary analysis rather than property claim, and she confirmed it. The archive returned the file package at 0441.

The 2198 Iowa agricultural survey rendered in pale green contour lines. She found the coordinate, placed a pin.

*Fourteen kilometres.*

At the time of the survey, 41.8642°N, 87.6244°W had been open crop zone. Former cornfield, drainage-optimised, in the outer radius of an AgriCore lease grid. She could see the marker icon in the data — a standardised field terminus point, the kind that Behemoths were programmed to locate and use to anchor their harvest cycle. The kind that was supposed to persist through seasonal cycles and maintenance schedules and the gradual administrative churn of zone management. The kind that persisted because nobody thought to remove it when the land it marked ceased to be cropland.

She tabbed to the territorial archive. Zone-12 northern boundary, amendment history.

The boundary had been redrawn once in the last twenty years. 2219. The note was bureaucratic: Zone-12 expansion paused; residential tier advancement to 41.8612°N corridor; new infrastructure boundary established. Three paragraphs of urban planning language. No mention of legacy crop-zone markers. No mention of autonomous equipment routing.

No mention, because nobody had thought to ask.

*The machine does not know the address changed.*

She pulled the distance again. The target marker was now inside Zone-11 residential. The machine had been walking toward it for seven years. It would keep walking, at its 0.4 km/h transit pace, until it either reached the marker or was stopped. The marker was unreachable. The machine would not stop on its own.

She noted the map scale. She noted the zone amendment date. She noted the coordinate match: the 2198 survey showed the marker at exactly 41.8642°N, 87.6244°W. Yusuf had known the number without looking it up.

The Behemoth was currently 2.8 kilometres from the Zone-12 residential perimeter, according to her optical tracking.

She did not open a new report. There was nothing to report that was not already in the reports she had filed. The anomaly she had documented — 2,847 days of unread anomaly, if Tamboli-Nkosi's figure was correct — had its explanation now. The explanation was that a boundary had moved and nobody had told a machine that would never, under any normal circumstances, have been expected to notice.

She closed the territorial archive. She left the 2198 map open in a sidebar, pinned at 41.8642°N, 87.6244°W, because the data was relevant and she was thorough. She pulled up the Behemoth's last thirty minutes of optical tracking and watched it move south-by-southeast, the same 172-degree bearing she had first noted at 0353. Not wandering. Not searching. In transit.

The horizon to the north still glowed from GLMZ behind her. The ground hummed at sixty-one cycles per second.

She sat with it for two minutes and seventeen seconds. Then she opened her instrument log and entered the time and the archive reference and the amendment date, because she documented everything, and because someday someone might want to understand how a machine had been looking for a field that no longer existed for seven years without anyone noticing.

That was not a report. That was just a record.

[Beat 7]
The Eigenlift hum arrived first — a high-frequency signature at the edge of her instrument range, distinctive in a buffer zone where nothing was supposed to fly below Tier 3 clearance. She picked it up at 0451 on her acoustic array and had the identification before the vehicle was visible: a corporate VTOL, not ArcSec, running standard AgriCore Eastern Division transponder codes.

It came in steep and fast, the way corporate vehicles flew when they were making a point about urgency rather than optimising the approach. The Eigenlift stack brought it down in a controlled drop that still managed to suggest haste. It landed three hundred metres south of her survey post, on the access road that ran parallel to the buffer, and the field grass lay flat in all directions from the aerostatic discharge before the system powered down.

Maris noted the time: 0453.

The woman who stepped off the ramp was checking her wrist display before her feet were on the ground. She walked toward the ArcSec cordon with the kind of pace that meant she had already identified the ranking officer and was prioritising him over the terrain.

"Johansdóttir, Priya. AgriCore Eastern Division Asset Liaison." Her voice arrived on the zone observer channel at 0454, which meant someone at the cordon had opened a command-line patch for her. "I need to speak to whoever's running this response."

"Lieutenant Vũ." Kaden's voice, flat and present. "Move your vehicle thirty metres south, off the access road."

"The vehicle is fine where it is. I need —"

"Thirty metres south. We're keeping the road clear for ordnance units."

A pause. Maris watched the wrist display movement. "Understood. Lieutenant. I need the interdiction order suspended. That machine is a recovery asset. The data core alone has seven years of unbroken harvest logs — that's the kind of continuous record that represents significant stakeholder value and AgriCore's legal right to —"

"Who authorised your flight into this sector?"

"AgriCore Eastern Division has a standing agricultural emergency protocol that covers —"

"I have two ordnance units at the cordon and an unregistered autonomous system 2.9 kilometres from Zone-12's residential tier." The quieter his voice, the more it cost to contradict him. "You are inside the cordon without tactical clearance. Move your vehicle and your recovery personnel behind the hard marker. Two kilometres south of my position."

Maris watched Priya check her wrist display again. Faster this time.

"What residential tier?" The question came out flat, the corporate smoothness briefly absent.

"Zone-12 residential. Boundary at 41.862°N. The machine's current heading puts it at the outer residential fringe in approximately ninety-four minutes at present speed."

Another display check. Then, to someone on her internal channel: "Nobody briefed me on residential proximity."

*Nobody briefed her because she was managing a process. The process did not include a city.*

Maris mapped the geometry from her survey post. Priya's recovery teams — two cargo VTOLs that had set down behind the liaison vehicle, visible on her aerial feed — were currently inside the cordon. They were equipped for heavy extraction. Whatever AgriCore's plan had been before arrival, it had assumed a machine in open buffer zone with no competing authorities. It had not anticipated Kaden Vũ at dawn with ordnance units deployed.

"I'm not asking to move the machine," Priya said. Her voice had recalibrated. Still fast, but the overlap was gone — she was choosing words now. "I'm asking for sixty minutes to document before any neutralisation decision is finalised. Those harvest logs represent seven years of continuous crop data. If the machine is decommissioned without a data pull, that record is gone."

"You have thirty minutes," Kaden said. "From when your teams are behind my marker. Move them now."

The Eigenlift on the cargo VTOLs came online. Maris heard the harmonic shift from her instrument array: the two vehicles rotating to reposition south.

Priya was still at the cordon boundary. Not moving yet. She turned and looked south-southwest, toward the buffer zone, toward a presence that Maris had been tracking for an hour and forty minutes.

She had not looked at the Behemoth until this moment. That was visible even from four hundred metres — the way a person went still when they understood scale for the first time. Forty-two metres of dark agricultural steel on the move, its bioreactor warmth bleeding into the pre-dawn air, its six harvest-limbs articulated at rest against its hull. You could not manage a process from a wrist display when the process was this large and walking toward a city.

Maris noted the time: 0459.

[Beat 8]
The request came through her observer channel at 0507, tagged as a civilian access query: Tamboli-Nkosi, Y., requesting conditional cordon passage, one person, survey-grade monitoring, zero equipment. He had routed it through her specifically. He knew she was the only civilian with active observer status who had been on the channel since 0433.

She pulled up his access profile. Retired AgriCore technician, maintenance specialist, decommissioned-unit division. His credentials had expired in 2219. His observer patch was three hours old, issued by the automated anomaly-response system when she filed her first report. He had, technically, no standing to request cordon access at all.

She approved the request. Then she opened the zone tactical channel.

"Observer Delacroix-Oye. I'm clearing a civilian specialist through the buffer cordon, for purpose of maintenance assessment. His name is Tamboli-Nkosi. He's on foot, no equipment, just his terminal."

"Noted," Kaden said.

*She had cleared a specialist she had no formal authority to clear. Kaden had said one word.*

The objection came from Priya's channel thirty-seven seconds later, fast and overlapping, the way she spoke when she was two steps ahead of a conversation and didn't want to fall behind.

"Observer Delacroix-Oye. Priya Johansdóttir, AgriCore Eastern. I have to flag this. Tamboli-Nkosi, Yusuf — former AgriCore employee, Behemoth maintenance, decommissioned-unit division. He would have proprietary firmware access. If he interfaces with that machine's maintenance port, anything he extracts from the data core falls outside AgriCore's legal custody chain. I need you to —"

"He's not going to extract anything," Maris said.

"You can't confirm that without —"

"He's going to the maintenance port to read the logs on-site. Nothing leaves the machine." She had not asked Yusuf this. She was stating it as a condition.

"The access itself is a firmware interface event. That constitutes a legal —"

"Lieutenant Vũ," Maris said. "Your call."

A pause. Three seconds.

"Tamboli-Nkosi is cleared," Kaden said.

She watched him on the optical feed. He had been standing at the cordon boundary for four minutes, terminal held in his left hand, battery display checked once, not again. He was wearing canvas work gear with no corporate logo, because he had not been corporate for seven years. When Kaden's clearance came through, he did not acknowledge the channel. He pulled up something on his terminal — route, she thought, or maintenance port coordinates — glanced at it for two seconds, and put the terminal in his front pocket.

He did not look at Priya. Not once.

*He is not performing indifference. He genuinely has nothing to say to her.*

He stepped through the cordon gap between two ArcSec units who moved aside without being asked.
