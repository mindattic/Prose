# Bushido Coda

_The blade does not lie. Only the hand holding it._

## Part I: Teeth

[Beat 1]
She said his name wrong. Three times, and Kyle didn't correct her. The apartment smelled like camphor and fear-sweat and something frying in old oil three doors down. Her husband sat in the corner with his jaw wired shut into a brutalist art installation. He breathed through his nose like a man struggling to learn a new woodwind instrument. She pressed the credstick into Kyle's hand — a physical transfer, warm chip, slightly damp. He felt what she'd paid. He felt what she hadn't kept.

[Beat 2]
He took the job at half rate. He told himself it was the jaw. The jaw, wired shut, a man reduced to breathing and waiting. He didn't think about the girl standing in the hallway behind the curtain, watching him the way children watch things they've already decided to memorize.

[Beat 3]
The building was less a structure than a welded disagreement between two towers, twelve stories slotted in and forgotten by the CorpoNation property registry sometime in the 2190s. Kyle had ridden the freight elevator up because the resident elevator had been repurposed as a makeshift dump and an open-air toilet. Looking out the apartment's one window: the canyon between the towers, the rain coming straight down in the cold pillar of air between two structures that had not been designed to leave a gap and had been forced to leave one anyway. On the underside of the maintenance rail bridging the gap, someone had chalked hash marks in tidy groups of seven, the last group stopped at seven and started fresh, never run to eight. A VTOL crossed the gap forty stories up, running lights amber, moving toward something on the south face. Some board director was late to dinner. Some kid was getting their first cyberware. Someone in the Glooms was falling in love for the first time, or being kidnapped for the first time. Kyle filed the running lights and didn't look up again.

[Beat 4]
The motorcycle was parked under an overhang that mostly kept it dry. The overhang was an Axiom cable gantry — the power CorpoNation's hardware, if not its attention — half the block spliced into this forgotten artery because some automated system had never flagged the bleed as significant enough to warrant a repair crew. The math just wasn't there. Kyle's bike was a custom job. Matte black, unbranded, plate magnetized to the underside of the rear cowl for easy swap out. The kind of bike that disappears into the visual noise of any GLMZ block; the kind that's there when you get back because you didn't make it interesting enough for anyone to steal.

[Beat 5]
At Cermak and Loomis the light did the thing it sometimes did at Cermak and Loomis — a quality of flatness, a moment pressed between two surfaces, the shadows of the elevated signage falling at the wrong angle for the time of day and then immediately not. His hardware clocked the anomaly, noted it, and declined to offer an explanation. The GLMZ was full of places like this: intersections where the physics had been used badly at some point in the previous century and had not fully recovered. A 19Hz hum buzzed from no direction and every direction at once. Somewhere down the block a bellwether rat sounded its two-tone cry, high then low, twice, and along the wet storefronts people stepped back into their doorways and stood there saying nothing, and stayed standing there as he passed. Some of them were in the databases. Most weren't. You learned them the way you learned everything useful in this city — by riding through them until they stopped surprising you.

[Beat 6]
He rode south to the Gray Zone's eastern strut. The route was twenty-two minutes of switchbacks down through tiers the architecture had stopped pretending were planned somewhere around the fortieth floor. By the time he was at the bottom of the strut the rain was warmer and the buildings overhead had closed the sky to a strip. He put a hand on the strut wall to steady the bike on the last grade and the ferrocement was warm under his palm, an hour past the last of the light, the way it always was down here. He didn't think about that either. He thought about the jaw. He thought about the camphor smell. He thought about what half-rate meant when half-rate had been pressed warm and damp into your palm by a stranger's hand.

[Beat 7]
He parked the bike a block out and walked the rest. The crew on the loading dock would not be expecting him. Surprise was free.

The loading dock sat at the bottom of the Gray Zone's eastern strut, where the architecture stopped imitating architecture and became what it actually was: welded industrial grief. Kyle came in through the front. The personnel door beside the roll-up had a steel pull handle worn mirror-bright in one long oval, the exact shape of ten thousand hands closing on it in the same place, while the rest of the door went on rusting around it. No hood. No approach angle. He walked in through the front because he wanted the moment — that specific, clarifying moment when six people who have spent weeks feeling like a force of nature looked up and saw the weather approaching without pretense.

[Beat 8]
Kyle is six feet of nothing. That's the first thing. A swimmer's build stripped past lean into something the body shouldn't sustain. His cheekbones throw shadows in low light. His forearms, emerging from rolled sleeves, are rope and tendon and zero forgiveness — no softness anywhere, no insulation, as if the hardware in his skull has been running a going-out-of-business sale on his body mass for years. Thirty-two thousand electrodes in his brain don't come free. The neuretics lattice bills him in calories and he has been paying the invoice in flesh since he was sixteen.

[Beat 9]
The katana across his back is matte black — a 102-centimeter draw, hilt over the right shoulder, the ito wrapping dark with use. The blade has a name; that name is Silence. He has, when pressed, a vague recollection of where the name came from, and does not press. Because digging around in old wounds with dirty fingernails is just asking for trouble.

[Beat 10]
On his low left hip, holstered but not secured, sits the Torii TSS-3 — bird's-head grip, no stock, the silhouette of an oversized revolver under a coat. Five 410 slugs in the cylinder. Cacophony. This one he did name himself. Silence was for the rooms he could cross. Cacophony was for the ones he couldn't — the distance the body couldn't eat fast enough, the angle the blade couldn't reach. He kept the count the way other men kept time. Five shells was five chances to discover you should have planned better, and Kyle planned for a living, which meant Cacophony mostly stayed quiet, which suited the joke of the thing — the loudest object in his loadout was the one he most preferred not to use.

[Beat 11]
Kyle stepped across the threshold that separates the weather from the room, and stood still, and let them look.

One of them had a chrome arm — a full replacement job, the shoulder-socket kind, myomer-bundled, hydraulic-assisted, capable of punching through light vehicle plating. Across the table from him sat a woman the array flagged before Kyle's eyes found a reason to: slight, still, her weight already on the balls of her feet, and hardware nested at every hinge of her — actuator housings at the wrists, the elbows, the knees, the toes of her boots. Retractable blades. A body renovated joint by joint into a knife block, by someone with skill, for someone with intent, and nothing about the work decorative. One had sub-dermal thoracic plating, the seams visible at his collar like a man wearing himself wrong. One had a facial modification at the jaw that had been done badly enough to pull the skin tight on the left side — someone who'd needed it fast and cheap, who had a history of getting his face broken and had run down the original parts. The fifth was younger, chrome at the knuckles only — discharge plates, the kind that spit sparks on contact and let a twenty-year-old hit like a forklift, provided whatever he hit agreed to stand still. He was already rolling his shoulder. The sixth was the one Kyle tracked first — no visible augmentation, which meant internal, which meant expensive, which meant he was the one who'd made the decisions.

[Beat 12]
They were playing cards. Drinking something amber from unlabeled bottles. The laughter died at the pace that laughter always dies when the math changes in a room.

[Beat 13]
Kyle's chest warmed — the array. A deep, cellular warmth behind the sternum, like a coal blown to life, the firmware coming up from idle into the resolution it kept for rooms like this. Thirty-two thousand electrodes, two hundred and fifty-six threads, processing at a speed his mouth could not follow.

The room rendered itself. Six bodies. Heat signatures. The capacitor whine of knuckle plates waking up at the card table. The doubled click of wrist housings unlocking under a woman's sleeves, discreet as a watch being wound. The boy's shoulder telegraphing its intention before the intention had finished forming.

*Ballistic precognition* — the NeoCortex program's term, which Kyle had always found imprecise. The array did not predict. It read the half-second already in motion and put it on the page where he could read it too, and his body was already moving before the thought resolved. He had stopped trying to explain the distinction to anyone who asked.

It rendered one thing more, and rendered it the way it rendered nothing else: the plumbing. Every major artery in the room laid in as a bright forbidden line, the body's own load-bearing infrastructure annotated in light. Chrome was fair game. The lines were law. Cut everything; cross nothing. That was the whole of the code, and the array kept the map so the hand could keep the promise.

[Beat 14]
Silence cleared the saya in one continuous draw — right hand over the right shoulder, the steel-carbon-nanotube meta alloy blade alive in the air at the bottom of the motion. No flourish. The kawagane was matte black and stayed matte black and the dock's amber overheads found nothing on the blade to reflect because reflection was not what the blade did. He brought it across his body and settled into the stance and his right foot slid back a quarter step into the line Seo had drilled him into for nine years before letting him work alone.

The stance was the stance. The specific afternoon the foot-line had been corrected for the last time; the exact memory he could not locate, which had stopped bothering him at some point he also could not locate.

[Beat 15]
Cacophony stayed on his hip.

The room was crossable. That was the whole calculus, run before his boot crossed the threshold: twenty-some feet of dock floor, six bodies, a card table, and a route between them the array had drawn in the half-second before anyone thought to stand. The gun was for plans that failed. He had not failed to plan.

Silence moved. What the room heard was almost nothing — a chair going over, a bottle ringing on concrete, the wet exhale of men understanding that the weather had already arrived. The loudest sound on the dock was still chrome hitting the floor. The blood made no sound at all. It simply went where the cuts sent it, and every cut had been told where to stop.

[Beat 16]
The boy threw first, because the boy had thrown first his whole short career. The haymaker came up out of the chair with his hips and his heart in it, knuckle plates discharging on the upswing so that the fist swung round trailing sparks like a grinder kissing steel. It was a proven punch. It had ended arguments. It had broken jaws — older jaws, slower jaws, jaws attached to men who had asked for one more week and stood there hoping while it wound up — and every jaw it broke had taught the boy the same wrong lesson, which was that the punch worked.

The punch worked on people who could not afford to move. Kyle had already moved. He stepped inside the arc — not away from it, inside it, into the dead zone where a haymaker is nothing but a man falling in a circle — set his palm against the boy's shoulder blade as the mass went by, and helped. Physics handled the rest. The card table took him at the waist and folded; cards and amber liquor went up; the sparking knuckles hit the wet floor and ground their charge out in it, fizzing like the end of a fuse. And somewhere inside that fall, almost in passing, Silence had opened the boy's forearm along the flexor line — four centimeters, clean, deep enough to unstring the fist, wide of the radial artery by the width of a decision — so that when he put his hand down to rise, the hand declined. He sat back down in the wreck of the table, holding his arm, watching his blood feather into the spilled liquor, and learned what every collector learns the night the arithmetic reverses: the punch had never been the weapon. The fear had been the weapon, and Kyle hadn't brought any.

[Beat 17]
The second cut found the sub-dermal plating man as he rose — but he rose shooting. A compact auto off his belt, three rounds inside the half-second the array needed to close the line: two into Kyle's lower back as he turned through the second man's lane, a third skipping off at an angle that burned a furrow above his hip. The mesh under his skin took the two square hits the way a net takes a diver — total arrest, no penetration, and a cost the net does not advertise. The breath went out of him in one flat concussion. His line shifted a handspan off its mark, and for the duration of one shortened step the room contained the possibility of a different ending.

The plating man had organized his entire fighting philosophy around the assumption that nothing would penetrate his vitals, and nothing did. Silence took the power coupling at his collar seam instead — the thumb-wide gap where the thoracic plate drank from his spinal bus — and the stroke's exit wrote a thin red line across the trapezius above it, shallow as a caution, blood welling up over armor that had kept every promise except the one that mattered. Sixty pounds of plate went from asset to anchor between one heartbeat and the next. He folded under his own chrome and stayed folded, the pistol skating away from him across the corrugated steel. He'd been in fights before. He knew he was out of this one before it had properly begun. His only hope now was to stay still. And quiet. Still and quiet, and bleeding politely, and the only thing of his that died tonight would be hardware.

[Beat 18]
The chrome-arm man was already swinging by then, which was the correct response from a fighter with hydraulic-assisted reach and a head start on the geometry. The arm came around flat and hard at the height where Kyle's collarbone had been, torque enough behind it to cave a vehicle door, and the array read the motor bus the way it read all chrome — clean electromagnetic bloom, full lateral extension, intercept at the throat.

You do not block an arm like that. You audit it. Kyle stepped under the arc and let the better part of a decade of financing swing through the space he had stopped occupying, and as it passed overhead he brought Silence up through the shoulder seam — the thumb-wide service gap where the actuator cabling surfaced between housing and socket, the exposed wire bundle every full-replacement rig carries and every owner forgets — and severed the bus in the time the swing took to finish believing in itself. The arm completed its arc without instructions. The mass that powered it kept going, took the man's balance along as a courtesy, and laid him out across a stack of crates with the limb still flexing on residual hydraulic pressure — formidable, severed, salvage. He stared at it with the precise expression of a man watching eleven years of payments declare themselves a total loss, and he did not get back up.

[Beat 19]
The fourth cut took the badly-done jaw man's jaw — the rig, not the jaw, though the jaw paid its tithe. Kyle had saved it for the count's last beat because the array had already mapped what would happen: the anchor line of the cheap facial armature, the shear angle, the way the hardware would skid into the drainage channel when it let go. The array had been correct in the way the array was always correct about the physics of falling chrome. The rig tore free with the sound of cheap work surrendering, and the gums the bad surgery had conscripted as mounting posts came away bleeding around the anchor points, and the man went down to his knees with both hands at his face, spitting red into the runoff — alive, awake, and ruined in the same cheap way he had already been ruined once and had financed rather than fixed. The teeth went with the rig, scattering across the floor like a macabre game of dice.

[Beat 20]
Four seconds. Four cuts. Four down and not getting up, and every one of them alive, and the dock floor already threading thin red lines toward the drainage channels — every line of it placed. The array held the map of what a body could lose and keep living, and Silence had gone wide of every bright forbidden line by margins Kyle no longer had to think about, because the thinking had been done years ago, in a dojo he could not entirely locate, and the array merely kept the books.

He stood in the middle of it with two points of deep percussion blooming under the mesh in his lower back and a wet line above his hip where the third round had opened skin, the torn jacket showing a few square centimeters of the dull grey weave underneath — the lattice doing the one thing it existed to do, which was not the same as doing it for free. He had read the plating man as armor and missed the pistol. That was the mistake, and he was wearing it now, and he would be wearing it tomorrow. The array had not seen it coming because the array read chrome — motor buses, power couplings, the electromagnetic bloom of hardware declaring its intentions — and the gun had been nothing but a gun.

Cacophony's cylinder still held five, and the count stayed unspent, because the woman had not rushed him. That was the thing worth knowing about her, and it had been worth knowing since the threshold: while her crew spent itself, she had stepped back out of the spray, chosen clean floor, and let the blades out.

[Beat 21]
They came out of her the way good hardware comes out of good cabinetry — the wrists first, twin lengths of pale-edged alloy running back along the forearms; then the elbows, short and triangular, built for the turn; and when she rose onto the balls of her feet the boot toes opened and showed steel, and a flexed knee showed more. Eight edges on a slight frame, every housing flush, every deployment silent except for that doubled click she hadn't bothered to disguise. The array lit her up like a switchboard — actuator blooms at every joint — and priced her honestly: faster than the boy, smarter than the swing, the only fighter on this dock besides the seated man who had ever trained against people who knew what they were doing.

She came in on an angle that took the card table out of his footwork and put the crates at his back, which meant she had been reading the dock as a fighting floor while her crew had been reading it as furniture. Kyle revised her price upward and gave ground exactly once, to put the spilled liquor between them, because wet floor taxes whoever lunges.

[Beat 22]
The first pass was hers. Wrist blade, backhand line; a feint off the elbow that became the knee; three edges inside a second and a half, and the array read each actuator fire its half-second early, and Kyle was never quite where the edges arrived. He didn't counter. A counter is a bid, and he was still pricing the table. She spun out of the third strike clean, reset her angle, and came again — committed this time, full extension, her whole slight mass behind the wrist blade, because from where she stood the half-second was invisible and a man who keeps almost being hit looks like a man getting lucky, and you press luck before it settles.

The commitment was the door he had given ground to build. Kyle turned his hips off the line, received her forearm on his free palm the way you receive something handed to you, and added four inches to a lunge that had been measured to the centimeter. Past full extension, a blade is cargo. While she paid for those four inches, Silence traced the underside of her forearm — not the flesh line, the service line: the shallow channel where the wrist actuator's cable bundle surfaced between housing and grip, the maintenance gap her conversion clinic had promised her no one would ever find in a live fight. The bundle parted with a static pop. The wrist blade died at half-extension, locked, a dead weight bolted to a living arm.

She was good; she converted without a breath, rolling through into the elbow blade on the turn — but it was the same turn the array had now watched twice, and Kyle was already under it, and Silence crossed the back of her thigh above the knee housing. A hand's width of opened muscle, depth governed to the millimeter, wide of the femoral line by the same deliberate margin as every cut tonight, and exactly deep enough that the leg resigned. She went down onto the knee whose toe blade scraped one bright useless arc in the corrugated steel, and she did not scream. She exhaled — long, furious, professional — put her free hand flat on the floor, and retracted everything that still answered, one joint at a time: the surrender of someone who understood precisely what had been left intact, and that it had been left on purpose.

[Beat 23]
Silence found the seams, and where there were no seams it found the depth — the exact depth at which a body stops fighting and keeps living. That was the whole instrument: a sword in a room full of chrome, a scalpel in a room full of meat, and a hand that knew which room it was standing in from one cut to the next. Five down, and not one of them dying. Not because the blade had been gentle — because the blade had been exact, and exactness was the only mercy on offer. The dock floor looked like a chop shop after a raid — an arm, a forearm, a jaw rig, a wrist blade locked at half-mast — and through the salvage the blood ran thin and honest toward the channels, every line of it survivable, every line of it placed there on purpose, which was the part the survivors would think about later, in the quiet, with the lights off.

[Beat 24]
Kyle was already turning.

[Beat 25]
The boss had stood up.

[Beat 26]
Kyle was already turning when the boss stood up. He had not drawn during the four-cut count. He had not drawn while the bladed woman was being dismantled an edge at a time. He had stood up with the unhurried economy of a man who had sat at a table and watched his crew dismantled and had decided, calmly, that it was now his turn — and the hand that went to his hip was not reaching for a sidearm. He carried his own katana. Kyle had not registered the weapon earlier because the saya was inside the long coat, no protrusion, no tell, no detail the array had a category for in a room full of chrome. The boss stepped clear of the table, chose his ground with his eyes before his feet took it, and set his heels on the one stretch of dock floor that held neither cards nor chrome nor blood. House manners. Old ones.

[Beat 27]
The boss drew. Clean. The blade came out at the right angle in the right tempo, point neither high nor low, weight settling into a guard that was not borrowed from any school Kyle could name and which therefore was not a school's guard at all but a man's. He had been trained — specifically, somewhere expensive, for a duel he had no reason to believe would ever be granted to him. Kyle could see it in the way his weight stayed off his front foot, in the way he held the line: a man spending something he had been saving for a long time.

Then the boss did the thing the century had no category for: he bowed. Shallow, eyes up, hands where they were — the bow of a man acknowledging the other half of his own education. Around them the dock dripped and an amber running light crawled across the gap forty stories up and five people lay quietly bleeding into the concrete — blood and money both — and inside all of it the two swords made a small, older room. Kyle returned the bow to the centimeter. You honor the form, or you are just a man holding a museum piece.

[Beat 28]
*The duel. He has been waiting for the duel. The chrome was the ticket price.*

[Beat 29]
*He has been saving this his whole life. And he stood up with a sword when there was a perfectly good exit behind him. Respect that. Answer it properly.*

[Beat 30]
Neither moved. The rain worked the roof. Somewhere under the dock a pump cycled, and the severed forearm relaxed its fingers with a sound like a dropped glove, and neither man's eyes went to it.

Then they closed.

[Beat 31]
First exchange — the boss tested the line. A short measured cut at Kyle's right shoulder, no commitment in it, the kind of strike a swordsman uses to read his opponent's reach and tempo and the specific way he holds his weight. Silence rose to meet it. The blades touched edge to flat at a shallow angle. Steel rang, a clean sustained note in the concrete, and the force came back through Kyle's wrist into Kyle's shoulder into Kyle's stance the way force had been coming back through swords since the iron age. The boss recognized the read he had been given — Kyle's reach, Kyle's tempo — and stepped back without expression. He had wanted that information and he had paid one exchange for it. His face showed nothing. His weight stayed off the front foot. But his guard moved a quarter inch in a way that the array catalogued and filed.

[Beat 32]
*Patient. Correct. Rehearsed. He has fought this duel a thousand times in training and never once against a stranger. Use it.*

[Beat 33]
Second exchange — Kyle pressed. A two-step advance, the cut coming high and right, and the boss met it with a parry that was not a parry but a deflection, his blade sliding along Silence's flat to redirect the energy past his shoulder. Smart. Conservative. He had watched four cuts cross a room in three seconds and he was not going to bet his line against that speed; he would spend himself carefully or not at all. Kyle adjusted. The boss had told him something in return: *I will not let you charge me cleanly. I will work the angles.*

[Beat 34]
*He's fighting the duel he rehearsed. You are fighting him. These are different fights.*

[Beat 35]
Third exchange — the boss came in committed. He had read enough. He cut high left, fast, and Kyle's parry was a fraction late because the array's micro-tremor read on the boss had been clean but the boss's body had been clean in return, no telegraph, no shoulder bloom, just a man who had decided to commit and committed. Silence took the cut at the proper angle but the boss had already let the blade slide off the parry into a follow-through, and the follow-through grazed Kyle's left forearm — three inches of skin opened, not deep, the cut shallow enough that the subdermal mesh registered the pressure and decided not to engage. Blood came up immediately. Kyle's interior catalogue tagged it without comment: *vein not artery, two centimeters lateral of the radial, will not bleed out, will impair grip on the shotgun side in approximately ninety seconds.*

Kyle stepped out of measure and dipped his point an inch — the old acknowledgment, the dojo's nod: the touch is yours. The boss received it without expression, but his shoulders came down a millimeter, the way a man settles when the stranger across from him turns out to speak his language.

[Beat 36]
The boss saw the blood. He understood what the cut meant — that he was inside Kyle's range, that he had landed first, that the fastest thing he had ever seen cross a room could be touched. A man can be forgiven for letting that knowledge move his weight forward. Confidence travels downhill, from the eyes to the hands to the feet, and the boss's feet were already a half-step more committed than they had been an exchange ago.

[Beat 37]
*Laugh or you'd cry. One touch and he is already writing the story of the night. He has stopped studying you and started believing the rehearsal. Let him.*

[Beat 38]
Fourth exchange — the boss thrust. A straight committed thrust at Kyle's center mass, the kind of strike that asked everything of the blade and the arm and the line, and Silence came down to meet it not at the edge and not at the flat but at the seppa — the small collar between the tsuba and the blade, the part of the sword that classical schools said you should never use — and the thrust caught there, blade to blade, locked. Kyle drove forward. He kept the lock. He let Silence's edge ride along the flat of the boss's blade like a skate on ice, the edge tracking down the boss's steel from the tip toward the middle toward the tsuba, and where the boss's blade met the boss's hand, the edge of Silence met the boss's right wrist.

[Beat 39]
The edge met the wrist — and the wrist was not flesh. Lab-grown dermis parted over an actuator collar; carbon-fiber carpals; a myomer bundle wound tighter than any tendon ever managed. Covert chrome, tower-grade, the kind that costs more for looking like nothing than other men's hardware costs for looking like war. The internal augmentation the array had priced from the doorway had been under the sword hand all along. Silence did not care about that either. The edge passed through the hand's architecture the way it passed through anything when the angle was right and the line was committed, because Silence was a sword, and what swords cared about was the geometry of where the edge met the joint, and the geometry was correct.

[Beat 40]
The boss's right hand and the boss's katana fell together to the corrugated steel. No blood followed them down. The stump glistened — a clean cross-section of sealed lines and silvered bone-analogue, engineering some tower surgical team had been very proud of — and the neural bus sent enough static up his arm that his knees went, not from any shock of the body but from the shock of the wiring. He took the fall the way he had taken his guard: correctly. He came down kneeling. He looked at his hand on the floor, still holding his sword. He looked at Kyle. He said something in a language Kyle did not speak — three words, very quiet, possibly a name, possibly a prayer, possibly an instruction to whoever had trained him — and then, from his knees, with his ruin in front of him, he bowed. Kyle bowed back. Deeper than the first time. The duel had been real. Both of them had paid for it honestly.

[Beat 41]
He did not bleed out. There was nothing to bleed. The array, which timed everything, priced it instead: more Φ lying on the corrugated steel in that one severed hand than the rest of the crew had spent on themselves combined, and no clinic in the Glooms able to reattach what a sword had cross-sectioned at that angle. Flesh heals for free. Chrome bills at list price. Everyone on that floor would walk out of the Gray Zone tonight, stitched and limping and alive, and everyone on that floor would spend years paying for it in installments. That was the sentence. The mercy was incidental.

[Beat 42]
Twenty-three seconds for the duel. Thirty-four on the dock floor end to end.

It should have been twenty. The duel he would replay for weeks was the duel as fought: half a beat slow on the third exchange, the breath not arriving where the form expected it, the rotation pulling short over the bruising in his lower back — and the boss had seen the short side, and gone at it, and been correct to. The follow-through that opened Kyle's left forearm lived inside that half-beat. He had won on line, not on speed. The difference had a price, and he was still bleeding it.

[Beat 43]
Kyle cleared Silence with one flat snap of the wrist — chiburi, the old form, a thin line of the crew's blood leaving the steel to join what they had already lost — and then wiped the blade properly along his sleeve, edge away, two passes, before the saya took it. A gray smear of actuator fluid went with the blood: the evening's full forensic record, written in two inks. He felt for the edge with three fingers along the flat as the blade seated. No new fractures. The boss's katana he left exactly where it lay, in the hand that had earned it — you do not take a swordsman's sword, even when you have taken everything it was for. Cacophony had never left the holster. Five in the cylinder. He registered the count the way other people registered the time: unspent.

[Beat 44]
He looked at the dock floor. An arm, a forearm, a jaw rig, a wrist blade locked at half-extension, a compact auto gone cold where the plating man had dropped it, and one hand still wrapped around a katana. Most of a decade of installment payments, laid out on corrugated steel, going dark. The blood was already finding the drainage channels. The blood would stop. The payments wouldn't.

[Beat 45]
Kyle's hands were steady. They would shake in four minutes. He had learned to use the window.

[Beat 46]
He moved through the bodies. Methodical. The badly-done jaw man was face-down beside the drainage channel where the fourth cut had unmade his afternoon of cheap surgery, cheek against the corrugated steel, breath fogging in the runoff. Kyle crouched. He found the jaw rig where it had skidded, and the teeth the bad work had conscripted as mounting posts — two lying loose in the runoff, one still seated in the dead hardware and requiring grip and twist and a specific application of force that Kyle performed without expression, against the rig, not the man. He collected all three in his left hand, wet and slick and smelling of saliva and machine oil, and he did not think about what he was doing in terms that required examination.

[Beat 47]
*Her father's jaw. These three teeth. The math of a correspondence.*

[Beat 48]
He was still crouched at the drainage channel when he registered the breathing behind the ventilation unit. Quick. Shallow. Not panic — panic doesn't hold that long without breaking. This was something controlled. Something stubborn. He'd tracked it since the threshold. He'd known it was there. ## Interlude I: Something Fixed

[Beat 103]
The hinge on Kyle's closet door had been misaligned for four months. He knew it the way he knew the precise weight of Cacophony on his left hip and the specific sound the landing made three steps from the top. The door dragged at the bottom when you opened it past forty-five degrees and caught again when you closed it. He had adjusted his use of the closet accordingly.

Pixel was across the hall with her tool bag before he had finished his first coffee.

She didn't knock as much as announce herself — one knuckle, the door already opening. She had a flathead and a Phillips and a small plastic level she set on the floor beside the closet before she'd said anything past *morning.*

[Beat 104]
Kyle watched her from the kitchen. She wore the flannel she slept in and her hair was still compressed from the pillow. Her feet were bare on his concrete floor.

"Left three nights ago," he said.

She looked up from the level. "What?" she asked.

"The hinge. Started dragging three months ago, not four."

Pixel looked back at the door. "Three months is still too long," she said.

[Beat 105]
She had the screws out in under two minutes. The hinge plate had worked loose at one anchor, and she re-drilled at a sharper angle — the bit catching on old concrete, a dry smell of ground chalk — then pressed a plug of something gray into the cavity with her thumb before she drove the screw home. The anchor was an old Axiom collar, the company thirty years dead and its hardware still holding half the doors in the district. The whole operation took eleven minutes. She tested the door twice — full open, full close, no drag — and seemed satisfied in the way a calibration satisfies: precisely and without ceremony.

When she packed the tools back into the bag, she didn't move toward the door right away.

[Beat 106]
"You eat?" she asked.

"Coffee."

She made a short sound through her nose. She sat on the arm of the couch and held the tool bag in her lap. Kyle stayed in the kitchen doorway.

"The door," she said, "has been dragging since before the Depot job. The one that came in on a Friday and left the smell of burnt insulation in your jacket for three days after."

Kyle said nothing.

"I know because I heard you going in and out at night and the drag was there when you left and not when you came back. Different handling when your hands are full."

He looked at her. He became aware that he was holding his breath and let it go. She looked back. She didn't say what the reading was. She didn't have to.

[Beat 107]
She stood and went across the hall and closed her own door without any particular hurry.

Kyle stayed in the kitchen doorway a moment longer. He wasn't sure when it had shifted from her knowing him to her keeping track of him.

He went and opened the closet. Full open. Full close. No drag. He put his hand on the hinge plate for a moment, then let it go.

## Street Meat

[Beat 108]
The stairs up to Hua's kitchen ran past a pawnshop with a decommissioned tactical hardshell bolted to the wall outside its grate. A finch had moved into the open codpiece and built a nest. The pawnbroker was filling a water dish balanced on the gauntlet when Kyle passed. Three eggs. Nobody had stolen the armor.

The kitchen on the second floor was hot and loud and smelled almost exactly like Mrs. Chen's — pork bone, fermented bean, chili oil burned onto steel. Almost. Close enough to be on purpose. Hua had been running operations four blocks from Mrs. Chen's stall for years, Mrs. Chen had never mentioned it, and Hua had picked this room knowing what the smell would do to him.

He sat down across the prep table anyway.

[Beat 109]
"Extraction," Hua said. No greeting. A thin folder hit the steel between them. "Atlas-program continuation lab. Sealed cargo. Out."

"And the cargo?"

She looked at him. The folder stayed closed under her hand.

"You'll know what to do with it when you see it. I'm not telling you more, because telling you would frame the decision in advance." A beat. "The decision is yours."

Behind her, the line cook dropped a rack of skewers on the pass. The wok hit the burner ring twice, a flat bright note like a struck bell. Nobody in the kitchen looked at their table.

[Beat 110]
Kyle opened the folder. Eight lines. No cargo name, no contacts, just a facility in the northern outskirts that the CorpoNation registry listed as long-term storage. He'd scouted it twice from the outside. Long-term storage didn't need a security envelope two tiers deep, and it didn't sit on a dead Axiom shell where the cameras never quite slept.

He read the delivery method three times.

"You want me to die."

"I want you to be delivered," Hua said. "The facility buys bodies. Carrion Enterprises holds the disposal contract — pickups go in through the morgue intake on the north dock, no scanner, no escort, a nurse who signs without looking. It is the only door that opens from the outside." She turned the folder back around with one finger. "Dead is the credential."

[Beat 111]
The pitch was one line near the bottom, and she'd known he'd reach it: the facility had been running an Atlas-program variant since 2218.

The Atlas program. The people who'd seeded hardware like his into children too young to be asked. His file on the program held five names, four of them with no current route, and one question he'd been carrying since he was sixteen. Three years he'd been looking for a way inside anything with Atlas in its blood.

"My hardware can fake it," Kyle said. "That's not the problem. The problem is the ride. A dead man can't watch his own body. Whoever drives that bus owns me for an hour — and Carrion crews part out better cargo than me on slow nights."

[Beat 112]
"Yes," Hua said. "So you will need a body man you can trust."

"There's no such thing."

"No," she agreed. "There isn't." She slid the folder across. "Build one."

[Beat 113]
Carrion Enterprises ran its Tier 3 fleet out of a depot on Ashland where the wash bays never stopped. Sixteen buses, matte gray, the carrion-bird patch small and tasteful on the doors, because the company had gone semi-legitimate the way a knife goes into a drawer — same knife, better optics. Body disposal on municipal contract. Organ repossession on CorpoNation paper. The crews still called themselves Vultures. The billing department did not.

Kyle sat in a noodle bar across the street for two evenings, eating slowly, watching shift change. He wasn't looking for a good man. He was looking for a man with a crack in him, and he already knew the man's name.

[Beat 114]
Bus 31. Crew chief: Renko Moss. Eleven years on the meat run, a fat man's laugh in a thin man's body, hands certified for field cardio work — Carrion crews carried defibrillators for the customers whose organs were worth more warm.

Fourteen months ago, on a job that had nothing to do with Moss, Kyle had been on a rooftop on 47th when bus 31 took a pickup off the street below. The pickup was a Tollgate lineman with a financed heart, called in dead by an address drone. Kyle had watched Moss kneel over the man, check the pulse, and find one.

He'd watched Moss look up and down the empty street.

Then he'd watched Moss sign the slate — no signs of life — because a dead man's heart was a repossession at full margin and a live man's heart was a hospital's problem, and the difference was eleven thousand and the street was empty.

[Beat 115]
Except it wasn't, because Kyle was on it, forty meters up with his optics running, and Kyle kept receipts.

The lineman had lived. That was the part Moss didn't know. Kyle had come down off the roof after the bus left, found the man breathing in the recovery position the drone had filed him in, and carried him four blocks to a clinic that asked no questions because Kyle paid it not to. It had cost him his evening and two hundred Φ, and he'd kept the manifest copy his optics had pulled off Moss's slate, because keeping it cost nothing and the world had a way of making receipts useful.

[Beat 116]
Now he crossed Ashland in the rain, walked into the wash bay where Moss was hosing fluid off a gurney, and held up the manifest where the man could read it.

Moss read it. The hose kept running, flat on the concrete.

"That's a forgery."

"The lineman's alive," Kyle said. "Works a substation in Pilsen now. Different heart — the clinic pulled the financed one and fitted a gray-market unit, so Tollgate's repo file is still open, which means the case is still open. He remembers the bird on the door. He'd remember your face."

Moss shut off the hose. He was very calm, the way men are calm when they're choosing between two animals inside themselves.

"You want money."

[Beat 117]
"No."

"Then you want something worse."

"I want a pickup," Kyle said. "One ride. I'm the body."

Moss stared at him. Then he laughed, the fat man's laugh, no joy anywhere in it. "You want into Northpoint. You absolute lunatic. You want me to sign you in dead and roll you through the one door in the Glooms that God himself doesn't audit." He threw the hose into the drain channel. "And if I just put a real bolt in you instead and part you out? Solves my manifest problem."

"The receipts are with two people you can't find," Kyle said. "If I don't walk out, they walk in. And you don't have a manifest problem, Moss. You have a me problem. The manifest is just the leash."

The wash bay dripped. Out on the lot, somebody's bus started up, throttled, settled.

"One ride," Moss said. "And after, I'm done. Paper burned."

"Paper burned," Kyle said. "Standard rate for your time."

"You're going to pay me? For blackmailing me?"

"I pay for work," Kyle said. "The blackmail's free."

[Beat 118]
Pixel found out the way Pixel found out everything, which was that he told her, at her kitchen table, because there was a version of this where the next time she saw him was on a slab for real, and he wasn't going to let that version arrive unannounced.

She didn't say anything for a while. She took his left wrist and turned it over and pressed two fingers there like she was confirming something still ran.

"Cardiac arrest," she said. "On purpose. Field paddles, off-protocol, administered by a man who hates you."

"He doesn't hate me. He resents me. Hate costs extra."

"And your array catches you on the way down." She wasn't asking. She'd already pulled the specs into her bench display: the Atlas hardware's partition routine, the autonomic clamp, the intake-mimic profile that would read to any scanner as four hours dead. "There's a window, Kyle. Between the arrest and the clamp. Eight seconds, maybe ten, where you are not pretending." She looked up at him. "Where you're just a man somebody stopped."

[Beat 119]
"Then we should make sure Moss charges the paddles right."

"That's the joke you're going with."

"It's the only one I brought."

She put his calibration through twice that night. She didn't play music while she did it. At the door, she said it to his back, the thing she said when the night wasn't finished with him:

"Eat something before you die."

[Beat 120]
The ambulance smelled like disinfectant losing an argument. Kyle lay on the gurney stripped to the waist, contact gel cold on his chest, while bus 31 ran the lower arterials north and the city came up through the floor — recycler exhaust, the wet-metal smell of an atmospheric processor working a slow rain, the Pulse running slugs through ferrocrete too deep to hear.

Moss rode in back with him. Up front, a driver who hadn't been introduced, because crews don't introduce themselves to corpses.

"Northpoint intake reads core temp, pulse, and the death cert on the slate," Moss said, taping the second paddle lead down with more force than tape requires. "It does not read carefully, because nobody breaks into a body farm. You'll have four hours before the cooler rotation. After that you're inventory, and inventory gets opened."

"Four hours."

"And your hardware does — whatever it does." Moss looked at the readout, then at Kyle, and his face did something between disgust and arithmetic. "You know what I do to people who run games on me."

[Beat 121]
"You sign them out dead," Kyle said. "Lucky me. I'm volunteering."

At the Halsted checkpoint, a Crucible Genomics scanner pulsed the cargo bay amber and waved them through. A few months on this corridor, and they were still checking for the things a biotech corpo checks for. A live man asking to be killed wasn't on the list.

Moss charged the paddles. The whine climbed and held.

"Last chance to be a smarter man."

"Receipts, Moss."

[Beat 122]
"Yeah." He set the paddles. His jaw worked once. "I should throw you in the trash, you son of a bitch."

"You will," Kyle said. "Aim for the heart."

The world went white, then specific: the taste of metal, Pixel's two fingers on his wrist, a stairwell railing from 2222 under hands that were almost his. Then the clamp came down and folded him, labeled him, and put him in the drawer.

The last thing in the drawer with him was the count. Eight seconds, maybe ten, where he was just a man somebody stopped.

He didn't get to know how many it took.

[Beat 123]
Cold. Steel. Seventeen degrees, and the smell that only exists in one kind of room.

First rule, kept like a tooth for nineteen years: do not move until the cortex is all the way back. Dead men don't twitch. He lay with his eyes shut and let the neuretics walk itself home — autonomics, then time, then the count.

Four minutes. He sat up.

Morgue intake, Tier 4, retrofitted — the light hung for a different ceiling. Two other slabs, both occupied, both occupants the real thing. His chest carried two paddle burns, ringed and raw, the exact shape of Moss's resentment. The room had not been visited since his arrival. The plan budgeted four to forty minutes for an intake nurse. His count put it at eleven.

He took stock of his hands. Made fists. Opened them.

They felt like his.

[Beat 124]
"Proceeding on that assumption," he said, to nobody.

His clothes were a bag in facility storage. The case with Silence and Cacophony was, if Hua's map held, sitting at the external supply intake on the north dock with a Carrion sticker on it. Between him and everything he owned: a facility that bought bodies, four hours of cooler rotation, and a sealed cargo nobody would name.

Down the corridor, a wheeled cart was rolling closer.

He went out the door the other way — bare feet silent on cold composite, counting doors, hunting.

[Beat 125]
He found scrubs in a supply alcove and a dead man's clearance fob in a laundry bin, because facilities that buy bodies get sloppy about the things bodies can't use. The sub-levels ran quiet. Whatever Northpoint stored long-term, it didn't generate visitors.

Level B3 was colder than the morgue.

The room at the end of B3 held forty sealed units in racks, each the size of a coffin and engineered by someone who'd never once thought the word. Thirty-nine of them dark. One of them running — power feed humming, status strip amber, condensation bearding the seam. The unit's plate read ATLAS-9 CONTINUITY, and under that, in the hand-lettering of a technician who'd never expected an audience: DO NOT ROTATE. STANDING ORDER. ASK MARROW.

Kyle stood in front of it for a moment. Hua's voice in the folder: you'll know what to do with it when you see it.

[Beat 126]
He cracked the seal.

Inside, packed in gel webbing and breathing two breaths a minute, was a woman in her sixties. Gray hair in a braid gone loose. Hands folded on her sternum like someone had arranged them and felt bad about it. A medical bracelet, old-style, physical: N. OKAFOR-VOSS, and a date of intake eleven years back.

Eleven years. The same eleven years the hum had been in his chest.

He could close the lid. That was a real option and he made himself look at it: close the lid, find the supply dock, walk out clean, tell Hua the cargo was a question he'd declined to answer. The facility would never know. The woman would never know. Nobody would ever know except him, and he'd carried heavier.

The wake-cycle on the unit was a single mechanical dial, because the engineers had trusted technicians and not networks. That told him something too. Whatever she was, somebody had wanted her findable by hands and invisible to every system in the city.

[Beat 127]
He turned the dial.

It took eleven minutes, and he spent them watching the door with a fire extinguisher for a weapon, and then the gel drained and the woman opened her eyes like a person surfacing from deep water — no panic, taking inventory, finding the stranger in stolen scrubs standing over her.

She looked at him for a long moment. Not at his face. At the line of him, the stance, the way his weight sat — the way Pixel looked at a machine she'd built.

"Oh," she said. Her voice was eleven years of disuse, sanded down to wire. "They actually built one."

"Built one what?"

"Walk me out of here, young man," said Dr. N. Okafor-Voss, "and I'll spend the rest of my life answering that."

[Beat 128]
The supply dock at the north end took deliveries until 0400 and disposal pickups around the clock, because waste, unlike supply, never stops. At 0340, a Carrion Enterprises bus backed up the ramp, and a thin man in coveralls ran two sealed medical-waste bags out on a flatbed dolly, and if the dock camera noticed the bags breathing slow, it kept the observation to itself. The case with the bird sticker went on last.

Bus 31 cleared the outer fence at 0351.

Three klicks south, Moss pulled into a dead gantry's shadow and came back and unzipped the bags, and stood over the two of them — the lunatic and an old woman the manifest swore was forty kilos of biohazard — and shook his head slowly, with the expression of a man who has decided to stop asking questions on the grounds that the answers have been getting worse all night.

"Told you," he said, "that I'd throw you in the trash."

"You did," Kyle said. "Best ride I've had all year."

[Beat 129]
He sat up, found his clothes in the case, dressed, and counted out Moss's pay. Standard rate, exact, plus the consult fee — and then, separately, folded flat, the manifest copy from fourteen months ago.

Moss looked at the paper without touching it.

"The other copies?"

"There were never other copies," Kyle said. "There was a roof, and a slate, and a man who signed it. And then there was a lineman in Pilsen who got to keep being alive, which is the only part I ever cared about."

Moss took the manifest. He held it over the gantry's burn barrel and let it go, and watched it become nothing, and the two animals in him settled somewhere — not friendship, nothing so expensive. A zeroed ledger.

"You ever need another ride," Moss said, climbing back into the bus, "pay full freight like everybody else."

[Beat 130]
Hua was in her kitchen at 0500, because Hua was always in her kitchen, the wok cold at that hour and the room smelling like Mrs. Chen's anyway. She looked at the woman in Kyle's spare coat for a long time. Then she set out two bowls, and a third, and didn't say a word about either decision — the one she'd refused to frame, or the one he'd made.

"Standard rate's already cleared," she said instead. "Carrion invoice came through. He charged you a consult fee?"

"He earned it."

Dr. Okafor-Voss ate like the starving woman she clinically was, both hands on the bowl, and between the second bowl and the third she looked up at Kyle with the same surveyor's eyes she'd opened in the cold unit.

"Sixteen," she said. "When they finished your installation. You'd have been — sixteen."

The kitchen went quiet enough to hear rain.

"You're going to want to write down everything you remember about the years after," she said. "And I am going to want a very secure room."

Kyle thought about the cage three blocks east of Mrs. Chen's. About a hum that knew when it was being listened to. About five names in a file, four of them dead routes — and the fifth, eating soup across the table, asking for paper.

"I know a room," he said.

## Interlude II: Half a Step

[Beat 131]
He came up the stairs at two in the morning with one shoe.

The other one was still on the West Town cross-street where Mira's crew had found him, or it was in the gutter where he'd moved out of someone's line, or it was on the roof of the dry goods warehouse where he'd gone to get above the engagement and decided the shoe wasn't worth the time it would take to find it in the dark. He hadn't looked. He'd walked the thirty blocks home in a dress shoe on the right foot and a gray sock on the left and registered the imbalance in his gait the way his hardware always registered things: clinically, without judgment, as data that mattered for some calculations and not others.

Somewhere around the twentieth block the count had come out wrong — a short stretch of Ashland his neuretics declined to log. He had been here. He had not been here. His hardware logged it next to the shoe and he kept walking, because the alternative was standing on a thin spot at two in the morning asking it questions it would not answer.

Pixel's light was on under her door.

She opened it before he knocked. She was already moving toward the kitchen, which meant she'd heard him in the stairwell, and the particular asymmetric sound of his footfall had told her enough that she didn't need him to explain.

[Beat 132]
He hadn't looked for the shoe. The job had a shape he hadn't finished reading, and thirty blocks of wet sock had not clarified it. He still didn't have the name of whatever had been hiring through that shell, or a reason for the routing that didn't bother him, and neither of those were problems he was going to solve in a stairwell at two in the morning.

[Beat 133]
She set two cups down. The kettle was already hot.

He sat in the chair at the corner of her kitchen table — not the one that faced the window, not the one that faced the wall, the one that faced the room — and set Cacophony on the table because the holster had been abraded by the evening and the draw angle had shifted two degrees and he would fix it here or he would fix it later but he would fix it. Silence stayed on his back. Taking it off required both hands in sequence, and his left hand was carrying a grade-two compression injury from the third engagement. He wasn't going to ask for help. She wasn't going to offer.

[Beat 134]
The tea was not a blend Kyle would have chosen. It was something she made when she was tired — he knew this now — something her mother had made, from a region that no longer existed as a political entity, which she'd once described as tasting like an argument about whether you should go back to bed. It tasted like that now. He found it acceptable.

[Beat 135]
At some point between the first cup and the second, she slid a small yellow case across the table without looking up. Merithadol tabs — the standard ones, the kind every medic kept within reach of wherever she worked, the kind that had gotten so common over the last thirty years that nobody thought to call them drugs anymore. She took one, snapped it on the edge of the table with two fingers, put half in the space between her cup and his.

He looked at it. He picked it up. He swallowed it with the tea.

She swallowed hers. The case went back in her apron pocket and she went back to her tablet and the city continued its low industrial project two kilometers away and neither of them mentioned it because there was nothing to mention.

[Beat 136]
They sat. The near-dark of her kitchen at two in the morning had a specific quality — the workbench lamp still on in the other room, a sliver of light from under her bathroom door, the city doing something low and industrial two kilometers away. Down in the street a cartographer fish was clicking under a drainage grate, slow and even, like a tape measure rewinding, and then it stopped.

His cup was already on the table when he sat down.

[Beat 137]
He also knew, with the same precision that tracked hinge angles and drive distances, exactly what he was. And what holding on to that would cost anyone who tried.

He took the half-step back. He always took the half-step back. Not out of the room and not out of the tea — he set his cup down and looked at the window.

She watched him do it without commenting.

[Beat 138]
They stayed for another forty minutes. He adjusted the holster angle on Cacophony. She read something on her tablet that she didn't tell him about. At a quarter to three he stood and took the cup to her sink and she said *your shoe's probably still on Erie* and he said *probably* and she said *the third drain grate past the loading dock has a shelf* and he said *I'll check* and she said *okay* and he went across the hall.

He did not check. He went to bed with one shoe under the nightstand and the sock still on the other foot.

## The Quiet Hour

[Beat 139]
The meet was a corner, not an address, which was the first thing Kyle liked about the job. Halsted and a cross-street whose sign had been pried off years ago for the scrap value of the letters, a noodle vendor folding his cart for the night, the smell of fryer oil cooling into the wet. The vendor pinched off a thumbnail of the magnesium tape that hung from his awning and touched a flame to it, the way he did at the close of every night, and for a breath the corner went white and silent before the dark came back and took its change. Nobody who worked clean ever gave you a door. A door was a thing that could be watched, recorded, served with paper. A corner was just weather and the people standing in it, and if it went wrong you could be three different streets away before anyone agreed on what you'd looked like.

Stash got there first, because Stash was always first, a wide flat man leaning against a shuttered pharmacy with two cases at his feet that he would not let anyone else carry. He ran logistics for half the freelancers Kyle respected and most of the ones he didn't, and the rule with Stash was that you did not ask what was in the cases until he opened them and you did not ask where they'd been before.

Echo came out of the dark on the other side, hood up, a thin person with a voice like a radio tuned a half-step off the station — recon and comms, the one who'd be in everyone's head once the neuretics synced. No earpieces. Earpieces were a tell, a thing a guard could see and a scanner could find. Everyone on a clean crew ran the channel behind their own eyes, grown into the skull young, and Echo was the one who'd be holding the whole conversation together from somewhere none of them would ever stand.

The fourth was already at the corner when they arrived, or had become part of it between one moment and the next — standing between the pharmacy doorway and the space where the noodle vendor's cart had been, occupying the exact position that gave him the most sight lines with the least exposure, with the stillness of something that had finished its reconnaissance.

"Ledger," he said, when Kyle looked at him. The codename and nothing more.

Synthetic, by the eyes — irises a beat too steady under the street lamps. Good position. He had already counted the exits. Four of them, and the client wanted five.

[Beat 140]
The fifth came up the block the way ordinary people came up blocks, which was to say Kyle didn't clock her until she was already inside the loose triangle the three of them made, and that bothered him more than if she'd dropped out of a window. She was medium. Medium height, a coat the color of a coat, hair pulled back the way a person pulls it back when they've stopped thinking about it. The kind of face that a room would round down to nothing. You could share an elevator with her and a week later swear you'd ridden up alone.

"Nü," she said, which was the codename and the whole of the introduction, and she said it the way you'd give a barista a name you didn't expect to hear called.

Kyle read her: quiet, polite, forgettable on purpose, which was a skill and not an accident, and skills that took that much work to look like nothing were usually pointed at something. He had no idea what she did. The brief hadn't said. On a clean crew the brief never said more than your own piece — Echo knew the building, Stash knew the gear, Kyle knew that his piece was to stand between everyone else and whatever came through a door — and the fact that he didn't know Nü's piece meant her piece was the one the client cared about.

"You're the muscle," she said to him. Not a question. She had a read of her own. Then, tilting her head the way someone does when they've already decided to like the answer: "What do I call you?"

Kyle opened his mouth. He got one syllable in — maybe less, the K of his name, a principle of Bushido that he'd never fully stopped applying to strangers who asked — and Echo's voice came into the channel, flat: *No real names.*

He closed his mouth. He looked around. The corner. The rain. The shuttered pharmacy with Stash's weight still denting the doorway. And down near the base of the step, where the gutter had deposited it from somewhere else, a glass soda bottle with a faded orange label.

"Soda Pop," he said.

Nü snorted. It was a real snort, unguarded, the first thing she'd done that wasn't careful, and it did something to her ordinary face he hadn't expected. "That won't do."

Kyle picked up the bottle and threw it against the pharmacy wall.

The glass went everywhere. Sharp. Sudden. The small violence of it carrying a weight out of proportion to the sound it made.

"Shatter," he said.

She looked at the pieces on the wet concrete. She looked at him. Something moved in her face, there and gone — amusement, maybe, or a reappraisal, or both — and when it settled the almost-smile it left behind meant something different than the earlier ones.

"I can work with that," she said.

He went back to watching the street, and did not examine why it mattered to him whether she could.

[Beat 141]
Echo ran the brief once, in the channel, so it lived in five skulls and nowhere a court could find it. A tower in the prestige blocks, close enough to the Loop that the rent was a sentence you said to frighten children. Vant/Meadowlark Trust, a holding company that did nothing you could name and owned things you couldn't afford to be near, the sort of CorpoNation whose business was being the wall other businesses leaned their secrets against. On the thirty-first floor they kept a node that touched no network. Air-gapped — a machine with its hands cut off from the world, deaf to the Pulse, deaf to the Network, a vault you could only open by standing in the room with it. Whatever lived on it was worth more than a thing you could simply steal across a wire, because a thing you could steal across a wire was a thing someone could steal back.

"So we walk in," Stash said, "and we walk out, and the trick is the walking."

"The trick is the elevator," Echo said. "Lobby's contractor-staffed. Arcturus."

Arcturus Civil Security. Private badges who answered to whoever signed the building's services contract and to nobody who lived in the city, which made them honest in the only way that mattered — they would do exactly what they were paid to do and not one motion more. You could not bribe a cop, Kyle thought, because the city had stopped keeping them. You could read an Arcturus shift roster like a tide table.

"And the node," Kyle said. "Who opens the node?"

"Nü handles the node," Echo said. "Every door between the lobby and thirty-one, that's hers too." And that was all the channel offered, and Nü stood there and let it be all.

"Ledger has the dock," Echo said. "Exterior approach, egress staging. If it goes wrong, the loading bay is the door."

"Understood," Ledger said. One word, like a line item in a report already filed.

## A Waterfall and No Chairs

[Beat 142]
The lobby had a waterfall and no chairs. A sheet of water two stories tall slid down black stone, and there was nowhere to sit. You were not meant to stay in this lobby. You were meant to be approved and moved along.

They came in as a delivery. Stash had the cases and the work order, a real one, bought off a real subcontractor who would swear under oath he'd dispatched it because as far as his records knew he had. Kyle had a vendor's jacket and the particular dead patience of a man who carried other men's furniture for a living. Echo was a parked van and a voice in the back of the skull. Ledger held the loading dock two levels down. Kyle reads it the way his neuretics reads everything — the bio-compute grown through his skull and worked by thought alone, no jack required — sorting the part of the scene that is load-bearing from the part that is merely confident. Down at the base, the scavengers have already gathered — not the building's own people, but the other kind. Vultures, and the fledglings who would like to become Vultures, because a hundred floors of old bodies kept alive on financed organs, all of it about to be relocated into a gray zone that keeps no records, is not a tragedy to them so much as a clearance sale. Two of them watch his bike roll in and price it without much interest; it is the cargo they are waiting on, not the machine. One is telling the other about a board posting from earlier in the week — a salvage run on this exact tower, listed clean, the operator field blank where there is always a name, the client resolving to a shell that resolved to nothing when he chased it. Came from nowhere, the fledgling says, half spooked and half delighted, like the building hired itself. Kyle catches it on the way past and adds it to the count: four times this season he has heard a job described that way.

And between the scavengers and the door stands an Arcturus relocation detail — Civil Security, the closest thing this city still keeps to law, which is to say a service corporation that happens to bill by the hour. There are soft helmets and clipboards and the unhurried manner of men doing paperwork, and the sheer banality of it is the threatening part.

[Beat 188]
He could fight his way in through the lobby. He has the inventory for it, and the night is still young. But a fight at the door would wake the whole nest, and he has an old woman to bring back down through these same people on the way out, so he spends the smarter currency instead and goes around.

The smarter currency is Sparrow, who lives in the seam between what a corporation bothers to lock and what it forgets to lock — a whole economy, that seam, and Sparrow pays the rent on a life inside it. Kyle pings a number that only works on alternate weeks and gets a single line back: a service door on the loading side, a freight car still drawing power on Axiom's own circuit because the sweep crew has been using it to carry people out, and a price that is fair, which from Sparrow is its own small miracle.

On the loading side a dog is making its rounds — a rangy gene-stray gone the color of nothing in particular, nosing the dock and the alley mouth and the dead foot of the freight ramp in the same order it has plainly nosed them a thousand nights, the way another man's watch would do it. It takes the half-strip of jerky Kyle offers from his coat with the neutral courtesy of a toll collector, no more grateful than a turnstile, and goes back to its rounds. Two relocated tenants sit on their cases by the cordon and one of them says, without looking up, that the dog is early tonight, and the other one answers that it always comes round before the lifts quit, and neither of them finds anything in that worth saying twice.

The service door opens on the second wrong knock. The freight car is a steel room that smells of other people's lives being carried out of it — wet cardboard, and a child's chalk drawing taped at toddler height that nobody ever came back for. The control panel is original Axiom, the dead corp's wheatsheaf logo worn pale under forty years of thumbs, still wired to a bus standard nothing has manufactured since the company went under and somehow still load-bearing for the building's whole spine. Kyle thumbs the panel past the floors that still have power, and the car begins to climb.

It dies at sixty-one. The doors part on a hallway already gone half-dark, the corridor lights dropped to the dim red of emergency cells and the air turned close and warm where the vents have quit, and above him the remaining twenty-three floors to eighty-four are nothing but stairwell and a clock that is still running. So he starts up them on foot. Somewhere around the sixty-eighth landing the count goes wrong on him — he climbs nine flights and the read insists he has climbed eleven, the numbers stenciled on the concrete disagreeing with the ones in his skull, and under it all a low hum arrives a half-second before the boot that should have made it. He notes the discrepancy and keeps climbing.

[Beat 189]
He meets the sweep on the seventy-second landing — two Arcturus officers in soft armor, walking an entire floor's worth of residents down ahead of them. An old man carries a birdcage; a woman carries a sewing machine she will be made to set down somewhere before the lobby. The Civil Security pair are not cruel, which is the part people never quite believe. They are polite, and bored, and certain, and they have a quota of floors to clear before the dark catches up to them as well.

The lead officer raises a hand. "Building's decommissioned, friend. You're going the wrong way."

Kyle lets him see the katana riding over his shoulder — matte black, an edge that takes none of the light, nothing remarkable about it at all except the hand that has had a very long time to practice with it — and lets him do the arithmetic for himself. Most encounters end right about here, and this one does.

"Medical retrieval," Kyle says, which is true in the way he needs it to be true. "Eighty-four. I'll be back down before you are."

The officer looks at the blade, and then at Kyle's face, and then at the clipboard that has no line on it for any of this, and decides that his contract does not pay nearly enough to find out how the rest of it goes. "We cut eighty-four in forty minutes," he says, stepping aside. "Anything still up there when it goes is salvage. That's not me deciding that — that's just the schedule."

"I know exactly whose schedule it is," Kyle says, and keeps climbing.

[Beat 190]
Floor eighty-four is hot and close and still has its lights, which means it still has its people. Some doors stand open, where the persuaded have already gone; others stand shut and chained, where the rest have decided to make Axiom come and do the work by hand.

Behind the door of 84-12, a man is talking to the door itself.

"Is that the lift?" the voice asks — old, and courteous, and hopeful in a way that costs Kyle something to stand there and hear. "Wait — wait just a moment, I can't find my other shoe. Tell them I'm coming. Tell them I only need my shoe."

Kyle stops, and the clock stands there at his back with its hand on his shoulder. Twelve doors down the hall there is a woman he has been paid half-rate to carry out of here, and below them both a tide of dark is coming up to meet everyone on the floor, and right here in front of him is a closed door with a courteous voice behind it, asking the night to please wait while an old man finds his shoe. The decision costs him one heartbeat, and the code wins, the way it always wins. Tonight he hates it more than usual.

"I'm not the lift," he says, to the door. "Stay inside, lock it, and don't open up for the soft helmets." It is the best he has to offer. It is nowhere near enough.

"I only need my shoe," the voice says again, to no one in particular.

So Kyle leaves him there, and goes to find Comfort.

## Eighty-Four

[Beat 191]
Mrs. Okwuosa-Brandt's door is not chained. It stands open, propped with a cinder block, because she has decided that a woman who has done nothing wrong does not hide behind her own door. She sits small and upright in a chair that faces the hallway, dressed as if for church, a hard-sided case balanced on her knees and her hands folded on top of it. The first thing Kyle's neuretics does — the read he stopped saying out loud at nineteen and has never once managed to switch off — is tell him that her people came from four different places that have no earthly business sharing a single face: Igbo, German, somewhere in the Caribbean, and somewhere cold. A whole century of the world's mixing, sitting in a chair in a tower that a corporation is in the middle of switching off.

The second thing he notices is the smell of citrus — green and bright and frankly impossible, eighty-four floors up a concrete column that has not smelled like anything but other people in forty years.

The source of it is out past the open balcony glass, where there is a tree. An actual tree: a Meyer lemon in a barrel she must have carried up here in pieces, taller than she is, with four fruit on it gone gold in the dark and its leaves turning in the wind that never quite stops at this height.

"You're not my granddaughter," Comfort says. She has a voice like a closed door.

"She sent me anyway," Kyle says. "She's stubborn. I can't think where she gets it."

[Beat 192]
"I'm not going," Comfort says, before he can open with whatever it is she expects him to open with. "I know what's coming. I've known since I was younger than you are now. They take the top of the building, and then they wait for the bottom to come down on its own, and they call the whole arrangement a clause." She says the word the way a person holds something that has bitten them. "I am eighty-four years old. I have lived in this building longer than that boy downstairs has been alive. I am not going to be carried out of my own home so that a company can put a number where my window used to be."

"Okay," Kyle says, and sits down on the arm of the other chair — which surprises her, because as a rule men who climb eighty-four floors with swords on their backs do not sit.

"Okay?"

"I'm not going to carry you anywhere you haven't agreed to go." He nods at the case on her knees. "What's in the box?"

She studies him for a long moment, deciding. "My husband," she says at last. "What's left of him that the city would let me keep."

Kyle does not tell her he's sorry. People say that to fill a silence, and the silence belongs to her, so he waits.

"And the tree," she says, and her voice changes on the word — the only crack in the door all night. "He grew it from a pip in a coffee cup the year we moved in. It has been alive up here longer than half the people in this building. I am not going to leave it behind to be — relocated."

[Beat 193]
The lights brown out, hold, and brown out again — the tide line, four floors up and still coming. Kyle's neuretics hands him the number with no maybe left in it: the men who cut eighty-four will be in this corridor in under thirty minutes, and the dark will beat them here by ten.

There is no version of tonight in which that barrel goes down the stairs. He weighs it anyway, the way he weighs everything — two hundred pounds of wet soil and forty years of a dead man's care, against a stairwell that is a black corkscrew full of people who would cut the cardiac assist out of her chest for the financing still owed on it. He cannot carry both the woman and the tree. With the clock where it is, he is not sure he could carry the tree alone.

So he does the thing he is worst at: he tells her the truth, and then he offers her the lie that might just hold it together.

"I can't take the tree," he says. "I can take you, and I can take him" — the box — "and I can take that." He nods at the tip of the lowest branch, where the new growth has come in green. "A cutting. One branch. You start it in a cup of water the way he did, and inside a year you've got the same tree standing in a different window. You wouldn't be leaving anything behind. You'd just be moving it."

She knows it is not nearly that simple, and he can see that she knows; a cutting is a coin toss that would make a botanist wince, and they are both of them too old to pretend otherwise — she in years, and he in mileage.

"Will it take?" she asks.

"Yeah," Kyle says, which is the first lie he has told her, and he catches himself in the telling of it. Some lines you say anyway.

[Beat 194]
She makes him do it properly. There is no time and she makes time regardless — a clean knife from the kitchen, a cut taken on the angle just below a node, the way her husband taught her and the way she will not allow a stranger to do badly even now. She wraps the cut end in a square of wet paper towel and then a sandwich bag, and holds it cupped in both hands the way she holds the box, which she has refused to let Kyle carry. The box itself rides in a cloth sack knotted across the front of her. She has done some version of this before, Kyle thinks — not this exactly, but the shape of it, the leaving of a place with only what your two arms can hold around — and the whole tower is full of people who learned that particular lesson young.

He gets her as far as the door, and the lights go.

It is not a brown-out this time. The whole floor drops into black at once, the way a held breath ends, and the warm dark fills up with the sound of eighty-four's life spinning down — the pumps, the vents, the refrigerator hum you never once notice until the moment it isn't there. Somewhere down the corridor a chained door begins to rattle as the soft helmets test it. The tide has arrived, and the two of them are standing in it.

"Stay on my left," Kyle says, and his neuretics floods the black with what it can still give him — heat, motion, the remembered geometry of a hallway it mapped on the way in. Her hand finds the back of his coat. "Keep your eyes on my back, and don't look at the doors."

"Why not?" Comfort asks.

"Because some of them are open," he says.

## One Shoe

[Beat 195]
They pass 84-12 again on their way to the stairwell.

The door is open now. The cinder block that should have held it shut is sitting out in the hall where someone kicked it aside, and the dark inside the unit is the dark of a room already cleared. On the floor by the threshold, where a man sat down to put his shoes on and managed only one of them, there is a single shoe.

Kyle's neuretics, which has had an answer ready for everything tonight, has nothing at all to add. The soft helmets came while he was sitting on the arm of a chair being told about a lemon tree — or perhaps it was the other ones who came, since eighty-four is salvage now and the schedule said as much on the stairs. He had told the man to lock the door. The man had wanted his shoe.

Comfort starts to turn toward the open door, toward the small wrong shape of the shoe on the floor, and Kyle puts his body between her and it, gently, the way you would turn a child away from a window. "Keep walking," he says, in the flat and low and deliberately boring read-out voice he keeps for exactly this. "Eyes on my back."

He does not tell her whose door it was. He walks her on to the stairs.

[Beat 196]
The stairwell is a black concrete throat with the emergency cells dying in it one landing at a time, and it is not empty. The fledglings have come up to meet the salvage halfway, because carrying a thing all the way down is work, and letting the building deliver it into your hands is not.

They come down the stairs the way the young come at everything — too fast, and from above. Kyle hears them well before the read paints them in: boots, and the eager quiet of people certain the dark is on their side. He sets Comfort against the inner wall, one flight below a landing, where the geometry does the work of a second man for him. The shaft narrows to a single body's width at each switchback and the turns are all blind — a place one man could hold.

"Don't move," he tells her. "Whatever you hear."

The first one comes around the turn with a salvage hook in one hand and a headlamp on his brow, and the headlamp is a mistake, because it tells Kyle exactly where the rest of him is. He doesn't draw the katana for this; he takes the hook out of the boy's hands at the wrist-lock, turns him into the wall, and lets the next two trip over the lesson on their way down. Cacophony stays on his hip and the blade stays on his back, because these are children with debts and bad ideas, and the code has a line about children that he has never once crossed.

And then the fourth one rounds the turn with a gun in his hand, which changes the shape of everything that comes next.

[Beat 197]
The fourth one has a gun, and a gun rewrites the next three seconds entirely. He's young under the headlamp — a jacket too new for the work, the price tag very possibly still tucked in a pocket — and his hands are not steady, which is worse rather than better, because a frightened hand pulls early. Kyle's neuretics paints the shot before the trigger has finished the thought: a hard red line laid from the muzzle, down the length of the landing, through the space where Kyle is standing and on into the stairwell below, where Comfort is pressed against the inner wall a single flight down. Precognition tells him what it always tells him — vacate the space the round is going to fill — except that the space behind him is the only thing standing between that line and the old woman, and if he steps off it the round he so neatly dodges finds her half a second later. For once in his overclocked life he cannot simply move out of the way, and so he does the only thing the geometry leaves him: he moves the way instead.

He draws Cacophony left-handed, the squat five-shot revolver shotgun clearing its throat off his hip, thumbs the cylinder around to the breaching load, and puts a round straight into the stair-rail bracket beside the shooter's knee. The whole structure up there comes apart in noise and orange sparks and a length of railing that was load-bearing half a second ago, and the kid does what any body does when the floor it has been trusting starts to shout — his shot goes wild into the ceiling. Kyle is up the flight and on him before the echo has finished, and he takes the gun, and the fight, off the boy.

It costs him. Behind his eyes the overclock runs hot enough that he can feel it, a slow pressure building at both temples as though a thumb were being pushed into each one, and a thin line of blood starts from his nose. He thumbs it off his lip without thinking about it. The white crowds in at the edges of his sight and he blinks it back by main force. He has been spending the read all night like a man who is certain he will never need it again tomorrow.

Below him, Comfort has not moved an inch. The cutting is still cupped in both her hands.

"Still with me?" he asks.

"I buried a husband, and I outlived the state I was born in," she says, from the dark below him. "A boy with a hook is not going to frighten me."

## The Loading Side

[Beat 198]
They come out of the stairwell on the loading side, into the half-lit dark of the loading dock, and after eighty-four floors of dead ventilation the night air tastes almost like a second chance. Two of them have already moved off the bike and onto the dock, because they understand the trade rather better than Kyle would like: salvage doesn't leave a building by the front. It leaves by the back, and the back is exactly where they have been waiting.

These are not children this time. One of them wears a patch — a Vulture grown well past the fledgling stage on precisely this kind of evening — and he carries the calm of a man who has done the cutting before and slept soundly enough afterward.

"That's a financed unit you're walking off with," the Vulture says, with a nod toward Comfort and toward the cardiac assist he can apparently read on her clear across a dark dock. "Cardiac, a couple of genemod joints, corneas. She's underwater on every bit of it. By rights—"

"She's not a unit," Kyle says.

[Beat 199]
"By rights," the Vulture says again, and his hand drifts toward the hook on his belt — and that word, together with the gesture, is what finally draws Silence.

It comes off Kyle's back in the one motion he has practiced more than any other, matte and edgeless, and settles level in front of him. Here is the thing the Vulture cannot see in the dark: Kyle is white at the edges and down to the dregs of a battery he has been overdrawing since the sixty-first floor, and his right hand wants very badly to shake — the fine tremor of a man running on empty, which happens to be the exact tell this particular Vulture has built an entire career on reading off of marks and merchandise. A man who can price the cardiac assist on an old woman from clear across a black dock can certainly read a shaking hand at four feet.

So Kyle simply does not let it shake. He spends the last of something he does not actually have left to spend, holding the blade dead still — as still as the flat of it, as still as a man with all the time in the world and nothing left to lose — and the stillness is the entire lie, and it is a very good one.

The Vulture looks for the tremor, the way he looks for it on everyone he meets. And because he does not find it, he does something the easy marks never make him do: he takes a step. One unhurried foot forward, the hook coming off his belt now, his grip closing around the handle with the particular confidence of a man who has decided the body in front of him is already inventory. He has weighed it. He is coming.

The blade does not move. That is all Kyle has — the not-moving — and he holds it through the whole length of that step, through the half-second where the arithmetic could break either way, while every overdrawn cell in his right arm screams to betray him. The hook stops a foot short. The Vulture's eyes go to the edge of the katana, then to Kyle's wrist, then back, looking for the one twitch that would tell him he can afford this. He weighs a steady blade against a financed corpse one last time, runs the arithmetic he has run on a hundred easier nights — and finds, to his evident irritation, that it does not quite come out in his favor. He takes his hand back off the hook.

"Not worth the paperwork," he says, to save his own face, and steps back, and pulls the fledgling along with him.

"No," Kyle agrees. "It really isn't."

[Beat 200]
The granddaughter is waiting two blocks out, beyond the building's cordon, where Kyle told Sable to tell her to wait. She doesn't run when she sees them; she walks, fast and careful, the way you walk toward someone you are afraid of finding changed from how you left them.

She stops a foot short of her grandmother, as though that last step was the hardest one of all. "Grandma." "I sent him anyway."

"I know what you did," Comfort says. She lets the girl take the box from her back, and she does not say thank you, because between the two of them a thank you would land as an insult; letting her carry it is the thank you. What she does not let the girl take is the cutting. The girl's eyes go to the bundle — the one thing her grandmother will not hand over — and she does not reach for it, because she has learned which things you do not ask for. The wet paper-towel bundle stays cupped in Comfort's own two hands, and Kyle understands that it will stay there until it is standing in a cup of water on some windowsill lower down.

"You came up for an old woman who told you not to," Comfort says to him.

"I was paid to," Kyle says, which is true, and is also the lie hiding underneath the true thing, and she is eighty-four years old and hears both of them.

"Mm," she says. She looks at him a moment longer — the reading-back look, the one he usually only gets from Sable — and then she says the thing he is going to keep. "There was a boy on my floor. Twelve doors down from me. Some nights I could hear him through the wall, talking to his radio." She does not bother to make it a question. "You couldn't carry everyone."

"No," Kyle says. "I couldn't."

"I know," she says — and it is not forgiveness, because she is not the one with the standing to give it, and they both understand that too.

## The Receipt

[Beat 201]
He watches them go — two women, a box, and a branch sealed in a sandwich bag, walking south into the part of the Glooms that still has its lights — and then he turns back to look at the thing he climbed.

The Roost is most of the way dark now. The tide has come down past eighty-four, so that her window is black along with all the others, and on the balcony behind that black glass a lemon tree he could not carry stands in the wind that never stops at that height, four gold fruit still on it, waiting on a power that is not coming back and a cold that will find it by morning. Forty years of a dead man's patience, and it ends tonight. On a lit sill a few floors below the tide line someone has folded their relocation notice into a paper crane and set it back down on the windowsill it was served on, beak to the dark, the official Axiom letterhead folded into one wing — and Kyle walks past it without slowing. He thinks about the boy with the one shoe, and about the tree, and about the distance between what he was paid to save and everything there actually was to save.

His neuretics, slow and shaky and only now coming back online, offers him the one comfort it has to give, which is a number. The small light in the corner of his mind's eye is still red from the stairwell — one flash, the man who seeded this hardware in a child too young to be asked once told him, for about a year off the far end of his life. He spent a year tonight, and what he got for it was an old woman, a box, and the coin-toss of a branch.

He runs the arithmetic. The branch alone would have been enough. He waits for that to bother him. It doesn't. Then he gets back on the bike and turns south, and in the dark he does the thing he would tell anyone to do in it: he names the next ordinary thing for himself, and then the next, the curb and the corner and the long block home, and he keeps on naming them until the tower is finally behind him and the street is only a street again.

## The Interview

[Beat 202]
Two months earlier there was a corridor in Bucktown, and the corridor was where the trouble started, although Kyle would not learn whose trouble it was until the invoices came due.

The client was a small woman with flour on her sleeves who had spent nine years running somebody's counting room and had decided, on a Tuesday, to stop. The contract said escort, dockside, one night, double rate for any hour past midnight. It had come through clean. What the contract had not said was that the somebody was the Lotus Syndicate, and that the syndicate's position on retiring counting-room clerks was under active review by fourteen people in the corridor between her safehouse and the dock.

The corridor was service infrastructure, two meters wide, no light worth the name. They came in fours. There was no room to read anyone, no floor to give ground on, no half-second to find a clutch or a lockout or a seam — there was the client's breathing going high behind him and the dark coming in waves with knives in it, and Silence answered the way Silence answered when the question was put that way.

He got her to the dock. She sailed at 03:40 with the flour still on her sleeves. He rode home wet to the bone, racked the blade without the morning check, and slept eleven hours. He did not count the corridor. The corridor had not left him the time to count it.

[Beat 203]
The pay cleared at dawn. By the end of the week, the city had begun, in small ways, to close.

The parts man on Ashland was out of stock — out of stock of everything, suddenly, for him specifically, with an apology that did not survive eye contact. A carrier who had run his packages for two years stopped answering. The second week, a door that had always been open at the Damen lift was locked when he reached it, and the man who kept it watched him try the handle through the glass and did not move.

Kyle took inventory the way he took inventory of wounds: pressed each one once, catalogued what it gave back. Somebody with reach was spending money to make him feel the size of the city. Reach like that came from exactly three places, and two of them would have just shot him.

[Beat 204]
Sable said it flat, through the cracked terminal, in the voice she used for line items.

"You're flagged. Lotus paper, syndicate-wide, two weeks old. No contract that touches their territory routes to you — and before you ask, their territory is a third of the working city. The third with the money in it."

"The Bucktown job was clean."

"The job was clean. The client was theirs. A counting-room clerk walks out with nine years of their arithmetic in her head, and the man who walked her out is either a rival's asset or the most expensive accident of the quarter." A pause, the length of a decision. "Here's what I'd want you to notice. When a syndicate decides you're a fly, the paper says kill. This paper doesn't say kill. It says hold."

"Hold for what?" he asked.

"That," Sable said, "is the interesting part."

[Beat 205]
The invitation came on the eighteenth day, and it was not a hit, which by then was itself information.

A kid in a dry coat — dry, in that week's rain, which said car, which said money — stood at the mouth of the alley behind The Pivot at a polite distance and held out a card with both hands, the way you hand over a thing that belongs to someone more important than you. Heavy stock. A single blossom stamped in the corner, five petals, no name. An address in the Narrows. A time, two days out, early evening.

And one line, brushed by hand: BRING THE SWORD.

The kid waited until Kyle took the card, bowed eight centimeters, and left without selling anything. Kyle stood in the alley and read the line twice. Every crew in the city would have written come unarmed. He had just been invited by the only people who considered the sword part of the man.

[Beat 206]
Lotus territory announced itself by subtraction. The streetlights all worked. The gutters ran clear. The walls carried no tags, the boys on the corners stood like staff instead of weather, and nobody begged. Kyle catalogued how much enforcement it took to make a neighborhood this quiet, and kept his hands easy at his sides.

Then the arcade opened ahead of him, and the trees made their argument.

Cherry trees, eleven of them, planted in steel collars down the arcade's spine, in full bloom in the wrong month — engineered stock, the blossoms running white to a pink with blood in it, lit from below so the canopy floated on the dark. Petals came down with the rain and lay on the wet concrete like the receipts of some enormous purchase. A vendor's grill smoked under the third tree. Two children ran a circuit around the trunks, screaming on the inhale, and nobody hushed them.

It was the most beautiful street Kyle had seen in eleven years of working streets, and someone had built it on purpose, with money that had blood in it the same way the pink did, and both of those things stayed true at once all the way down the arcade.

[Beat 207]
The address was a ramen-ya at the arcade's end — eight stools, a cedar counter gone gold with handling, a noren curtain the rain had not been allowed to touch. The smell arrived before the door did: tonkotsu run long past patience, char off the burners, something green cut fresh in the last minute.

Three of the eight stools were taken. Kyle read the room on reflex: a heavyset man with a cleaver-arm conversion, eating with the careful manners of the recently dangerous; a woman whose coat was worth more than guns; a courier still wearing her route. Crews from rival ledgers, eating in a row, because the counter was the counter. Weapons worn openly. Nothing checked at the door but behavior.

Kyle sat at the empty end. Nobody asked him to surrender anything. Nobody pretended not to see the saya. The cook looked at him once, the way Mrs. Chen looked at him, made a decision involving extra char siu, and built the bowl without taking an order.

It was, he admitted, around the fourth mouthful, the best bowl in the city. The admission felt like a small betrayal. He ate it anyway, and the betrayal got smaller with every mouthful, which was probably the point of the bowl.

[Beat 208]
The man came in at the hour on the card, alone, shook the rain off an umbrella and left it furled by the door. Mid-sixties. A suit without one concession to fashion in it. He took the stool beside Kyle with a small exhale, and the cook had tea in front of him before the exhale finished, and everyone at the counter got four centimeters taller without looking up.

"Branch Manager for the Narrows," he said, by way of a name. "You cost us eight people and a counting room."

"The contract was an escort. I escorted."

"Yes. We reviewed it. The contract was legitimate, your conduct was correct, and our crew engaged first." He drank his tea. "The eight are our failure — we sent fourteen to do a thing we had not priced. The clerk is our failure twice over; people do not leave rooms they are treated well in. You are the only part of that night that performed to specification."
