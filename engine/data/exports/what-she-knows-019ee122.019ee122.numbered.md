# What She Knows

*Amara finds her batch number in the archive. 847 people. Her own name is one of them.*

[Beat 1]

The permissions gap had been live for eleven days before she found the sub-archive.

She had not reported it. That was not strictly procedure, but it was consistent with how she worked. She logged anomalies and waited to understand them before she brought them to anyone, because a half-understood anomaly escalated wrong almost every time, and escalating wrong cost more than waiting. She had built seven years at Cellvault on that habit, and no one had complained about the results.

The gap was a sub-contract access tier that handed her calibration-interface permissions it had no business handing her — not a wide opening, a narrow one, the kind that came from a misconfigured junction between Orison's administrative tree and Cellvault's maintenance overlay. Orison was the arcology two zones east, the one with the white-noise ambient track running on every floor so its residents never noticed the absence of outdoor sound. Someone over there had patched a field-access protocol and left a residual thread open. She had found it while pulling a client's baseline history, when the system offered her a directory she held no documented authorization to see.

She had flagged it in her personal log. She had not clicked into it.

Then she had clicked into it.

That was eleven days ago. She had been working the archive in forty-minute intervals, pulling what she could reach, logging it, moving on. The data was internal Orison documentation, administrative rather than clinical. Procurement records. Batch certification files. Calibration protocol specifications filed under contract addenda that Cellvault's standard access tier would never have surfaced. She had recognized what she was looking at on day three and had not stopped. Anomalies were her professional instinct. She logged them until she understood them.

The server room sat at the back of Cellvault's Zone 6 maintenance floor, behind a thermal door that wanted two credentials and a calibration-port handshake. She had worked in this room for four years. She knew the sound the cooling fans made when a rack ran hot, a pitch that climbed by roughly a third as the thermal load rose, consistent enough that she could estimate the load without checking the display. She knew the four-second lag in the overhead lighting when you first came in, and the place on the east wall where a conduit ran too close to the surface and the plaster had gone warm to the touch. The room smelled of recycled air and hot aluminum, the dead-air smell of a space cleaned constantly and inhabited for work and nothing else.

She sat at the console and pulled up the batch certification files.

Batch 44-C. Certified 2222. The document header listed seven authorized facilities, cross-referenced to an Orison distribution log. She recognized the facility code on the fourth line. It was the installation center she had used for her own neuretics upgrade three years ago — a standard referral from her employer at the time, an authorized procedure, a certification appointment she had completed and returned from and not thought about again, because the documentation had been in order and the work was done correctly as far as she had been able to determine.

She found the contamination flag on the thirteenth page.

She did not look up.

It was not formatted as a warning. It was formatted as an internal classification, a status field in a sub-table attached to the batch record, the kind of field that lived in documentation nobody outside was meant to read. It had been updated twice since the original certification, once in 2223 and once in 2224. The status read *managed liability.*

She read it again.

*Managed liability.*

She pulled the field's metadata. It referenced a secondary file under the same batch record. She followed the link.

Batch 44-C. 847 recipients. Documented accelerated degradation rate beginning 18–24 months post-certification, manifesting in associative node clusters. Internal classification: *managed liability.* See Addendum C-4 for remediation protocol.

She pulled Addendum C-4.

The remediation protocol was filed as a maintenance optimization addendum, accessible only to technicians briefed on the batch list. It targeted a specific cluster of associative nodes and named them by their anatomical designations: medial prefrontal cross-links, associative relay junctions on the upper limbic shelf. The procedure did not restore function. It targeted the nodes where the degradation was visible and reshaped the surrounding architecture well enough that the drift stopped appearing in standard diagnostic reads. It did not fix the degradation. It hid the evidence that the degradation was happening.

She had been flagging drift in exactly that cluster for eight months.

The fans cycled up through their third-pitch register and locked there. Somewhere in the rack behind her a drive was working harder than it should, the harmonic vibration traveling up through the floor and the chair and into the base of her spine. She did not file it for later. She did not move.

Eight hundred and forty-seven people had received neuretics from batch 44-C. Every one of them had a scheduled maintenance cycle. The calibration visits for batch 44-C recipients were not maintenance. They were management of the evidence.

The contractor briefing ran two pages — a procedural overview and a list of approved technicians at eight authorized facilities. Cellvault's Zone 6 maintenance floor appeared on page two.

Three names were listed. None of them hers.

She had been attending her clients' calibration visits and watching the procedure run on them without knowing what she was watching. She had been reporting Type C drift in their tissue, logging it carefully, noting that it did not respond to standard recalibration — and the reason it did not respond was that it had never been treated, only hidden from the reads she was authorized to see. She had been performing quality oversight on a system that was simultaneously performing quality oversight on her. The contractor briefing she had never been given. The authorization she had never signed. The addendum that did not exist in any documentation Cellvault shared with its maintenance staff.

She did not know how far up that went. She did not need to know that yet.

She had attended her own calibration visits through the same scheduling system her clients used. The visits were logged as standard maintenance in her Cellvault personnel record, the same as any other year. The documentation on her side of the access tier showed only what any maintenance record showed: date, duration, technician ID, outcome flagged nominal.

Her own neuretics were batch 44-C. She had the certification on file. The degradation she had been tracking in her own logs — fourteen months of numbers that didn't fit the standard decay curve — was in the contamination flag. Documented. Expected. The accelerated degradation rate beginning 18–24 months post-certification was not a variable. It was the product.

She was at month thirty-six. The variation in her logs was not variation. It was progression. The batch documentation's internal model placed thirty-six months as the threshold at which degradation began manifesting in externally visible executive-function markers — the kind that would show up in a standard Orison calibration read. Not just the Type C drift she had been logging in her personal archive, but flag-level data, the kind that triggered escalation to a different classification tier entirely. Her next maintenance visit was not management of the evidence. It was the handoff.

Her hands went flat on the console. Not a decision — the body had already moved. The warm east wall was three steps behind her, and she had not walked to it, but her right palm was against the plaster anyway, the heat from the conduit climbing through her wrist, and she was standing without remembering having stood. She breathed out through her teeth. The fans were loud up here, the full-pitch register, and the recycled air tasted of aluminum and the last person's exhaled breath and nothing else.

She stayed there until the standing made sense. Then she went back to the console.

For over a year she had carried a private explanation: the installation had not gone cleanly in her body, the recovery had been harder than it should have been, and whatever she was measuring was a failure she hadn't caught at the time. She had told no one, because the data was about her own body, and she had needed to understand it before she let anyone else have the numbers. The explanation was wrong. She set it down.

The contamination had been documented before she received the batch. Someone had made a decision on her behalf without her knowledge, and then someone had been coming to her calibration visits to manage the visible record of that decision. The procedure had been applied to her while she sat in the chair and looked at the technician's hands, and she had driven home afterward and made dinner and filed the visit as complete. She was one of 847 people who did not know this.

She went back to the data.

The professional reflex was to secure it, then process the implications. She had learned that the first time she found an anomaly worth reporting and spent forty minutes reading it before she thought to log where she'd found it; by the time she went back, the path had been reorganized and the original file location was gone. She had not made that mistake again.

She sent the batch certification record to the secondary storage path Seto had configured two weeks ago. The path routed through Seam relay infrastructure — three hops through the gray zone between Zone 6 and Zone 7, where the admin frequency bands ran unmonitored and the relay beacons still carried legacy firmware nobody had updated since 2218. From outside any system that would have cared, it looked like ordinary calibration cache traffic. She had checked the architecture when he set it up, because she needed to understand the routing before she trusted it.

Then the contamination flag. The secondary file with the recipient count. Addendum C-4 and everything attached to it — the cross-references, the distribution log, the briefing summary, the contractor list, the maintenance protocol in full with its anatomical designations and its outcome metrics, which were phrased not in terms of function restored but in terms of diagnostic visibility reduced. She pulled that language twice to be sure she had read it correctly.

She routed it in stages, not a bulk push, the kind of movement that read as routine cache operations in the access log. When you found a permissions gap, you did not announce your presence in it. You moved as if you had always been authorized to be there, and you understood what you had before anyone knew you had been inside.

It took nineteen minutes.

When the transfer was complete she closed the archive and sat with the permissions gap still live on her screen. She could report it now. She would, eventually. She did not yet know what reporting it would flag, or to whom. Until she knew that, the gap stayed unreported and the data stayed in the secondary path, which was the only version of correct she could work with right now.

Her next maintenance visit had been in eight days, before she pushed it. Now ten. She understood now why Seto had asked her to push it. He had found the batch list. He had found her name on it. And he had given her the only thing he could give her without breaking the shape of how she worked: time to get there on her own.

She would have done the same.

She sent through the channel.

*I found my batch number.*

The response came back with the texture of a message that had been drafted and held.

*I know. I found it two days ago. I was waiting for you to find it yourself.*

She held that, and then she stopped holding it, because holding it was a way of not answering, and she did not have the time.

*847 people,* she sent.

*Yes.*

A pause, and then:

*Did you find the addendum.*

*I found everything,* she sent. *Month thirty-six. The handoff.*

The response took longer this time. Before it arrived, something came through the channel that wasn't words — the texture of someone sitting still with a weight they have been carrying a long time and are about to set down. When the message came it had dropped the procedural register entirely — four words with no framing:

*I know. I'm sorry.*

She had not expected that. It landed somewhere she had not prepared. She put her hand back on the warm plaster of the east wall and stood there with the fans running high and the recycled air in her lungs and the weight of what he had carried for two days while he waited for her to arrive at it.

*My visit was in eight days,* she sent.

*Ten now. Not enough.*

She looked at the gap still open on her screen. She did not know how long it would stay live, and she had no way to find out from inside it. Somewhere in the Seam relay infrastructure her data was moving through legacy beacons on frequencies nobody monitored, and she did not know what waited on the other end of the route, or who else had found the gap, or whether Orison's administrative tree logged access events she could not see from her side.

What she had was the data and ten days.

*What do we do with it,* she sent.

*We move it somewhere it can't be reached.*

*Where.*

*Outside. A Seam drop — somewhere the maintenance chain has no jurisdiction. I've been building the route for six weeks.* A pause, and then: *There's something else. When you have time. Not on this channel.*

Then, while she was still at the console, a maintenance system notification appeared in her record. The system didn't message — it logged. *Calibration appointment C-8273, previously scheduled 06/29, rescheduled per provider request. New date: 06/24.* Five days earlier. Not a change she had submitted.

The entry auto-cleared after six seconds, the way maintenance records always did.

She sat with the new number. Ten days. Now five. She did not know whether someone inside Orison's system had triggered the change, or whether it was a routine batch-scheduling adjustment. She did not have enough information to determine which answer would be worse. Either the provider had flagged her account and moved her appointment closer because of something the archive access had surfaced — or they were moving batch 44-C clients routinely, ahead of some threshold she hadn't found yet.

Neither interpretation gave her more time.

She looked at the gap. The warm wall was at her back. The plaster was still warm.

She did not ask what the something else was. She knew the shape of a disclosure that had to be delivered in person.

She had five days. She needed to be outside the maintenance chain before the twenty-fourth.

Eight hundred and forty-seven people.

She closed the archive and started building a list.
