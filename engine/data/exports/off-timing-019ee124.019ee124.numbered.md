# Off Timing

*Amara is running the infrastructure audit query when Ciro appears at her workstation unscheduled. He has the wrong coffee. These are the tells the reader sees in retrospect.*

She had the infrastructure audit logs open at 09:22.

Not the clinical panel. The infrastructure side — the system-level logs that recorded when calibration protocols ran, which protocols, which equipment signatures. She had the access now (had it since 08:45 yesterday, before Ciro's message arrived) and she had learned the query syntax from a facilities manual that lived in the non-clinical index. She was three queries deep when she found the pattern.

Not the data. The shape of the data. The suppression protocol appeared as a gap — an equipment signature that would normally log a specific calibration event, and then nothing. The gap had a timestamp. The gap was systematic. If she ran the batch number against the gap timestamps, she would know whether Batch 44-C was the only batch with gaps, or whether the pattern went further.

She typed the batch number and hit run.

A notification appeared in the corner of her screen: *Fonseca, C. — approaching workstation area.*

She minimized the query window. The progress bar was at 31%.

He appeared at her workstation sixteen seconds later, which was enough time for her to look at the secondary monitor and appear to be reading a calibration request queue. He was holding a coffee. The cup was the kind from the seventh-floor machine — the tall paper cups that the cultivation floor used. Ciro drank espresso from the sealed-glass dispenser near his office. She had never seen him with a paper cup.

"I was passing," he said.

She nodded. He didn't usually stop when he was passing. Most Account Liaisons waved. He set the paper cup on the edge of her workstation — not offering it, just setting it down — and looked at her secondary monitor.

"How's the queue?" he said.

"Manageable," she said.

"Good." He was looking at the secondary monitor but not, she thought, seeing it. He was looking at the room with his peripheral vision. She had done this herself when she was in the relay shop, watching foot traffic. "I wanted to confirm — the thirty-two access came through without any issues?"

"Yes," she said. "Yesterday morning."

"Good." He said it again. The same word with a slightly different weight. He picked up the paper cup, held it for a moment, then set it back down. He had forgotten he was holding it.

"Is there something you needed?" she said.

He looked at her directly. The attention was there — the attention she had come to understand as both real and calibrated at the same time. "No," he said. "Just checking in." A pause. "You've had a busy week."

"It's been eventful," she said.

He looked at her for a moment longer than he needed to, then he said: "Let me know if you need anything," and he left. He did not take the paper cup with him.

She waited ninety seconds.

She opened the query window. The progress bar was at 31%.

The query had not run. It had paused at the exact moment she minimized the window — not completed, not failed. Paused. Waiting.

She looked at the results for the 31% that had loaded: Zone 1 through early Zone 3. Fourteen gaps. All in Batch 44-C. No gaps in any adjacent batch. Fourteen.

She ran the rest manually, in sections, with the secondary monitor open to the calibration request queue.

By the time she reached Zone 7, she had one hundred and twelve gaps.

One hundred and twelve. She stared at the number. She looked at the paper cup Ciro had left on the edge of her workstation. She looked at the corner of her screen where his proximity notification had appeared. She looked at the number again.

She wrote it down on paper. The only paper in her workstation. She folded the paper and put it in her jacket pocket, beside the drive.
