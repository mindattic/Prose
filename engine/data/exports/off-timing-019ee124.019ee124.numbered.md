# Off Timing

*Amara is running the infrastructure audit query when Ciro appears at her workstation unscheduled. He has the wrong coffee. These are the tells the reader sees in retrospect.*

She ran the first query at 09:22 with her hands flat on the desk and her calendar open on the secondary monitor so anyone walking past would see a schedule review.

The infrastructure logs were not the clinical panel. They were the system-level records: when calibration protocols ran, which equipment signatures fired, which events were logged versus which were allowed to pass silently. She had access to this tier now — had it since 08:45 yesterday, before Ciro's message arrived — and she had spent an evening learning the query syntax from a facilities manual that lived in the non-clinical index. She was three queries deep when she found it.

Not the data. The shape of the data. The suppression protocol appeared as a gap — an equipment signature that would normally log a specific calibration event, and then nothing. The gap had a timestamp. The gap was systematic. If she ran the batch number against the gap timestamps, she would know whether Batch 44-C was the only batch with gaps, or whether the pattern went further.

She typed the batch number and hit run.

A notification appeared in the corner of her screen: *Fonseca, C. — approaching workstation area.*

She minimized the query window. The progress bar was at 31%.

He appeared at her workstation sixteen seconds later — enough time for her to open the calibration request queue on the secondary monitor and look like she'd been reading it for an hour. He was holding a coffee. The cup was the kind from the seventh-floor machine — the tall paper cups that the cultivation floor used. Ciro drank espresso from the sealed-glass dispenser near his office. She had never seen him with a paper cup.

"I was passing," he said.

She nodded. He didn't usually stop when he was passing. Most Account Liaisons waved. He set the paper cup on the edge of her workstation — not offering it, just setting it down — and looked at her secondary monitor.

"How's the queue?" he said.

"Manageable," she said.

"Good." He was looking at the secondary monitor but not, she thought, seeing it. He was looking at the room with his peripheral vision. She had done this herself when she was in the relay shop, watching foot traffic. "I wanted to confirm — the thirty-two access came through without any issues?"

"Yes," she said. "Yesterday morning."

"Good." He said it again. The same word with a slightly different weight. He picked up the paper cup, held it for a moment, then set it back down. He had forgotten he was holding it.

"Is there something you needed?" she said.

He looked at her directly. The attention was there — the attention she had come to understand as both real and calibrated at the same time. "No," he said. "Just checking in." A pause. "You've had a busy week."

"It's been eventful," she said.

He looked at her for a moment longer than he needed to, then he said: "Let me know if you need anything," and he left. He did not take the paper cup with him.

She watched him go. She did not open the query window until she could no longer see him at the corridor junction. Then she waited fifteen more seconds, because the junction had a camera she knew the coverage arc of.

She opened the query window. The progress bar was at 31%.

The query had not run. It had paused at the exact moment she minimized the window — not completed, not failed. Paused. Waiting. The proximity notification had done more than alert her. It had suspended her active query and logged the interruption. The infrastructure log now had a record of a query she had started and not completed.

She ran the rest manually, in sections, with the secondary monitor open to the calibration request queue.

By the time she reached Zone 7, she had one hundred and twelve gaps.

She sat back from the bench. The depot was quiet around her, the afternoon shift clearing out, the standby lighting warming up toward the day-end cycle. One hundred and twelve. She looked at the number on her screen and felt her breathing change slightly — not anxiety, something more structural, the recognition of a thing that was larger than she had expected and was not going to stop being large.

She had the drive in her jacket pocket. She had the paper with 112 on it, which she had written in the first moment she understood, the kind of thing you wrote because writing it made it real outside your own head.

She folded the paper twice and put it back. She closed the query and logged out of the bench cleanly, the way she always did, the way a person logged out who had found nothing unusual and was going home at the end of a normal shift.
