# Crimson & Chrome

[Beat 1]
Mrs. Chen put the bowl down in front of Soraya before Soraya had ordered, which was Mrs. Chen's way of saying she'd been seen coming a block off, and the broth went up in a sheet of steam that fogged the seam of the booth's old privacy glass. Across the table Rook ate with her right hand and kept the tally with her left, the way she always did — three fingers tapping the table edge in a pattern that wasn't nerves and wasn't music. The coat hung open enough to show the Reibo's grip riding her hip, matte where the crimson light off Mrs. Chen's sign couldn't get a hold on it. Chrome takes the neon and gives it back prettier. The Reibo gave back nothing. That was the point of it.

"Don't tell me the number yet," Rook said. "Tell me the shape."

*She'll tell me the number anyway. She always leads with the number when it's bad.*

"Twenty-one," Soraya said, and set her chopsticks down with a care that had nothing to do with the chopsticks. "The Axiom job. The ones they wrote off as burned in the vault fire. They're alive, Rook. Scattered, hunted, and somebody on a registry desk finally put the count together and started selling it."

Rook stopped eating. The three fingers kept going — tap, tap, tap — and then stopped too.

The number sat in her chest before her mind had a name for what it was doing there. Twenty-one going in. Fourteen out. The gap had always been seven, not twenty-one, and she had kept it that way deliberately — seven was the manageable wound, the one she could carry without letting it change the arithmetic she ran for everyone else. Twenty-one made the wound the whole job. Her lungs did something she did not authorize, a single short compression.

"Yes," she said.

The word was already gone across the table before she'd decided to spend it, and she let the booth go quiet around it. Her right hand had stopped on the bowl's rim, the chopsticks bridged across it, broth steam climbing past her knuckles warm enough to read. Behind Soraya the sign bled its slow crimson through the fogged glass and laid a wash of it across the table, across the backs of both their hands, across the unmoving chrome at her hip that took the color and kept it and offered nothing back. She watched the steam instead. Fourteen out of Axiom. Twenty-one going in. The seven had lived in the gap between those counts for four years and she had not opened the gap on purpose and she was not going to pretend otherwise.

*Wennick.* The name moved through her the way it always did when she let a count get away from her — the shape of a voice she'd been carrying at the bottom of the arithmetic since before the Axiom job, the one that had taught her why the count mattered, why a runner who got sloppy about the numbers was a runner who had already decided some of them could be wrong. She had promised herself, at a different window over the same lake, that she would never be sloppy again.

Her right hand stayed flat on the bowl. She kept it there.

"You said yes before I told you where they are," Soraya said quietly. "Before I told you who's hunting them."

"I know." Rook picked the chopsticks back up. "Tell me anyway."

[Beat 2]
﻿Soraya laid the routing map face-up on the table between the bowls.

Rook looked at the bowls first, because Mrs. Chen had already put them down. Bowls meant Soraya had been here long enough to be known by the rhythm of the kitchen, which meant the routing map had been folded in her pocket while steam was still coming off the broth, which meant she had been carrying it here before she decided how to carry it in. That was Soraya's method: arrive before the argument, wait until she could feel the room, open when it was ready. Rook had watched her do it with data since the third year of knowing her, and had never decided whether it was patience or something cooler than patience.

The map was the kind that didn't print coordinates. Seventeen circles connected by junction lines, a zone-status symbol for each, and the circles marked in two colors ??? some once, some twice, three in a red darker than the neon overhead.

"Fifteen confirmed or near-confirmed," Soraya said. "Five circled twice ??? I had four before Scout rode past two of them this morning and added the third. She's already running the grid." She touched one of the dark-red circles. "Those three I have within the zone. Not the block." Her fingers stayed on the circle a moment, and Rook watched the stay ??? not hesitation, the specific care of a woman who had spent four years holding a fact she couldn't say and who was now saying it, and was being precise about each piece of it in case precision was the only way to say it cleanly. "The rest I don't have yet."

"How many unmarked?"

"Six."

Rook counted the circles. Fifteen confirmed or near-confirmed, six unknown. That was not a rescue. It was a search operation running parallel to a rescue, two different problems against the same clock. She had run harder. She mostly believed it.

"Timeline," she said, because the timeline was the thing she needed to put a shape on before she could put anything else in order.

"The registry desk that compiled the list ran the data in parallel. The primary buyer got it eighteen hours ago." Soraya's mouth went flat in the way it went flat when a number was worse than she'd modeled. "The secondary window opens in five days. Same desk, second track. Whoever the primary team doesn't close in the first forty hours, the secondary picks up."

Forty hours was not the live window. Forty hours was the edge at which the secondary problem started and the primary problem ended, and the problem with the secondary was that it would have the primary's leftovers ??? the ones who had run, who had found a wall, who were harder to locate and therefore needed the more careful approach. Rook held the math still until she could feel the shape of it, then let it become work.

"Wet-squad?"

"Operations. Registry enforcement model ??? flyers, two-person contact teams, low visibility." Soraya set her cup down and held it with both hands, the warmth almost gone. "They're not collecting them violently. They're offering transport. Registered Reads in processing condition." The last phrase came out in the data register ??? precise, flat, held at arm's length. "The Marrow doesn't break what it uses."

Rook felt the register land. Not the words; the register. Soraya had learned that voice in four years of sitting with a fact she couldn't act on, and it had become the voice she put on when the fact got too close ??? a glass wall between her and the thing she was saying, so she could say it without the saying catching. Rook had heard it in her own voice once, a long time ago, when she was still counting at Axiom and telling herself that the math was someone else's problem.

She filed that and kept running the intake.

"The Marrow location," she said.

"Tier-eighteen block in the Loop." Soraya traced a line on the map without pressing ??? just the movement of a finger indicating a direction. "Helix subsidiary. Covered as cold-storage and specialized transport. The operations team runs out of a separate address, but the pickup routing terminates there." She lifted her finger. "The building would look clean to anyone who wasn't reading the routing."

Chrome on the outside, all the way through. A corp that priced people as substrate had built the whole apparatus to look like logistics. Rook filed the location the way she filed every place she'd walked into uninvited ??? as geometry, nothing more, until she was inside it and could take its measure from the inside.

"Tell me about one of the twenty-one," Rook said.

Soraya looked at her across the table. The question had no operational content. They both knew it.

"Her name is Sefi Okonkwo," Soraya said, and the data register was gone from her voice, which cost her something Rook could hear in the cost of saying it. "Nineteen when she went into Axiom's program. She's twenty-three now. Licensed Read, 3.2 signal rating ??? the kind of signal that makes a corp's calibration division quietly persistent. She said no. They put her on the barge." She pressed her thumb against one of the circled circles ??? the near-confirmed ones, the fourteen-hour window. "She's been living under a gray-zone cooperative in Z6, under someone else's residential tag. Repairing neuretics bridges for cash. Fixing what she has the skill to fix, not registering any of it, because she knows what the registry does with her name."

Rook held that still for a moment. Nineteen going in. Four years under a tag that wasn't hers, building a life out of what was small enough not to be seen. She knew the shape of that arithmetic ??? not from the outside, from the inside, from the way it felt to keep yourself under the count long enough that the count started feeling like shelter.

She knew what it cost to come out from under it.

She put her chopsticks down and left the broth unfinished, which was the first time she had left Mrs. Chen's broth unfinished in four years. Mrs. Chen collected the bowl without a word. That was the other thing she was good at.

"Fourteen-hour window," Rook said. "We move on Sefi first."

[Beat 3]
Soraya named them the way she always did, in the order they'd say yes — Boiler first, because Boiler always answered a wall before he answered a person.

"He'll want the building before the job," she said, and Rook nodded, because that was Boiler exactly. Four years ago that read had put her on the right side of a collapsing floor — a half-second, his hand flat on a pillar, his chin tipping toward the east stair, no words, just the tip of the chin — while two men she'd never gotten the names of went down into the dust with the rest of the slab. She could still feel the floor go in her knees if she let it: the lurch, then the absence, the particular silence a building makes in the quarter-second before it admits it's done. Boiler had felt it coming through his palm a breath sooner than the floor knew. That breath was everything.

Somewhere under the booth the Pulse came through the slab as a low even hum, the city's only honest heartbeat — the vacuum-tube rail that threaded GLMZ's spine at Mach six, slugs seating and burning, a vibration you stopped hearing the way you stopped hearing your own pulse until the night you needed it gone and couldn't have it.

"Scout's in the Gray Zone still," Soraya went on, "walking it on foot like the maps owe her money. She's got the dog."

Rook almost smiled. The dog was Gerald, a salvaged quadruped Scout had named something stupid on purpose, because a thing called Gerald doesn't read as a weapon on a checkpoint scan — it reads as a girl's project, a junk pet, a reason to wave you through. Scout didn't believe in any access she couldn't reach with her own two feet; she'd turned down clean Pulse berths to walk the gray between zones, six hours through ferrocement canyons and checkpoint queues, because — Scout's words — *the train tells you where the doors are; the walk tells you where they put the ones they don't mention.*

"Lace will come because Lace wants to see who breaks first," Soraya said. "And Vox —"

"Vox will say no, then show up two hours late with coffee and pretend she was always coming." Rook set the chopsticks down.

On the Axiom job Lace had called which of the hired hands would fold under pressure three days before the pressure came, and she'd been right about all of them.

And Vox — Vox would grumble down the line that she was retired, that she had a life now, that Rook had some nerve, and then the booth glass would fog and she'd be standing in it with two cups going cold in her hands and that look that dared anyone to mention she'd come. Four years gone and Rook could already taste the coffee. Always terrible. Vox always brought it anyway.

"That's five." She breathed out slow. "Five I can carry."

She let the steam off the bowl climb past her face before she said the rest, because the rest was the one with weight.

"Stave won't answer his line. He left it on the table the last time — the whole rig, the comm, everything. Said he was done being someone people called when it got bad." *And I'm the bad. I'm the call he was talking about.* "But Stave's the only one who can get us into the registry vault clean, and he knows it, and he knows I know it. So somebody's going to have to go knock on a door that's been shut on purpose for four years."

Soraya had been quiet through all of it — not the waiting quiet of a woman who'd finished her list, but the quiet of one who was watching the list become a weight and deciding whether to say so. She set her spoon down carefully.

"I asked you first," she said. "Before I asked any of them. I wanted to know if *you* would come."

Rook looked at her. Soraya's face was the face of a woman who had done her own counting for four years and had been very precise about what she put in which column.

"You already knew I'd come."

"I knew you'd say yes." She picked up the spoon again, turned it over in her hands, set it back down. The motion had nothing to do with the soup. "That's not the same thing. I needed to know if you'd come because you wanted to — or because this is the kind of arithmetic you can't leave unbalanced." She looked up then, level, the scientist's eyes that never offered a charitable reading when the precise one was available. "Because if it's the arithmetic, I brought you a job. And if it's the other thing, I brought you the one I should have brought you four years ago, when I had the count and didn't trust it enough to say it out loud."

The steam rose between them and cleared and rose again.

"I should have told you," Soraya said, and the three words were smaller than anything else she'd said, stripped of the data, and Rook felt them land on the side of her neck where the draft came in under the booth's old seal. "About the twenty-one. When I had the count. Before the registry desk started selling it. I had it and I held it and I told myself I was protecting them by not saying it anywhere a ledger could hear."

Rook held still on that. She didn't fill it in. She let Soraya own the whole of it.

"You were protecting yourself," she said.

Soraya's jaw set. She didn't deny it.

"Yes." A breath. "And I'm asking you to come anyway. Both things are true and I'm asking anyway, because you're the only one whose arithmetic I trust to carry more than it was rated for."

The hum came up through the slab again, steadier now, a slug seating into its berth somewhere down the line with a settling she felt more than heard. She set the bowl aside and reached for her slate.

"Then we're both asking for something we should have said earlier," Rook said. She did not say what she should have said. She wrote Scout's address into the slate and held the booth door open with her shoulder while Soraya put her coat on, and outside Mrs. Chen's the night was the same night it always was in GLMZ, which was cold and indifferent and lit from below, and the two of them stood in it a moment before they went their separate ways to knock on doors that had been shut on purpose.

The door knock came in three days later, but it wasn't Stave who opened it. It was the manifest.

Soraya laid it on the booth table folded to the wrong page — the page nobody at the registry desk was meant to read in sequence, the one that only made sense if you turned the order inside out and read it backward against itself. She set it down without a word, three fingers flat on the paper, and slid it under the crimson light where the steam from Mrs. Chen's kitchen kept fogging the laminate and clearing again.

Rook spread the manifest flat and read it the way Boiler reads a column: not the words, the load. She let her eyes go past the letterhead and the Voluntary Relocation header and the stamped department seals, all the apparatus a thing wears when it wants to be believed, and she found the shape of the weight underneath. Twenty-one names. A single barge out of Old Harbor, departure logged to the minute, arrival never entered. And the names didn't disperse into twenty-one destinations the way relocated people are supposed to disperse. They funneled. They ran down the page into one column, under one repeated word, the word stamped beside every name like a checkmark — *processed.*

Below that column, smaller, was a routing code — a chain-of-custody tag, the kind a corp stamps on freight it expects to be questioned about.

She put her finger on it and did not lift it.

She'd seen that code before. Four years ago, burned into the side of a tank, and she'd read it then as a serial number, the kind of thing you don't think about because the thing it's printed on is already trying to kill you. She'd carried it out of Axiom in the part of her memory that keeps receipts whether you ask it to or not. Now it sat under her fingertip beside the word *processed*, beside twenty-one names that had walked onto a boat, and the two facts found each other in the dark and clicked.

*That's not a transfer order.* Her finger stayed where it was, pressing the paper flat against the table as if holding it down would keep it from being true. *That's a bill of lading. They didn't lose seven the way you lose a name in a flood. "The Reclamation ran the barge," Soraya said, and let it sit a moment before she went on, the way she always did with the bad ones. "Registered Reads, all of them — neuretics-capable people the corps log and covet, the ones any facility would pay to have live and cooperative. The woman came in behind the steam.

Mrs. Chen put a bowl down for her without being asked — set it at the empty place at the end of the booth with the small economy of a hand that has done it many nights running — and that, more than anything Soraya could have said, told Rook how long this woman had been folded into the back of this room, fed and waiting. She had a left hand that didn't fully close. At the wrist the chrome and the flesh argued, a seam where something had been done and then undone, gone in and come back out wrong, and she carried the hand the way you carry a thing that used to be yours.

She set a paper photograph on the table, face-up.

Twenty-one people on a barge deck, squinting into a morning.

Rook looked at it and did not pick it up. They were standing the way people stand when somebody's told them to gather for a picture and they half-believe it's good news — shoulders not yet braced, one of them caught mid-laugh, a man at the rail with his hand up against the low sun, a kid turned the wrong way to see the water. Morning light coming in flat and clean off the lake, the kind of light that doesn't lie about anything, that just shows you what's there. They didn't know yet. Something turned over low in Rook's chest, under the ribs, before she had a thought to attach to it — the involuntary lurch of a body recognizing the weight of a thing before the mind catches up. She let it. Then she set her jaw against it and put it where she put the rest, because the photograph wasn't asking her to feel it. It was asking her to do something about it.

"Sefi Okonkwo," the woman said. "There were twenty-one of us when we walked on. I can give you nine." She didn't look at the photo. She'd had four years not to look at it.
