# Crimson & Chrome

_Trilogy finale: Rook's crew, Adalemo, Soraya and Stave vs a Nano Triumvirate body-bank harvesting registered Reads. The clues from MGUN and NRST pay off; the win is not clean._

[Beat 1]
Mrs. Chen put the bowl down in front of Soraya before Soraya had ordered, which was Mrs. Chen's way of saying she'd been seen coming a block off, and the broth went up in a sheet of steam that fogged the seam of the booth's old privacy glass. Across the table Rook ate with her right hand and counted with her left, the way she always did, three fingers tapping the table edge in a pattern that wasn't nerves and wasn't music — it was inventory. The coat hung open enough to show the Reibo's grip riding her hip, matte where the crimson light off Mrs. Chen's sign couldn't get a hold on it. Chrome takes the neon and gives it back prettier. The Reibo gave back nothing. That was the point of it.

"Don't tell me the number yet," Rook said. "Tell me the shape."

*She's going to tell me the number anyway. She always leads with the number when it's bad.*

"Twenty-one," Soraya said, and set her chopsticks down with a care that had nothing to do with the chopsticks. "MGUN. The ones they wrote off as burned in the vault fire. They're alive, Rook. Scattered, hunted, and somebody on a registry desk finally put the count together and started selling it."

Rook stopped eating. The three fingers kept going — tap, tap, tap — and then stopped too, which was the tell, the only one she had left after years of teaching herself out of the rest.

"Yes," she said.

*Say it before she finishes. Say it before you can do the arithmetic on what it takes off you, because if you wait for the arithmetic you'll do the smart thing, and the smart thing leaves nineteen of them in the ground by spring.* She heard the want in her own voice and hated that it sounded like appetite. Twenty-one. She'd carried fourteen out of MGUN and counted twenty-one going in. The seven were the math she'd been not-doing for four years, and here was Soraya, handing her the rest of the subtraction across a bowl of noodles, asking her to finish a sentence she'd left open on purpose.

"You said yes before I told you where they are," Soraya said quietly. "Before I told you who's hunting them."

"I know." Rook picked the chopsticks back up. "Tell me anyway. I just didn't want you to think I needed convincing." She found a noodle, lifted it, let it hang there steaming. "Now I get to find out what it costs while I'm already in. That's the only honest way I know how to do it."

[Beat 2]
Soraya named them the way she always did, in the order they'd say yes — Boiler first, because Boiler always answered a wall before he answered a person. "He'll want the building before the job," she said, and Rook nodded, because that was Boiler: he'd put his palm flat against a load-bearing column and tell you which way it wanted to fall, where the old rebar had gone soft, which stairwell would hold three people and which would hold one and a lie. He read structure the way other people read faces, and he trusted it more, and four years ago that read had put her on the right side of a collapsing floor while two men she didn't have names for went down with the rest.

*That's three of the seven I can name. Boiler doesn't get to be a number to me. None of them do, that's the whole sickness of it.*

"Scout's in the Gray Zone still," Soraya went on, "walking it on foot like the maps owe her money. She's got the dog." Rook almost smiled. The dog was Gerald, a salvaged quadruped Scout had named something stupid on purpose because a thing with a stupid name doesn't read as a weapon on a checkpoint scan, and Gerald had walked point through every hard door GLMZ had to offer because Scout didn't believe in any access she couldn't reach with her own two feet.

"Lace will come because Lace wants to see who breaks first," Soraya said. "And Vox—"

"Vox will say no, then show up two hours late with coffee and pretend he was always coming." Rook set the chopsticks down a second time. "That's five. Five I can carry."

She let the steam off the bowl climb past her face before she said the rest, because the rest was the one with weight. "Stave won't answer his line. He left it on the table the last time — the whole rig, the comm, everything. Said he was done being someone people called when it got bad." *And I'm the bad. I'm the call he was talking about.* "But Stave's the only one who can get us into the registry vault clean, and he knows it, and he knows I know it. So somebody's going to have to go knock on a door that's been shut on purpose for four years."

She looked at her own right hand around the chopsticks, the hand that had carried fourteen out and would now be asked to go back for seven, and she thought about how the same hand was going to have to knock soft enough that Stave opened it.

[Beat 3]
The door knock came in three days later, but it wasn't Stave who opened it. It was the manifest — a flat NRST relocation order Soraya laid on the booth table folded to the wrong page, the page nobody at the registry desk was meant to read in sequence. Rook spread it under the crimson light and read it the way Boiler reads a column: not the words, the load. Twenty-one names under a Voluntary Relocation header. Old Harbor barge, departure logged, arrival never. A relocation that relocated people into a column of the same word — *processed* — and below it, a routing code she'd seen four years ago burned into the side of a tank.

*That's not a transfer order. That's a bill of lading.* She put her finger on the routing code and did not lift it. *They didn't lose seven. They harvested seven, and they wrote it like freight, and somebody got paid by the head.*

"the Reclamation ran the barge," Soraya said. "Registered Reads, all of them, walked aboard at Old Harbor under their own names because the order said sunrise, said sky, said a place the processors can't redirect. They believed it because it was the one promise this city keeps." She turned the page back the right way, gentle, like covering a face. "One of them came back. She's the reason I have the count at all."

The woman came in behind the steam, and Mrs. Chen put a bowl down for her too, unasked, which meant Mrs. Chen had been feeding her a while. She had a left hand that didn't fully close — chrome and flesh argued at the wrist where something had been done and undone — and she set a paper photograph on the table face-up, twenty-one people on a barge deck squinting into a morning. "Sefi Okonkwo-Reyes," she said. "There were twenty-one of us when we walked on. I can give you nine. The other twelve I watched go through a door, and I can tell you the door, and I can tell you it locked behind them, and I can tell you nobody on that registry desk wrote down the sound it made." She slid the photo across to Rook's right hand. "You carried fourteen out of MGUN. I counted you going in too. So don't tell me the number's too big. Tell me when we leave."

[Beat 4]
Rook turned the photograph over because that was where she put things she couldn't look at yet, and found the routing code again on the back, stamped, smaller, the way a printer stamps a chain-of-custody on freight it expects to be questioned about later. She'd seen that code twice now. Once on a tank four years ago. Once tonight, under the crimson light, on a bill of lading dressed as mercy. She set her finger on it the same way Boiler set his palm on a column, not to read the words but to feel which way the weight wanted to fall, and the weight wanted to fall on her.

"Soraya." She didn't look up. "Who bought your way out of Axiom?"

The three fingers tapped once and stopped, and that stop told her Soraya had already done this arithmetic and had been waiting, the way you wait holding a door for someone whose hands are full, to see if Rook would walk into it on her own. "A broker. Clean paper, no name I could keep. Why?"

*Because the job that got you out was too available. Because it landed in my lap the week I was Q-dry, and I took it because I needed the QUANTA and not because I asked who needed me to need it.* Rook lifted her finger off the code. *MGUN wasn't a vault we cracked. MGUN was a sample run. Somebody wanted to know if the Reclamation could be pulled live and walked out under their own names — and they used me to prove it could, and they used you as the deliverable, and the seven I left behind weren't a failure. They were the yield.*

"The buyer was Helix," Rook said. The crimson light hummed over the booth and she let it. "MGUN got you out clean for them — that was the proof of concept. They lost the survivor list somewhere in the move, the way corponations lose what they don't want auditing. Then NRST's partition cracks open four years later and surfaces the count, and the same registry desk that misfiled it starts selling it back. We didn't stumble onto an audit trail." She looked at Sefi's left hand, the one that didn't close, then at her own right one, the one that had carried fourteen out and counted twenty-one going in and never once asked who'd been counting alongside her. "I've been a contractor to my own ending. I ran the rehearsal. They've been refining the public Class V version off what I taught them was possible, and they paid me by the head and called it a rescue."

Sefi didn't flinch. She'd had four years and a door that locked to teach her how not to. "Then you know the work better than they think you do," she said. "When do we leave?"

[Beat 5]
The door to the booth had been shut three days and Adalemo opened it himself, which Rook noticed before she noticed his face — that a man who'd spent four years routing other people through doors had come to this one and put his own hand on it. He didn't sit. He set a NRST partition slate on the table beside Sefi's photograph, aligned the long edge to the long edge the way a clerk squares a stack, and left his fingers on it. "I'm the desk that misfiled the survivor list," he said. "I want that on the table before the food gets cold. I didn't lose it. I was paid to lose it. Helix routed the QUANTA through a relocation contingency line and I signed it because the line existed and the signature was already half-printed and I told myself a missing list isn't a body."

*He came in through the front. Stave left his comm on a table to never be the call, and this one walked in the door I'd have made somebody else knock on.*

"There were twenty-one on that barge," Adalemo went on, and now he turned the slate so the column faced Rook, the same *processed* she'd been reading sideways for three days, "and the manifest I cleared listed forty names across the quarter. Forty I audited. I never read a forty-first." He moved one finger down the column and stopped it the way she stopped hers on a routing code, not on the words. "That's the part I came to say out loud. I built the count clean enough that nobody upstream had to know there was a count. I'm the reason it surfaces now as something they can sell instead of something somebody can prove. So whatever you think you're owed off me — I owe more than that, and I know the exact figure, which is more than they ever wanted me to."

Rook looked at his hands on the slate, both of them flat, and then at her own right one still turned over on the photograph where she'd hidden Sefi's face from herself. Two people who counted for a living, who'd each been handed a ledger by someone who knew exactly which column they'd serve, and who'd served it. She didn't reach across to shake on anything. There was nothing clean enough between them to shake on. "Then we're not allies," she said. "We're two people who finally read the same book the same direction. You bring what PEREGRINE saw. I bring the seven I left and the fourteen who'll move for me. Stave opens the vault, if I can knock soft enough." She lifted her finger off the routing code at last and set it flat beside his on the slate, not touching, the two hands a centimeter apart over the same word. "First thing you do for me — you find me the forty-first name. The one you never read. Because somebody on that barge was never on your manifest, and that's the head nobody got paid for, and that's the one they'll have left somewhere they think nobody's still counting."

[Beat 6]
Sefi turned her left hand over on the table so the palm faced up, and the chrome at her wrist caught the crimson light and held it the way the slate had held Adalemo's fingers — a seam where flesh stopped being flesh and nobody on the registry desk had logged the date.

"You want to know what a door that locks is for," she said. "I'll tell you the room. They walk you down a corridor that smells like nothing — that's the first wrong thing, a place that processes bodies and has no smell, because the air's been scrubbed to spec. The walls are that white that isn't a color, it's an absence somebody paid for. And there's a hum. Not the floor. Lower. You feel it in your back teeth before you hear it, and you think it's the machines, and it is, but it's also us — the ones already in the racks, the Reads they pulled and kept warm. Class V doesn't run on dead tissue. It runs on tissue that still half-remembers it was a person."

She closed her hand. The chrome argued with the flesh and the flesh lost, the way it had lost on the barge. "They don't call it killing. There's no word in the manifest for it. They call it intake, and conditioning, and substrate yield, and every one of those words has a line item and a QUANTA figure beside it, and the figure goes down the longer you sit in the rack because you degrade. We're inventory with a shelf life. A registered Read costs them more fresh than spoiled, so the throughput's tuned to keep us *useful*, which is a worse word than dead. I watched twelve go through that door and I want you to understand they didn't die in there. They got priced."

*The corridor with no smell. The hum in the teeth. She walked it and walked back out and she's sitting here saying line item like it's a thing a mouth can hold.* Rook kept her right hand flat on the photograph and did not lift it, because lifting it meant looking at Sefi's face on the barge deck squinting into a morning before anyone had a routing code for her.

"Helix didn't build a vault," Rook said, and her voice came out level the way it came out level when the level was the only thing holding. "They built maintenance they don't have to do. The city stopped fixing the people the second it figured out the people were the part it could sell. Same chrome, same seamless surface, same clerk squaring a stack — they just put the deferral on the inside of us and ran it as throughput." She set her finger back on the routing code, on the word *processed*, and this time she read it forward. "And they used me to prove the racks could be stocked live. So when we knock soft on that vault, we're not cracking a bank. We're walking into the one room in GLMZ they kept the lights on for, because it's the only infrastructure they never let rot — and that's the part I owe. Find me the forty-first name, Adalemo. I want to know who they warmed and never billed."

[Beat 7]
Adalemo cleared the slate of Sefi's column and brought up a different one, and Rook watched him do it the way she'd watch a clerk she didn't trust yet — the economy of it, no wasted motion, a man who'd squared too many stacks to fumble the one that mattered.

"Before you spend the fourteen," he said, "you spend this." A face came up on the partition. Vox's face, rendered in NRST gray, the resolution of an asset that had been bought once and filed. "Stave paid for this in the same currency you all pay each other in — a face, traded for a vault crack he hadn't done yet. He logged it. The trade exists. And Helix doesn't pull threads, Rook. Helix pulls the one thread the whole garment hangs from, and then it audits the unraveling for line items." He moved his finger to the corner where a routing code blinked. "This is the thread. They ID Vox, they walk the trade backward to Stave, and Stave is standing in the only door you've got."

*The one who opens the vault is the one they ID first. Stave left his comm on the table to never be the call, and the call's already been made — three trades ago, in a face that wasn't even his to sell.*

Rook didn't reach for the slate and she didn't lift her hand off the photograph. She ran it instead, the way she'd run a barge manifest in the dark — twenty-one she could prove, fourteen who'd move on her word, seven she'd already left somewhere she'd have to count again. Now add the thread. Add the man in the door. Add what it costs to walk into a room they kept the lights on for, against a facility you can't starve and can't blind because the power and the bodies are the same inventory.

"Then the way in isn't a way in," she said, level, the level holding by its fingernails. "It's a way Vox doesn't surface until after, and a way Stave's standing somewhere that isn't the door when they walk the trade back. I build two cracks. The soft knock, and the one that opens if the soft knock costs me Stave. I don't put a person on a number I can't carry twice."

She set her finger flat beside Adalemo's again, a centimeter from his, both of them over the dead gray of Vox's face this time instead of the word *processed*, and she made herself name it the way Sefi had named the rack. "Here's the figure nobody wanted me to know either. The fourteen who'll move for me — they move because I count right. The day I count wrong, I'm the desk that misfiled them, and I'll have done it for the same reason you did. Because the line existed." She lifted her finger and put it back down, the small correction of someone reading a column forward who'd spent four years reading it sideways. "So I'm not leaving Vox's face on this table to go cold. Find me the forty-first name. And tell me what it costs to make sure the thread they pull comes up in my hand instead of theirs — because it's coming up in somebody's, and I'd rather it be the one already holding the count."

[Beat 8]
Adalemo's hand stilled over the slate. He didn't clear Vox's face. He looked at the woman across the table from him — not Rook, the name three crews used, but the one squaring a column nobody had asked her to square — and something in the line of his shoulders gave, the way a stack gives when you stop pretending the bottom sheet isn't bent.

"You said find you the forty-first name like I'd have to dig for it," he said. "I don't. I've had it since the day I logged out of Helix the way you log out of a thing you're stealing from — quiet, and with more than you came in for." He turned his own left hand over now, the chrome at his wrist catching the crimson light the same as Sefi's, the same seam, the same unlogged date. "I didn't only walk out with my body. I walked out with the substrate ledger. Names, intake codes, the rack assignments. Live inventory and the deferred kind. I've kept it dark four years because the day I opened it I stopped being a man with a useful past and became a man with a debt I couldn't pay twice."

*She put her finger a centimeter from mine over a dead gray face and said she'd carry the count twice. I have spent four years being the one who doesn't, because the version of me that does is the one they pull the thread on first.*

He keyed it — not a search, not a routing code, the whole thing, the buried contents of the partition unfolding across the partition wall in a gray that was not NRST's filing gray but the older gray of a manifest read by a person who refused to stop counting. "There's your forty-first," he said, and the figure beside the name had no QUANTA mark next to it, because they'd never billed her, because she'd been intake they never sold. "And here's the credential set that walks you into the only door they kept the lights on for, because I'm still on it. They never revoked me. You don't crack that vault, Rook. You knock with my hand inside it. That's the key, and it only works while I'm the one holding it, which means I'm standing in the door you said you'd build two of — and you're going to count me as the one who can't be carried twice, and I'm asking you to anyway. Because I'm done being the clerk who deferred herself."

[Beat 9]
The map went up over Vox's dead gray face, and Rook let Adalemo's name sit on the door where he'd put it, because moving it now would be a lie about what she'd already decided. She gave the room the shape of the thing instead.

"Vault's on the substrate level, which means it's under the bodies, not beside them. We don't go through a wall. We go down through a working floor — clerks at stations, intake on rolling beds, the racks they kept the lights on for humming the whole way. Boiler, that's your hum. Tell me what it does to your kit when there's four hundred warm deferrals in the concrete instead of four hundred dead drives?"

Boiler had been quiet the way he got quiet, which wasn't quiet at all — humming under his breath, thumbs working the resonance kit's calibration wheel, the smile still on but not reaching the part of his face that did the arithmetic. "Empty concrete sings clean," he said. "I drop a null wedge, the structure tells me where the cavities are, I read it off the return like a man reading rain off a roof. Four hundred people in it, the return comes back *wrong* — they're each their own little resonance, augments idling, BCIs filtering, hearts. I'd be reading a choir and calling it a wall." He set the wheel down. "I can still find you the door. But I can't promise the people between you and it don't feel the wedge in their teeth before you're through them. There's a floor on how soft I go, and the floor is them."

*Four years he's kept a manifest dark and I just put it on a wall in front of three crews. The kind man's limit is that he won't lie about the cost, and the kind man just told me the cost is in somebody's teeth.*

Scout came off the wall where she'd been leaning, and the way she came off it told Rook the answer before she said it. "Perimeter doesn't work on a building you go *down* into. There's no outside to hold once you're under the bodies. I ride in. I'm the one who comes back up the working floor with the count if the soft knock turns hard, and I'm the one who's standing wrong if Boiler's wedge wakes the choir." She pulled her gloves on, finger by finger, Reibo slung where she could reach it without looking. "Gerald rides too. I'm not leaving him in the dark on a clock I can close from the inside — if I'm in, he's in, and you all hear him before you hear me. That's the deal or I'm no good to you on the perimeter that doesn't exist."

Rook ran it one more time — twenty-one she could prove, fourteen on her word, the forty-first with no QUANTA mark, the man in the door who'd asked to be counted as the one who couldn't be carried twice, and now the rider who lived in absence asking to be present out loud, with the boy named. She didn't reach for the slate. She set her finger flat on the working floor where the down-route met the racks, a centimeter from where Adalemo's credential opened the only door GLMZ never let rot. "Then here's your clock," she said, level. "Helix audits its own ledger every shift change. Adalemo's credential reads valid until the next audit walks the rack assignments and finds his hand somewhere it filed as gone. That's the storm. We're not racing a guard. We're racing the moment the building reconciles itself and notices the clerk it deferred is standing in its only lit door." She lifted the finger, put it back. "Boiler, you read the choir and find me the seam anyway. Scout, you and Gerald ride the count up. I knock soft. And the second the soft knock costs me Stave, the second crack opens and the first one was never the way in. Everybody hold your frame. We go before the floor remembers us."

[Beat 10]
The down-route was a stairwell that had stopped being a stairwell — Helix had poured a service ramp through the old fire core, a working slope wide enough to roll beds up and down, and the lights along it were the kind they never turned off, sodium-warm and humming at a pitch Boiler had named for them on the way in. Scout took it on foot with Reibo across her back and Gerald a half-step off her right shoulder where she could feel him without turning, and the first deferral bed came at her out of the ramp's curve before she had a frame for it: a person, intact, breathing, eyes taped, a rack assignment stenciled on the bed rail in the older gray of a manifest nobody had revoked. Not a drive. A woman, maybe forty, the cabling at her temple dressed clean the way you dress a thing you intend to keep.

*Four hundred warm deferrals in the concrete. He said it like a number. It's not a number, it's a hallway, and the hallway has faces.*

Boiler's wedge went into the floor two levels down and came back the way he'd promised it would — wrong, a return that wasn't a wall but a choir, and Scout felt it arrive in her own teeth a half-beat before the bodies on the beds felt it in theirs. A ripple went up the working floor like wind crossing a field of something that should not be able to feel wind. Heads turned that had been taped still. A clerk three stations down stopped scanning and put her hand flat on a bed rail the way you steady yourself, or steady someone else. And then the count Scout had ridden in to find was in front of her, intake bay C, twenty-one beds in two rows under the lit door's spill, and the ninth one in the near row had a name on the rail that Rook had said out loud across a partition wall — Sefi, no QUANTA mark, intake they never sold — and her eyes were not taped, and they were open, and they were tracking the wedge in her teeth straight to Scout's face.

"Gerald, eyes up, they're waking," Scout said into the open channel, low, and heard him breathe before he answered, the whole crew hearing the boy before they heard her, the deal she'd made holding even here.

Then the lit door at the end of bay C opened from the inside on a credential that wasn't Adalemo's, and the figures that came through it moved the way Civil Security moved when Civil Security wasn't the answer — PEREGRINE-grade, Helix's now, the seam at their wrists catching the crimson light same as Sefi's, same as Adalemo's, same unlogged date, and Scout understood in the half-second before the first one cleared the frame that the building had not waited for shift change to reconcile itself.

*The vault's a person and the lock's a person and the guard used to be one too. I came down here to carry the count up. I'm going back up holding it in my arms and there's a choir behind me that's awake and afraid and counting on a soft knock that just went hard.*

[Beat 11]
The PEREGRINE in front did not draw. It tilted its head the way a thing tilts its head when something behind its eyes is doing the asking, and Gerald's breath came through the open channel just before the voice did — Helix's voice, routed up through the working floor's own address system so it landed in every ear at once, soft as a clerk's.

"Channel four, we have a face. Vox. Late of NRST, retail-grade, sold the second tier in the Gray Zone. You went dark four years ago and a building remembers what a building bought." The bay's sodium lights held their pitch. "We are not addressing the crew. We are addressing the woman who has been a hole in our audit since intake. Come up the rack assignments and we close the door on the rest. One name for twenty-one beds. Helix is offering you the math."

In a basement under a building that filed her hand away as gone, Vox sat with the open channel warm against her jaw and did the arithmetic the building had handed her, and found that for once in four years it came out clean. *The thing I sold so I'd never have to be it again, and they walk it back to me like a debt with my own face on the coin.* She had spent four years being the channel and never the body on it — the rider who lived in absence, Rook had said it kinder than it was — and the whole architecture of her had been built so that no scan, no manifest, no ledger could ever put her in a lit room and make her stand there as a person who could be carried twice.

"You want a hole in your audit to stand up where you can see it," Vox said, and she said it into the loud channel, the one Helix had opened, the one the whole working floor could hear in their teeth. She stood. She heard her own chair go back and did not flatten it out of the sound. "Fine. Here. Vox, late of NRST, retail-grade, the face you bought. I'm walking up the east fire core and I'm going to keep talking, and every clerk on every level is going to hear exactly which beds you deferred and exactly whose hand you filed as gone, and you can spend your whole reconcile chasing the one voice that wants to be found instead of the twenty-one that don't." She was moving now, away from the down-route, away from Scout, a body deliberately wrong in the dark. "Scout. Boiler's seam holds. Go. I've got their attention. It's the only thing I ever had worth giving them."

In bay C the PEREGRINE turned — the whole frame of it pivoting off Sefi's open eyes toward the east core where the loud channel was walking itself into the light — and Scout got her arms under the count she'd come for and heard, under the choir waking in their teeth, the whole crew close around the absent one made loud: Boiler dropping a second wedge to muddy the floor for the woman walking it, Rook's flat voice in the channel saying *east core, all of you, she's not walking it alone*, and Gerald — the boy named, the deal that held even here — breathing out the only thing he had, which was the count, twenty-one, steady, so the floor that was about to remember Vox would have to hear the children first.

[Beat 12]
Anneke Oyelowo-Sato came down the east core herself, because she did not delegate the thing she could close in person, and she met Rook at the landing between levels where the loud channel was walking up and the count was walking down, and the two arithmetic women looked at each other in the crimson light with the wedge-frequency still in both their teeth. She was not armed. She had a tablet the way other people had a sidearm, balanced flat on her forearm, the reconcile open on it in twenty-one rows, and she did not close it when Rook arrived. She let Rook see the rows. That was the opening move. *She wants me to read the ledger. She thinks the ledger is the argument.*

"You priced them," Rook said, flat, the way she said most true things, and she put her hand on the rail the way the clerk three levels down had put a hand on a bed rail — to steady the thing, or to steady herself against it. "Sefi. Adalemo. The woman at forty with the clean cabling. You ran the number on each one and the number came out keep, so you kept them, and you filed the hand that didn't price as gone."

"We pay for a thing," Anneke said, unhurried, and she turned the tablet so the rows faced Rook fully, "and someone else receives it. The gap between the paying and the receiving is the only honest room in this city, and I have furnished it carefully for thirty years. You stand in it too. You took a contract for a count. You're carrying it up a ramp right now in another woman's arms. Tell me the math is different because you say the names out loud."

And Rook drew the Reibo.

It came up out of the coat in the one motion she had practiced ten thousand times and used on a planned job exactly never, the flechette muzzle finding the soft hinge of Anneke's jaw, and for one clean second the arithmetic was simple in a way it had not been simple in three jobs: end the woman, end the program's hand, walk the count out. Her finger took up the slack.

Anneke did not move. She did not look at the gun. She tilted the tablet a half-degree more, and on the reconcile one row burned amber where the other twenty stayed green — *Okonkwo-Reyes, S.* — and beside it a live cursor blinked in a guard's hand four levels down, holding on a word Anneke had not yet spoken. "The flechette is faster than the gun," she said, meaning the harvest gun, the one already trained on Sefi's open eyes in the bay below, "but it is not faster than a word, and I have not finished the word. Put it down, or I finish it."

And Rook's hand stopped. Boiler had spent his whole working life listening to load — the particular complaint a stair made under more than it was rated for, the way a wall told you what it was carrying before the carrying killed it — and now he stood in the sub-basement with both hands flat on the harvest manifold, the cold one, the one NRST had built into the spine of the building to draw what it priced down through forty levels of bed and clerk and cabling, and he felt it the way he felt everything, structurally. *It's a stair. He had been whistling the whole job, low, tuneless, the thing the crew teased him for, and he heard himself stop and did not start again.

He cut the manifold by the only method a man who works on foot has, which is to make himself the failure point — wedge his shoulder under the conduit junction where the draw concentrated and lift it off its seat, so the load it was pulling had nowhere to flow but back through the body holding the line. The Sojourn sat where NRST's tier met the Gray Zone seam, a long counter and a long window, and the window gave the lake the way only the cheap places did — nobody on this tier paid for a view of water that predated their lease. She had carried two counts out of three diners in this city and both times the count came out as a number — eleven, then nine — and both times the number was clean because a number is clean, a number never says who didn't make it, that's the mercy of arithmetic and that's the lie of it.
