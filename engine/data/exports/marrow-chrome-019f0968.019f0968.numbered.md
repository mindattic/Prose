# Crimson & Chrome

[Beat 1]
Mrs. Chen put the bowl down in front of Soraya before Soraya had ordered. That was Mrs. Chen's way of saying she'd been seen coming a block off. The broth went up in a sheet of steam that fogged the seam of the booth's old privacy glass. Across the table Rook ate with her right hand and kept the tally with her left, the way she always did — three fingers tapping the table edge in a pattern that wasn't nerves and wasn't music. The coat hung open enough to show the Reibo's grip riding her hip, matte where the crimson light off Mrs. Chen's sign couldn't get a hold on it. Chrome takes the neon and gives it back prettier. The Reibo gave back nothing. That was the point of it.

"Don't tell me the number yet," Rook said. "Tell me the shape."

She didn't pick the chopsticks back up. Her right hand stayed flat on the table edge, just past the bowl.

"Twenty-one," Soraya said, and set her chopsticks down with a care that had nothing to do with the chopsticks. "Off Old Harbor. The ones the registry wrote off when the barge's manifest went dark. They're alive, Rook. Scattered, hunted, and somebody on a registry desk finally put the count together and started selling it."

Rook stopped eating. The three fingers kept going — tap, tap, tap — and then stopped too.

The number sat in her chest before her mind had a name for what it was doing there. Twenty-one going in. Fourteen out. The gap had always been seven, not twenty-one, and she had kept it that way deliberately — seven was the manageable wound, the one she could carry without letting it change the tally she kept for everyone else. Twenty-one made the wound the whole job. Her lungs did something she didn't authorize, a single short compression.

"Yes," she said.

The word was already gone across the table before she'd decided to spend it, and she let the booth go quiet around it. Her right hand had stopped on the bowl's rim, the chopsticks bridged across it, broth steam climbing past her knuckles warm enough to read. Behind Soraya the sign bled its slow crimson through the fogged glass and laid a wash of it across the table, across the backs of both their hands, across the unmoving chrome at her hip that took the color and kept it and offered nothing back. She watched the steam instead. Fourteen out of Tidewell. Twenty-one going in. The seven had lived in the gap between those counts for eleven years and she hadn't opened the gap on purpose and she was not going to pretend otherwise.

*Wennick.* The name moved through her the way it always did when she let a count get away from her — the shape of a voice she'd been carrying at the bottom of the count since Tidewell, the one that had taught her why the count mattered, why a runner who got sloppy about the numbers was a runner who had already decided some of them could be wrong. She had promised herself, at a different window over the same lake, that she would never be sloppy again.

Her right hand stayed flat on the bowl. She kept it there.

"You said yes before I told you where they are," Soraya said quietly. "Before I told you who's hunting them."

"I know." Rook picked the chopsticks back up. "Tell me anyway."

[Beat 2]
Soraya named them the way she always did, in the order they'd say yes — Boiler first, because Boiler always answered a wall before he answered a person.

"He'll want the building before the job," she said, and Rook nodded, because that was Boiler exactly. Four years ago that read had put her on the right side of a collapsing floor — a half-second, his hand flat on a pillar, his chin tipping toward the east stair, no words, just the tip of the chin — while two men she'd never gotten the names of went down into the dust with the rest of the slab. She could still feel the floor go in her knees if she let it: the lurch, then the absence, the silence a building makes in the quarter-second before it admits it's done. Boiler had felt it coming through his palm a breath sooner than the floor knew. That breath was everything.

Somewhere under the booth the Pulse came through the slab as a low even hum, the city's only honest heartbeat — the vacuum-tube rail that threaded GLMZ's spine at Mach six, slugs seating and burning, a vibration you stopped hearing the way you stopped hearing your own pulse until the night you needed it gone and couldn't have it.

"Scout's in the Gray Zone still," Soraya went on, "walking it on foot like the maps owe her money. She's got the dog."

Rook almost smiled. The dog was Gerald, a salvaged six-legged crawler Scout had named something stupid on purpose, because a thing called Gerald doesn't read as a weapon on a checkpoint scan — it reads as a girl's project, a junk pet, a reason to wave you through. Scout didn't believe in any access she couldn't reach with her own two feet; she'd turned down clean Pulse berths to walk the gray between zones, six hours through ferrocement canyons and checkpoint queues, because — Scout's words — *the train tells you where the doors are; the walk tells you where they put the ones they don't mention.*

"Lace will come because Lace wants to see who breaks first," Soraya said. "And Vox —"

"Vox will say no, then show up two hours late with coffee and pretend she was always coming." Rook set the chopsticks down.

On the Tidewell job Lace had called which of the hired hands would fold under pressure three days before the pressure came, and she'd been right about all of them.

And Vox — Vox would grumble down the line that she was retired, that she had a life now, that Rook had some nerve, and then the booth glass would fog and she'd be standing in it with two cups going cold in her hands and that look that dared anyone to mention she'd come. Four years gone and Rook could already taste the coffee. Always terrible. Vox always brought it anyway.

"That's five." She breathed out slow. "Five I can carry."

She let the steam off the bowl climb past her face before she said the rest, because the rest was the one with weight.

"Stave won't answer his line. He left it on the table the last time — the whole rig, the comm, everything. Said he was done being someone people called when it got bad." *And I'm the bad. I'm the call he was talking about.* "But Stave's the only one who can get us into the registry vault clean, and he knows it, and he knows I know it. So somebody's going to have to go knock on a door that's been shut on purpose for four years."

Soraya had been quiet through all of it — not the waiting quiet of a woman who'd finished her list, but the quiet of one who was watching the list become a weight and deciding whether to say so. She set her spoon down carefully.

"I asked you first," she said. "Before I asked any of them. I wanted to know if *you* would come."

Rook looked at her. Soraya's face was the face of a woman who had done her own counting for four years and had been very precise about what she put in which column.

"You already knew I'd come."

"I knew you'd say yes." She picked up the spoon again, turned it over in her hands, set it back down. The motion had nothing to do with the soup. "That's not the same thing. I needed to know if you'd come because you wanted to — or because this is the kind of count you can't leave unbalanced." She looked up then, level, the scientist's eyes that never offered a charitable reading when the precise one was available. "Because if it's the count, I brought you a job. And if it's the other thing, I brought you the one I should have brought you four years ago, when I had the count and didn't trust it enough to say it out loud."

The steam rose between them and cleared and rose again.

"I should have told you," Soraya said, and the three words were smaller than anything else she'd said, stripped of the data, and Rook felt them land on the side of her neck where the draft came in under the booth's old seal. "About the twenty-one. When I had the count. Before the registry desk started selling it. I had it and I held it and I told myself I was protecting them by not saying it anywhere a ledger could hear."

Rook held still on that. She didn't fill it in. She let Soraya own the whole of it.

"You were protecting yourself," she said.

Soraya's jaw set. She didn't deny it.

The three fingers stopped. Rook's hand closed on the bowl's rim — the grip had too much in it — and she set it down.

"Yes." A breath. "And I'm asking you to come anyway. Both things are true and I'm asking anyway, because you're the only one whose count I trust to carry more than it was rated for."

The hum came up through the slab again, steadier now, a slug seating into its berth somewhere down the line with a settling she felt more than heard. She reached for the slate and held it flat under her right hand, one breath, before she picked it up.

"Then we're both asking for something we should have said earlier," Rook said. She did not say what she should have said. She wrote Scout's address into the slate and held the booth door open with her shoulder while Soraya put her coat on, and outside Mrs. Chen's the night was the same night it always was in GLMZ, which was cold and indifferent and lit from below, and the two of them stood in it a moment before they went their separate ways to knock on doors that had been shut on purpose.

[Beat 3]
The door knock came in three days later, but it wasn't Stave who opened it. It was the manifest.

Soraya laid it on the booth table folded to the wrong page — the page nobody at the registry desk was meant to read in sequence, the one that only made sense if you turned the order inside out and read it backward against itself. She set it down without a word, three fingers flat on the paper, and slid it under the crimson light where the steam from Mrs. Chen's kitchen kept fogging the laminate and clearing again.

Rook spread the manifest flat and read it the way Boiler reads a column: not the words, the load. She let her eyes go past the letterhead and the Voluntary Relocation header and the stamped department seals, all the apparatus a thing wears when it wants to be believed, and she found the shape of the weight underneath. Twenty-one names. A single barge out of Old Harbor, departure logged to the minute, arrival never entered. And the names didn't disperse into twenty-one destinations the way relocated people are supposed to disperse. They funneled. They ran down the page into one column, under one repeated word, the word stamped beside every name like a checkmark — *processed.*

Below that column, smaller, was a routing code — a chain-of-custody tag, the kind a corp stamps on freight it expects to be questioned about.

She put her finger on it and did not lift it.

She'd seen that code before. Four years ago, burned into the side of a tank, and she'd read it then as a serial number, the kind of thing you don't think about because the thing it's printed on is already trying to kill you. She'd carried it out of Axiom in the part of her memory that keeps receipts whether you ask it to or not. Now it sat under her fingertip beside the word *processed*, beside twenty-one names that had walked onto a boat, and the two facts found each other in the dark and clicked.

*That's not a transfer order.* Her finger stayed where it was, pressing the paper flat against the table as if holding it down would keep it from being true. *That's a bill of lading. They didn't lose seven the way you lose a name in a flood. They wrote it like freight because freight gets a routing code and a count and a destination, and people get a story, and somebody figured out you could pay for one and file it as the other.*

"The Reclamation ran the barge," Soraya said, and let it sit a moment before she went on, the way she always did with the bad ones. "Registered Reads, all of them — neuretics-capable people the corps log and covet, the ones any facility would pay to have live and cooperative. They walked aboard at Old Harbor under their own names, in daylight, because the order said sunrise. Said sky. Said a place the processors can't redirect you to and back. They believed it because it was the one promise this city keeps — that if a relocation says open water and morning, it means open water and morning." She turned the page back the right way then, slow, smoothing the fold flat with two fingers, gentle, the way you'd draw a sheet up over a face. "One of them came back. She's the reason I have the count at all."

The woman came in behind the steam.

Mrs. Chen put a bowl down for her without being asked — set it at the empty place at the end of the booth with the small economy of a hand that has done it many nights running — and that, more than anything Soraya could have said, told Rook how long this woman had been folded into the back of this room, fed and waiting. She had a left hand that didn't fully close. At the wrist the chrome and the flesh argued, a seam where something had been done and then undone, gone in and come back out wrong, and she carried the hand the way you carry a thing that used to be yours.

She set a paper photograph on the table, face-up.

Twenty-one people on a barge deck, squinting into a morning.

Rook looked at it and did not pick it up. They were standing the way people stand when somebody's told them to gather for a picture and they half-believe it's good news — shoulders not yet braced, one of them caught mid-laugh, a man at the rail with his hand up against the low sun, a kid turned the wrong way to see the water. Morning light coming in flat and clean off the lake, the kind of light that doesn't lie about anything, that just shows you what's there. They didn't know yet. You could see in every face that they didn't know yet.

Something turned over low in Rook's chest, under the ribs, before she had a thought to attach to it — the involuntary lurch of a body recognizing the weight of a thing before the mind catches up. She let it. Then she set her jaw against it and put it where she put the rest, because the photograph wasn't asking her to feel it. It was asking her to do something about it.

"Sefi Okonkwo," the woman said. "There were twenty-one of us when we walked on. I can give you nine." She didn't look at the photo. She'd had four years not to look at it. "The other twelve I watched go through a door. I can tell you the door. I can tell you it locked behind them. And I can tell you that nobody on that registry desk wrote down the sound it made — because the sound it made wasn't on the manifest, and if it's not on the manifest it didn't happen."

She slid the photo across the table to Rook's right hand and left it there.

"You've carried a count like this before," she said. "Fourteen for twenty-one, off Tidewell — Soraya told me there's a name at the bottom of it you don't say out loud. So don't sit there and tell me the number's too big. Tell me when we leave."

The booth went quiet. In that quiet Rook looked at Soraya — not to ask anything, not to confirm. Just to look, the way you look at the person who was standing next to you when the floor went. Soraya was looking at the photograph, at the morning light on twenty-one faces that didn't know yet, and her hands were very still in her lap. She felt Rook's eyes and did not look up, which was its own answer: *I know. I've known for four years. I'm sorry I waited this long to put it in a room where something could be done about it.*

Rook picked up the photograph. She turned it face-down.

"Get me the full barge manifest," she said. "All forty names. I want to know who isn't on it."

[Beat 4]
"The buyer was Helix," Rook said, and saying it out loud put it past the place where she could pretend she didn't know. "That job got Soraya out clean for them — proof the harvest could be pulled live. They lost the survivor list in the move, the way corponations lose what they don't want auditing. Then her partition cracks open four years later and surfaces the count, and the same registry desk that misfiled it starts selling it back at a markup. We didn't stumble onto an audit trail." She heard her own voice flatten into the register she used for bad reads in front of a crew. "We are the audit trail."

She looked at Sefi's left hand, the one that didn't fully close, chrome and flesh still arguing at the wrist. Then at her own right one, which had carried fourteen out of Tidewell and never once asked who had been counting alongside her, marking the same heads with a different pen.

"I ran the rehearsal, and it wasn't Axiom," she said. "Tidewell was seven years before Soraya. Twenty-one names on a contract that called itself a rescue, paid by the head, and every job Helix has run since has been them refining what I proved was possible there — that a harvest could be walked out looking like mercy. I cashed that payday and called it a good outcome for years. The seven weren't the cost. They were the product."

The booth held the line after she said it. The dam's hum came back in. The broth was still producing faint steam over Soraya's bowl, the kind that happened when food had been ordered and sat and was now going the direction food went when no one touched it. Rook watched the steam thin.

She had a method for bad numbers. You looked at the figure, you confirmed it was the figure, and then you did the next thing. The method worked because doing the next thing was a choice, and choices were countable, and countable things could be managed. The figure was that she had been paid by the head and the heads had been people and she had believed the word they put on it because the word was a door and she had needed a door out of something and she had walked through it with her hands full. She had confirmed the figure. Now she needed the next thing.

She did not look at Soraya. She looked at the routing code, the one in front of Soraya's bowl, a document she had been reading for three days as a way in. Looking at it now she could see both things simultaneously — the route and the thing she'd made herself not see while she was reading it — and this was a skill that had its uses but cost something every time you exercised it. Her right hand was still on the table. She moved it to her lap and let it close around nothing and held it there until the pressure came back.

Across the table Soraya had gone very still, the stillness of a woman doing the kind of counting that does not allow for rounding. Her hands were flat on the booth, palms down, and Rook watched one of them — the right one — curl inward slightly at the fingers, slow, a motion that had no place to go and stopped before it became a fist. She was looking at the routing code. Not at Rook. At the code, the way you looked at a sentence that has made the whole paragraph's meaning change.

"They needed me willing," she said, and her voice was the data-reader's voice, precise, the one she used when the figure was worse than she'd modeled. "Not just compliant. Willing. If I'd fought, they couldn't have proved the research could be walked out clean. The whole sample had to be voluntary — a researcher who believed she was being rescued." She pressed both hands flat again. "I believed it because I wanted to believe it. Because someone gave me a door that opened the right direction and I didn't look at whose hand was on the latch." She finally looked up. Her eyes found Rook's and stayed. "You opened that door. And I walked through it, and I told myself I was lucky."

Sefi didn't flinch. She'd had four years and a door that locked behind twelve people to teach her how not to.

"Then you know the work better than they think you do," she said. "When do we leave?"

The question held the room a second — the pragmatics of a woman who had already done the accounting of guilt and need and found the two could coexist, and had decided which one to act on. Rook recognized the method. She used it herself.

She set her finger flat on the routing code and held it there one breath. Then she lifted it, and reached for the chopsticks, and ate — because she had confirmed the figure, and the next thing after a confirmed figure was always the next thing, and the next thing was forty names and a barge and twenty-one people she had not reached yet.

She felt Soraya's eyes on her profile and did not look back.

[Beat 5]
The door to the booth had been shut three days and Adalemo opened it himself, which Rook noticed before she noticed his face — that a man who'd spent four years routing other people through doors had come to this one and put his own hand on it. The booth's privacy seal was rated to be opened from a slate, from a desk, from anywhere but the threshold itself.

She let the door close behind him before she let him speak. That was the pause: the dam swallowing the booth's hum back down to the low crimson nothing it kept for paying tables, the smell of the noodle broth going from steam to grease as it cooled. A man who walked his deliverables through doors did not stand inside one. He stood near the latch the way you stand near an exit you've used on other people.

He didn't sit. He set a partition slate on the table beside Sefi's photograph, aligned the long edge to the long edge the way a clerk squares a stack, and left his fingers on it. "I'm the desk that misfiled the survivor list," he said. "I want that on the table before the food gets cold. I didn't lose it. I was paid to lose it. Helix routed the QUANTA through a relocation contingency line and I signed it because the line existed and the signature was already half-printed and I told myself a missing list isn't a body."

"There were twenty-one on that barge," Adalemo went on, and now he turned the slate so the column faced Rook, the same *processed* she'd been reading sideways for three days, "and the manifest I cleared listed forty names across the quarter. Forty I audited. I never read a forty-first." He moved one finger down the column and stopped it the way she stopped hers on a routing code, not on the words. "I built the count clean enough that nobody upstream had to know there was a count. I'm the reason it surfaces now as something they can sell instead of something somebody can prove. So whatever you think you're owed off me — I owe more than that, and I know the exact figure, which is more than they ever wanted me to."

She didn't answer.

She let the figure sit in the air the way he'd set the slate down, squared, waiting. There was a thing people did when a man confessed clean — they reached, they reassured, they made the room warm again because a cold room with a confession in it was unbearable. She'd watched fixers do it her whole life and never once seen the reach cost the man anything. So she let the room stay cold. She let him stand at the latch with what he knew and no one to hand it to. The broth ticked as it cooled. Somewhere past the dam a slug hit the Pulse's vacuum tube and the building took it in its molars and let it go.

Then she looked at his hands on the slate, both of them flat, and then at her own right one still turned over on the photograph where she'd hidden Sefi's face from herself. Two people who'd each been handed a ledger by someone who knew exactly which column they'd serve, and who'd served it, and who'd told themselves the ledger wasn't a body right up until the body had a face that squinted into a morning.

She recognized the shape of that. She had been in a version of this booth, across from a version of herself, three days ago — the figure confirmed, the method applied, the next thing chosen. She had done it fast enough that Soraya hadn't seen her do it. She had done it the way she did everything that couldn't be unseen: by looking directly at it and then continuing, the speed of the continuation being the difference between a professional and someone who needed the room to be warm. Adalemo had done the same thing. He had done it before he crossed the threshold. He was here because he had already looked at his figure and done his next thing, and his next thing was her.

That was useful. Men who'd confirmed their own figures and arrived at a professional anyway were the ones whose count you could trust, because they had no room left for the comfort of denial.

"Then we're not allies," she said. "We're two people who finally read the same book the same direction. You bring what PEREGRINE saw. I bring the seven I left and the fourteen who'll move for me. Stave opens the vault, if I can knock soft enough."

She lifted her right hand off the photograph at last — felt the gloss let go of her fingertips, the routing code's ridge releasing — and she did not turn the picture over even now. She reached instead to the slate and set her hand flat beside his, palm down on the cold partition glass, the column of *processed* running up between them, and stopped a centimeter short of his fingers. Close enough to feel the warmth come off them. Not close enough to be touching.

"First thing you do for me — you find me the forty-first name. The one you never read. Because somebody on that barge was never on your manifest, and that's the head nobody got paid for, and that's the one they'll have left somewhere they think nobody's still counting."

[Beat 6]
Sefi turned her left hand over on the table so the palm faced up, and the chrome at her wrist caught the crimson light and held it — a seam where flesh stopped being flesh, put there by a Splicer whose name wasn't on any manifest either, and nobody on the registry desk had logged the date. She didn't show it like a wound. She showed it like a fact she'd checked twice and wanted entered into the record before the next thing she said could be argued with.

"You want to know what a door that locks is for," she said. "I'll tell you the room. They walk you down a corridor that smells like nothing — that's the first wrong thing, a place that processes bodies and has no smell, because the air's been scrubbed to spec. The walls are that white that isn't a color, it's an absence somebody paid for. And there's a hum. Not the floor. Lower. You feel it in your back teeth before you hear it, and you think it's the machines, and it is, but it's also us — the ones already in the racks, the Reads they pulled and kept warm. Class V doesn't run on dead tissue. It runs on tissue that still half-remembers it was a person."

She paused, and her tongue moved against the inside of her teeth like she was checking whether the hum had ever fully left them.

"And then you hear the doors," she said. "All down the corridor, the same sound, exactly the same, like one door learned to be every door. A soft seat and a click. No air in it. The good ones in the GLMZ wheeze — you know the sound a thing makes when it's been opened ten thousand times and nobody's oiled the second hinge. These don't. These got maintained. You stand in that white and you count the clicks and you understand that somewhere in this whole stack of us, the one thing they kept running right is the part that closes."

She closed her own hand. The chrome argued with the flesh and the flesh lost, the way it had lost on the barge. "They don't call it killing. There's no word in the manifest for it. They call it intake, and conditioning, and substrate yield, and every one of those words has a line item and a QUANTA figure beside it, and the figure goes down the longer you sit in the rack because you degrade. We're inventory with a shelf life. A registered Read costs them more fresh than spoiled, so the throughput's tuned to keep us *useful*, which is a worse word than dead. I watched twelve go through that door and I want you to understand they didn't die in there. They got priced."

Rook didn't answer.

She was aware first of her right hand on the photograph — pressing it flat, heavy, not moving. Then her jaw, which she found already set, the back teeth pressed together hard enough that she made herself notice it and didn't unclench. She'd thought, walking in here, that she'd come to plan a crew and a vault and a cost. And the hum Sefi was describing had gotten into the room with her — not the sound of it, she couldn't hear it, but the shape of it: there was a frequency low enough you'd feel it in your teeth and call it the machines and be wrong.

Across the table Adalemo hadn't moved either, hadn't squared anything, his clerk's hands flat and useless on the slate. Then — slowly, without looking like he meant to — he turned one of them over. Palm up, the way Sefi's was. He looked at his own palm for a moment, then placed it flat again, and the placing cost him something Rook could see in the set of his neck.

Soraya had not spoken since Sefi began. She was looking at the table — not at the manifest, not at the photograph, at the laminate itself, the neutral surface, the thing you look at when you are holding a model in your head that you built to be theoretical and it has stopped being theoretical. Her breathing was controlled and she was controlling it on purpose.

The crimson light held on Sefi's chrome and on the dead photograph, and the booth went quiet the way rooms go quiet when the thing in them is too heavy for anyone to be the first to lift it.

"Helix didn't build a vault," Rook said, and her voice came out level the way it came out level when the level was the only thing holding. "They built maintenance they don't have to do. The city stopped fixing the people the second it figured out the people were the part it could sell. Everything in the GLMZ they stopped maintaining, they stopped because the bill came due somewhere they could see it. This is the one place the bill comes due on the *inside*, where the asset pays it down by degrading — and they tuned the doors to close right so nothing they could still sell got out early."

She set her finger back on the routing code, on the word *processed*. "And they used me to prove the racks could be stocked live. So when we knock soft on that vault, we're not cracking a bank. We're walking into the one room in GLMZ they kept the lights on for, because it's the only infrastructure they never let rot — and that's the part I owe. Find me the forty-first name, Adalemo. I want to know who they warmed and never billed."

[Beat 7]
Adalemo cleared the slate of Sefi's column and brought up a different one, and Rook watched him do it the way she'd watch a clerk she didn't trust yet — the economy of it, no wasted motion, a man who'd squared too many stacks to fumble the one that mattered. Vox was where she'd been the whole sit, at the edge of the booth where the crimson light went thin against the partition, turned three-quarters away with her shoulder to the table and her good ear toward the door. She hadn't said a word in an hour.

"Before you spend the fourteen," Adalemo said, "you spend this."

A face came up on the partition. Vox's face, rendered in Cinderblock gray, the resolution of an asset that had been bought once and filed — and the live woman three feet from it didn't turn to look, just lowered her chin a degree, the way you tilt your head off a thing you can already feel on the side of your neck.

"Stave paid for this in the same currency you all pay each other in — a face, traded for a vault crack he hadn't done yet. He logged it. The trade exists. And Helix doesn't pull threads, Rook. Helix pulls the one thread the whole garment hangs from, and then it audits the unraveling for line items." He moved his finger to the corner where a routing code blinked. "This is the thread. They ID Vox, they walk the trade backward to Stave, and Stave is standing in the only door you've got."

*The one who opens the vault is the one they ID first.*

Rook didn't reach for the slate and she didn't lift her hand off the photograph. The figure on the wall and the woman at the edge of the booth were the same entry, and Helix would only ever audit one of them, and it wouldn't be the one with the pulse. The booth's air handler held its one low note. Vox shifted her weight off one foot and didn't turn, and the small sound of it — boot leather on grated floor — was louder in Rook's ear than the routing code blinking on the wall.

Helix didn't build crooked. It built the straightest possible run from the asset it could sell to the body that had handled it, and then it sent a clerk down the run with a stack to square. Vox's face, filed. The trade, logged. The thread, blinking. Stave, in the door. It was a clean line. That was the trouble with it.

"Then the way in isn't a way in," she said, and her voice came out flat and held, the flatness doing the work her hands weren't allowed to do. "It's a way Vox doesn't surface until after, and a way Stave's standing somewhere that isn't the door when they walk the trade back. I build two cracks. The soft knock, and the one that opens if the soft knock costs me Stave. I don't put a person on a number I can't carry twice."

At the edge of the frame Vox let a breath go through her nose, slow, the sound of agreement from someone who'd been waiting to hear whether she was a line item in this room or a name in it, and had her answer now and didn't trust it yet.

Then she turned around.

Anneke Oyelowo came down the east core herself, because she did not delegate the thing she could close in person, and she met Rook at the landing between levels where the loud channel was walking up and the count was walking down, and the two women who counted looked at each other in the crimson light with the wedge-frequency still in both their teeth. Inventory. The same tell, in another woman's hand, and Anneke caught Rook seeing it and the fingers went still all at once, the way Rook's did, the only tell either of them had left.

"You priced them," Rook said, flat, the way she said most true things, and she put her hand on the rail the way the clerk three levels down had put a hand on a bed rail — to steady the thing, or to steady herself against it. "We pay for a thing," Anneke said, unhurried, and she turned the tablet so the rows faced Rook fully, "and someone else receives it. "It's different," Rook said, "because you just lied to me with your hands and I watched you do it."

Anneke's fingers did not move and that was how Rook knew she'd landed it.

"You count the same way I do," Rook said. "You learned it the same place — somewhere that taught you a number was the only thing that would hold still while everything around it came apart. You tap it out so the count stays under your hand where you can trust it more than you trust your own face. I know that hand, Anneke. I've got the same one. So don't sell me the honest room. You don't believe the gap is honest any more than I do. You believe it's *quiet*. You furnished it so nobody would have to hear the receiving."

Something moved behind Anneke's eyes then — not fear, Rook had been braced for fear and it wasn't that. It was the stillness of a woman doing a count she had decided thirty years ago she would never do again, and finding the figures still clear. Her jaw set. Rook watched it set and recognized the set, because she wore it.

And Rook drew the Reibo.

It came up out of the coat in the one motion she had practiced ten thousand times and used on a planned job exactly never, the flechette muzzle finding the soft hinge of Anneke's jaw, and for one clean second the read was simple in a way it hadn't been simple in three jobs: end the woman, end the program's hand, walk the count out. Her finger took up the slack.

"The flechette is faster than the gun," she said, meaning the harvest gun, the one already trained on Sefi's open eyes in the bay below, "but it is not faster than a word, and I have not finished the word. Put it down, or I finish it." And then, lower, almost to herself, the planner's voice slipping a notch the way Adalemo's clean cuff had slipped: "You will make me say it. You will make me be the one who said it, in front of myself, after thirty years of paying other people to be the mouth."

And Rook's hand stopped. The Reibo stayed level, the slack taken up, the shot a quarter-pound of pressure and a whole world away — because the woman in front of her had built the room so the trigger spent the exact thing Rook had climbed into the Reclamation to carry out. She held it there, unable to fire and unwilling to drop it, the one motion she was best at frozen against a ledger. That was the cost, and she felt it land — the count in her chest going to twenty-two, herself the new entry, the woman who could end this if she would only let Sefi go back to being a number — and she would not let go of it, and she would not fire, and the standing in that was its own kind of bleeding. "You said the word's not finished," Rook said, and her voice came out level the way it came out when the level was the only thing holding. "Then you haven't said it yet. That's the only second you've got. Stand in it."

"You let the twenty-one run," Rook said, lowering the Reibo a hand's width — not in surrender but because the gun had never been the argument and they both knew it now. "Years ago, and again just now. You pivoted your guard off Sefi's column to chase one loud voice up the west core, and twenty-one beds went unwatched for the count to reach them. You did the mercy and you didn't price it first. Anneke looked at the line. Rook watched her do it — watched the woman who never stopped counting count the one thing she'd refused to enter, the twenty-one against the satisfaction of the catch, and find it wouldn't balance, had never balanced, had only ever been kept off the page. Her thumb came off the tablet. The amber row stayed amber. But the cursor four levels down blinked once and went dark, because the hand holding it had stopped getting an order — and Anneke did not say *put it down* again, and she did not finish the word, and the not-finishing cost her something Rook could see go out of her face like heat off cooling steel.

It did not look like surrender, because it wasn't one. Rook did not answer that, because there wasn't an answer that wasn't a lie, and she was done with the lies that made a cold room warm.

She holstered the Reibo — the tremor still in her hand — and stepped sideways onto the down-route without turning her back on Anneke. "I came down here for twenty-one beds. I've got twenty-one. You came down here for one, and you don't. Short by the only entry you made the trip for." She put her eyes on the ramp, not on the woman behind her, and started down toward the count. Boiler had spent his whole working life listening to load — the complaint a stair made under more than it was rated for, the way a wall told you what it was carrying before the carrying killed it — and now he stood in the sub-basement with both hands flat on the harvest manifold, the cold one, the one Cinderblock had built into the spine of the building to draw what it priced down through forty levels of bed and clerk and cabling, and he felt it the way he felt everything, structurally. *It's a stair. It's just a stair carrying more than it's rated for. Except the load is people, and the load has names, and I read the names same as I read a beam.* He stopped whistling. He had been whistling the whole job, low, tuneless, the thing the crew teased him for, and he heard himself stop and did not start again.

He cut the manifold the only way a man on foot cuts a thing rated for machinery: shoulder under the conduit junction where the draw concentrated, boots set wide, and lift. He drove up with his legs first, the way you take a fallen beam off a person — controlled, not fast — and the junction broke its seat with a sound like a hinge shearing, cold metal giving up the contact it had held for thirty years in one clean pop, and then the full load of the draw came down onto him.

The conduit was colder than the room, colder than metal had any business being, the chill of a thing that had spent thirty years drinking heat out of warm rooms forty floors up and never once giving it back, and the cold went straight through his jacket and his shoulder and into the joint before the weight did. He found the seat of the junction with the meat over his collarbone and held, and the load came onto him all at once — not a number, a *pressure*, the entire downward want of the manifold finding the one place in the city that had agreed to stand under it. *Twenty-one. Boiler, you can hold for twenty-one. You held the boiler seam for Scout, you can hold this.* The first surge went through his left arm like a cable taken past its rating, and he heard the sound his own joint made — not a crack, lower than that, a wet structural give, the exact note a fitting makes the instant before it stops being a fitting — and his body did the thing bodies do, which was tell him in one white flood to set the weight down and step off the line. He kept lifting. He counted instead of listening to it. He had spent a life trusting what load told him, and this once he made himself the wall that lies, the wall that carries the thing it was never rated for and says nothing, and he held the give in his arm shut by main force the way you hold a door, and somewhere four levels up the crimson light hitched, dimmed, and the draw on the working floor went slack — and the count Gerald was holding reached the rooms it was meant to reach, and the rooms held.

Adalemo's crossing finished on the bay-C feed, which Rook had left open on purpose so the building would have to log its own witness. Helix's voice was still routing through every speaker in the building, still asking for the count back. Adalemo did not answer it. He turned instead to the panel in front of him and finished rerouting the alert his own hand had built the door for, and that was the whole of the crossing — not a rescue, not a redemption speech, just a man who had spent four years deferring people finally putting his weight on the other side of the ledger before anyone could revoke the credential that let him do it. The Sojourn sat where Cinderblock's tier met the Gray Zone seam, a long counter and a long window, and the window gave the lake the way only the cheap places did — nobody on this tier paid for a view of water that predated their lease.
