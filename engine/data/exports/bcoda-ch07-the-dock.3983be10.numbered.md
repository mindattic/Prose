# Chapter 7: The Dock

[Beat 1]
The fifth came up the block the way ordinary people came up blocks, which was to say Kyle didn't clock her until she was already inside the loose triangle the three of them made, and that bothered him more than if she'd dropped out of a window. She was medium. Medium height, a coat the color of a coat, hair pulled back the way a person pulls it back when they've stopped thinking about it. The kind of face that a room would round down to nothing. You could share an elevator with her and a week later swear you'd ridden up alone.

"Null," she said, which was the codename and the whole of the introduction, and she said it the way you'd give a barista a name you didn't expect to hear called.

Kyle read her: quiet, polite, forgettable on purpose, which was a skill and not an accident, and skills that took that much work to look like nothing were usually pointed at something. He had no idea what she did. The brief hadn't said. On a clean crew the brief never said more than your own piece — Echo knew the building, Stash knew the gear, Kyle knew that his piece was to stand between everyone else and whatever came through a door — and the fact that he didn't know Null's piece meant her piece was the one the client cared about.

"You're the muscle," she said to him. Not a question. She had a read of her own. Then, tilting her head the way someone does when they've already decided to like the answer: "What do I call you?"

Kyle opened his mouth. He got one syllable in — maybe less, the K of his name — and Echo's voice came into the channel, flat: *No real names.*

He closed his mouth. He looked around. The corner. The rain. The shuttered pharmacy with Stash's weight still denting the doorway. And down near the base of the step, where the gutter had deposited it from somewhere else, a glass soda bottle with a faded orange label.

"Soda Pop," he said.

Null snorted. It was a real snort, unguarded, the first thing she'd done that wasn't careful, and it did something to her ordinary face he hadn't expected.

Kyle picked up the bottle and threw it against the pharmacy wall. The glass went everywhere — sharp, sudden, the small violence of it carrying a weight out of proportion to the sound it made.

"Broken Glass," he said.

She made the same sound again. The snort had company now; she was working to keep it contained. She looked at the pieces on the wet concrete. She looked at him. Something moved in her face — amusement, maybe, or a reappraisal, or both — and when it settled she said:

"I'm just going to call you K."

He went back to watching the street, and did not examine why it mattered to him whether she could.

[Beat 2]
The thing that came up the ramp had been a man once and had spent a great deal of money disagreeing with the fact. Four arms. Three of them ended in shotguns — not arms holding shotguns, arms that were shotguns, the barrels socketed straight into the wrist mounts, short and wide-bored and built for a hallway. The fourth arm was wrong in a way the other three weren't. It ended in a launcher tube, a stubby rack of cells where a rocket battery would sit if it had one, and the rack was dark. Whether it had one was not information he could afford to assume.

He wore his money like scar tissue. Whoever fronted the augmentation had not paid for the part that made it look like anything but what it was, and so he came up the ramp as a wide low silhouette of barrels and intent, unhurried, a man who had done this enough times to know that the running started later.

Kyle put the dock's geometry in his head in the second it took the shape to clear the top of the ramp: concrete pillar to his left, the steel stairwell behind and right, Stash and the cases between him and the bay door, Null at his shoulder. Echo was on the exterior approach, covering the freight alley — voice only tonight, no sightline into the bay. Ledger was somewhere behind and to the right. The vehicle had come from below, from inside, from a route the brief hadn't covered. Nobody had covered it.

"War Dog," he said, by way of telling them his name was none of their business. The codename, the whole introduction, the way you'd give a barista a name you didn't expect to hear called. "Only here for one of you."

He looked past Kyle, past Stash, past the cases, to the woman in the coat, who had gone very still.

"The Read," War Dog said. "Client wants the head that holds the file. Rest of you can walk."

She made a sound through her nose — not quite a laugh, not quite a sigh, the particular sound of someone whose working hypothesis has just been confirmed at the worst possible time. Kyle registered it the same way he registered everything that mattered: once, cleanly, filed. She did not look at War Dog. She looked at the dock geometry — the pillar, the stairwell, the bay door, the cases — the same inventory he was running, the same math, done in the same second. Her chin came up a fraction.

She had pulled a CorpoNation vault out of a man's skull four hours ago and she was doing the arithmetic now with the same attention she'd given that.

"Kyle," she said, which was not a question.

[Beat 3]
The math finished itself. The job had been clean because the job had never been the data — the data was bait, and a Read who'd just pulled a CorpoNation vault out of a man's skull now carried it behind her eyes, where the only thing that could delete it was coming up the ramp.

Kyle did not draw. He moved one step, an ordinary step, the kind of step a man takes to ease a stiff knee, and it put him — *almost* — between the barrels and the coat.

The lead arm was already moving. Not at Kyle. Past him. War Dog had done this before, had read the exact step and fired through it before it could close, and the blast went past Kyle's shoulder at the range of a handshake.

Null went down without a sound. The sound was everything else: the slap of pressure against the side of his face, close enough to feel as heat; the dock ringing off concrete; and underneath it something small — the rasp of her coat going down, the sound a sleeping person makes turning over. His body had already turned and crossed half the distance to her before his mind caught up and told it there was no work to do there.

Stash said something in the channel that was a single clipped syllable and then nothing, and the channel had four voices where there had been five.

Kyle stood between the barrels and the coat that was now on the floor. The lead arm was cycling to chamber another round, three more behind it, and War Dog was looking at him with the patient professional eyes of a man who had already completed the job he was paid for and was taking stock of what remained.

"She's done," War Dog said. It was not cruel. It was simply an accounting.

A stillness came into Kyle that had nothing to do with calm. He drew Silence.

## Three Barrels

[Beat 4]
The first shotgun spoke and the loading dock answered in dust. Kyle was not where the dust was. He had read the lead arm's settle a quarter-second before the wrist committed — the way the shoulder loaded, the way a man with three barrels still aims with his eyes — and he'd gone low and wide, off the line, behind the concrete pillar that would hold up against buckshot, which a freelancer would not. The blast took a bite out of the bay door behind where he'd been and the noise of it was a flat hand against both ears.

His neuretics ran the arithmetic and it came back simple: Cacophony held five rounds, the belt-fed left arm held more than five, and nobody wins a trade against a wall of fire. He kept Silence in hand. Silence asked for distance closed, and the only way through a wall was to be already past it before the wall knew you'd moved.

So he used the dock. The bay was a stacked geometry of crates and lift platform and the steel stairwell climbing to the loading office, and Kyle made War Dog spend his fire on plywood — not running from the guns, running the room around them, so that every shot was a shot at where Kyle had been and every half-second the three arms cycled their breeches was a step closer. The torso turned with him on a ring independent of the legs, and Kyle felt the lead arm track him through a stack of cartons and threw himself off the angle a breath before the boards became air.

"Pillar's chewed," Echo said, fast, in his ear. "You can't trade. He's got a belt-feed on the left arm, he doesn't run dry—"

"I know what he doesn't do," Kyle said, and went up the stairwell.

It was the wrong direction. That was the point. A man fighting three shotguns ran for the exit; Kyle ran for the high steel, because a stair was a corridor and a corridor made three barrels into one.

[Beat 5]
War Dog came up after her, after the file, after the thing the client had paid for, and that was his mistake and Kyle had built it for him. The loading office at the top of the stair was a glass box, a supervisor's perch with a long window that looked down over the whole bay, and Kyle let himself be seen going into it because a man who'd run himself into a glass dead-end was a man you took at your leisure. War Dog came through the door sideways, the only way the wide architecture fit, the lead arm first — and that was the half-second, the one barrel committed and the other two still turning the corner, the wall made into a doorway, the doorway into a single point.

Kyle did not cut the arm. The arm was the strong part. He stepped inside the swing of it, past the muzzle, into the place where a shotgun is just a heavy stick — and there were two seams in reach. One was the cabling at the shoulder ring, the soft junction no plate covered because no engineer expected anything to get that close. The other was the throat, which no engineer had touched at all, and the blade did not care which. Kyle had the quarter-second and both seams in his eye, and the man had killed Null in front of him at the range of a handshake, and he took the shoulder.

Silence went through hydraulic line and signal bundle and the lead arm went dead mid-rotation, dropped, hung. One barrel down. Not a killing cut. A disarming one, in the oldest sense of the word — and the cost of it was already arriving, because a disarmed man turns and a dead one doesn't.

War Dog screamed, and it was a man's scream. There was still enough man in there to feel it.

[Beat 6]
The other two arms came around then, both of them, the belt-fed left and the third, and at that range in that box they could not miss and Kyle could not be there to be missed. He went the only direction left, which was down — dropped under the swing, took the floor on his shoulder, and the double blast went through the glass wall above him and turned the supervisor's window into a falling sea of it, a thousand bright edges coming down across both of them. War Dog flinched from the glass the way anything with a face flinches, and Kyle came up out of the roll already moving, glass grinding under his palms and a hot stinging line opening across the back of his neck, Cacophony in his hand now because the distance had closed to the kind where five rounds was plenty, and he put two into the left arm's belt-feed, into the cycling breech where the mechanism was open and stupid for the third of a second it took to chamber.

The arm jammed. It jammed loud, a wet metal cough, and bled hydraulic fluid down its own elbow in a hot sheet Kyle smelled before he saw — oil and cordite and somewhere in the wreck of the box the burnt-sugar stink of insulation cooking.

The dead launcher arm swung up — reflex or desperation, something the augmentation had learned before the rack went empty — and found nothing to fire. It dropped.

Now War Dog had one working gun and a spent launcher and a severed lead and a glass box full of a man who had spent the whole fight refusing to stand still long enough to be killed. He did the math. Kyle watched him do it. The torso ring rotated, the one good barrel coming around, and behind the man's eyes a calculation finished that had nothing to do with the barrel — the calculation of a professional who'd been paid for the attempt and was not, it turned out, being paid enough to die in a glass box over a woman he could find again on a slower night.

War Dog threw himself out the window.

Not the glass wall, which was already gone — the outer window, the one that looked off the back of the building over the alley three stories down, and he went through it backward and shoulder-first with the casual violence of a man who had done it before and budgeted for the landing. There was a sound like a side of meat hitting a loading ramp, because that was, more or less, what it was. Kyle made the window in three steps and looked down in time to see the wide low shape drag itself off the alley concrete — and leave something behind. The jammed belt-feed arm hung wrong from the fall, dead weight on torn mounts, and War Dog stopped in the shadow of the dumpsters and did the professional thing: braced it against the steel lip and tore it the rest of the way off. He left it lying in the alley like a transmission dropped out of a truck, and went into the dark one arm lighter than he'd arrived, moving wrong and moving anyway.

He'd live. Kyle had known the window was an exit and not a kill, and had taken the shoulder seam anyway. Cacophony had two rounds left, and an alley was a bad place to spend them on a thing that was already leaving.

It was not a win. War Dog would file a report. *Attempt made. Target acquired. Fee invoiced.* Somewhere a client would read it and mark it closed, and Null had spent her last working night making a tired man feel thirty again. Kyle stood in the window with the rain coming in, glass from a supervisor's box in his hair and a week of picking it out of his hands ahead of him, and made his own entry, the kind that needs no writing down: a wide low shape, down to two arms now, a six-figure rebuild before he works again. He would know him again from a rooftop in the rain at four hundred meters.

## Who Vouches for You

[Beat 7]
Stash had pulled the van back from the bay and was standing in the gap between the rear bumper and the dock's concrete wall. He'd laid his coat over her. The shape under it was small and final, and the coat's hem was dark with something that would not wash out. His hands were still at the hem where he'd smoothed it down — the hands of a man who had run out of work to do and hadn't moved them yet.

Kyle stopped. He put Silence away.

"She was already —" Stash started.

"I know," Kyle said.

Ledger stood on the far side of the van. He was looking at nothing in particular, which was the look Ledger had when he was paying the most attention. He had been at his position. The vehicle had come from below. He would put it in the report, and the report would not make it make sense, because there was no sense to be made.

"We need to move," Stash said. "Echo's already clear."

"I know," Kyle said again. The dock smelled like old concrete and Lake Michigan cold and the hydraulic fluid still bleeding out of the left arm three stories up. He owed her one beat, unhurried, the way he'd owe anyone who'd stood their ground in the last moment they had.

Then he got in the van.

[Beat 8]
In the back of the van, under Stash's coat, what was left of Null rode quiet. Ledger wrote the after-action from the front passenger seat. Nobody had asked him to.

Three blocks out, Kyle said, "Stop the van."

Stash stopped the van. Kyle got out and walked back through the rain to the corner where the pieces of a glass soda bottle still lay on the wet concrete — a broken arc of it with half the orange label still on, from before any of this, from when the dock was just a dock. He crouched and picked one piece out of the gutter and put it in his coat pocket.

When he got back in, Ledger looked at him, held the look a beat too long, and recorded whatever the Ledger records.

"My cut goes to the funeral," Kyle said. "Whatever it costs to do one right."

"Did anybody know her?" Stash asked.

"Nobody knew her," Kyle said. "That was the job."

"Then where do you even hold it?"

"Counter place on Halsted," Kyle said. "Thursday. Drive."

The streetlights along the block sighed up to full as the van passed under them, a long soft capacitor exhale. He watched the wet streets unspool, one hand in his pocket, the edge of the glass pressed against his palm. The face was already going. The snort wasn't. She'd called him Broken Glass for two jobs and then one morning on comms she'd just said K and kept going, and that had been the whole ceremony. He had the snort, and the glass, and a funeral to price in the morning.

At the ramp, Stash found his eyes in the mirror. "You keeping the name?"

"She made it stick," Kyle said.

[Beat 9]
At the second light north, the channel chirped once.

A fresh posting. Pushed wide to every freelancer band in the van's radius — not targeted, not narrow, the kind of push the relay gave anything it flagged as time-sensitive. Clean scope. Listed retainer. A client field that resolved to a shell, and the shell to nothing.

Stash went quiet in the specific way of a man who has just run a number and doesn't like where it lands. He kept his eyes on the road. He did not check the mirror.

Ledger read the posting. Then he looked at the back of the van. Then he read it again.

Kyle sat with the glass through the lining of his coat pocket and didn't open it. Twelve minutes since the dock. The relay didn't know that — or it didn't care — or it had known before they left, and the posting had been ready, and the second light north was the exact second light north, which was the timing of a thing that had been waiting for the shot to land.

He didn't think about that too directly. He watched the rain on the windshield and didn't think about it.

He would open it in the morning. He would take the job. He had a funeral to price.
