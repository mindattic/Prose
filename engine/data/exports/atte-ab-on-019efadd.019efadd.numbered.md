# ATTE AB ON

_Cross-district truancy investigator Yemina Fola investigates three children who vanished from schools in the Pilsen Veil — each one preceded by a neuretics burnout flag, each disappearance logged as an unexcused absence before anyone connected the cases. The schools were under three different corp contracts; nobody had connected them because nobody else could see all three. When she sweeps the third school's bathroom with a resonance sensor, she finds two traces: an echo above the child's usual seat where the bleed's signal tuned him, and a shadow in the bathroom stall where he crossed. In her car afterward, someone else's hands complete a search query in her neuretics: 47 missing children, GLMZ-wide, 22 months. The 48th entry is her own name. The onset date is six weeks ago._

## Attendance

[Beat 1]
The seam between Tessera-administered Cicada Lawn and the open Gray Zone ran down the middle of 63rd Street like a fold in cheap paper, and Yemina Fola crossed it on foot at 07:14 because the contractor-issue car had no clearance for the Tessera side and she had stopped, years ago, expecting it to. On the bonded side the pavement was swept and the relay towers hummed their cicada hum, that 63rd Street frequency every resident pretended not to hear because pretending was free; on the open side the sweepers didn't come, and so the curb held a week of takeout wrappers and the rinds of someone's GeneStable doses popped from their foil. She kept her badge folded in her jacket. Showing it changed how people stood, and she needed them standing the way they stood when no one was watching.

*The car can't cross. Note who decided that, and when, and whether it was a decision or just a default no one bothered to override.*

The school was a converted relay substation, three floors of subsidized neural-curriculum carrels behind a door that asked for credentials Yemina had and the children mostly didn't. She thumbed it open. Inside smelled of warm plastic and the synthetic citrus they used to mask the warm-plastic smell, and the attendance terminal at the front desk was already lit with the morning's reconciliation: enrolled, ninety-one; present, eighty-three; flagged unexcused, eight. Three of the eight names carried a small secondary marker she had seen on her own queue before driving out — a neuretics burnout advisory, amber, the kind the system logged as a wellness note and filed where wellness notes went, which was nowhere a parent could reach.

*Eight absent. Three flagged twice. The system called the second flag a coincidence and moved on, because the form has a field for absence and no field for the thing that connects them.*

The clerk behind the desk was maybe nineteen, neuretics-bright, and answered before Yemina finished the question — said the three were just truant, that families on this side moved, that kids aged out and stopped logging in and the towers kept counting empty carrels because no one had told them to stop. It was a clean answer, delivered fast, and it would have closed a lesser case. Yemina wrote down the three names and the times of their last successful login and the fact that all three last logins had landed inside the same forty-minute window on the same Tuesday, and she thanked the clerk, who was already looking past her at the next person in line, and she did not say that a coincidence does not keep a schedule.

[Beat 2]
She asked to see the carrels. The clerk hesitated — not a refusal, just the small administrative reflex of someone calculating whether a request had a form behind it — and Yemina let the silence do the asking, which it did, because silence on the open side of the seam was not free the way the cicada hum was free, and the clerk wanted it to stop. Third floor, the clerk said. End of the row. Maintenance had it tagged for decommission anyway.

The decommission tag was the kind of detail that closed cases for people who collected details to close cases. Yemina collected them to open things back up.

The carrels on three were a row of nine, eight of them dark, and the three she wanted sat together at the far end against the wall where the relay substation's old conduit still ran behind the drywall, close enough that she could feel it before she reached it — not a sound exactly, more a pressure that arrived in the back molars first and named itself second, the hum of current that had been moving through that wall since before the building taught children, indifferent to whether anyone sat in front of it. She stood where the three of them had sat. The chairs were the cheap molded kind, and two of the three had worn pale at the armrest the way a chair wears when the same small hands grip the same place every morning for a year.

*Same window. Same Tuesday. Same wall. Three kids who gripped the armrest hard enough to bleach the plastic, and the form has a field for the chair and a field for the absence and nothing — nothing — for the wall they were sitting against when they stopped logging in.*

She put her hand flat on the drywall over the conduit and held it there a moment longer than a person checking for vibration would, then took it away and wrote down the carrel numbers and the decommission tag's date, which was the Wednesday after the Tuesday, and she understood that she had just been handed a reason and a door at the same time, and that the door was already scheduled to be sealed.

## Chapter 1

[Beat 3]
She found the maintenance work order in the school's own queue, because the clerk had said decommission and decommission meant a ticket, and a ticket meant a name. It was a standing order, filed under preventive infrastructure, scope vague enough to cover a wall: relay conduit abatement, third floor, sectional. The requesting entity was a Tessera facilities subcontractor with a routing code she half-recognized, the kind that handled the bonded side's substations, the cicada towers, the current that hummed under 63rd Street whether or not anyone admitted hearing it. The work order predated the three last logins by nine days. Filed first. The children stopped after.

*A wall gets a ticket before the kids who sat against it go quiet. That's sequence, not coincidence — the abatement was scheduled, then the absences happened, then maintenance tagged the carrels to match. Note the order. The order is the whole thing.*

She photographed the work order with her own retinal capture rather than pulling it to her queue, because pulling it left a record on the Tessera side that she had touched it, and a record on the Tessera side was a record that someone on the Tessera side could read, and she wanted to be the only one holding both halves a little longer. Then she sat in the worn chair, the bleached armrest under her palm, and felt the molar-pressure of the conduit come up through the cheap molding into the bones of her hand the way it must have come up through three smaller hands every morning for a year before they stopped.

The chair held her weight without comment. Behind the drywall the current moved the way it had always moved, before the building taught anyone, indifferent to the schedule and the ticket and the eight empty carrels and the one investigator sitting where the children had sat. Yemina wrote down the subcontractor's routing code. Then she wrote, beneath it, *who do they bill,* and did not yet have a field to put the answer in.

[Beat 4]
She stayed in the chair past the point of usefulness, which she logged the way she logged everything she did that a supervisor would query — *forty seconds, no observable purpose, justify on request* — and the justification was that the hum did not change. She had expected it to. She had expected that sitting where they sat, with the conduit pressing into the bones of her hand, she would feel the thing the form had no field for. Instead she felt current. Old, indifferent, the same frequency the towers on 63rd pushed into the air for free, the same pressure she had driven through on the open side of the seam an hour ago without noticing, because nobody on this side noticed it anymore, because the body stops filing what it cannot escape.

*That's the part the work order gets right and I keep getting wrong. The wall isn't doing anything to them. The wall is just a wall with current in it. Somebody decided the current mattered, and scheduled a wall to be abated, and the deciding is upstream of everything I can touch from a molded chair.*

She pulled her hand back and the molar-pressure went with it, clean as a switch, and she sat there flexing the fingers that had just held three children's worth of mornings, the same fingers that had folded a badge into a jacket so people would keep standing the way they stood when no one watched. Hands that took photographs instead of pulling records. Hands that wrote *who do they bill* in a notebook with no field for the answer. It occurred to her — once, fast, and then put away — that she had a daughter's age in her head for each of these three before she had a single fact about where they'd gone, and that the arithmetic was the one part of this she had never been trained to file.

Then she stood, because the carrel was tagged for the Wednesday and this was already a Thursday, and a sealed wall did not answer questions, and the subcontractor's routing code sat in her notebook above a line she could not yet complete. The decommission had a date. She did not. She wrote that down too — *abatement scheduled, not yet executed, confirm whether my being here moves it up* — and understood, putting the stylus away, that she had just made herself a variable in a schedule she did not control, and that the system would file her visit as a wellness check, amber, somewhere a parent could not reach.

[Beat 5]
The custodian was on three when she came back down the row, a man older than the building wanted its custodians to be, pushing a wet-vac that hadn't drawn power in long enough that he was steering it more than running it. He had the look of someone who had been told about her before she arrived — the clerk's call ahead, the amber flag finding its way down the floors faster than she had — and he stopped the wet-vac square in the middle of the row, not to block her, just to be a thing in the way that had a reason to be there.

"You're the one come about the carrels," he said. Not a question, so she didn't answer it. He looked past her at the three chairs against the wall, at the bleached armrests, at the conduit nobody had ever asked him about in eleven years of running a mop past it. "They're gonna take that wall out next week. I asked the ticket what was wrong with the wall." He set both hands on the wet-vac's handle, the way a man does when he's decided to say the thing he drove down here to say. "Wall's fine. Wall's been fine since I started. You want to know what's wrong with a wall, you don't ask the wall and you don't ask the ticket. You ask who's tired of looking at it."

*Who's tired of looking at it. Not what's broken — who's inconvenienced. He just handed me the question and doesn't know it's the question, the same way the kids didn't know the armrest was evidence, the same way I keep asking the wall what it did to them.*

She wrote it down, because she wrote everything down, and the writing bought her the half-second she needed to not say the thing rising under it — that her daughter was nine, that nine was the age she had assigned the smallest of the three before she had a name for any of them, that *who's tired of looking at it* was a sentence with no field on any form she had ever filed and would be the first line of every report she wrote after this one whether the supervisors could read it or not. "How long," she asked instead, "between the ticket coming down and you noticing the chairs were empty?" The custodian thought about it, and the wet-vac hummed its small dead hum against the floor, and behind the drywall the current went on moving the way it had always moved, and he said a number that was smaller than nine days and larger than zero, and it did not fit the order she had written down, and she understood that she had come for a door and been handed a different wall.

[Beat 6]
The number sat between them, smaller than nine days and larger than zero, and it did not fit. She had built the order on the work order — abatement scheduled, then absences, then the tag finding the carrels to match — and a custodian noticing three empty chairs *before* the ticket came down meant either his memory had drifted in eleven years of running a mop past a wall nobody asked him about, or her order was wrong, or the order was right and the ticket was the second thing the wall had been told to do, not the first. She wrote the number down with the date he gave it, which he gave the way old men give dates, by what was happening to him at the time rather than what the calendar said, and the anchoring event he used to place it was his sister's surgery, and she would have to convert that to a date on the Tessera side later, and converting it would leave a record.

*Three orderings now and only one notebook. Confirm the sister's surgery without pulling a medical record I have no standing to pull. The seam isn't between true and false, it's between what he remembers and what they filed, and I am the only person standing in the gap holding both, and the gap is closing on a Wednesday I don't control.*

"You said you asked the ticket what was wrong with the wall," she said, keeping her eyes on the wet-vac's dead handle and not on the three chairs, because the three chairs were where the arithmetic lived and the arithmetic was not hers to do on the clock. "Did the ticket answer?"

He almost laughed, the sound a man makes when the question is so much smaller than the thing it's pointing at. "Tickets don't answer. Ticket gave me a category. 'Environmental abatement, scheduled, no action required floor staff.'" He pushed the wet-vac an inch and let it settle, the small dead hum against the composite the only thing in the row that agreed to keep moving. "No action required. That's the part I drove down to tell you. Eleven years on this floor and the one time something's actually wrong, the ticket's the thing telling me there's nothing to do." He looked at her then, pale tiredness all the way down. "You came about the carrels. The carrels are the action they don't require. You write that down too?"

[Beat 7]
She wrote it down. Not because she needed the words — she would carry *the carrels are the action they don't require* out of this building intact whether or not the stylus ever touched the screen — but because the custodian was watching her hand, and a man who drove down three floors to say a true thing needs to see it land somewhere that isn't his own memory, somewhere that leaves a record he can point to later when the wall is gone and the ticket has closed itself and no one else remembers there were three chairs. So she wrote it the way he'd want it written, plainly, with his number and his sister's surgery noted beside it as the anchor, and she let him see the stylus move.

*And this is the part I'm good at. Not the arithmetic — the receipt. I can't tell him a nine-year-old is gone, I can't tell him my notebook has no field for who's tired of looking at it, but I can make a man feel filed when filing him is the only honest thing left to do.*

"I'll write it the way you said it," she told him, and she did not say *thank you*, because thank you was a thing you said when someone gave you a door, and he had given her the opposite — a confirmation that the wall she'd come to read had been read by someone with less standing than her and read more truly, eleven years before the ticket arrived to tell him there was nothing to read. The wet-vac hummed its dead hum. Behind the drywall the current went on doing whatever the current did, indifferent to the abatement date, indifferent to the Wednesday, indifferent to her, and she felt the cold edge of the conduit's field through the composite the way you feel a draft before you find the gap it comes through.

Then she capped the stylus and looked at the three chairs one last time, because she would not get to look at them again — the wall came out next week, no action required floor staff, and whatever the armrests still held would go into a skip and out to the lake-fill with the rest of what the city was tired of looking at. She had come for a door. She had a custodian's date that didn't fit her order, a routing code with no name to bill, three ages she'd assigned to children before she had their names, and a decommission she'd just made herself a variable in. The door was still closed. But now she knew it had been closed *on purpose*, by someone, for a reason a wall could not give her — and that was worse than a locked door, because a locked door at least admits there's a room behind it.

[Beat 8]
Her neuretics flagged the routing code again, the way they had been flagging it since the work order first opened in her queue — not an alert, just a soft insistence in the corner of her attention, the cognitive equivalent of a tongue finding a chipped tooth. She let it surface this time. The abatement ticket carried a billing path: every line item in a Tessera-adjacent district routed to a cost center, because nothing in this city moved without someone's account number absorbing it. She pulled the path. It terminated in a code she didn't recognize, four characters where she expected a department prefix, and when she asked the system whose center it was, the system told her the field was populated and declined to populate it for her.

*Populated and unreadable. Not blank — blank I could escalate. Someone filled this in and then set the visibility above my clearance, which means it isn't a clerical hole, it's a wall someone built and then painted to look like a floor.*

She stood very still in the empty row, the wet-vac's dead hum the only sound, and ran the only move she had left — she forwarded the work order to her own cross-district inbox with a routine annotation request, *clarify cost-center ownership, abatement Pilsen Veil, three carrels flagged*, the kind of nothing-query a truancy investigator files forty times a week. It would go somewhere. It would touch a desk she couldn't see, attached now to a code that didn't want to be seen, with three children's empty chairs sitting in the body of it whether anyone upstream read that far or not. The send confirmation came back in under a second. *Acknowledged. Routed.* No name. No department. Just the small green certainty of a system telling her the thing was now in motion and refusing to say toward what.

The custodian watched her face do whatever it did and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy. She had come to read one wall and found a second one behind it, and the second one she had just knocked on by name — and the knock had been answered the way every closed door in this city answered, by going quiet in a way that meant it had heard. She capped her notebook. Behind the drywall the current kept moving, indifferent, and somewhere upstream of all of it a code she couldn't read now held her name beside three she didn't have yet, and she understood that whatever came next would come *to* her, on a Wednesday she still didn't control, and that she had just made certain of it.

[Beat 9]
The drive back to the cross-district annex was eleven minutes on the surface streets and she took the surface streets, because the maglev would have put her under the canal where her neuretics lost the open band and she wanted the open band tonight, wanted the routing code sitting in her queue where she could feel it the way the custodian felt the wall — present, wrong, on someone else's schedule. The contractor-issue car drove itself in the slow lane the way contractor-issue cars did, deferring to every Tessera commuter that wanted the gap, and she let it, because the part of her that would have fought for the gap was busy doing the arithmetic she hadn't let herself do on the clock. Three carrels. Three burnout flags. One abatement date that arrived eleven years late and named no action. She had carried worse home. She told herself she had carried worse home.

*That's the lie I tell to get the key in the door. I've carried bigger. I've never carried one that knew my name back.*

The honest move was the one Aagje would tell her to make if she called him, and she knew exactly what it was because she had watched him make it forty times across a career: file the annotation, log the anomaly, let the unreadable code be someone else's wall, and go home, because a truancy investigator who chases a populated-and-restricted cost center into a Tessera-adjacent district is a truancy investigator who gets reassigned to vehicle audits in a district with no children in it at all. The work would survive her dropping it. The children — if they were children, if three ages she'd assigned to empty chairs were anything more than a pattern her tired mind had drawn to feel less alone in the row — the children would not get less gone because she went home. That was the arithmetic. That was the whole of it, clean as a closed ticket.

She did not go home. She had the car turn off at the annex instead, into the underlit lot where the night-shift autos sat charging in their indifferent row, and she sat in the dark a moment with her hands flat on her knees, palms down, the way she sat when she was about to do a thing she could not later claim she had drifted into. She would not call Aagje. She would pull the three burnout flags herself, tonight, off the clock, on her own access, and put names to the three ages before the system decided the field was populated and declined to populate it for her — because a locked door admitted there was a room, and she had decided, somewhere between the wall and the lot, that she would rather be the kind of person who knocked than the kind who wrote *no action required* and let the wet-vac hum it true.

*I file people for a living. Tonight I'm going to find out if I can do the opposite.*

[Beat 10]
She did not pull the flags from the car. The lot's open band was open enough for the routing code but not for what she meant to do, and she knew the difference the way she knew the difference between an annotation and a query — one of them the system forgave, one of them it remembered. So she went up. The annex at this hour was the building she liked least and trusted most: motion-lit, half-charged, the cross-district floor a grid of dead terminals and one custodial synthetic working the far carpet in long indifferent passes, registering her as occupancy and nothing more. She took her own desk. She did not take the visitor terminal by the window where the logging was softer, because the visitor terminal was the move of someone who wanted deniability, and she had decided in the lot that she was done buying deniability on credit she couldn't cover.

*Aagje would say the desk is the mistake. Aagje would say if you're going to knock, knock from a phone you can throw in the canal.*

She brought up the burnout registry on her own clearance, which covered it — burnout flags were attendance-adjacent, well inside a truancy investigator's reasonable interest, and that was the thin honest floor she planned to stand on if anyone ever asked why a woman who chased unexcused absences had been reading neuretics-overload reports at 2340 on a Wednesday she didn't control. Three flags. She had the carrel assignments; the registry keyed to seat, not name, the same blind routing that had let her age three empty chairs before she'd had anything to age. She put the cursor on the first one. Her hand was steady on the input the way it was steady on a stylus, because hands did the work whether or not the rest of her had caught up, and she let it be steady, and she pulled the first flag.

A name came back. Just a name — no flourish, no warning, the system handing it over as plainly as it handed over a parking citation, because to the system a child's burnt-out neuretics and a vehicle audit were the same shape of record and it had no category for the difference. She read it once. She read it again, slower, the way you reread a thing not because it's unclear but because clarity is the part you weren't braced for, and the custodial synthetic made its long pass behind her and logged her still sitting there, occupancy and nothing more, a woman at a desk with a name on her screen that the wall upstream had filled in and painted over and decided she would never need.

*One. There are two more chairs. I asked for this, and it is going to ask me back.*

[Beat 11]
She pulled the second flag before the first name had finished arranging itself into a person, because waiting was the door she didn't trust herself not to close. The system gave it over with the same flatness — a string of characters keyed to a seat, then a name where the seat had been, then nothing, no record of where the name had gone after the flag, because going was not attendance and attendance was the only verb the field knew how to conjugate. She set the two names side by side in her own notes, off the registry, in the local file the synthetic's long indifferent passes couldn't read, and she looked at them next to the ages she'd assigned to empty chairs, and the ages fit. They fit the way a key fits — not because you forced it, but because someone had cut it for the lock years ago and walked away.

*I aged those chairs to feel less alone in the row. Now there's a name on the cold side of two of them and the math I made up is the math that's true.*

She put the cursor on the third and did not pull it. Her hand stayed steady on the input the way it had stayed steady on the first two, but the rest of her had finally caught up to the hand, and the catching-up was a cold thing with a clean vertical edge to it, the kind of cold that arrives before the mind has a word for why. Two names she could carry home and call a coincidence she was too tired to dismiss. Three would be a pattern with a heartbeat, and a pattern with a heartbeat was not a thing a truancy investigator filed and went home from — it was a thing that, once read, would route to her on a Wednesday she still didn't control, and she had promised herself in the lot that she was done buying the deniability that would have let her stop here.

She pulled it. The name came back as plainly as the first two, the system handing over the third burnt-out child the way it handed over a citation, and the custodial synthetic made its pass behind her and logged her still sitting there at 2351, occupancy and nothing more — a woman with three names on a screen the wall upstream had filled in, painted over, and decided she would never need. Then the terminal did a small thing it was not supposed to do. A query-flag bloomed in the corner of her own clearance, soft amber, the registry noting that an attendance-grade access had read three burnout records keyed to a populated-and-restricted cost center, and noting it not as a violation but as occupancy of its own — a record being made, somewhere upstream, of the woman who had finally knocked.

*There. It heard me. Now we both have a name we didn't have an hour ago.*

[Beat 12]
She read the amber flag the way she read everything: for what it admitted. It did not say *unauthorized*. It said *acknowledged* — a query-grade notice, the registry's politest verb, the one it used when it wanted a thing on record without committing to caring about it. Which meant the access was clean enough to survive a glance and interesting enough to be glanced at, and those were two different upstreams, and she did not get to choose which one came down the line first. She copied the three names into the local file, then copied the file again, because a record that lived in one place was a record someone could decide she never needed.

*Aagje would say the second copy is the tell. Aagje would say the woman who copies twice is the woman who already knows she's going to need it in a room with a door she can't open.*

She did not call him. She wrote the timestamp instead — 2351, her own hand, her own clearance, three names and three ages that fit a lock cut years before she'd found it — and under it she wrote the thing she would not be able to unwrite: *not truancy.* Two words, in a field that only knew how to conjugate going, and they sat there being the first honest entry the case had ever held, and being also the entry that would route to her now whether or not she clocked in to receive it. The custodial synthetic finished the far carpet and started back the way it had come, logging her, 2352, occupancy and nothing more.

*I wanted the opposite of filing people. This is the opposite. It looks exactly the same and it costs the other thing.*

She closed the registry but not the local file, and she sat in the motion-lit dark with the three names arranged in a row like chairs, and for one beat she let herself be the person these names had been — six years old and never again allowed silence, a hum installed where the quiet used to live, a feed that did not stop until the chair went cold. Then she armored back up, because the synthetic was making its turn and the lot was four floors down and somewhere upstream a soft amber notice was already deciding what she was. She powered the terminal to standby. She did not power off the part of herself that had read three names and could no longer be a woman who hadn't.

[Beat 13]
The hallway outside Records ran the length of the floor with a single live strip overhead, the rest of the panels dimmed to standby the way the building dimmed everything it had decided was unobserved. She walked it at the pace she always walked it, neither slow nor fast, because a synthetic logged gait as well as occupancy and a woman who hurried out of a room was a woman who had found something in it. Her badge cleared the inner door. It cleared the second. At the third — the one between the records floor and the elevator bank, the one she had badged through a thousand times without reading it as a door — the panel stayed red.

*Cleared three minutes ago. Cleared every Wednesday for nine years.*

She badged it again, slower, the way she'd reread the first name, and the panel held its small red verdict without explanation, because a door did not owe you a reason any more than a registry owed a child a category. There was no error. Errors were a kind of mercy — they told you a thing was broken and could be fixed. This was the other thing, the clean thing: a credential that had been valid at 2351 and was something less at 2354, downgraded between the timestamp she'd written and the standing-up she'd done to leave, by an upstream that had read her amber flag and decided, with the registry's politest verb, exactly which kind of woman she was.

*Acknowledged. It said acknowledged. I read it for what it admitted and I missed what it was promising.*

She stood at the door with the local file warm in her clearance and the three names arranged in it like chairs, and she did not badge a third time, because a third badge was a record too and she had learned tonight what the third of a thing cost. The strip overhead held its single live light. Somewhere four floors down was a lot and a gray contractor-issue car, and between her and it was a credential the building had quietly decided she would no longer need, and she thought of Aagje saying the second copy was the tell, and understood that the door was the building's second copy — the wall upstream making its own redundant record, in steel, of the woman who had finally knocked. She took out her terminal, not to call him, and wrote the timestamp under the other timestamp: *2354 — door, west bank, red.* Then she turned, unhurried, gait clean, and went looking for the stairwell, because the stairs were a door the building had forgotten to decide about, and a woman with three names did not get to wait for the elevator anymore.

[Beat 14]
The stairwell door read her badge without complaint — a service door, fire-rated, the kind of access the building granted on a different logic than the elevator bank, occupancy law rather than clearance law, and she went through it grateful for the gap between the two and not trusting the gap to stay open. The well dropped four flights in poured concrete, unlit except for the green run of exit markers, and the air in it had the dead, processed cool of a space the atmospheric system serviced because code said it must, not because anyone was meant to breathe here long. Her steps came back to her off the walls a half-second late. She counted landings instead of names, because the names were already counted and the landings were a thing she could still finish.

Three flights down, the man was sitting on the half-landing with his back to the wall, and he was not surprised to see her.

He was old in the way that read across a dark stairwell — the white beard kept to a precise line, the dark hands folded on one knee, the pale blue eyes catching the exit-green and holding it. Aagje did not have her cleared, which meant he had come up the way she was coming down, which meant he had badged a service door of his own somewhere and walked, on foot, the long redundant way that left no elevator log. He did not stand. He looked at the terminal still warm in her hand and then at her face, and he said it the way he said everything, gently, as a thing already decided.

"You copied it twice."

*Of course he knows. He taught me the second copy. He's the reason there are two.*

"Three names," she said. "Keyed to a cost center that's populated and restricted. I read them at 2351 and the west door went red at 2354."

"Then you should not have come down the stairs to find me," Aagje said, and unfolded himself off the landing one joint at a time, tall again by the time he reached his feet. "You should have stayed in the lot, where the amber flag could decide you were a tired woman who pulled one record too many and went home. A door that knows you took the stairs is a door that knows you did not want to be seen taking them." He looked past her, up the green-lit well, at the four flights that logged nothing. "I came the same way. So now there are two of us the building cannot account for, and that is one more than a quiet thing can hold."

## Chapter 2

[Beat 15]
"I didn't come down to find you," she said. "I came down because the elevator stopped being mine. You were just on the way."

He took that the way he took the timestamp — without correction, which was its own correction. He came down two steps to her landing, slowly, and the exit-green slid off the precise line of his beard and pooled in the folds under his eyes, and he looked, for a beat, less like the man who had taught her the second copy and more like a thing the building had also decided about, a long time ago, and filed.
