[Beat 1]
# The Dry Corridor

*Seto maps the silence in the gray zones — and something maps him back.*

The handoff was at 14:00, a logistics firm in the Seam between Zone 3 and Zone 4 with no sign outside and no name he'd ever been given. Just an address and a frequency, the way most of his clients preferred it. Seto had run data for them on and off for three years, long enough that the contact protocols had simplified from six verification steps to two. Trust calculus. The most honest math he knew.

He came in off the underpass on the bicycle, coasting the last hundred meters to let his neuretics finish the ambient cycle. Zone 3's Arcturus Civil — environmental processing, air-quality management, the clean-infrastructure CorpoNation most Zone 3 residents had never left — ended at the street. A hard administrative edge, the air managed to the curb and not one meter further. Zone 4's Orison-adjacent overlay started twenty meters east. In the gap between them the air was unfiltered, both corps' beacon frequencies overlapping in a wash his tissue read as fullness, a resonance he'd learned the way a navigator learns tide. Seam air smelled of what the city was actually doing: exhaust off the freight roads, someone cooking at a canteen window three floors up, the wet-metal smell of recycled condensate venting from the Arcturus boundary exchange. Carrier-grade tissue didn't chatter the way consumer implants did. It listened. The background peeled apart as he rolled — freight authentication beacons, the low hum of an Infrastructure Authority monitoring node, a commercial relay pushing discount pricing from something four blocks east. Nothing from the client building. Which was correct. Which was what you wanted.

The bicycle was a 2189 Ogaki track conversion, steel-lugged, the kind of frame that took actual knowledge to maintain. He preferred to know what was happening inside the things that were his.

He locked it to a conduit housing outside the building and took the freight elevator up. The contact was a woman in her fifties with a data coordinator's posture, slightly forward, slightly compressed, someone who spent her days making decisions about latency. She took the transfer chip without ceremony, ran the verification, handed back the receipt.

"Clean?"

"Sealed," he said.

That was the whole conversation. He was back on the bicycle in four minutes.

---

He ate at a counter in Zone 4 before pulling the map — a bowl of githeri, which he'd been eating since childhood because his mother had made it, because it was cheap and filling, and because some habits were structural rather than nostalgic. The counter was loud enough. Nobody was paying attention to a man looking at a screen.

The map was encrypted at three layers, which was more than most people bothered with for a personal archive and less than he would have used if he'd known, eight months ago, what it was going to contain. He'd started it as professional notation. He'd kept route-quality data for years — more intuition than record at first, the kind of thing you develop after a decade of gray-zone courier work, when you start reading traffic the way you read wind. A corridor going quiet meant competition, pricing shifts, occasionally enforcement pressure from one CorpoNation or another staking out territory. Dry routes had explanations. You found them, adjusted, moved on.

The map had three tiers: black for active, gray for degraded, white for gone.

What he'd been looking at for eight months was the white.

A dry market went quiet the way a drought went dry — evenly, gradually, in rough proportion to distance from the source. What he'd been tracking didn't behave that way. The white routes had a geometry. They clustered. They had a center of gravity.

He zoomed to the sector he'd mapped most recently, an industrial crescent between Zones 5 and 7: processing facilities, climate-management infrastructure, a medical-maintenance sector he'd run data through under an Orison-adjacent contract eighteen months back. Orison was the CorpoNation that handled neuretic maintenance for most of the mid-zones — walk into any of their service corridors and the lights were the color of a waiting room that had stopped waiting. Low-margin logistics, the kind of work you took when the premium jobs were quiet. He'd been in and out of those corridors for six weeks.

There was one stretch of the crescent he routed around, not from data discipline but because his sister lived three blocks off it, in a building he'd helped her move into four years ago. He didn't check on her the way some people checked on family. It was enough to know the corridor was active, the relay tower at its head still green on his map, the neighborhood still metabolically present. He hadn't looked at that stretch this week because it was still black. He looked at the white.

The white routes traced the crescent almost perfectly. Not all of it — there were active corridors running through, which was why the pattern wasn't immediately obvious. But when he overlaid eight months of notation, filtered by sector type rather than by zone, the shape clarified. A radius. A direction.

He couldn't name what it was the shape of yet.

A cartographer's discipline: you didn't move to conclusions before the evidence earned them. You drew the coastline as it was, not as it looked like it might be.

He finished the githeri, closed the map, paid in cash, and went back to the bicycle.

---

There were two people he'd been thinking about the way he thought about the white routes — not with alarm but with a specific quality of attention.

Bayo had been a logistics coordinator in Zone 3 for as long as Seto had known him, which was six years. He was reliable the particular way people who'd grown up without reliable systems became reliable: deliberately, as a practice, something he renewed rather than assumed. They'd worked adjacent to each other a dozen times without ever working together, which was the cleanest kind of professional relationship. No shared clients, no debt either direction.

Bayo had stopped taking his calls in October. Started again in December.

He hadn't asked about the gap directly. That wasn't how he operated, and it wasn't how Bayo operated either. But he'd paid attention to the texture of the conversations since December, the way you pay attention to something that's been repaired, looking for the seam. Bayo was fine. Bayo was himself, more or less. But October through November was simply absent from their working history in a way Bayo seemed to accept without examination, and Seto knew what Bayo-under-stress looked like. He'd seen it when Bayo's daughter was hospitalized, when a Zone 3 corridor purge took out three of his primary clients at once. Bayo under stress was present, watchful, wound tight in a specific way. Whatever October through November had been, it wasn't that.

Shen was different, and more troubling.

He'd carried data for her twice — medical research notation, the kind of thing a freelance med-tech produced working the gray zone between hospital contracts. Technically specialized, commercially modest. She'd been doing Orison-adjacent work since early the previous year — the neuretic maintenance program, he'd gathered, which meant her days were spent in those same waiting-room-colored corridors doing service calls on people who'd had the batch procedure done at an Orison facility. The kind of employment shift couriers noticed the way shift workers noticed changed schedules: not judgment, just updated routing.

He'd spoken to her four times since. She was the same in every important way — knowledgeable, careful, dry in her humor. But her knowledge of her own work history had developed a selective quality. She moved through it the way someone moves through a familiar room from which several objects have been quietly removed. She didn't reference the contracts she'd had before the Orison work. Not avoided. Absent. He'd tested it once, carefully — mentioned a specific data exchange, a specific piece of research notation he'd carried for her. Nothing in her response indicated recognition.

She didn't know it. That was the part that stayed with him.

It was possible she'd simply moved on, reorganized what she kept at the surface. People did that. Trauma, transition, a deliberate choice to stop carrying certain things. Possible.

The geometry of the white routes made it less possible.

He was the only person outside the system who held both halves of the shape. The silence geography lived in eleven years of route data that existed nowhere except his head — no log, no archive, no record anyone with resources could pull. The contact network was the Seams, and the Seams didn't file reports. If he stopped mapping, the shape went dark. Bayo would not find his two missing months. Shen would not notice the room that was no longer there. There was no one he could hand the map to who would know how to read it.

---

He'd been running cross-references for two months, slowly, careful not to generate a pattern of inquiry that looked like inquiry. Public GLMZ health data was accessible through Infrastructure Authority portals — aggregate, anonymized, the kind of information that got published because the disclosure requirements were old enough that nobody had updated them to close the gaps. He pulled what he could. Zone infrastructure utilization. Maintenance contract records. The particular texture of high-traffic medical corridors showing reduced billing under Orison-adjacent facilities management.

He cross-referenced those against his map, against what he knew of the affected sector's commercial profile eighteen months back.

The radius tightened.

He didn't have a name for the shape yet. He had a shape.

---

The Zone 7 relay point was in a building that smelled of concrete and recycled air, the smell of functional Zone 7 infrastructure — not neglected, just unglamorous. Transit district. Relay-heavy. The kind of place where data moved through rather than stopped. He preferred it to the premium zones, where the architecture was always trying to tell you something.

The handoff was a standard drop, physical chip, no verbal exchange. The relay operator was a young man who'd been at the post maybe eight months, judging by the way he still verified twice when once was sufficient. Seto said nothing about it. You didn't advise relay operators on their security protocols unless you wanted to be remembered, and he had no interest in being remembered.

Back on the bicycle.

He took the long way home along the freight grid — not the optimal route, which threaded through Zone 6 at lower elevation, but the longer cut that stayed higher, where the city's noise was different. More industrial. The sound of things moving through systems. He thought best in motion. Proximity to the route mattered. You read the city with your body, not just your neuretics.

The zone transitions were legible at speed in a way they weren't on foot — the light quality changing, the beacon frequencies cycling through their registration bands, the background data environment shifting from one administrative fingerprint to another. He knew this stretch the way he knew the bottom bracket on his bicycle. The Seam between Zones 6 and 7 ran past a freight-transfer bay where one relay tower had been broadcasting at 118.4 MHz for as long as he'd been riding this route. He'd logged it a hundred times without noting it, the way you stop noting a thing that's always there.

Tonight it was broadcasting at 118.6.

Two-tenths of a megahertz. Within tolerance. Not the tower's tolerance — he knew its tolerance from eleven years of passing it — his. He noted it the way he noted anything that was not the same as it had been, without drawing conclusions, without altering his pace.

He was two kilometers from his building when his neuretics cycled through the standard ambient check.

Navigation overlays. Traffic density estimates. Relay-band signal quality. The background noise of ten million people running consumer hardware that broadcast their presence in every direction, all the time, the kind of thing you stopped noticing the way you stopped noticing the pressure of your own clothing.

And then something else.

He almost missed it. It didn't announce itself. It surfaced not as a transmission but as a weight — his hands on the grips registering a torque he had not applied, the precise micro-adjustment of someone seating a fastener at a bench, the muscle memory of fingers sorting stacked records in a way he had never done and did not know how to do. His thumb pressed inward against a tool that wasn't there. It lasted two seconds at most. Then it was gone and his hands were his again, holding the bars at the angle he always held them.

Beneath that: numbers organized by a mind that was not his, carrying the quality of someone who sat with data differently, a patience built on a different architecture than his own. Clinical. Precise. Where his own data sense moved laterally — routes and adjacencies, the way corridors connected — this moved vertically: delta columns, figures without geographic referents, depth in a single domain. It arrived in the part of him that was not supposed to be reachable from outside. It occupied space. It had weight. It did not ask permission.

He counted the seconds without deciding to count them. Twelve.

Then it was gone.

He had three options, and he worked through them in the six hundred meters before his building.

He could destroy the map. The shape existed only in his head and in the encrypted archive. Delete the archive, stop riding the crescent, let the silence stay silent. Bayo keeps his missing months. Shen keeps her missing room. Nobody loses anything they're aware of having lost.

He could contact Bayo tonight. Not to explain — he had nothing to explain — but to test: mention October, watch the texture, see if the gap was still the shape it had been in December. One conversation, low risk.

He could do nothing and run the diagnostic first.

He locked the bicycle in the basement and took the stairs.

---

The sweep took forty minutes, which was the proper length for a sweep done correctly. He ran it in three passes, different methodologies, one of them a manual review of his neuretic logs by timestamp — his specialist in Zone 7 had given him read access to his own tissue data, which was not standard, which was why he'd chosen her. No Orison affiliation. No shared reporting structure with anything he'd been inside eighteen months ago.

The sweep came back clean.

Clean. Clean. Clean.

He sat with that for a while. The signal had left no trace his architecture could find, which meant one of two things. It had been clean enough to evade three-pass methodology — unlikely, but a possibility that implied infrastructure. Or it was something his diagnostic framework had no category for. He had spent eleven years building that framework. Four hundred and twelve people had handed him data under the guarantee that his sealed system produced no readable output. A contact that left no trace in three-pass methodology was exactly the contact he had told four hundred and twelve people could not happen. That was not an abstract failure. That was four hundred and twelve specific people who had taken his word.

He filed it under diagnostic anomaly, the category he kept for things he was not yet ready to fully name.

Then he checked the relay bounce pattern in the sweep logs.

The signal had entered through a tower in the industrial corridor between Zones 7 and 8. He recognized the tower's registration signature. He had passed within two blocks of it eleven times in the past eight months, running route checks on the crescent. The corridor it anchored had been one of the first sections to go white on his map.

The tower he'd logged at 118.6 tonight instead of 118.4 was three hundred meters from this one.

He sat with this longer than he'd sat with the sweep results.

He had been mapping a silence. The silence had reached back.

He opened the map. The crescent was there, white in the places he'd charted, black in the places he hadn't yet. His sister's corridor was still black. He looked at it a moment longer than he needed to. Then he closed the map without deleting it, which was the first decision, and pulled up Bayo's contact frequency, which was the start of the second.

He opened the channel.

Outside, the relay beacons pulsed at their thirty-second intervals, indifferent, the tower at Zone 7's edge broadcasting two-tenths above its registered frequency into the city's permanent background noise — a deviation so small that only someone who had passed it a hundred times in eleven years would know it was wrong, and had been wrong, he now understood, for longer than tonight.

The connection request went out. He didn't compose a message. He waited to see whether Bayo would answer, and what version of Bayo it would be — and he stood up and went to make tea while it rang, because standing in the kitchen doing something with his hands was how he thought when the thinking was too large for the desk, and because his sister was three blocks off a corridor that was still black, and he had just decided he could not afford to keep it that way.
