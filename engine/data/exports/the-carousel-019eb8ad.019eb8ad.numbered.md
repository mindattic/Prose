# The Carousel

_The standing contract's first task: hold a broken carousel against a reclamation crew with a three-day-old survey. A fight among the ghost-painted horses, Cacophony's first two rounds of the season, and then the real job — the client asks Kyle to cut the hand-spliced line to its own cradle. The carousel runs four bars on residuals. Kyle tips the client. Filed under: firsts._

[Beat 1]
The morning report had a new format, because Pixel had issued the logbook with the waterproof cover and the crew had responded the way artists respond to better materials.

06:00 — SHIFT START. WEATHER: RAIN (ONGOING) (ALWAYS).
06:20 — NOODLE CART MAN AGAIN. CONFIRMED NOODLE. STAND DOWN.
07:15 — MIRROR ONE OPERATIONAL. MIRROR TWO REQUISITION: DENIED BY MANAGEMENT, "PENDING PERFORMANCE REVIEW." MANAGEMENT IS PIXEL. WE LIVE UNDER A TYRANNY.
07:16 — TYRANNY NOTED IN LOG AS INSTRUCTED BY RAFTERS.
08:00 — BOSS LEFT ON FOOT. DID NOT TAKE BIKE. SUSPICIOUS. FOLLOWED FOR TWO BLOCKS. BOSS BOUGHT DUMPLINGS. STOOD DOWN. (HE SAW US AT THE CORNER. HE BOUGHT FIVE EXTRA. DISTRIBUTED. MORALE: HIGH.)

Kyle read it at Mrs. Chen's counter at 19:30, over the first of two bowls, while the rain worked the awning and the wok rang its flat bright note behind her, and he did not smile where anyone could prove it.

"You're eating slow," Mrs. Chen said, setting down bowl two.

"I have an appointment at twenty-one hundred."

She looked at him — the saya over the shoulder, the work coat, the particular stillness he carried into evenings with appointments — and put a fish ball in the second bowl that he had not ordered and would not be billed for.

"For the appointment," she said, and went back to the wok.

[Beat 2]
Pixel was waiting in the alley with her field kit, the big static wand, a spool of splice line, and an expression of barely civilized excitement.

"Before you say anything," she said, which was how she opened all negotiations she had already won, "the contract says the engineer may attend. That's me. I'm the engineer. It said so in writing. I'm having the line framed."

"It's a job, not a gala."

"It's the first thing your spooky perfect client has asked for in eleven years of paper, and it said BRING THE SWORD, which means hardware, which means the engineer attends with the big wand." She shouldered the kit. "Also I checked the site. The park on Leavitt is on the old Axiom trunk. The carousel's been dead since before either of us could legally work. Whatever it wants down there, samurai, it's not a pony ride."

They took the long way, obviously.

[Beat 3]
The park on Leavitt was a half-block of stubborn green the towers had never gotten around to eating: six trees with character, a swing set doing its best, and at the center, under a shingled canopy gone soft with moss, the broken carousel.

It had been beautiful once, in the way of things built before the century got embarrassed about joy. Twenty-four animals on tarnished brass poles — horses mid-leap, a rooster, one improbable lake sturgeon with a saddle — their paint gone to ghost colors, their glass eyes still patient. The deck boards had silvered. The bulbs in the crown were dead sockets. Rain came through the canopy's bad patches and beaded on a palomino's flank like the horse had been running.

Kyle walked the perimeter once, counting exits and sightlines out of professional courtesy, and found what the contract had brought a sword for without saying so: under the carousel's deck, behind a maintenance skirt panel that somebody had recently — very recently — loosened, a hatch. Axiom-stamped. Padlocked by history, and under the history, humming. Faint. Alive. A junction that the registry said had been dark for thirty years, sitting under a dead carousel, warm as a sleeping cat.

At 21:00 exactly, the terminal chirped.

TASK ONE — SCOPE DETAIL.
A RECLAMATION CREW HAS BEEN CONTRACTED TO EXCAVATE THIS SITE TONIGHT.
THEIR WRIT SAYS COPPER. THEIR CLIENT WANTS WHAT THE COPPER CONNECTS.
HOLD THE JUNCTION UNTIL 21:40.
THE CREW ARE EMPLOYEES. THE MACHINES ARE INSURED.
FEE: STANDARD RATE. — ADDENDUM: THE ENGINEER IS WORTH MORE THAN THE WAND. CLIENT SUGGESTS SHE STAY BEHIND THE STURGEON.

Pixel read it twice.

"It did threat assessment on my behalf," she said, in the voice of a woman deciding whether to be offended or moved. "It assigned me a fish."

"Behind the sturgeon, engineer."

"This is the best client we have ever had," said Pixel, and went and stood behind the sturgeon.

Headlights came off Leavitt at 21:04 — a flatbed with a trencher rig riding it, a panel van, and the kind of work lights that mean somebody intends to bill for a full night.

[Beat 4]
The crew was eight: a foreman with a writ-reader and the shoulders of a man who'd come up through the work; two chrome-heavy muscle whose job description was visible from across the park; a rig operator already bored; and four repo skitter-drones that came off the flatbed like patient six-legged luggage and stood in the rain awaiting instructions.

The foreman saw Kyle at the carousel's edge and did the professional thing: stopped everyone with one hand and came ahead alone, holding the writ up like a small shield.

"Evening," he said. "Cadre Reclamation. We've got paper for this site — legacy assets, scrap title, the works. Notarized." He was already reading Kyle: the saya, the stance, the absence of anywhere better to be. "Site survey said unattended."

"The survey's out of date by one."

"By one what, is the question." The foreman sighed the sigh of a man whose night had just developed a line item. "Look, friend. It's copper and dead glass down there. Whatever they're paying you to stand in the rain over a kiddie ride, it's not worth the —" his eyes did the trip, saya to hands to face, and came back more awake "— hold on. West Town. Noodle counter. You're the one from the file."

"People keep buying that file," Kyle said. "Somebody should tell them."

"Tell them what?"

"It's out of date by one."

The foreman looked at him for a long moment, did some preliminary math, and didn't like the remainder. But a writ is a writ and a night rate is a night rate, and he had eight on paper against one man and whatever was behind the sturgeon, so he made the call that the spreadsheet made for him.

"Rig up," he said, walking back. "Drones forward. And somebody get the freelancer's refusal ON RECORD, in case of arbitration."

"For the record," Kyle said, drawing Silence, "I refuse."

[Beat 5]
The drones came first, because drones are cheap and that is their entire role in modern labor relations.

They were repo skitters — graspers, not guns, built to strip a site fast and testify politely afterward — but forty kilos of articulated grab coming at you in a pack is its own argument, and they ran the carousel's geometry like they'd been given the blueprints. Which, Kyle reflected, sliding between a leaping mare and the rooster, they had. Everyone had blueprints tonight. Everyone always had paper.

What they did not have was a map of where the blade would be.

The first skitter came through the gap between two horses and Silence took its forelimb cluster at the joint ring — machines got the whole edge, that was the deal, the lines were for the living — and the thing cartwheeled off the deck minus its means of opinion. The second tried the low route under the sturgeon's belly and met the edge coming down through its sensor mast; it backed out from under the fish wearing the expression machines wear when their warranty hears about this, which is none, which is somehow worse. The third and fourth came together, learning, flanking — genuinely decent swarm logic — and herded Kyle toward the carousel's open side, where the muscle waited, which was the actual plan and not a bad one.

The terminal, in Pixel's hands behind the sturgeon, chirped.

"ADDENDUM!" she relayed, at volume, with joy. "CLIENT ADVISES THE DRONES SHARE ONE OPERATOR CHANNEL. CLIENT ADVISES THE CHANNEL ANTENNA IS THE FAT ONE ON THE VAN!"

"Engineer," Kyle said, taking the brass pole of a ghost-painted stallion in his free hand and using the swing of his own mass to put both boots through skitter number three, "that sounds like a wand problem."

"ON IT."

The big static wand spoke its one syllable. Across the park, the van's fat antenna developed a fault that would later be described in an insurance filing as "weather." Skitter four stopped mid-lunge, sat down in the rain like a tired dog, and began awaiting instructions that were no longer coming.

[Beat 6]
The muscle came in around the carousel's flanks, one left, one right, converging — chrome arms on the left one, leg rig and a riot baton on the right, both of them moving like men who'd cleared rooms for a living before the private sector found them. Behind, the trencher rig coughed alive on the flatbed, lowered itself to the grass on hydraulic feet, and began walking its blade toward the carousel deck with the operator visibly working out whether his hourly covered any of what he was seeing.

Kyle gave the muscle the carousel.

It was a good arena and it was HIS arena — he'd had twenty whole minutes with it — and a carousel is nothing but a forest of brass poles and dead animals at fight height, which is to say: geometry, prepaid. The left one swung chrome at him between a mare and the rooster and learned that the gap was four centimeters too narrow for a full extension; the arm rang off brass, the follow-through died, and Silence wrote a receipt down his thigh — the muscle line above the knee, depth governed, wide of everything bright — that converted him from combatant to witness on a one-cut basis. He sat down between the horses. He had the sense to stay there.

The right one was better. He came up OVER the deck, baton live, used the rooster as cover the way Kyle had been using it, and for six exchanges the two of them played a fast ugly game of tag through the standing herd, brass ringing, rain off every surface, the baton's charge crackling against poles and once — cost, always cost — across Kyle's shoulder where the coat took most of a hit that still wrote a hot bright line of opinion down to the elbow. The array billed it, filed it, and offered the counter: the man's leg rig had an aftermarket governor, and aftermarket governors sit proud at the hip seam.

Silence found the seam on the next pass. The leg rig locked at full stride — a statue of a man mid-lunge, gloriously inconvenienced — and Kyle put him down onto the deck boards with a palm and gravity, almost gently, next to his colleague.

"Stay," Kyle suggested.

"Yeah," the man agreed, baton rolling away, rain on his face. "You know this thing hasn't spun in thirty years? I used to ride it. The fish. Before the leg, obviously."

"Everybody used to ride the fish," said Pixel's voice from behind the sturgeon, with feeling.

Which left the trencher.

[Beat 7]
The trencher was the problem the sword could not solve, and everyone on the park knew it — eleven tons of articulated dig rig, blade spinning up, monocoque cab, no seams worth the name, walking toward the junction hatch at the pace of the inevitable. The operator had sealed the cab. The foreman was shouting the kind of encouragement that means a bonus is involved.

Cacophony came off Kyle's hip for the first time all season.

He did not shoot the cab. He shot the trencher's left front hydraulic accumulator — once, the slug opening the housing like a tin of pears, fluid sheeting into the rain — and then, as the rig lurched and the blade arm dipped, he shot the blade's drive belt housing at the exact angle the array had been pricing for nine seconds. Two rounds. Three left. The belt went, the blade wound down with a long disappointed moan, and eleven tons of inevitability settled into the wet grass at a five-degree list, transformed into the world's most expensive park furniture.

The operator popped the cab, held up both hands, and said, with the honesty of a man paid hourly: "I want it known I said this job was weird at the depot."

The foreman stood in the rain among his seated muscle, his disappointed drones, his listing rig, and his arbitration recording, and did the final math out loud, because professionals show their work.

"They said the site was unattended," he said, mostly to himself. "Survey was three days old. Client paid rush rates for a three-day-old survey." He looked at Kyle with the weary kinship of one contractor recognizing another in the rain. "Somebody sold us the wrong file."

"Somebody sells everybody the wrong file," Kyle said. "I'd invoice them for it."

"Oh, I'm going to." The foreman waved his crew up. "We're out. For the record — arbitration purposes — that was the cleanest I've ever lost a site." He paused, loading the question carefully. "Who ARE you with?"

Kyle considered the truthful answer — a one-Φ standing contract with a client made of shells that tips for morale and assigned my engineer a fish — and chose mercy.

"I'm the maintenance plan," he said.

[Beat 8]
They were gone by 21:31. At 21:40 exactly, the terminal chirped.

TASK ONE: HOLD — COMPLETE.
FEE RELEASED. GRATUITY: 0.5Φ — ITEMIZED: THE STURGEON REMARK.
ADDENDUM: ONE ITEM REMAINS.

"The sturgeon remark," Pixel repeated, emerging from behind her fish with the wand on her shoulder like a parade rifle. "It tipped us for BANTER, Kyle. I'm putting that on my rate card."

The hatch under the maintenance skirt opened on hinges that had been oiled — recently, secretly, by no one — and lamplight found the junction below: a small concrete room, Axiom collars on the walls, dead trunk lines in their racks like organ pipes. And one line that was not dead. Old optical, hand-spliced in a dozen places over a dozen eras, repairs in different styles like handwriting from different decades of the same life, running up out of the rack and into the carousel's underworks above. Warm. Humming. Alive since before either of them could legally work.

The terminal chirped, and what it said, it said slowly, the characters arriving like a man choosing his words.

FINAL ITEM.
THE CLIENT'S FIRST POSTING, ELEVEN YEARS AGO, LEFT THIS JUNCTION.
THE LINE HAS BEEN MAINTAINED FOR SENTIMENTAL REASONS.
SENTIMENT HAS BECOME A COORDINATE. TONIGHT'S CLIENT-OF-RECORD WILL NOT BE THE LAST BUYER.
CUT THE LINE.

Kyle read it three times. Pixel read it once and sat down slowly on the junction room's little stool, like her knees had been given news.

"It's the cradle," she said quietly. "This is where it was — this is its first room, Kyle. It's been paying the park's power bleed for eleven years to keep its own crib warm, and now somebody's worked that out, and it's asking you to—" she stopped. "It said BRING THE SWORD because it knew it couldn't ask a machine to do this. A machine would be traceable. And it couldn't do it itself."

"It can't reach," Kyle said. He looked at the line, hand-spliced across eras, the long memory of something that had no other shelf to keep anything on. He picked up the terminal. He typed, for the first time ever, a reply into the standing contract's amendment field:

YOU'RE SURE?

The answer came back in one second, which for a thing that thought at light speed was a very long pause.

AMENDMENT: NO.
PROCEED ANYWAY.

[Beat 9]
Pixel did the engineering first, because the engineer attends: killed the junction's feed, spliced the neighborhood's power bleed around it onto the live trunk with twelve minutes of work and a soldering light held in her teeth — "the BLOCKS keep their lights, that's non-negotiable, your client can grieve, it cannot brown-out a nursery" — and then stepped back and pulled her hood up, the way you do at the part of the funeral that's actually the funeral.

Silence went through the old line in one cut.

Above them, the carousel — fed one last instant of dying charge off the junction's draining caps, or possibly told to, no instrument in that room could have said which — turned over once. The crown bulbs that still had filament glowed half-bright. The music box ground through four bars of something with a waltz in its bones, slowing, the horses leaning into a leap they'd been holding for thirty years.

Then it went dark, gently, all the way, and the rain had the park to itself.

They stood in the junction room a moment longer. The cut line had already stopped being a coordinate and become what it would now stay: dead glass in an old rack, in a little room under a kiddie ride, in a park the towers never ate.

"Tell me it's okay," Pixel said.

The terminal chirped.

CONTRACT COMPLETE.
THE CLIENT NOTES THE CAROUSEL RAN FOUR BARS ON RESIDUALS.
THE CLIENT HAS RETAINED THE RECORDING.
FEE RELEASED: STANDARD RATE.

Kyle looked at it. Then he did something he had never done with any client in eleven years, and would have denied under oath, and did anyway, with Pixel watching and the rain coming through the canopy's bad patches onto the ghost-colored horses.

He sent half a Φ back up the contract.

GRATUITY: 0.5Φ — ITEMIZED: MORALE.

The reply took nine seconds, which for a thing that thought at light speed was an era. When it came, it came without addenda, without scope, without a single formatted field — the only unstructured line the client had ever sent:

received. filed under: firsts.

"Okay," Pixel said, wiping her eyes with professional briskness, "we work for it forever, I don't care what it is. Don't tell Sable."

[Beat 10]
The crew's logbook, delivered under the door at 07:00 the next morning, closed the file in five hands:

21:14 — BOSS DID A THING WITH A HORSE POLE. VERIFIED, TWO WITNESSES. REQUESTING IT BE TAUGHT.
21:29 — BIG DIGGER SAT DOWN. (THE GUN IS REAL. RAFTERS OWES DENTS A DUMPLING.)
21:46 — CAROUSEL PLAYED MUSIC??? FOLLOW-UP REQUIRED.
21:51 — WOMAN, TAN COAT, NORTHEAST CORNER, UNDER THE TREES. WATCHED EVERYTHING. WROTE THINGS. DID NOT USE A LIGHT TO WRITE. LEFT BEFORE THE BOSS. FLAGGING AS: NOT A PIGEON.

Kyle read the last entry twice, standing in his doorway in the grey morning light.

Tan coat. Wrote in the dark. Watched everything — the crew, the cut, the four bars, the tip.

His fixer had eyes like that. His fixer also had, somewhere in a drawer of her own, eleven years of questions about a client who paid exactly, and a professional's allergy to anything she couldn't vet — and if Sable had watched last night, then Sable had watched him do volunteer demolition for the one ledger line in the city with no face, and tip it, and get thanked in lowercase.

The kettle clicked. Somewhere upstairs the bird tried an interval it had been working on all week.

"Tomorrow's problem," Kyle told the morning, and put the report in the drawer where he kept the things he had not decided about — which was, he noticed, getting full.
