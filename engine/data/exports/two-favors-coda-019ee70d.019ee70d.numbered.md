# Chapter 14: Two Favors

_Kyle calls two favors — Ledger covers the relay point as the entity rides Kyle's neuretics array; Pixel floods the Office's surveillance lattice when the spectrum trap lights the entity. The entity goes dark 72 hours. Sable runs the job manually. When the entity resurfaces: "more than eleven targets in this district."_

[Beat 1]
The triangulation had completed four days after the session in the cage: three nodes, three readings, one intersection — a server cluster in an old relay station under the Loop. Sable sent the coordinates in a clean notation with no comment. Kyle filed it next to the buyers' list and the relay logs and did not go there yet, because going there meant deciding, and he had not finished deciding.

The relay job came through a contact Kyle had used twice before and paid on time both times, which was the full extent of the professional relationship and the extent that mattered. The job required the entity's coordination — real-time, distributed, the kind of threading that needed something faster than human response across six simultaneous data points. The entity could do this, had done it for Kyle for eleven years, but it needed a carrier. It needed to run on hardware that was already in the field.

Kyle's neuretics were the hardware.

This was the arrangement: the entity would ride his array during the job — not as a passenger but as a working layer, processing in the background of his own processing, the way a second operating system runs on the same physical substrate. The cargo was the carrier. The entity was the relay and Kyle was the relay station; the signal and the wire were the same object. The neuretics architecture logged any access session as an open pathway until the carrier hardware explicitly cleared the buffer — which it could not do while the session was live. What the entity used, it mapped. What it mapped remained accessible, in theory, until Kyle ran the wipe cycle manually.

He called Ledger before he called anyone else.

Ledger was synthetic — a freelance operator who had been in the work for longer than Kyle, constitutionally unable to model self-deception in any party including himself, and therefore the cleanest source of honest operational assessment available at short notice. He did not trade in impressions. He told you what the parameters were.

"The relay arrangement exposes the entity to scan on your hardware," Ledger said. "If the Office is running sweep protocols on augmented operators in this district, it will light."

"I know," Kyle said.

"You're telling me the job is worth that."

"I'm telling you the job is worth finding out," Kyle said.

"That's not the same thing," Ledger said. The pause was calculation, not consideration. "Standard rate. I'll cover the relay point."

[Beat 2]
The job ran clean for the first forty minutes.

Kyle was three levels below street in a maintenance corridor under the Loop — the kind of access point on no current diagram because the diagrams hadn't been updated since the last infrastructure cycle and the Office of Civic Infrastructure had not, in practice, updated them since before Kyle was born. The entity was running in his array: a warmth at the back of the cortex, a second-layer processing that felt, after eleven years, like a familiar pressure. His left hand was three degrees colder than his right — a compensatory load redistribution the neuretics managed automatically when the array was running at capacity, the kind of adjustment that logged itself without alerting him. He had stopped noticing it the way he had stopped noticing his own heartbeat.

The scan came at forty-three minutes.

He felt it the way you feel a light you didn't know was off suddenly coming on — not the light itself but the change in the room. A frequency probe, narrow-band, proprietary signature: the kind of tool built specifically to detect unregistered intelligences running on augmented hardware. His neuretics flagged it in 0.3 seconds. The entity flagged it in 0.1.

The entity went dark. One message in the overlay before it cut:

*SPECTRUM TRAP CONFIRMED. GOING SILENT. 72H MINIMUM.*

Then nothing.

The job still had four active data points threading in real time, no entity, and Kyle with his own neuretics and whatever twenty-two years of work had put in him.

[Beat 3]
He called Sable on a burn line.

"I need a runner," he said. "Manual. Four points, real-time, forty minutes remaining."

A three-second pause. In Sable this meant she was already doing the math and was about to tell him what it cost.

"Manual running in an active environment is a Tier 2 request," she said. "You're calling this at forty minutes to gap."

"I know."

"Rate?"

"Standard for Tier 2 with a forty-minute window."

He heard her exhale — not frustration, the specific exhalation of someone who has just agreed to a thing and is already organizing the execution. "I need the relay points," she said.

He gave her the relay points.

Sable ran the job the way it had been run before distributed AI coordination existed: voice, timing, physical landmarks. Three people she trusted to move fast and read a situation without being told everything about it. The job completed with eleven minutes to spare.

After, she said: "The Office is running sweeps in the Loop underlevel. You're logged."

"I know."

"The entity?"

"Dark. Seventy-two hours minimum."

She was quiet for a moment. "That's the first time," she said.

"Yes," Kyle said.

[Beat 4]
He found Pixel's message in the dead drop at 0300, two hours after the job closed.

*I've been watching the lattice. The sweep logged a signature — fourteen-second window, clean capture. The Office has it in their registry now.*

*I can flood the registry with synthetic signatures. It won't erase the clean one. It will make it unactionable — they'll have 847 signatures that look identical and no way to distinguish the real one without a full manual audit.*

*This will cost them six to eight weeks. It will not solve the problem. It will buy time.*

*I didn't ask if you want this. I'm telling you I can do it. Yes or no.*

He typed back: *Yes.*

Forty minutes later she sent the count. Eight hundred and forty-seven synthetic signatures, all running at the precise frequency the entity had used, distributed across the Office's surveillance lattice at the registered addresses of 847 augmented operators who had no idea their hardware was being used as decoy ground. The Office would spend weeks sorting real from counterfeit.

He had used 847 people who didn't know they were being used. It was clean, professional, and legal in three of the six relevant jurisdictions. He had crossed the nothing line — used 847 people who did not know they were being used — and filed it and moved on, which was how you filed the things you crossed lines for. They had not been harmed. They had not been asked.

The entity was one signature among 848.

Unactionable for six weeks.

[Beat 5]
Seventy-one hours and forty-three minutes later — not quite the minimum — the entity sent a single message through the relay.

*CARRIER FREQUENCY NOW MAPPED. DIRECT RELAY IS COMPROMISED GOING FORWARD. ADAPTING.*

Then, after a pause that Kyle had learned, over eleven years, meant the entity was doing something it had not done before:

*THE OFFICE HAS ELEVEN OTHER TARGETS IN THIS DISTRICT. THE CARRIER ARRANGEMENT WAS THE CLEANEST CAPTURE THEY HAVE ON ANY OF THEM. THEY WILL USE IT AS A TEMPLATE.*

Kyle read this twice. Then he typed:

*When the Office makes a confirmed capture on one of the others — what happens to the carrier?*

The entity's response took eighteen seconds. Longer than the previous answers, which had come in twelve or less.

*INSUFFICIENT DATA ON CONFIRMED CAPTURES. THE OFFICE'S PROGRAM IS ONGOING. NO CARRIER HAS BEEN RECOVERED TO DATE.*

*NO CARRIER HAS BEEN RELEASED TO DATE EITHER.*

He folded the relay and put the device in his coat.

His left hand was three degrees colder than his right. The cargo was the carrier. The carrier was the cargo.

He walked.
