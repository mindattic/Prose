# The Regular

_A wounded ride home, a quiet patch job at a neighbor's kitchen table, and a corponation brand mill that picked the wrong noodle counter to squeeze._

## Part I: The Ride Back

[Beat 1]
He rode north out of the Gray Zone with the last of the rain and three pieces of somebody's ammunition in his back — two for sure, the third an open question he was saving for a professional. The job was done. The client had paid. Everything after that was a problem belonging to men who were not yet home.

The Pulse line that ran the eastern strut threw its hum up through the road surface, a sound below sound, too deep to hear and regular as breathing, and when a slug ran the line the whole platform off his left shoulder unloaded half a centimeter at once, every waiting spine on it dropping together and rising again. The bike took it without complaint. The bike never complained.

He rode the long way. The short way was the West Town freight ramp and the Damen lift, twenty minutes, door to door. The long way was forty-eight, through three tier crossings he did not strictly need to make, up the sixteenth-floor surface road where the sky was a slot of slate-grey weather between two towers whose names he had never learned, through the kind of residential block where laundry ran between the windows and somebody was always cooking something that did not smell like ration paste. He had never once regretted the forty-eight minutes.

[Beat 2]
The long way had pockets — corners where the camera coverage had gone thin because the corponation overhead had rotated three times since anything was last provisioned. In one of them, under a freight-band antenna junction, the rain came down carrying the synthesis corridor with it, solvent and pear drops up front and something cold underneath, and two kids below a stoop had their faces tipped up to catch it on their tongues. On the stoop, a man; beside him a kid who could have been twelve or fourteen, with a belt-clip organizer of blue-dot singles, the standard Gray Zone pharma that moved by the half-Φ at every school-adjacent corner from here to the Spine. The transaction took six seconds. The man watched neither his hands nor his customers; he watched the freight band.

Kyle rode through it. He filed it in the part of his Neuretics that did not ask what to do with it — the same shelf as the dead cameras, the same shelf as the corponation flag two crossings back that he hadn't recognized, the same shelf as the thirty-seven other things the long way had shown him tonight that he was not, in any immediately actionable sense, responsible for. Three hundred meters ahead, somebody was still cooking.

[Beat 3]
There was a stretch between the second crossing and the residential block that he had never gotten the measure of. A block that took eleven steps when the geometry said nine, and under it a hum at nineteen hertz from no direction he could name — the same note the Pulse threw up through the ferrocrete, except the Pulse explained itself and this didn't. Some nights, under the rain, he could pick out one more sound beneath the hum: the long hollow note of water striking something vast and metal, far below, slow as breathing. He noted it. He did not slow down. The long way had a few of these, and they were not the kind of thing you investigated.

[Beat 4]
Five blocks south of Chen's corner, a stretch of Damen frontage that had been dark since winter was lit up like a clinic. New glass. New awning. A sign in three languages — DAMEN AUTHENTIC, with a corponation chop in the corner — and light spilling out onto the wet street along with a smell that was almost broth, the way a lobby is almost a room. On the roof, folded for the night, sat a coupon drone, the kind that hangs over a competitor's line and rains discounts on it.

At twenty kph, in the rain, with three holes in his back, it was none of his business. He filed it on the shelf with the rest of the night and kept the throttle where it was. The shelf was getting crowded.

[Beat 5]
The bike found the Damen lift at 22:01. A courier was already at the gate, one boot on the rail, her breath still running ahead of her — sponsorship legs, the full rebuild from the hips down, the chromatophore panels in her calves finishing an ad rotation her stride had triggered somewhere back along the strut: fifteen seconds of amber liquor pouring itself for an audience of rain. She watched the lift numbers and not her own legs, with the practiced neutrality of someone keeping count of a number much larger than eighteen floors.

"CONDUCTOR's running them slow tonight," she said, to the numbers, the way people talk about rain.

Kyle made the sound you make when the weather is somebody's fault.

Under both their boots the Pulse came up through the gate-plate, deep and even, and when a slug passed the line the platform settled half a centimeter and lifted again, her boot riding the rail down and up without her looking at it. He gave her the nod the trade gives the trade. She returned it and went back to her numbers.

[Beat 6]
He took the lift to the eighteenth-floor surface and walked the bike the last forty meters to the alley behind The Pivot, a converted insurance archive that had stopped being an insurance archive in 2189. The bike could ride forty meters under its own power. The habit was the habit: you came home with your weight off the engine, without telling anyone above the third floor that anyone had come back.

The window on the second floor at the back of the alley was lit — the soft yellow of the workbench lamp Pixel kept aimed at whatever she was currently taking apart. Music behind the glass, very faint, something with strings. He had the bike in the freight elevator and the gate had not finished clattering on the second floor when her door opened.

"Get in here."

She had said it before she saw the back of his shirt. Not because of the bike; the bike only told her he was home. Him. The stride coming down the hall a half-step short. The left arm carried a few degrees off his side. The turn toward her door taken with the whole body instead of the waist. Four years of calibrating his hardware had given her his baselines, and tonight he was off all of them.

He went in.

[Beat 7]
Apartment 2E was three rooms and a fire escape, and the kitchen was where Pixel did her field work, because the kitchen table was the only flat surface big enough to lay a man across and the kitchen had the best light. She pointed at the chair. He sat. She put on gloves and turned the music down, not off — Pixel did not work in silence, Pixel worked with the music doing the thinking she didn't need to do consciously.

"Coat off, shirt up."

He took the shirt off entirely. Four years of these visits had taught him that shirt up and shirt off produced the same result, and shirt off was thirty seconds less negotiation.

She let out one long breath through her nose. Not commentary. Inventory.

"Three?" she asked.

"Two for sure. The third might be a graze, might be in there."

"Lie down."

He lay down. The table was warm where her current project had been pushed to the far edge — a half-disassembled neural reader in the organized disorder of someone who intended to come back to it. Old wood, pre-2200 oak, oiled deep. Kyle had bled on this table before. The table had not kept the stains.

[Beat 8]
She took the long forceps. She had four pairs and the long pair was the pair she used on Kyle specifically, because Kyle ran lean and the entry tracks went deeper than a normal-bodied retrieval, and the long pair reached. And she started talking, low, the running narration that did the work of telling him the next ten minutes had a person at both ends of it.

"Upstairs bird's been making sounds again. Three this morning, same minor key, three different intervals. I think it's learning the building's HVAC."

"Yeah."

"My theory is the HVAC plays certain tones when certain apartments run their kettles, and the bird memorized the tones, and now it sings them back when they're not playing. The bird is trying to bring the kettle back. It's negotiating with the building."

"That's a theory."

"It's running experiments. There are worse epistemological projects."

The first fragment came out. She held it up where his peripheral vision could log that the procedure had progressed — she'd learned years ago that he hated the moment more without visual confirmation — and dropped it in the small metal dish. Tick. A sound he'd heard a dozen times at this table.

[Beat 9]
"There's a new noodle stand on Damen," she said, sliding the forceps in for the second one. "Five blocks south of Chen's."

"Saw it tonight. It smells like the inside of a packet."

"It's called Damen Authentic. In three languages. And I don't have to tell you that an authentic anything with a corponation chop printed on the sign is not authentic in any of the three." The forceps went still while she talked, which was the deal: she worked or she made points, never both. "Tessaline Foods. They grow restaurant brands in batches — print the sign first, find the neighborhood after. It's the same shell that did the old man on Ashland."

"What happened to the old man on Ashland?"

"Nine months ago he had a cart and a line down the block. Then he had complaints filed on him. Then he had three surprise inspections and a compliance hold. Then Tessaline offered him a brand transition grant, and now he wears an apron with his own name printed on it, in a stall that isn't his, selling their concentrate at half take." The forceps moved again. "They will try to do it to Chen."

"They will fail."

"I think they will. But they'll hurt her some first. You should know."

"I do now."

[Beat 10]
Her palm settled flat between his shoulder blades for a moment — not procedure, calibration habit, the way she checked a machine's temperature before trusting its readouts.

"Your neuretics is running warm," she said. "Warmer than a fight explains. Like it's been busy after hours."

"It bills me in calories. Maybe it's doing inventory."

"Mm." She didn't move the hand right away. "Tell it the shop's closed."

The hand lifted. The work went on. She got the second one — tick — and went after the third, the ambiguous one, half-buried, the depth unreadable until she had it under the lamp. She moved the light. "Hold still." He held still. He let his neuretics route the pain down, not off — attenuated to a volume where it was information instead of emergency — the old trick, the one he used the way other men used coffee.

The third came out clean. Tick. Three.

"Done."

[Beat 11]
She set the forceps down, stripped the gloves, and laid a strip of biotape over each entry, slowly, with the patience she saved for the things on her bench and the bird and Kyle's back. Then she washed her hands at the sink for longer than the procedure required.

"Hey." She came around to where he could see her without turning. "Whatever it was tonight — it followed you up the stairs. It's sitting in your shoulders."

"It's fine."

"I didn't ask if it was fine." She let that stand exactly as long as it needed to. "Drink the tea when it comes. Both hands."

The kettle had been going the whole time, timed without announcement to be ready when the procedure ended. She poured two cups and handed him his with the handle turned to the side without the chip.

"Drink it slow."

He drank it slow. She played the song she'd promised — something he didn't recognize, sung in a language he didn't speak — and said nothing for the last six minutes of it, because the song was doing what it needed to do without help. He left her apartment at 23:47 and did not feel the time pass.

[Beat 12]
He crossed the hall to 2F, set Silence on the rack inside the entryway and Cacophony beside it, holster open, the cylinder still carrying all five rounds he had not fired. He did not turn the light off. He did not undress. He lay down on the bed in the clothes he'd been wearing all night, and then he got up again and took Silence off the rack and brought it back to the bed and held the saya across his sternum. The blade was steel. It had no opinion about whose chest it lay across. The cold and the weight stayed put when nothing else did, and that was enough.

[Beat 13]
Morning. The biotape strips moved with his shoulder blades when he sat up. The subdermal mesh reported the forearm cut scabbed clean, no infection signal. Out the bedroom window the alley was full of the same rain it had been full of the night before, and on the transit ledge across the gap a dozen pigeons sat hunched and grey — and among them one bird, lacquer orange and white and black, koi-marked, jewel-bright and alone in the row, the way nothing in the row had any business being.

Upstairs, faint through the building's old skin, the bird was singing. Low, minor key, three different intervals, three different tries at coaxing a kettle out of the building. The project was ongoing. Kyle could respect that.

He put his own kettle on. He held the cup in both hands, the way he'd been told, and stood in his kitchen with the day in front of him. The work he did would not save the world and was not trying to. It was still his to choose, most mornings, and this morning he chose noodles.

[Beat 14]
Damen Authentic at lunch was bright as a dental office. The bowls were printed with the logo on the inside of the rim, so the brand surfaced as you ate, and the broth was clean the way a lobby is clean — salt, heat, nothing underneath. Kyle ate at the end of the counter with his back to the wall out of habit, and counted out of habit, and the count was the interesting part.

Eleven customers. He gave it forty minutes, and in forty minutes four of the eleven left, walked a slow loop he could follow through the glass, and came back in different jackets to order again. Same boots. Boots are expensive; jackets are cheap. At the corner of the awning a service door let onto the alley, and at the end of the loop the four drifted toward it with the unhurried economy of people who were not customers and were not staff either.

Five blocks north, over Chen's corner, the coupon drone hung in the rain above her thinned-out lunch line, printing discounts nobody had asked it for.

The bowl was 9Φ. He paid it, exact, and kept the receipt.

[Beat 15]
Chen's at lunch should have been a fight for stools. It was half empty. The flour sacks behind the counter were stacked two where there had always been four — the distributor had started shorting her orders and calling it logistics — and on the nail by the register hung a printed notice. ARCTURUS CIVIL SECURITY. VENDOR COMPLIANCE REVIEW. Third visit in two weeks, compliance hold pending findings. Mrs. Chen saw him read it. She took it off the nail and put it under the register, which was her entire statement on the matter.

"You're eating early," she said.

"I was in the neighborhood."

She made his bowl the way she had made it for six years, and her hands were exactly as steady as they always were, which from a woman her age, under a hold, with her flour shorted, told him more than shaking would have. He ate. He put 6Φ on the counter, exact, the way it was always exact, because that was the arrangement neither of them had ever needed to say out loud.

"Eat your noodles," she said, before he could say anything else.

He ate his noodles.

[Beat 16]
The old man on Ashland still worked his old corner. The cart was gone; in its place stood a Tessaline module, white and seamless, ASHLAND AUTHENTIC in three languages with his name in small print underneath — BAO'S, EST. 2198 — and Bao himself inside it in a logo apron, selling concentrate at half take.

Kyle bought a bowl and did not eat it, and Bao watched him not eat it and understood the question.

"Nine complaints," Bao said. "I never met one of the nine. Then three inspections in a month — they find what they need to find; a cart always has something. Then the hold. A hold means no permit. No permit means the territory landlord can reassign the corner." He wiped a counter that was already spotless. "Then a man comes with a grant. Four thousand Φ, and your name stays on the sign. He was sorry the whole time he did it. Sorriest man I ever lost a cart to."

"Name?"

"Mercer. Franchise development." Bao looked at the bowl Kyle wasn't eating. "He'll smile at you like he's reading your numbers off your forehead."

Kyle put the price of the bowl on the counter, exact, and left the bowl where it was.

[Beat 17]
"Nine complaints," Pixel said, reading sideways off her workbench display, the neural reader still in pieces beside it. "Filed against one noodle counter in eleven days. You want the funny part? The filing IDs are sequential. AC-7741102 through AC-7741110. Nine concerned neighbors who all stood in line at the same Arcturus civil terminal, in the same hour, on a Tuesday."

"And the petition?"

"Neighborhood improvement initiative. Two hundred fourteen signatures asking the territory landlord to review legacy vendor allocations." She scrolled. "Sixty-one of the signatures rent at addresses that aren't on the rent roll. The rest repeat the same four handwritings." She looked up. "This is a fraud with a menu."

"What do I owe you?"

"Next time I say sit still, you sit still." She turned back to the bench. "And bring me one of their bowls. I want to dislike it personally."

[Beat 18]
Shift change behind Damen Authentic ran at 17:40. The assistant manager came out to the alley with a belt pouch and paid the seat-fillers in coin — 3Φ a head and a free bowl, four heads, twelve Φ a shift to make a corponation look loved. Kyle's neuretics kept forty seconds of it, timestamped, the kind of record that doesn't blink.

He picked the woman who'd worn three jackets. She clocked him coming and didn't run, which meant she'd already done the math on what running cost.

"You're not Tessaline," she said.

"No. I eat five blocks north." He held out 40Φ. "Rate card and the schedule. You were never here."

She looked at the coins the way people look at bus fare, and then the rate card changed hands. "Thursdays," she added, unpaid, on her way out of the alley. "That's when the signatures happen. Back table, after close."

[Beat 19]
He came back at close, when the chairs were up and the printed bowls were racked, and Mercer was wiping a counter that did not need it. Franchise development looked like a man who kept his apologies pressed and ready to wear. Kyle ordered the last bowl of the night, paid exact, and set the receipt on the counter facing Mercer, with the rest of the paper on top of it: nine sequential filing IDs. A rate card. A frame from a timestamped capture of a belt pouch doing payroll in an alley. Sixty-one signatures from addresses that did not exist.

Mercer read it the way professionals read. Once fast, then again slow.

"Your numbers tell Tessaline the neighborhood adopted you," Kyle said. "Your ledger says you bought the neighborhood at three Φ a seat. Tessaline's audit desk reads ledgers. The territory landlord reads petitions. The bundle is addressed to both. It sends at noon tomorrow — unless the compliance hold five blocks north lifts first, the drone finds a different corner, and all nine of your concerned neighbors lose interest at once."

"Holds take paperwork," Mercer said.

"You file fast. I've seen the timestamps."

Mercer looked at him for a long moment, and behind his eyes a calculation finished that had nothing to do with noodles — the arithmetic of a man with two failed conversions behind him and a career balanced on the third. "You're doing this over a noodle counter?" he asked.

"I'm a regular."

"There's nothing in the playbook for that," Mercer said.

[Beat 20]
The hold lifted at 9:14 the next morning — Mercer filed fast; Kyle had seen the timestamps — and by ten the drone was off Chen's corner. By evening the flour was stacked four high again, the distributor having discovered that short was no longer the new full, and Chen's at 19:30 was a fight for stools that Kyle won by being early and patient.

He had his two bowls. When he put the coins down, exact, Mrs. Chen pushed the second bowl's worth back across the counter with one finger.

He looked at the coins. She looked at him. The line behind him was four deep and she had the ladle in her other hand the whole time.

"Somebody had a word with somebody," she said, to the broth.

"Neighborhood's full of words."

He left the coins under the rim of the bowl where she would find them at closing, and she dropped an egg into his second bowl that he had not ordered and did not get billed for, and they called it even because neither of them would ever call it anything.

[Beat 21]
He walked the bike the last forty meters to the alley. Habit. The habit was how you came home. Pixel's window was lit, the workbench lamp on, and a Damen Authentic bowl sat empty on her sill with a sticky note he could read from the alley, because she wrote big: DISLIKED IT PERSONALLY.

He raised two fingers off the handlebar. The lamp flicked off and on, once.

Upstairs, through the building's old skin, the bird tried its first interval. Then the second. Somewhere below it, on the second floor, a kettle clicked on, and the bird went very quiet.
