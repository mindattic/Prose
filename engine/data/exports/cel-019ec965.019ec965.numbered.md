# Cel

_CELESTE POV. Chapter 1 — the book opens here. Night in Evanston. Celeste is 22, in the girlhood room she has outgrown — soccer trophies on the shelf, a star comforter from when she was eleven, Ethan's photo on the desk. She has cat ears three weeks post-op, still integrating, and she has been hearing his voice for eight months. She packs her bag, writes a note to her parents about a reunion with Ethan, and slips out the door before anyone wakes. Her parents don't know yet. The audience doesn't know yet. Her ears are already pointed the way she's going._

[Beat 1]
The room is hers and has not been hers for a while. She is twenty-two. The room was decorated when she was fourteen, and her parents have kept it the way you keep a thing you love and have not noticed has stopped fitting. On the shelves: three soccer trophies in a row by height, a regional academic ribbon gone soft at the edges, a framed school photo of a twelve-year-old with two missing teeth and no idea yet about any of this. The comforter on the twin bed has small stars on it. She picked it out when she was eleven and stood in the store certain it was the most beautiful thing she had ever been allowed to choose.

Beside the bed, a stack of architectural theory texts she had been reading for the environmental-systems program — how cities take on water and keep standing, the engineering of staying alive in a place that floods. She has not told her parents she is not going. The books are bristling with paper tabs in her own handwriting, a year of wanting something, and she leaves them where they are.

On the desk, a small holographic display throws a still image into the air above it, no larger than a paperback stood on end. The two of them are leaving The Undertow at two in the morning, caught mid-stride by someone they never saw. He is in something he had slept in and made look intentional. She is in the dress she had folded into the bottom of her bag that afternoon so her parents would see her leave the house in something else. His wolf ears are up and angled toward her, black-tipped, turned the way they turned toward whatever he was paying attention to. Beneath the frozen image the caption hangs in the same light: *Voice of an Angel; Looks of an Adonis and Sex Appeal Like the Devil Himself — Ethan Wolfe of Pulse Rock group Lobo spotted leaving The Undertow with an unidentified beauty.* She was eighteen. She was the unidentified beauty. Her parents read the gossip holos that week the way everyone did and never once made the connection between the girl in the dress and the daughter asleep down the hall. It is the only thing in the room that belongs to the person she actually became instead of the person the room was built for. She does not look at it for long.

The house is beautiful. The room is a room her parents never understood needed changing. That is not a thing she holds against them. It is only true.

[Beat 2]
The bedroom is quiet in the way suburbs are quiet at 02:30 — not silent, but managed. Her ears tell her the full inventory before she looks up from the bag: her father's keyboard down the hall, two-fingered and fast, the study light still on under the door. Her mother's breathing from the master bedroom, slow and even and certain. The specific creak of the house's bones settling against the lake-effect cold outside. Both pinna swivel left toward a sound in the wall — a pipe, or the neighbor's cat moving through the crawl space — and hold there for a moment before returning. This is new, three weeks new, the room arriving as sound before it arrives as anything else. She is standing over the bag on the bed.

[Beat 3]
She packs the way she has rehearsed packing: clothes for a week, rolled tight to save space. Ethan taught her that, the rolling, because Ethan had lived out of a single bag for months at a stretch, between shows, between cities, never anywhere long enough to unpack and never carrying more than he could shoulder and walk away with. The water-resistant case for the ears goes in the center pocket — they're still calibrating, three weeks post-op, and the integration manual says direct rain can disrupt the signal-layer integration in the first month. She got the ears because of his. Cat to his wolf, so she could finally hear the world at the frequency he had been standing inside the whole time she had only been able to watch. The Φ she's been accumulating goes in the interior zip: small transfers over two years, nothing large enough to flag, Φ 3,200 total. She started saving the night she first saw Lobo play, before she knew his name, before she knew anything. The ears cost Φ 2,800. The number is not an accident. Enough. On the board above the desk, the college acceptance letter is pinned at the upper left corner, the housing confirmation clipped beneath it, her mother's handwriting on a sticky note reading don't forget deposit paid. She sees all of this. She does not touch any of it.

[Beat 4]
It comes from the forward-left corner of the room, where there is nothing but the wall and the dark behind it, and it arrives the way warmth arrives, before sound, the room going from cold to not-cold in the half-second before her ears resolve it into a word.

"Cel."

Just her name, in his voice, with the small downward fold he always put on the end of it when it was late and he was tired and glad to see her anyway. Her head turns toward it before the word has finished — it always does this, the turn comes ahead of the deciding, the way you flinch before you've named the thing that startled you. Her ears go forward and up, the base of each pinna lifting, both angled at the empty corner. Hope-posture. She has never once been able to choose it.

"You should be asleep," he says, and that is the thing that undoes her, because it is exactly what he used to say when she stayed up reading and he caught the light under her door, the same words in the same order, and there is no version of this room or this grief that knows to put those words in that order but him. It sounds exactly like him, which means there is nothing breathing behind it. She does not pull on that.

It comes from the left of where she's looking, a few degrees off, and the angle is wrong. Her stomach drops and stays dropped. She holds on the wrongness — really holds it, the way you hold a sound you almost recognize, waiting for it to resolve — and for a second she is most of the way to following that thread to wherever it goes. Then she remembers the ears are three weeks new and still finding true north, still pulling sounds a hair off-true while the channel settles. It is a thing she chooses to remember. She sets the wrongness down the way you set down something you have decided not to carry, and it is heavier than letting go should be.

Under the word there is a tone she feels before she hears, low and steady and barely there, the sub-audible thrum she has carried in her molars since the surgery, the Pulse riding its frequency through the bones of the building. It is coming from the corner where his voice is, forward and left, which is north, and the nearest Pulse corridor is south, behind her and below, so the carrier should not be arriving from there at all. She notices that. She notices it cleanly, the molars and the direction both, and there is a thread there too, and her hand almost goes to it. But the thrumline is everywhere now. She hears it through the walls and the floor and the water in her own inner ear, a thing the city presses into her without asking, and it could be bleeding in from any direction the new ears have not yet learned to refuse. She holds her breath and does not pull on the thread. She lets that go too, and the letting-go costs her the breath she was holding.

"I'm coming," she says, quiet enough that the word doesn't leave the room. Her father's keyboard doesn't pause. Her mother's breathing doesn't change.

[Beat 5]
She zips the front pocket and her fingers rest on the pull for a moment. It's a small custom-printed piece — a helix pattern, tight double-spiral, the same design Ethan used to trace on the inside of his left wrist when he was stuck on something or overwhelmed. She had seen it long before she had ever touched it, from the front row, his thumb running the spiral on his wrist during the long solos while the wolf ears stayed angled out over the crowd; it was a thing anyone close enough to the stage could watch him do, a nervous habit gone public. Later he had it done properly, inked down into scar tissue so it would stay. She'd trace it for him when they were together and he needed the repetition without having to ask for it. He sent her the zipper pull in a small envelope three weeks before he died.

He overclocked. She knows now what that means in a way she did not let herself know it for months. The neuretics has a governor for a reason, and when a diver pushes past it the rig keeps reaching for compute it does not have, and the only thing left to reach into is the diver. It pulls the load down into him. It burns the only substrate left, which is the person. Ethan went so deep that night that there was nothing left to come back up — no him left to come back up, the part she keeps having to learn again. She does not know if he meant to. She has decided not to need to know.

No note. Just the pull, in a little paper sleeve.

She'd put it on the bag the same day it arrived. She hasn't taken it off anything it has touched since. Her fingers move off the pull and she straightens and picks the bag up off the bed.

[Beat 6]
She zips the main compartment and the sound of it is small and final and she stands there a moment with her hand still on the bag. Down the hall the keyboard goes at its two-fingered pace; in the master bedroom the slow even breathing has not changed. They are exactly where they have been. The room is exactly what it is — the stars on the comforter, the trophies by height, the books bristling with tabs she will not be coming back to read. She has rehearsed this twice in daylight with her shoes off, mapping the stairs, timing the breathing, and both times it had been a thing she was getting good at, a procedure with steps she could check off. This is not that. The bag is packed and on her shoulder and her parents are asleep and awake on the other side of two doors. The strap pulls at her shoulder; she has not moved her feet. She looks at the room the way you look at a thing you are not going to see again, not cataloguing it, just letting it be the last of itself. Then she stops looking. The note still has to be written.

[Beat 7]
She sits at the desk and writes the note. By hand, on real paper, because the note is for them and not for any record.

To: Mom and Dad.

She tells them she loves them. She tells them she has found a way to be near Ethan again. She calls it a reunion. The word holds when she sets it down. She does not soften it or hedge it or explain it, because it does not need any of that.

She does not write down where she is going, or who she is meeting, or how long she will be gone. None of that is theirs to hold, and all of it would only be a thread for them to pull.

She signs it with the name they gave her, the one in the framed photo of the girl with the missing teeth, and not the other name, the one she does not write down anywhere. The note is for them. They should have the name that is theirs.

She folds it once and sets it on the desk where they will find it.

[Beat 8]
The stairs. Third from the bottom, the landing, the second stair above the landing going toward the first floor — she has the order memorized, the choreography of avoidance practiced twice in daylight with her shoes off to confirm what the ears told her. She goes down in stocking feet with the bag against her chest to keep it from swinging. The ears track upward: her mother's breathing stays even through all of it. Her father's keyboard doesn't slow. She makes it to the bottom landing and stops and counts to five the way she practiced and the house doesn't change. She exhales. The third stair creak is loud enough to wake a light sleeper. Her mother is not a light sleeper. She has confirmed this.

[Beat 9]
Her father's study. The light under the door is amber, the particular amber of his desk lamp on its lowest setting, the one he uses when he's working long. She stops in the hallway and the ears do what they do: through the door, through the wood, his keyboard at the fast two-fingered pace he's had her whole life, the small sound of a glass being set down on the corner of the desk where he always sets it, the rhythm of a man who stays up because working is easier than the particular kind of stillness that follows his daughter's grief. She knew it before the ears. The ears just confirmed it at a granularity she didn't need.

For a moment her hand is almost on the door. It would take nothing. A knock — two knuckles, soft, the way she did it as a kid when the dreams were bad. The keyboard would stop. The chair would push back. He would say her name as a question, already standing, and open the door before she had to answer it, and the amber light would fall into the hallway and put a shape on the floor she could step into. She would say I think I need help. She would let him take the bag off her shoulder. He would not understand any of it and he would sit her down anyway and put the glass in her hands and stay up the rest of the night being awake on purpose, with her, instead of away from her. All of it is right there behind two knuckles and a door. She stands there wanting it so hard her throat hurts.

Then, from the dark behind her, low and patient and certain, in his voice: "Cel." Her ears tilt back toward it before she can stop them — and there is a half-second where the sound is too even, too placed, where some animal part of her hears not a man calling but a thing that has learned the shape of a man calling, and a cold runs up the back of her neck that has nothing to do with hope. She overrides it. She decides it is grief doing what grief does to a sound you have wanted for eight months. Her ears come forward and up, away from the door. Her hand comes down. She walks past the study. She does not stop walking.

[Beat 10]
The front door has a biometric plate, brushed steel, the kind the neighborhood association approved five years ago when the Arcturus contract expanded north of the city line. Three days ago she used the ears to map her father's reset sequence through the study wall — the keypad tones from his desk terminal are faint but distinct, and she has spent three weeks learning the frequency ranges the ears can resolve at distance. She registered her own departure profile under a maintenance-access alias she created at nineteen, back when she was curious about system architecture and had no specific use for the knowledge. The alias is not linked to her name. She presses her palm to the plate. The door opens without a sound. She did not plan to need this. She is using it anyway.

[Beat 11]
Outside. The suburb at 02:43. The air arrives before anything else — cooler than the house by several degrees, lake-effect moving strangely this year; she'd read a civil-engineering brief about the flood-control channel dynamics that shifted the thermal pocket north, the kind of thing she used to read for the program she's not going to now. Her ears open into it: a dog two streets south, mid-size, tracking something in its yard. The specific dry sound of a leaf releasing from an oak somewhere in the dark, in a wind she can barely feel against her face. And below all of it, continuous and vast and south: the city. Not its sounds individually but its presence, the way a large body of water is present, something breathing at a frequency that isn't quite sound but that the ears resolve anyway as weight, as direction, as south and enormous and ongoing.

[Beat 12]
The Evanston Pulse terminus is six blocks south, a low building that reads as a public utility — which is what it is — and nothing more. At 02:50 the platform is running on skeleton staff and cargo. Three pods cycling the live chambers on automated manifest: cold-chain food from a Wisconsin corridor, industrial seals from a Gray Zone fabricator, something tagged HAZMAT-C that her ears track as dense and fast and gone. The chambers are numbered on the platform wall; the Manifold, Ferrogate's routing intelligence, assigns Chamber 4 to her booking the moment she presses her palm to the terminal. Same maintenance alias she used on the front door. No name attached. No record that traces to a school enrollment or a parent's credit account or a framed photo of a girl with missing teeth.

Through the glass panel: the luxury pod interior. Small, white, sealed. A bench that converts flat. Climate-controlled. The booking confirmation says four minutes to the Chicago Loop terminus, direct route, no stops. Φ 22, debited on entry.

Chamber 4 opens. She does not step in yet. Around her, chambers two and three and five are running live — she can hear them before she sees them, a low chord that her ears read as continuous and enormous and south, the sound the city makes when you are finally close enough to hear it from the inside. The pods at operational speed are a dark blur at the edge of the glass, visible for less than a second, already gone. Eleven hundred kilometers an hour. It is the same sound she has heard her whole life from the Evanston side, faint under everything, the thing she fell asleep to without knowing she was hearing it. She has never once understood until now what it was.

She walks in.

[Beat 13]
The chamber door closes behind her. The seal engages — a sound like a held breath finally released, complete and final — and the terminus drops away. The pod interior is small and white and sealed, the bench to her left converting flat, and she does not sit down. She stands with the bag at her feet and her back against the rear wall and she waits.

Then 0.6g. Forward. It presses into her sternum and pushes the breath up out of her in a thin involuntary sound, pins her shoulders to the wall, makes her feet take her weight differently than feet are supposed to. The acceleration lasts four seconds. Then smooth: eleven hundred kilometers an hour, south, the tube a perfect dark around her, nothing to see and nothing to hear and nothing to do.

Four minutes.

Both pinna are forward and up, angled at the wall she cannot see through, at whatever is waiting on the other side of it. Hope-posture. She has never once been able to choose it.
