# Cherry Trees

[Beat 1]
On the eighteenth day Kyle went to see the parts man on Ashland about a throttle cable, because the throttle cable on the bike had developed a twelve-millimeter stretch at the barrel end and he had been managing it for three weeks and was done managing it.

He had been going to Cho on Ashland for six years. The man sourced Axiom-era hardware out of a storefront with the footprint of a wide closet, sold nothing he couldn't hold to a light, and had never once been out of stock of anything Kyle needed. The stretch was survivable on surface streets but it put a half-second of slop between intention and execution, and in a corridor the slop was the kind of thing that invoiced.

"Throttle cable," Kyle said.

Cho was already moving. He opened the first drawer, held it to the light, closed it. Opened a second. His hands slowed. He opened a third, stood with his back to Kyle for a moment that went three seconds too long, and turned around wearing the expression of a man who has rehearsed an apology until it sits on his face like a function key.

"Throttle cable," Cho said. "I had some."

"You always have some."

"I'm currently out." He gestured at nothing. "I can check with the Damen yard."

Behind his shoulder, the third drawer was still open a centimeter. The label was in Cho's own hand: the cable spec, Kyle's spec, written there because Kyle had needed it often enough that Cho had pre-labeled the drawer for efficiency. The drawer was not empty. Kyle could see the shape of coiled cable through the gap.

"Thanks," Kyle said, and walked out, because there was nothing in the conversation worth finishing.

He rode home with the slop still in the cable. The half-second was still there.

[Beat 2]
The carrier who ran his packages — a woman named Freya who operated out of the Bucktown freight level and had never once been late in four years — stopped answering at 07:00 the next morning. Not a queue. Not a delay. Her terminal returned reads without delivering them, the way a door returns a knock without opening.

He had a job running that week: a skip-trace in the Pilsen corridor, a sitting client waiting on a delivery confirmation, and forty Φ on the back end contingent on the documentation landing before a civil filing deadline. He spent two hours routing through Freya's backup and the Loop carrier Ria used, and the Loop carrier told him, politely, that she was running at capacity. He sent the documentation through a walk courier service on Halsted at a surcharge of twelve Φ and it arrived twenty minutes past the filing window. The client paid the base fee and declined the back end, which was their right.

Pixel, at the far end of his table with a casing half-open and her tools fanned, asked without looking up if he'd tried the backup.

"Lost twelve on the courier," Kyle said. "Forty on the filing. Fifty-two total."

She set down her loupe.

"How many doors," she asked.

"Ashland is one. Freya is two. I found four more this morning — fixers who aren't returning calls. A rigger named Goss who normally takes anything with a fee."

"Goss," Pixel said. The word had a flat edge; she had used Goss twice and found him reliable. "That's deliberate."

"Yes."

The kettle went. She filled both cups and set his down, handle first, and went back to the casing without saying what they both already knew: that the invoicing was underway, and they were in the middle of it, and the question was how high the number went.

[Beat 3]
The number, when Sable called it, was seven doors.

She came up to the flat on Tuesday with her coat damp and her ocular implants running — the ring around the left one clicked once, the sound it made when the math was confirmed — and she sat down at the table without being asked, which was not how Sable usually arrived.

"Seven confirmed holding," she said. "Parts, carrier, two fixers, Goss, a courier depot on the Spine, and one bonded storeroom that won't name its client. Nobody's refusing you. They're holding." She reached into her coat and set a small folded card on the table. "Lotus paper."

Kyle looked at the card without touching it.

"Syndicate-wide. The word is HOLD, not AVOID, not FLAG." She turned it over to show him the back: blank. "No exit pricing on the back, which means it isn't a prelude to anything commercial. Someone is sitting on this rather than spending it."

"What's the difference?"

"A hold with a price is a negotiation. A hold with no price is a statement." She looked at the blank side a moment. "Someone senior enough to hold without naming a number is senior enough to lift without naming one either. You wait and see if the card turns over, or you find a door."

She left it on the table. The card sat next to the untouched bowl from that morning — Kyle had lost the appetite for two somewhere around the third door — and he spent the evening with the ribs he'd been favoring since the corridor pressing against the chair back, reminding him of their opinion on the whole arrangement.

[Beat 4]
On the eighteenth morning the crew's logbook had a new entry.

08:44 — KID ON THE STOOP. DRY COAT. CAR MONEY. ASKED FOR BOSS. HANDED AN ENVELOPE AND LEFT. DID NOT TAKE A DUMPLING (OFFERED). SUSPICIOUS.
NOTE FROM RAFTERS: DREW HIS FACE. FOR THE FILE. VERY UNMEMORABLE FACE. THIS IS SUSPICIOUS.

The envelope was heavy card stock, the kind that costs more than the message inside. Inside: a cream rectangle written in a single hand-brushed line above a stamped impression — five petals, open, one blossom, no name. The line read:

BRING THE SWORD.

Below the blossom: an address in the Narrows and a time, 20:00.

Kyle put it on the table next to Sable's card and went to find clean clothes.

[Beat 5]
The Narrows announced itself by what it did not have.

Every streetlight worked. He counted the first three and kept counting until the fixture rhythm stopped mattering because it never broke. In West Town you learned the patch-map by season — the dead stretch on Milwaukee, the dark third post that the block had rerouted its foot traffic around since last autumn without complaint, the way the city and its residents had arrived at a working arrangement with the absence of maintained infrastructure. Here the lights ran continuous and even. The geometry of sustained attention pulled something up the spine: not threat, but the recognition of an institution that had been keeping something for a long time, the kind that did not need to announce it.

Gutters clear. No tags on the support pillars. The corner positions staffed by men and women who stood the way staff stood, not sentinels: they observed without performing observation, occupied space without defending it, clocked Kyle on entry and returned their attention to the mid-distance without haste. Nobody begged. Nobody moved at angles.

He found the address two blocks in: a covered arcade between two building faces, open at both ends, roofed in mesh strung with fixed-spectrum strips close enough to touch. In steel collars bolted to the structural uprights, eleven cherry trees grew.

He stopped.

They were blooming — white to a pink with blood in it, not the faded pink of forced spring stock but something deliberate, a color that took horticulture years to breed into the wrong month. The lights were buried units, throwing illumination upward through the collar roots, catching each blossom from underneath the way a face looks in held light — present, specific, briefly eternal. Petals had come loose and lay on the wet concrete in clusters, small and exact.

He stood there with petals settling on his coat and let the trees be what they were: engineered, expensive, bought with every tariff Lotus charged the Narrows since whoever planted the first collar, and beautiful anyway, both of those things at once and neither canceling the other.

Children ran circuits under the arcade, six of them, screaming on the inhale at the specific pitch of a body moving faster than it can account for. Nobody hushed them.

[Beat 6]
The ramen-ya was eight stools and a cedar counter and a door that stood open.

He counted the stools on the way in. Hand-planed cedar gone the gold of long handling, a counter that knew what it was because people had brought their elbows to it for enough years to tell it. The cook stood behind it with the focused economy of a person who has no wasted motions left because they shed them years ago. The other stools held four people: a man in his forties with a collapsed baton at his hip, a woman with a vibro-edge unit in a shoulder rig, a younger man Kyle read as wire by the way he held his neck, and an empty jacket at the far end that had left a cup and a short blade on the counter beside it.

Weapons worn. Nothing checked at the door but behavior.

Kyle hung his coat on the hook at the entrance — the only hook, placed by someone who understood guests — and sat on the remaining stool. The cook looked at him once, turned to the pot, and began building a bowl.

Kyle had not ordered. The cook did not ask.

The bowl arrived in three stages: the broth first, the color of good age and the smell of something started days earlier and tended since; then the noodles, hand-pulled, landed with exactly enough time to finish their cooking in the broth; then the fat, rendered and precise, a thin lens on the surface, and from somewhere behind the counter the cook deposited a half-round of soft-boiled egg split with a knife held sideways so the yolk stayed whole.

Kyle ate the first mouthful.

He understood immediately what the bowl was for. Not hospitality in the ordinary sense — an argument, made in fat and salt and twenty years of practice. The argument was: we make things correctly here. It was probably the point of the bowl.

He was most of the way through it when the door changed.

[Beat 7]
The Branch Manager came through the doorway at the pace of someone who had walked this route for a long time and did not need to announce it — umbrella furled and tucked under his arm, mid-sixties, a suit without a single concession to fashion, not old-fashioned and not plain but the kind selected once by a man who understood fabric and had not needed to revisit the question since. He was half a head shorter than Kyle and had the stillness of a man who had never needed to take up space to fill it.

Everyone at the counter got four centimeters taller when he came in.

Not visibly. Not in any way you could name a number to. The body registered it before the brain confirmed it: the man who ran this territory had arrived, and the room was better for it. He sat at the empty jacket's stool, claimed his cup, and drank from it like a man resuming a sentence he'd stepped away from.

He looked at Kyle's face.

"You took the clerk out," the Branch Manager said. "Fourteen people in that corridor. Our people. Several of them went home significantly the worse for the meeting."

Kyle waited. His ribs registered the change in posture.

"The contract was legitimate," the Branch Manager said. "Your conduct was correct. Our crew engaged first." He turned his cup once in both hands. "We pay for what we break. It is most of what we are. The hold lifted as of this evening — the seven doors, the carrier on the Spine, the parts man on Ashland." He paused. "Cho is easily embarrassed about these things. He has the cable for you. With apologies, priced at list. The apologies are real. The pricing is how we mean them."

Kyle ate the last mouthful of the bowl.

"I appreciate it," he said.

"The fifty-two," the Branch Manager said, without inflection.

Kyle looked at him.

"The filing gap. The courier surcharge. Cho's hold cost you more than he cost you in stock." The Branch Manager set his cup down. "The apology accounts for the doors. The doors account for everything behind them. Priced at list."

He reached into his coat and set a small envelope on the cedar. Not touching Kyle's space. Close enough to acknowledge.

"Good?" the Branch Manager asked, and looked at the bowl.

"Best in the city."

"That," said the Branch Manager, with the faint satisfaction of a man whose argument has landed, "is probably the point of the bowl." He put two fingers on the counter's edge, thinking. "I have a second piece of business. Not a condition. The freeze lifts either way."

Kyle waited.

"I'm offering you a position on payroll. House colors. Infrastructure — parts, carrying, the counters in every territory we hold, a legal team that files on your behalf and bills to us. In five years, if the work is what I believe the work is, a chair at this counter with your name in the wood." He touched the cedar's edge with two fingers. "The cedar decides, eventually. It keeps certain names."

"No," Kyle said. "I don't wear colors."

The Branch Manager did not look surprised. He looked like a man reaching a confirmation he had arrived expecting.

"The clerk who sat in that seat nine years ago said exactly that," he said. "In exactly that register. She had been doing piece work in the Narrows for three years. She said no, I don't wear colors, and then she sat down anyway. It took eleven months and one specific kind of trouble she didn't want to carry alone. She runs the Bucktown counting room now." He picked up his cup. "The offer stands. Standing offers are how we say please."

Kyle looked at the counter. Cedar gone gold with handling across years. He could see where the grain ran different under the finish — the older darker sections at the older stools, the pale sections at the new ones. His stool was neither new nor old. Somewhere in the middle of the sequence.

The standing offer was a mechanism, and he understood its geometry: he would come back for the bowl. When he came back, the offer would be standing, because standing offers were exactly that. The courtesy was not a courtesy. It was infrastructure.

"I'll remember it," Kyle said.

"That's all it asks," said the Branch Manager, and rose, and took his cup to the far end of the counter, and the room did its four centimeters again.

[Beat 8]
The togishi was at the counter's far end, between the wire's stool and the wall, working the cook's knives.

Kyle had not noticed him arrive, or perhaps he had always been there — one of those people who occupy space so completely they become part of the scene before you count them. Small, older than the Branch Manager by ten years, with the hands of a man whose profession was the repair of attention. He ran the whetstone along a blade with the patience of someone for whom this was not maintenance but reading: each pass an inquiry.

The cook's knives were good blades. The togishi treated them the way you treated something that would outlast you.

Kyle watched for a while, because watching was the only polite thing.

"One Φ," he said eventually.

The togishi looked up. He looked at Silence's saya.

"One Φ for what?" he asked.

"Your read on the blade. One minute."

"Togishi does not work by the minute," the togishi said, and held out his hand.

Kyle paid first, because that was how it was done, and laid Silence across the man's palms.

The togishi held it without drawing, hands flat, feeling the balance point travel through his grip. Then he drew it, slowly, and held it to the light from outside — the cherry tree light, the pink-white coming through the arcade opening and falling soft on the blade's face.

He looked at it for a long time.

"Kept correctly," he said. "Good geometry. Good edge. No maker's mark." He turned the blade and examined the back. "No file history. Someone has worked this blade for a long time and left no record of working it." He looked up. "A blade with no parents. Either made entirely outside the guild tradition, or made inside it and the documentation deliberately removed, which is a different category of story."

"Which do you think?" Kyle asked.

"I think it is in good order," the togishi said, and returned it hilt-first. "I think it is also the loneliest piece of steel I have handled in forty years, and I have handled funeral swords."

Kyle took it, sheathed it, and said nothing, because nothing was the right thing to say and he was still learning how to say it correctly.

The togishi went back to the cook's knife. Kyle paid for the bowl — 8Φ, which was two over the going rate, which was probably also the point — put on his coat, and walked out under the trees.

The petals came down slow and even in the lit air. A child ran past him at full career, screaming on the inhale, trailing a laugh behind her like a cord she hadn't noticed she was paying out.

[Beat 9]
He took the long way back.

The city reopened in the arithmetic: seven counters, one carrier, the parts man on Ashland who would have the cable waiting when he arrived in the morning. A third of the operational surface back in service, priced in apologies at list, plus the envelope in his coat covering the fifty-two.

Cost: a standing offer he would now have to keep refusing. A bowl he would come back for, which meant the stool again, which meant the offer standing when he arrived, which was the whole geometry of it. The Branch Manager had not built the courtesy as courtesy. He had built it as a room you kept returning to voluntarily, a door that stayed open because you opened it yourself.

His terminal chirped.

STANDING CONTRACT — STATUS NOTE.
CLIENT ATTEMPTED TO LOCATE THE RAMEN-YA ON THE ASHGRAVE CORRIDOR LEDGER.
CLIENT QUERIED: COUNTER, CEDAR. FEE STRUCTURE. VARIABLE OCCUPANCY.
CLIENT QUERIED: TOGISHI, LICENSED. NARROWS DISTRICT. ONE-TIME SERVICE.
BOTH QUERIES RETURNED: NOT ON LEDGER.
CLIENT IS UNSETTLED. ENJOY THE NOODLES.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT PRICED THE BOWL AT 8Φ.
ADDENDUM: STANDARD BOWL, THIS DISTRICT = 6Φ.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT NOTES THE DELTA.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT FINDS THE DELTA CORRECT.

Kyle read it twice on the walkway under the last of the trees, petals on the screen and the Pulse humming through the tier below him.

He put the terminal away.

Three blocks out, at the Spine crossing, a woman in a gray work coat spoke low to the man beside her — two syllables, the shape of a name, gone before he could catch it. The man said: "Assessment pending. Nothing moves until Mira signs off."

Kyle walked past without slowing, because slowing was a tell and tells were expensive, and the price of tonight had been high enough already.

The freeze was lifted. The audition still owed itself.

He went home under the lights.
