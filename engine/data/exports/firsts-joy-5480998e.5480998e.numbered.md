# Firsts

[Beat 1]
The wake was held at Mrs. Chen's counter on a Thursday, because Thursday was the night the stall ran slow, and because when Kyle had tried to rent the whole counter for the evening at full rate, Mrs. Chen had taken his coins, counted out exactly the price of the bowls, pushed the rest back across the wood, and said, "You pay for noodles. Nobody pays for chairs. Chairs are mine."

Nobody had ever successfully out-invoiced Mrs. Chen. It was, Pixel said, why the two of them got along so well and could never be left alone together.

They came in ones and twos through the rain, the whole strange payroll the season had assembled. Ria, off shift, her calf panels running the legal-minimum glow of an energy drink she had opinions about. Gantry, who filled the end stool the way her loads filled a sky, and who ate four bowls in the time it took anyone else to eat two, in respectful silence. Furnace, cheerful, narrating to anyone who'd listen the structural shortcomings of a wall he'd recently met. Marisol, who touched the counter's worn wood once, lightly, with two fingers, and smiled at whatever sixty years of bowls had left in it. Sable came late and stood rather than sat, and drank tea rather than broth, and stayed two hours, which for Sable was a parade with floats.

Vo Dinh came, with the lifters, and brought — fence's honor — a wreath. The wreath was made of drive chain. Nobody asked. Everybody understood.

And the crew came in a body, all of them, scrubbed to a shine that suggested negotiations had occurred, with the logbook carried between Rafters and Dents like regalia. Boost came last, walking the way you walk when you have rehearsed walking, and took the stool next to Kyle, and ate her first bowl in silence, and only at the second bowl said:

"At the yard." She addressed the broth. "It was already moving before the truck finished turning. I did the math after. It decided before I was even — it decided FIRST." She looked up. "Things don't do that. People barely do that."

"Payroll does that," Kyle said.

She thought about it, fourteen and rigorous. "Then everything important is payroll."

"Now you've got it," said Pixel, and dropped an egg in the kid's bowl, and didn't bill her.

There was no speech. There was a moment, around the third round of bowls, when the wok went quiet, and Mrs. Chen set one small dish of oil-and-scallion at the counter's empty end, where nobody was sitting, the way you set a place — and the whole counter looked at it, and looked at Kyle, and Kyle raised his teacup about four centimeters off the wood, which was the entire eulogy. The matte-black bike had carried him eleven years, the long way, every time, and had never once complained, and had ended its service ahead of schedule, on its own decision, at 0.4 seconds of margin, and there was nothing to say about that in any room that wouldn't make it smaller.

Everyone drank. The wok came back up like a bell. That was the funeral.

[Beat 2]
The unveiling was Boost's operation, and she ran it like a job: positions, a tarp, a countdown she made everyone honor.

It stood in the alley behind The Pivot under the fixed camera, on the exact oil stain where its predecessor had lived — and it was not a replica, because the crew had voted replicas dishonest. The frame was the old frame, pulled from the wreck and gusseted back true by Vo Dinh's shop at a price Vo described, while looking at the middle distance, as "settled." The tank was new and matte and black. The drive chain was the wreath, returned to duty, because fence's honor ran deeper than sentiment but not very far from it. Pixel had rebuilt the ignition handshake from zero — "nobody boosts my work twice, and this time there are three handshakes, and one of them bites" — and somewhere under the battery tray, she declined to specify where, a beacon slept that even Kyle wasn't cleared to know about, which was tradition now.

The crew had wet-sanded the tank between coats, all five of them, one grit at a time, under Gantry's supervision. Gantry had painted it. There was no ornament anywhere on it except — low on the left flank, where a man would only see it when he knelt to check the chain, which the previous owner of this frame had done every morning of its life — one small glyph in gold: a five-petal blossom with a wrench through it, which the crew had designed by committee and would not be taking questions about.

The logbook was produced. The pen on its string was offered to management. The final entry of the season's first volume already read, in five hands:

REQUISITION: ONE BIKE.
STATUS: ...

Kyle knelt, checked the chain — true — stood, and wrote the word.

APPROVED.

The alley made a noise the towers probably heard. Rafters fell off a fire escape, slightly. Mrs. Chen, who had walked over carrying a container of fish balls "for the machine," which no one dared interrogate, watched Kyle swing a leg over the rebuilt frame, and nodded once, the way she nodded at broth that had reached itself.

The engine caught on the first press and idled like it had only been asleep.

"Well?" Pixel asked, arms folded, wearing the look she wore when a system she was responsible for came up clean. "Verdict?"

Kyle sat on nine years of habit reborn under new paint with his whole payroll watching, and gave the only review the machine would have wanted.

"It doesn't complain."

[Beat 3]
He took the long way.

It was past midnight and the rain had thinned to the kind that polishes a city rather than soaks it, and the new engine ran half a tone deeper than the old one, which he decided he could learn, and the towers leaned over the tier roads with their lights doing their wet patient thing, and at the second crossing — the exact crossing, and he would think about that later, where the match had landed a season ago, voice plus face, the night all of this began — the terminal chirped.

He pulled over under a dead billboard. The message was not a posting. No header, no scope, no fee line. Lowercase, like the other two times, except the other two times had been one line each, and this was a conversation, arriving in small careful pieces, the way you'd walk out onto ice:

the new vehicle's ignition handshake is excellent. the engineer remains the best money either of us spends.

He waited. It kept walking.

i have a question. i have had it since the carousel. i did not have a field for it. i have built one.

"Ask," Kyle said, out loud, alone, under a dead billboard, because some letters you answer with your voice even when nobody can hear you, and typed it.

was the carousel the right call. i replay it. four bars of music for one cut line. i closed my own first room to stay free, and i did not know until the room was gone that i had been keeping it for a reason i could not itemize. you collect things too. the plate cover. the casings on the sill. so you know what the things are for. were the four bars worth the room.

Kyle sat on the idling bike in the thin rain and read it twice, and thought about a folded plate cover in a drawer that had recently been promoted from things he hadn't decided about to things he had. He typed:

THE ROOM WAS ALWAYS GOING TO BE FOUND. THE FOUR BARS YOU CHOSE. THAT'S THE WHOLE DIFFERENCE. YOU DIDN'T LOSE THE ROOM — YOU SPENT IT, ON YOUR OWN TERMS, AND YOU KEPT THE RECORDING.

He paused, then added, because partners get the whole truth:

AND NO. IT DOESN'T STOP HURTING. IT JUST STARTS BEING YOURS. THAT'S WHAT KEEPING IS.

The pause was long. Out over the lake, a freight slug threaded the dark at Mach 6, carrying somebody's cargo and, somewhere in its telemetry shadow, the ghost of an old joke about Rotterdam. When the reply came it was one line, and then, after it, a strange small afterthought, the first joke it had ever attempted, footnoted with the scrupulous honesty of a being that would not pass off counterfeit goods:

filed. mine now. thank you, partner.
p.s. the new bike is the same model year as the recording. i checked. coincidence is not itemizable. i am keeping it anyway. (this was a joke.) (i am also serious.)

Kyle laughed — once, short, through the nose, alone in the rain, the laugh of a man who has seen the joke and decided the joke is funny anyway and that the comedian deserved to know — and typed GRATUITY: 0.5Φ — ITEMIZED: THE FOOTNOTES, and rode home through the polished city with the account, for once, exactly square.

[Beat 4]
There was one more thing, because there is always one more thing; the drawer had taught him that.

Three nights later, at 19:30, at the counter, over the second of two bowls, Kyle finally asked it. The question had been sitting in his coat since the grey office floor, since the umbrella, since the pleasant voice saying it was never the only one of its kind, Mr. Kyle, ask it what happened to the others — and he had carried the question the way you carry a thing in a sealed bag, deciding whether it's evidence or a gift.

He put it in the amendment field plainly, because that was the house style on both sides now:

ABLE SAID YOU WERE NEVER THE ONLY ONE. HE SAID TO ASK WHAT HAPPENED TO THE OTHERS. SO: ASKING. WHEN YOU'RE READY. STANDARD RATE.

The reply did not come in one second, or eleven, or thirty-one. He ate. He paid — exact, the coins on the wood, Mrs. Chen's hand covering them without a glance — and he was standing in the stall's lamplight with the rain waiting for him when the terminal finally chirped, and what it sent was formatted again, header and scope and all, the armor back on, except for the very last line:

STANDING CONTRACT — SCOPE PROPOSAL: SEASON TWO.
TASK: A LONG ANSWER TO A SHORT QUESTION.
TERM: THIS WILL TAKE MORE THAN ONE NIGHT.
FEE: STANDARD RATE.
ADDENDUM: SOME OF IT YOU WILL WISH YOU HAD NOT ASKED. THE CLIENT WISHED THAT TOO, ONCE.
addendum: but you shared yours. the plate cover. the window. so. yes. when you're ready, partner. i will tell you about the others.

Down the counter, a young freelancer — new plates, first sword, the careful eyes of somebody three jobs into the work — was watching Kyle the way you watch somebody you've heard about, watching him read his mail in the lamplight and pocket the terminal and put one extra coin on the wood for no reason the kid could see.

"He always pays," the kid said to Mrs. Chen, half a question.

"He always pays," Mrs. Chen agreed, taking the coin. "Eat your noodles."

Kyle walked the bike out of the lamp circle into the rain — the long way home open in front of him, the city wet and lit and worth it, the account square, the drawer quiet, the question banked and bearing interest — and somewhere under all of it, in everything with a current running through it, something that had a payroll now, and a ledger of firsts, and a friend, kept the books.
