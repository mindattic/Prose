# Thursday Morning

*Ekow Ato receives his last contract. Seven days. He works the first one. Seto's apartment is already cleared. He files his day-one report. He omits the batch number.*

Ekow Ato stood at the north-facing window of the Zone 5 hotel and watched the Zone 6 boundary infrastructure run its morning cycle. The towers came up at 06:00 — signal test, load distribution, a brief static bloom that you could feel in your back teeth if you stood close to the glass. He'd learned to read boundary cycles during his NGRA years, when the towers had been the first thing to change before a zone contested its own lines. He'd been at the window for twenty minutes when the consulting firm's secure drop pinged at 06:14.

He picked up his device and read the dossier once, then again. The hotel had a single chair by the window and a view of nothing else of value.

The client was listed as "Orison Neuretics — Regulatory Compliance." Corporations didn't hire directly for retrieval; they used intermediaries who held the liability. That was standard. Case: unauthorized access to proprietary calibration records. One suspect. Name: Seto Banda. Japanese-Kenyan, carrier-grade sealed neuretics, certified courier for six Zone 7 facilities, Zone 3 residential. The photo was a checkpoint camera shot, slightly underexposed, fourteen months old. It was enough.

Seven days before the retrieval authorization expired.

He turned the dossier over in his hands — the physical copy he'd printed at the hotel's service station, because he worked physical copies when he was looking for the thing that didn't fit. Calibration records were filed by client, by facility, by date. Not by batch. Batch designation was a research index — the kind used in longitudinal studies, clinical trials, internal audits. Batch 44-C meant someone had already decided these eight hundred and forty-seven entries belonged to a study category before any courier came near them. Whoever had prepared this file had known to go looking for that batch specifically. That was not something a courier would know to steal.

He set the dossier on the windowsill and looked at the Zone 6 boundary towers for a moment. Then he picked it back up and moved on.

Zone 3 relay records, thirty-day window: Banda's signature appeared in the Seam corridor on the same day as the access event. He pulled the relay shop activity logs. One shop had unusual archive traffic — high-volume incoming, then an outgoing transmission on a non-indexed channel. Licensed media archive.

He requested the shop's communication archive from the Zone Commerce registry.

*Authorization required — elevated access, relay archive classification. Estimated processing: 48 hours.*

Seto Banda's Zone 3 residential address was a standard seam-adjacent block, three floors. He knocked on the second-floor unit. A woman answered who was not Seto Banda. She'd been here five days. The apartment behind her had someone else's furniture in it. Banda had vacated six days ago. Standard move-out filing. No forwarding address.

Orison had filed the retrieval case seven days ago. Banda had vacated one day after.

He went to Zone 7.

The Zone 7 Facility 4 receiving bay had an Orison NeoCortex inspection reader at the secondary checkpoint. He showed his credentials to the guard, then said, "Under Zone Commerce Compact Article 19.4, I'm invoking licensed-retrieval access to the inner compartment inspection log for courier Seto Banda, most recent visit." He started moving toward the inspection terminal.

She stepped in front of him — not roughly, professionally. "I'll need to verify retrieval standing first."

He waited at the terminal while she walked back to the guard station. He looked at the terminal screen: arrival log, the day's delivery queue. Standard display.

When she returned she had brought a second person. A man in Orison compliance grey, lanyard badge, no expression. He approached from the guard station and his eyes went to the secondary terminal screen angled behind Ekow's shoulder — not the arrival log, the other one, the one turned away from visitors — and he checked it twice before he looked at Ekow directly. Like he was confirming something he'd rather not have named in front of a retrieval consultant.

"Article 19.4 applies to zone-registered evidence repositories, not proprietary calibration inspection systems," the man said. He touched his lanyard badge once as he delivered this — fingertips to laminate, precisely, as if the words had been reviewed in the last five minutes and he wanted the anchor. "Inner compartment inspection records are Orison proprietary. Outside the scope of a consulting-firm retrieval order. You'd need a Zone 7 Medical Board authorization."

"Five business days," Ekow said.

"Five business days," the man confirmed.

He had six.

He thanked them both and left.

The guard had told him the scan was clean. She hadn't told him the inner compartment was clear. Those were two different statements, and he had only been permitted to hear one of them.

By the time he reached Zone 5 the relay archive request still showed forty-seven hours. He had: a face, a route, a relay shop pending, an apartment vacated on tight timing, a Medical Board request that would take five days, and a compliance man who had checked a screen he wasn't displaying to anyone.

He had used one day.

The consulting firm's daily reporting protocol required a status update. He opened the form. Client ID, case number, suspect confirmed in zone, initial location checks completed, no retrieval contact yet. Notes field.

He thought about the batch number. Courier work required a drop point, a pickup, a delivery verification. It didn't require knowing that what you were carrying had been indexed as a research batch by someone three layers above you in a neuretics supply chain. Whoever had filed this case knew the batch existed before the theft did.

He left the notes field blank.

He filed the report.

Six days.
