# Thursday Morning

*Ekow Ato receives his last contract. Seven days. He works the first one. Seto's apartment is already cleared. He files his day-one report. He omits the batch number.*

The hotel in Zone 5 charged by the day and asked no questions about guests who used the service entrance. Ekow had been here before — the room had the same desk lamp with the same slight lean, the same window that looked north toward the Zone 6 boundary. The boundary infrastructure was always worth looking at. It told you which way the data was moving.

The dossier came through the consulting firm's secure drop at 06:14. He read it straight through without stopping, which was NGRA habit — exhaust the material before you form an opinion, because the thing that doesn't fit appears last.

The thing that didn't fit appeared fourth paragraph in. *Batch designation 44-C. Eight hundred and forty-seven patient entries.* Calibration records were filed by client, by facility, by date. Filing them by batch was a research designation. A courier wouldn't know to call them that. Whoever had written this brief had pulled from a research index, not a standard compliance database. He read the rest of the case and came back to it.

One suspect: Seto Banda, Zone 3 residential. Carrier-grade sealed neuretics. Certified courier for six Zone 7 facilities. Checkpoint photograph, fourteen months old, slightly underexposed. Client: Orison Neuretics, Regulatory Compliance, through the consulting firm's standard intermediary arrangement. Seven days before retrieval authorization expired.

He made himself a coffee from the room service unit and stood at the window with it. The Zone 6 boundary infrastructure did what boundary infrastructure did: registered, logged, forwarded. Beyond it: Zone 6 facilities, community clinics, the kind of block where a nutritionist or a maintenance tech kept fixed hours because people were expecting them. He drank the coffee and watched the boundary for a while.

Then he went to Zone 3.

---

The building was a standard Seam-adjacent block, three floors. He knocked on the door in the zone registry. A woman answered who was not Seto Banda — new furniture, new face, five days of occupancy. Banda had vacated six days prior. Standard move-out filing. No forwarding address.

He checked the date against the case timeline. Orison had formally filed the retrieval request seven days ago. Banda had moved out one day after that.

He stood in the corridor for a moment. The building had no CCTV on this floor — he had noted that on the way up, the same way he noted exit points and line-of-sight before he knew whether he'd need them. Old wiring. NGRA infrastructure in the forward operating bases had looked exactly like this, before someone who knew better pulled it.

Six days ago, the man had been one step ahead. The question was whether that was coincidence or whether Banda had a source inside the filing process.

He went to Zone 7.

---

Facility 4 had an Orison NeoCortex inspection reader at the secondary checkpoint. He showed his credentials to the guard and waited while she pulled Banda's visit record. When she looked up, her jaw was tight.

"He's been through," she said. "Last visit six days ago. He passed secondary inspection."

"The inner compartment — what did it contain?"

She looked at him. Not hostile. Careful. "The scan was clean. I logged the inspection complete."

"I'd like to see the inner compartment inspection log."

She asked him to wait. She was gone four minutes. When she came back she had a second person with her — a man in Orison compliance grey, lanyard badge, a face that had decided something before he walked into the room.

"Inner compartment inspection records are Orison proprietary," the compliance man said. "Not available under a consulting-firm retrieval order. Zone 7 Medical Board authorization required." He said it without particular emphasis, the way you state a regulation you know will hold. "Processing runs five business days."

Ekow had six days remaining. "I'll submit the request," he said.

He thanked them both and left.

Outside the facility he stood in the Zone 7 corridor for a moment. *The scan was clean* and *the inner compartment was clear* were two different sentences. He had only been allowed to hear one of them. The compliance man had arrived before the guard could say anything further, which meant the compliance man had been monitoring the checkpoint desk — or had been told to monitor it. The facility had been expecting someone to ask about the inner compartment.

The relay shop in Zone 3 had an outstanding archive authorization request — 48 hours processing. He couldn't reach the outgoing transmission until that cleared.

He had: a face, a route, an apartment vacated on suspicious timing, an inner compartment record blocked behind a five-day authorization, and a checkpoint that had been pre-covered. He had used one day.

---

He rode back to Zone 5 on the elevated line and watched the zone boundaries go past — the infrastructure transitions, the air quality shifts, the way the light changed when it had been filtered by different systems at different budgets. The batch number was still the wrong kind of classification. That was a small fact. Small facts accumulated.

The consulting firm's reporting protocol required a day-one status update. He opened the form on his device: client ID, case number, suspect confirmed in zone, initial location checks, no retrieval contact yet.

The form had a notes field.

A courier who had been retained to carry batch-designated medical data would need to have known what batch designation meant. The dossier was written by someone who had pulled from a research index. The inner compartment record was blocked by a compliance officer who had been waiting at the checkpoint. These were three separate things. He was not supposed to connect them in a day-one report — he was supposed to confirm the suspect's presence and close the field.

He looked at the notes field for a moment.

He looked north out the window at the Zone 6 boundary.

He closed the field blank and submitted the report.

Six days.
