# Read the Room

_A mind-reading girl and a noise-rock singer survive a post-show robbery in a Milwaukee dive club — she reads the room, literally; he goes back for his people; the weapon ladder ends with a shotgun and a conversation on the floor._

[Beat 1]
The last chord was still hanging in the air out front when Ethan Wolfe pushed through the green room door and let it swing shut behind him. The wall took the crowd and folded it down to a low roar, the kind you felt in the concrete more than heard. Reinforced walls did that. The Velvet Tine had been built to hold worse than a Tuesday-night noise-rock set, and the room around him — a box of gray concrete, a chain lamp swinging on its own draft, cables taped down in fat black rivers along the floor — held the sound the way a fist holds water. Not well. Enough.

He stood there a moment with his hands loose at his sides and let the shaking finish. That was the part nobody warned you about. Not the nerves before. The after. Eighty minutes of giving the room everything, and then a door, and then this — the body still running the engine with no road left to put it on. His shirt was soaked through the back. His pulse ticked in his throat like it had somewhere to be.

The couch against the far wall had held a lot of bad decisions over the years. It looked like it. He didn't sit. If he sat he'd stop, and if he stopped he'd feel every one of his thirty-six years land at once.

Rill came in behind him without a word, which was how Rill did most things. He crossed to the corner where his kit gear waited, crouched, and began coiling a cable around his forearm in slow, even loops. The dampeners in his wrists caught the lamp light — two faint seams under the skin. He'd played for eighty minutes without once looking up, and offstage he had the manner of a man politely waiting for a train. Present when it mattered. Gone otherwise. Ethan had stopped trying to pull him out of it years ago. There was nothing to pull out. Rill was just quiet all the way down.

"Good room," Ethan said, because someone should.

Rill tipped his head. That was the answer.

The door opened again and Mona came through backward, dragging the mic stands two at a time, her boot hooking the door to catch it before it slammed. She had a coil of gaffer's tape hanging off one wrist and a stylus tucked behind her ear and the particular walk she got on a work night, where every step was already spending itself on the next thing.

"Front of house says the low end was mud for the first three songs," she said, propping the stands against the wall. "I told him it's a two-hundred-year-old building with a PA held together by hope. He can send me a formal complaint. Anyone touched the rider yet?"

"No," Ethan said.

"Good. Don't let Dayo's people at the water before I count it." She crouched by the equipment table and started laying the night's takings into the case in flat, even stacks — a fan of QUANTA chits, the club's cut already skimmed, the numbers running behind her eyes. The light-strip under the skin of her forearm caught the movement and pulsed once, twice — a soft amber run chasing up toward her wrist, the last of the bass still living in it. Then her hands went still over the case and the strip went flat white, edge to edge, like a held breath.

Ethan watched it go pale. He always watched. He'd told himself once it was a bassist's habit, keeping an eye on the light. It wasn't a bassist's habit.

She looked up and caught him at it.

Neither of them said anything. That was the arrangement, though it had never been spoken out loud, which was how he knew it was real. Twice. Twice in four years, and both times the morning after they'd gotten up and made the band the wall between them, brick by brick, because the band was the thing that survived and they were not going to be the thing that broke it. He'd written a hundred lines about that wall. He'd never gotten the last one right.

"You're vibrating," Mona said. Not unkind. An observation, filed.

"I'm coming down."

"Sit down before you come down. You're making the lamp nervous." She went back to the count.

He didn't sit. He crossed to the little sink in the corner instead, ran the tap till the rust cleared, and put his wet hands under it. Cold. Real. The roar through the wall shifted as the between-sets track came up out front — the club had its own playlist, tuned to keep a crowd from leaving. It worked. He could feel the floor out there staying full.

His neuretics pulsed a soft ping behind his ear — the venue's settlement handshake, the club offering the night's numbers to Mona's mesh. She'd take it. She took all of it. He let it pass through him and go where it was going.

Rill finished his cable, set it in the case, and lowered himself onto the arm of the couch with the stillness of something switched to standby. Mona clicked the takings case shut and sat back on her heels, and for a moment the three of them were just there in the concrete box, the lamp swinging its slow arc, the crowd breathing through the wall.

This was the part Ethan liked. Not the stage. He'd have said the stage if you asked him, because that was the thing people wanted him to love, and he did love it the way you love a fire you're standing inside. But this — the after, the small rusted room, the two people who'd stood inside the fire with him and come out the other side — this was the thing he'd have kept if you made him choose. The show was the offering. This was the part where you found out you'd survived making it.

"How long till Dayo shows?" he asked.

Mona checked the count against nothing, an old habit. "Soon. He said soon." She stood, brushed her knees, and the light-strip stayed flat white on her still arm. "You should eat something before he gets here. You always spend it all up there and then wonder why you're shaking."

"I'm not shaking."

"You're shaking, Ethan."

He looked at his hands under the cold water. They were, a little.

"Yeah," he said. "Good show."

[Beat 2]
The door didn't so much open as get shouldered aside, and the roar came in ahead of anyone — that fat wall of between-sets sound the club pumped to keep the floor from thinning — and then a cold-box the size of a bar fridge slid across the threshold, matte and dew-beaded, and behind it came Dayo Ferreira, one hand on the handle, the other already lifting in a small flat wave that took in the whole room and asked nothing of it.

"Wolfe," he said. Then, to Mona, a half-nod. Then nothing. That was most of Dayo's talking done. The goods did the rest.

He was a slim column of expensive black — a jacket that hung the way only real weight hangs, cut close, catching the chain lamp along one shoulder seam and giving none of it back. Cedar-brown skin, a low fade going gray at the edges, forty-odd years worn like they'd cost him nothing. He set the cold-box down against the wall by the couch, crouched, and thumbed the seal. A breath of white vapor rolled out over his wrist and pooled on the concrete and died there.

"You're late," Mona said, not looking up from the takings case.

"I'm here."

"That's not the same thing."

"For you it is." He said it without any edge, laying the words down the way he laid everything down — flat, exact, take it or don't. He'd supplied the band four years. He and Mona had this the way two people who trust each other's arithmetic have it.

Two more came in behind him, and the room's weather changed.

The first was all noise and motion — tall, Nigerian, twenty-seven and already three sentences into a story nobody had asked for, a laugh riding under every word of it.

"—so I told him, I said, if the strut's condemned then the *bar built onto the strut* is condemned, and he looks at me like I invented gravity to spite him personally—" She swept in, clocked the couch, clocked Rill on its arm, and dropped into the cushions beside him with the ease of a woman who'd never once wondered if she was welcome. "Hi. Nkechi. You played tonight? You look like you played tonight. You've got the look."

Rill tipped his head a half-inch. Present when it mattered. She took it as a full conversation and kept going.

Ethan wasn't watching her. He was watching the second one.

She'd come in last and quiet, and she'd stopped just inside the door — small, maybe a head shorter than Nkechi, compact in the way that read as *built close* rather than *slight*. Blonde bob cut, a little damp at the temples from the walk. She was wearing a jacket she'd had for a long time — not circuit, just kept — and her hands, which Ethan clocked before faces, were the hands of someone who worked with them. Not soft. Strong in the knuckles, clean under the nails, a faint flour-whiteness at the inside of one wrist. Scandinavian, if he had to guess, and he was usually right about faces. Bright-eyed. Open-faced in a way that Ethan had learned, over a life spent reading rooms from a lit stage, to be careful of — the open face was often the one running the most behind it.

She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at *everything*. Her eyes went over the room in an easy sweep — the taped rivers of cable, the swinging lamp, the flat-white light-strip on Mona's still forearm, Rill going soft into standby, the cold-box, Dayo's hands, Ethan last — and she wasn't cataloguing exits or valuables the way a certain kind of visitor did in a room in The Low. She was just *taking it in*, the whole warm box of it, and whatever she found there made the corner of her mouth pull.

Almost a smile. She didn't explain it. She looked like someone who'd walked into a party she already half-remembered.

“She was at the barrier,” Dayo said. For Dayo, that was the whole story.

He’d found her at the floor barrier before the set thinned—standing still in the middle of motion, lit up with the music the specific way that wasn’t performance, wasn’t fandom, just someone who had really heard it—and he’d made his offer after the final number. She’d said yes. That was always the whole story with Dayo.

Ethan had done this himself, in smaller rooms. He understood the shape of it. He looked at her. She was already looking at him, and the corner of her mouth pulled.

"Faith," Ethan said.

"Ethan." Her voice came quick and dry, no runway to it. "I know. Mona sent Dayo the settlement handshake and it had the set list on it and I read the set list on the way over." She crossed to the equipment table, not the couch, and hitched a hip against the edge of it, near where Mona knelt. "You closed on the slow one. The one where the low end drops out for four bars and then comes back in like it's angry about something."

Ethan's hands were still damp from the sink. He dried them on his thighs.

"That's the one," he said.

"It's a good four bars." She said it plainly, a fact filed, and then her eyes went to Mona's forearm again — the strip, flat white, edge to edge, dead still. "That does that when you're not playing?"

"When I'm still," Mona said.

"Huh." Faith looked at it a second longer than a stranger would. "That's honest of it."

Mona glanced up. Something passed behind her face and got filed and didn't come out. She went back to the count.

Dayo had the cold-box open now, and he began setting the night's offering out along the table's clean edge in a short, unhurried line — the way a man sets out tools he knows the names of. He didn't announce them. He didn't have to. Ethan watched the small shapes come up out of the vapor and catch the lamp: a sleeve of something thin and translucent, a row of little ceramic thimbles standing on their ends, a strip of soft gold tabs backed on paper. The cold rolled off the box and touched the backs of Ethan's hands and felt, after eighty minutes inside the fire, like the first honest thing the night had offered him.

"Same as always," Mona said, without looking. "I count first, nobody touches the water, and if anyone's driving The Pulse home they say so now."

"Nobody's driving anything," Nkechi called from the couch. "The strut's condemned. We're all going to *die here*, together, warmly."

"Not in my green room you're not," Mona said.

Rill made a sound that in another man would have been a laugh.

Ethan stood at the edge of it and let the warmth come up into the room the way it did on the good nights — the cold-box breathing, Nkechi's story looping back on itself, Dayo silent behind his line of small exact things, Mona counting, Rill switched to standby, the crowd pushing its low pulse through two hundred years of concrete. And the new one, Faith, hipped against his equipment table like she'd been coming here for years, watching all of it with that pulled-down corner of her mouth, like the room had told her something and she was deciding whether to let on that she'd heard.

"So," she said, to Ethan, tipping her chin at Dayo's line of goods. "What's good?"

[Beat 3]
Dayo answered the way he answered most things, by moving. He drew a finger down the short line of goods on the table's clean edge, touching none of them, just naming them with the motion.

"Glass," he said. The sleeve of thin translucent wafers, each one the size of a thumbnail and about as thick as a fingernail. "Fade." The row of little ceramic thimbles, standing on their ends like a set of small chess pieces. "Candor." The strip of soft gold tabs backed on paper, gold gone amber where the lamp caught them.

"That's the tour," Nkechi said from the couch. "He's a poet."

Faith reached over and turned one of the gold tabs on its paper with a fingertip, not lifting it yet. "What's Candor do?"

"Turns the volume up," Dayo said. "On the people. You'll feel the room."

"I already feel the room."

"You'll feel it more."

She looked at him a second, then at the tab, then peeled it off the paper and set it under her tongue without any ceremony about it. Ethan watched her do it. She caught him watching and didn't mind, the way she hadn't minded anything since she came in.

"Sublingual," she said, around it. "Gold. Tastes like nothing. That's a lie, it tastes like a battery, but you're supposed to say nothing." She closed her mouth on it and hitched a little higher against the table edge and waited, easy, like a woman who'd taken a seat that came with a view.

Rill was up off the couch arm. He crossed to the table without a word and drew a single Glass wafer out of the sleeve, held it flat between two fingers a moment to be sure of it, then pressed it to the inside of his own wrist, over the dampener seam. He tipped his head back against the wall and shut his eyes. Ninety seconds, and it would go in through the skin and the night would arrange itself for him into a clean order, every choice the only choice there was. On a party night Rill went inward and the drums came out. Ethan had never once seen the two things happen apart.

"Ninety seconds," Nkechi announced, to nobody. "He's timing himself. Look at him. So *responsible*."

"Leave him," Mona said. She hadn't gotten up. She was still on her heels by the takings case, the count squared, the case shut, and she'd taken nothing off the table and wasn't going to. Work night. Ethan knew the shape of her on a work night the way you know a song you've played a thousand times — she'd be the last clear head in the room and she'd like it that way, or if not like it, keep it, which for Mona was the same thing. Her light-strip lay flat white along her still forearm, edge to edge, dead even in the lamp light.

Ethan picked up one of the ceramic thimbles. Fade. It sat cold in his palm from the box, dew coming up on it already in the warm room. He turned it over once. Eighty minutes he'd been up there giving the floor every last thing he had, and the engine was still running with no road under it, and Fade would take the after and dissolve the seam between what was happening and what he could make of it. Music on Fade went from sound to story. He'd be *inside* the between-sets track instead of hearing it through two hundred years of concrete.

"You closing another set tonight?" Dayo asked. A real question, for Dayo. The supplier's question. Don't wreck the man before he has to work.

"No," Ethan said. "We're done up there."

"Then." Dayo tipped his chin at the thimble. *Then it's yours.*

Ethan cracked the capsule against the table edge, the small ceramic snap of it, and drew the breath in slow. It went down cool and floral and faintly like ozone, and for a moment nothing, and then the roar through the wall stopped being a roar through a wall and started being weather — a thing with fronts and edges and a direction it wanted to go. He set the spent thimble down carefully, both halves, and found he cared about setting it down neatly the way you care about small things when the large ones have started to open.

"There he goes," Nkechi said. "Everybody's going somewhere but me and Mona."

"Someone's got to stay and drive," Mona said.

"There's nothing to drive. The strut's condemned."

"Then someone's got to stay and *watch us die warmly.*"

Rill made the sound that in another man was a laugh, eyes still shut, the Glass in him now, the room ordering itself.

Faith had come off the table. Ethan didn't see her decide to; she was just closer, hipped against the equipment case near him now instead of across the room, her face open and turned up at him, and the Candor was in her — he could see it land, a small settling in her shoulders, like a woman lowering herself into water that turned out to be the right temperature.

"You write that four-bar drop, or is it a happy accident?" she asked.

"I wrote it."

"On purpose?"

"You can't do it on accident. The bass has to know it's leaving."

She looked at him like he'd said something worth keeping. "The bass has to know it's leaving," she repeated. "That's the whole song, isn't it. Everything drops out and you find out if it comes back angry or not at all."

"That's one song," Ethan said. "I've got the one where it doesn't come back."

"Course you do." She laughed, quick and low, and it was easy, the whole thing was easy, the way it never was with someone he'd met an hour ago. He'd spent his life reading rooms from a lit stage, learning which open face was running the most behind it, and hers was running plenty and none of it aimed at him, and that was the part that undid the guard — she wasn't working him. She was just *here*, all the way here, in a way most people never managed.

Across the room Mona looked up from the shut case. She clocked the two of them, the small distance closed, the easy back-and-forth of it. Her face gave nothing. She filed it, whatever it was, the way she filed the low end being mud for three songs, and went back to squaring a stack of chits that were already square.

The lamp swung its slow arc. The cold-box breathed. This was what the good nights felt like — the box of warm concrete, the people who'd stood in the fire, the drugs laid out in a neat line and going gently to work, the crowd's low pulse coming through the wall like a second heart. Ethan turned back to Faith with the Fade opening the room into something he could almost walk around inside, and for a moment he'd have kept all of it, exactly this, if you'd made him choose.

"So," Faith said. "Tell me about the one where it doesn't come back."

[Beat 4]
Faith was still laughing at *I've got the one where it doesn't come back* — the low easy laugh that came up out of her without her deciding on it — when the room went strange.

It started small. A word that wasn't hers, arriving in the middle of her own thought, the way a stranger's voice sometimes surfaces under a song you know by heart. She almost didn't catch it. She was looking at Ethan's mouth, at the dry pull at the corner of it, waiting for him to tell her about the song where the bass leaves and stays gone, and underneath all that warm easy attention a thin clear line ran across the room and landed behind her eyes.

*—how many in the room, four maybe five—*

She blinked. Kept the smile on, because the smile was already there and it was easier to leave it than take it down. Candor, she thought. Strong batch. Dayo didn't cut anything but he didn't measure it either, and here it was doing exactly what he'd said — *you'll feel the room* — except the room she was feeling had a strange edge to it, a cold seam, like a draft coming under a door that was supposed to be shut. That was all. Chemicals. The gold tab under her tongue turning the volume up on the people, and this was just what up-too-far sounded like: everyone's low hum arriving as words.

She reached for the thread of what Ethan was saying and found it. "The one where it doesn't come back," she said. "Tell me."

"It's not finished," he said. "It hasn't been finished for four years."

"That's not a song, that's a haunting."

"Same overhead." He almost smiled again, and she felt the warmth come back up under the cold seam, the good thing, the real thing, the two of them in the small lit box of the room —

*—door's reinforced, try the side window catch, second one from the—*

It came in sharper this time. Not a hum. A voice with a shape to it, a plan folded inside it, and it wasn't anyone's she could see. Not Ethan, not Mona counting nothing on her heels, not Rill gone soft against the wall with the Glass ordering his night, not Nkechi mid-story, not Dayo silent behind his line of small exact things. She did the thing you do — she looked around the room to find the mouth it had come out of — and every mouth in the room was either shut or busy with something else, and the words had been about *this room*, about the door being reinforced, which it was, she'd heard Ethan say so, a building built to hold worse than a Tuesday.

Her light drink of a heartbeat picked up. She set it down again, hard. Candor. That was all. Candor amplified empathy circuits — she'd read that on the strip, she knew what it was — and empathy was just guessing well, and she'd always guessed well, all her life, *too perceptive*, her mother used to say it like an accusation, and this was that, turned up, the drug taking her ordinary too-muchness and running it past the red line. Somebody outside had a window on their mind because everybody in The Low always had a window on their mind. It didn't mean anything.

"You've gone somewhere," Ethan said.

"I'm here." She wasn't. She was cataloguing the room now the way she hadn't when she came in — not taking it in warm, taking it in *count-the-exits*, and she hated the switch she felt happen behind her own face. The rusted door. The little sink. The equipment table with its takings case. No window. No — there. High on the far wall, painted shut a hundred times over, a small hinged pane the size of a serving tray. Faith-sized, maybe. Not Ethan-sized. Second one from the — there was only the one.

"Faith."

"Say the thing about the bass again," she said. "The bass has to know it's leaving. Say it, I want to—"

The third one didn't come like the others. The first two had been quick and businesslike, a man doing sums. This one came in low and flat and *patient*, and it had no plan in it, just a fact already decided, and it dropped the temperature of the whole warm room by ten degrees the instant it landed.

*—the bassist looks strong, don't give her a reason—*

And she was looking straight at Mona when it landed. At Mona's flat white light-strip, dead even along her still forearm, edge to edge, the honest little strip that told the truth about when she wasn't playing. *The bassist looks strong.* Somebody outside this reinforced room, on the other side of the side entrance, was looking at Mona too — not with their eyes, they couldn't, but they knew she was here and they knew she was strong and they were already deciding, in that flat patient voice, what it would take to make her not a problem.

*Don't give her a reason.*

The laugh died in Faith's throat mid-breath. Just stopped, the way a needle lifts off a record — she'd been smiling, she'd been all the way in the good warm night, and now she was standing very still with the last of the laugh caught somewhere under her ribs and nowhere to put it. Her mouth stayed a little open. The gold tab was gone to nothing under her tongue and the room had gone to weather, Dayo's cold-box breathing at the wall, the lamp swinging its slow arc, Nkechi's story looping, the crowd's low second heart coming through two hundred years of concrete — and under all of it, threaded through it, three voices that belonged to nobody in the room, laying out a door and a window and a bassist and a reason.

She could still tell herself it was the Candor. She wanted to. She held the wanting for one whole second, the way you hold a breath till it burns.

Then a fourth fragment came, tired and small — *feet hurt, new shoes, tell him to hurry* — from a different direction than the other three, from *past* the side wall, and she understood with a cold dropping certainty that she wasn't hearing one imagined man. She was hearing several real ones, arranged around the outside of this concrete box, and they had positions, and they had a plan, and one of them had bad shoes.

Ethan was watching her now, properly, the Fade making his attention slow and deep and a half-second behind itself. "Where'd you go," he said. "You laughed and then you—"

"Ethan." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Quick and dry and level, her voice, doing the thing it always did while the rest of her caught up. "Don't move fast. Don't look at the side door. I need you to keep your face exactly like it is."

He kept his face exactly like it was. That was the first thing about him she'd remember later — that when a woman he'd known an hour told him to hold his face still, he did it, no runway, no argument, the Fade turning it into something almost graceful.

"Okay," he said. Quiet. "Why."

She looked at the little painted-shut window high on the far wall. Faith-sized. And she made herself smile again, a real-looking one, for the benefit of anyone who wasn't in the room but somehow, impossibly, still watching.

"Because there are people outside," she said, "and I can hear them thinking."

[Beat 5]
The thought came off the wall.

*How many in the room, four maybe five.* Clean and sequential and wrong for the room—not a party thought, not a Fade thought, not the texture of someone enjoying themselves. A counting thought. An inventory.

She stood there with her glass and did not show it.

The Candor had the room bright and close. That was what it was supposed to do—empathy amplifier, gold sublingual, feel-the-room compound—but what it was doing to her was something else, something sharper than warmth, a frequency she’d never hit in twenty-four years of being *too perceptive* and *good at reading people* and all the other words people used for the thing she’d been too careful to name. The room wasn’t warm to her. The room was open. She could hear where the sentences came from. The specific texture of each person’s thinking, shaped like that person’s voice.

Four of those textures were wrong.

She let them resolve—the tired one near the side entrance, new shoes, *feet hurt, tell him to hurry*; the flat patient one moving slow along the far wall; the two in the seam carrying the procedural weight of something planned, something organized, a job being done on a schedule. She let them come through all the way, and the shape of it was not ambiguous. These were people doing a job, and the job was this room.

She needed to get one person out without warning the rest.

She looked at Ethan.

He was standing at the far end of the equipment table, cooling down the way performers cooled down after—not relaxed, still carrying the current, the room still running through his hands even though his hands were empty. He had a glass he wasn’t drinking, and he was watching the door and watching Mona and half-watching Faith, and the Fade had his surface thoughts up near the surface: clear, vivid, specific. Unusually specific. The boundary between experience and imagination gone slack the way Fade made it go, and the results were in his face if you knew how to look, and she knew exactly how to look.

He’d been thinking about her since she walked through the door.

Not in the circuit-appropriate way. Not generically. She was getting pictures, and the pictures were specific: the way she’d moved in the doorway, the fraction-too-long eye-contact when he’d said *that one*, the angle of her face when she’d said *that’s a good four bars*, the back of her hand when she’d reached past him at the table. The Fade had opened him up, was all—dissolved the membrane between what he thought and what he allowed himself to think—and she was receiving the whole unguarded output in high resolution, and she filed all of it under *information*, and she was going to use it.

She wasn’t going to feel bad about it right now. She was going to use the information to get him out of the room alive, and then she was going to feel bad about it later, from a position of being somewhere that wasn’t here.

She put her glass down.

She moved across the room in a line that ended at him. She came up on his left, below his sightline, and turned to face him when she was close enough that close was a fact and not a question—and she tilted her face up at the angle she’d read he’d been imagining, which was exactly the angle, and she felt the recognition land through him like a plucked string. She held it there. Let him have it. The proximity and the tipped-up face and the particular thing happening in the space between them.

His eyes went to her mouth first. Then up.

She held his gaze and let her own drop to his mouth—to the fangs. Genemod, the lateral incisors replaced, tapered and real, the kind of work that cost enough to look like it had always been there. She’d clocked them across the room; up close they were sharper than they looked, canine geometry in a human face, and she held her gaze there one beat past natural and watched the effect register in the pull of his shoulders, the half-breath he didn’t quite take.

“Come show me the bathroom,” she said. Low, just for him. “The actual one.”

The corner of his mouth pulled. The fangs caught the lamp for a quarter-second.

She had his wrist before she’d decided to take it—her fingers closing over the tendons on the inside of it, over the pulse that was still running fast from the stage—and she pulled, once, the way you pull someone toward a joke you can’t tell in front of the others.

“Bathroom,” she said, bright, for the room. “Show me where it is, this place is a maze.”

It was not a maze. It was one concrete box with a rusted door in the corner that could only go one place. But she said it easy and she said it loud enough, and she watched Mona clock the two of them going and file it under the thing she'd already filed once tonight — the two who'd found the easy back-and-forth, slipping off. Let her file it there. That was the safest drawer in the room.

Ethan came. The Fade made him graceful about it, a half-second slow, letting himself be led like it was choreography he half-remembered. Nkechi said something behind them that ended in a laugh. The rusted door took the green room and folded it down to a line of light under the jamb.

The bathroom was the size of a closet a man had lost an argument with. One bulb. A sink with a permanent brown tear under the tap. And high on the outside wall, above the cistern, a hinged pane the size of a serving tray, glass gone amber with grime, a catch on it furred with old paint.

Faith-sized. Not Ethan-sized. She clocked that and set it down to deal with in a second.

“Okay,” Ethan said. The bulb put hollows under his eyes, and the space between them was about eight inches, and he reached.

She stepped back. Put one finger up. *Wait.* "You want to tell me what—"

"Four maybe five. That was the first one." She kept her voice under the tap's drip, quick and flat, laying it out exactly as it had come. And Faith moved too, and that was the thing she'd never be able to lay out clean afterward, in the six hours or the months — that she was doing two sums at once and neither of them was *save him.* She was reading the decision go from held to acting, the exact instant Koss's hand stopped waiting, and at the same time she was trying to solve the room, where the barrel was, where the cone of it would open, where the empty floor was — and a Read gets the mind exact and the meters wrong, and under that much load, with the pain of the whole night already opening something in her she couldn't shut, she put her body a half-step off. She moved into it. She'd been solving for out and her feet went the other way, into the space she'd meant to leave.

Koss fired.

The room took the shot and the shot took Faith — high on the left, shoulder and the ribs under it, a wet flat impact that turned her sideways and folded her down out of the lamp light before Ethan's own weapon had finished coming level. And the Wolfpack, when it went, went enormous. It filled the concrete box the way eighty minutes of noise-rock had filled it and then some, one great flat wall of sound that ate the ringing and the mist and the between-sets pulse still leaning on its low end through two hundred years of wall — and across the table Koss stopped being a decision holding still. It took him off his feet and off Mona's shoulder and down behind the cover he'd staged so carefully, and the light hand came off her and the geometry came apart all at once, and he was on the floor and not getting up and not, Ethan saw, gone — a man stopped, breathing hard and wrong, done being the thing that had walked her into the corner.

Then the silence, the real one, the kind that comes down after a room has been that loud.

Rill was against the wall with his hands still up, alive, the Glass in him gone to something that was almost serene and almost not, watching. Dayo breathed wet in the dark behind the couch, slow, still spending it. Nkechi came out from under the far table on her hands and knees, dragging one great ragged sob of air, alive, alive. Mona scrambled free across the cable river toward Ethan with the flat-white strip finally breaking into a run of frightened amber.

And Faith was on the floor with one hand laid flat over her ribs, the blood coming up between her fingers slow and dark, watching the ceiling — the chain lamp swinging its slow arc overhead, throwing everything twice — as if she were reading it too.

Ethan came down onto his knees in the cable river without feeling the concrete take them.

His hands arrived at her before he had any instruction for them. That was the thing he'd remember — that his hands, which had held a room for eighty minutes, which had racked the Wolfpack and squeezed an SMG's short vicious stutter into a man's stopped reel, had nothing at all for this. They hovered. One went toward the blood coming up dark between her fingers and stopped short, because pressing meant hurting and he didn't know the geography of it, didn't know which was the wound and which was just where the blood had run. The other one, useless, needing somewhere to be, came to rest on the side of her face. Palm to her cheek, thumb along the bone under her eye. It was the thing you did when there was no other thing. It was what you had left when the count came up empty.

"Okay," he said, to nobody, to her. "Okay. You're okay."

Faith was reading him.

She couldn't not. She'd told him in the doorway — *only when everyone's loud* — and now there was only him, close, his palm a warm flat weight on her face, and the pain had opened the aperture the rest of the way, jammed the shutter she'd never had against a wall, and he came flooding up the line into her at a range of no distance at all. She got the whole weather of him and it was clean, that was the surprise of it, clean as spring water: the terror first, honest, no performance in it, a man watching a person bleed and being simply and wholly afraid. Then the guilt, hard under it — *you moved into it, you moved the wrong way, I should have, I should have* — the arithmetic of a thing that couldn't be arithmetic'd. And then, under the guilt, running beneath the fear like the low end under a song, a bright astonished thing he had no defense against and didn't understand: surprise at himself. That she mattered this much. After one night. After two hours and six correct turns and a bathroom window. He was appalled by how much.

And then, under even that — down past the terror and the guilt and the astonishment, in the place where she'd found the flat closed figure in Koss and found nothing to give it — she found the other thing. The specific quiet thing. It had been there all night, she understood now. It had been there since the green room. It didn't have words on it either, but it wasn't a picture and it wasn't a closed decision. It was just quiet, and it was aimed, and it had her name where a name would go.

She laughed.

It came up out of her the way the first laugh had, four hours and a lifetime ago — without her deciding on it, low and helpless and entirely wrong for the room. A dying-man's-blood, ringing-silence, worst-minute-of-her-life laugh. It hurt. It pulled at the ribs and the shoulder and she did it anyway, because it was funny, it was the funniest thing, and there was no one to explain it to who would ever understand.

"Don't." His voice broke on it. His thumb pressed against her cheekbone like he could hold her here by the face. "Don't laugh. Faith. I need you to — don't, stay with me, don't do that—"

"Stop thinking about the blood," she said.

"What?"

"You're thinking about the blood. I can hear it, it's all you're — stop." She swallowed, and it cost her. "Think about something good."

"*What?*"

"Anything good." Barely voiced now, quick and dry going soft at the edges. "Doesn't matter what. Just — turn it that way. Loudest thing in the room. Give me something good to read."

He didn't know what she meant. She felt him not know it — felt him cast around in the wreck of the night for a good thing and find only blood, only the couch and the couch's one more bad decision, only Dayo breathing wet and Nkechi's ragged sobbing and the flat-white strip breaking into frightened amber as Mona came scrambling across the cables toward them. He didn't have a good thing. He didn't know how to make one to order. His face said so.

So she brought her free hand up — the one not flat over her ribs, the one gone tacky and dark — and she laid it over his where it held her face, and she left it there, and she stayed. And she read. Down past the terror he couldn't turn off and the guilt he couldn't set down, down to the specific quiet thing that had her name on it, the thing he'd been carrying without knowing its weight, the thing he'd have kept walled off for another four years if a woman he'd known for one night hadn't been lying in his cable river with his blood-warm hand under her cheek.

"You've been writing the same song for four years," Faith said.

Ethan went still.

"You think the last line's about Mona." Her eyes found his and held, running at whatever speed she ran at, all the way to the end of it. "It's not."

He went very still. The stillness of the doorway, the stillness of a man feeling the ground stop being ground — except there was no fear in this one, only the thing itself, arriving, landing, the last line of four years turning over once in the dark and coming up finally, impossibly, legible.

"Go finish it," she said.

And passed out.

Her hand slid off his. Her head went heavy in his palm. Somewhere very close Mona was already making the call — the real one, the emergency handle, the flare going up the mesh into the indifferent city with a bassist's level voice reciting an address forty meters over Milwaukee — and the next six hours came for all of them, the full trauma cart banging in through the ruined side entrance, the cold light, the hands that knew the geography his hadn't. The next six hours are not this story.

Three months later the Velvet Tine had a new PA and the same rusted lamp.

They'd rebuilt the room around its bones. New composite where the side entrance had folded in. A bar with three bottles and a tap that ran now. The equipment table was the same table, still faintly scorched along one edge where a round had found a full thimble and dumped a whole night's borrowed narrative into the air, and nobody had sanded it, and nobody was going to.

Out front the floor was full and pushing its low second heart through two hundred years of ferrocement. Fenris took the stage the way they always had, no announcement, Ethan to the mic in the dark and Rill driving from the first beat — driving, present, completely there behind the kit the way he was nowhere else, the Glass or no Glass, it never made a difference to the drums. Mona's light-strip ran amber up her forearm and stayed lit, chasing the bass, blazing where it used to go flat, and she watched the room the way she watched everything, the last clear head, filing it.

Ethan sang them out to the edge of the set and brought them down into the slow one — the one where the low end drops out for four bars and comes back angry, and then the other one, the one under it, the one where it doesn't come back. Four years of it. He got to the last verse and the room leaned in the way a room does when it knows a song is about to end and doesn't want it to, and he held the last line in his mouth for a moment. Just held it. A breath longer than the meter wanted.

Then he sang it.

The line that hadn't been there before. And the floor caught it — three hundred people catching something without knowing what, the way a crowd tilts before it knows it's tilting, a small collective inhale rippling back through the dark — and it landed and settled and was true, and the song, at last, was finished.

In the front row, arms folded, watching, stood a small figure with a blonde bob cut.

She was fine.

She already knew the words.
