# Street Meat

_Kyle recruits Renko Moss under leverage and infiltrates Northpoint body farm as a fake corpse to extract Dr. Nadia Park._

[Beat 1]
The bus was already rolling north when Moss taped the second lead down. He pressed harder than he needed to. Kyle noted it.

"Core temp needs to read forty-eight Fahrenheit by the time we hit the dock," Moss said. He was not looking at Kyle. He was looking at the readout on the portable monitor clamped to the gurney rail, watching the line of Kyle's vitals like bad news he'd written himself. "Northpoint intake scans core temp, pulse, and the death cert on the slate. It doesn't read carefully. Nobody breaks into a body farm."

"No one but me."

"You'll have four hours before the cooler rotation. After that you're inventory." Moss pulled the cooling blanket up across Kyle's chest with two sharp tugs. "Inventory gets opened."

Kyle lay still. The contact gel was cold enough to register through the leads, a wet chill spreading outward from each electrode like the bus itself was trying to make the point. Outside, the city came up through the floor — the particular vibration of northside asphalt, potholed and patched in a pattern Kyle knew without naming it.

Fourteen months ago he'd been on a rooftop on 47th, working a different job, and had watched Moss come out the back of the bus below and sign a living man as deceased on the delivery manifest. Kyle had kept the image in the same drawer he kept most things — sealed, labeled, available. He'd opened it when Hua handed him this contract and told him there was only one crew chief on the North Side who'd run this route.

The conversation that followed had been short.

"Your hardware," Moss said now. He stopped. He set down the tape. He looked at the monitor, then at Kyle, and his face did the thing Kyle had seen it do twice before — something between disgust and arithmetic, a man calculating a cost he resented paying. "It does — whatever it does."

"In theory."

"In theory." The word came out flat and contaminated. Moss's jaw worked once. "Atlas-grade NeoCortex in a field-temp drop. Theory. The last confirmed Atlas installation was a corpo lab with a dedicated resuscitation team and two surgeons on standby."

"Three surgeons," Kyle said. "The third was backup."

Moss looked at him.

"I read the file," Kyle said.

"The file is classified."

"The file was classified."

Moss said nothing for a long moment. The bus shifted north onto Clark, and the vibration changed — smoother here, the street surface a different quality of money. Northpoint was another six minutes.

Kyle had been installed at sixteen. He had not been asked. The three surgeons and their backup protocols were case history by the time he learned to spell NeoCortex. What the hardware could do under sustained low-temperature arrest was, functionally, unknown — a useful thing to know about yourself before you needed it to be true.

"You know what I do to people who run games on me," Moss said.

"I do," Kyle said. He kept his voice even. The blanket was getting there. He could feel the edge of it moving into his chest, temperature dropping in slow increments the monitor was recording for both of them. "That's not what this is."

Moss looked at the readout.

"No," he said finally, in the voice of a man who has stopped arguing with the math. "I suppose it isn't."

The bus hit the ramp to the service road. Kyle closed his eyes. Somewhere in the hardware above his brain stem, the NeoCortex noted the thermal data, tagged the trend line, and waited.

[Beat 2]
The Northpoint loading bay was lit in the flat white of a place that didn't care what it looked like. Moss pulled up to the scanner gate at 0318 and idled while the death cert transmitted. Kyle lay under the blanket with his eyes closed and his hands loose at his sides.

"Core reads forty-six point two," Moss said quietly. He wasn't talking to Kyle. He was reading a gauge on a piece of equipment he wasn't sure about. "Close enough."

The monitor showed flatline — pulse off, the cardiac suppressor running its one job with the efficiency of borrowed technology. Kyle could feel his own heartbeat the way you feel a sound through a wall: present, distant, no longer his problem for the next several minutes.

Moss came around to the gurney. He had the paddles out. He hadn't spoken in three minutes.

"You understand," Moss said, "that the scanner confirms absence of cardiac activity. Not low. Absence." He set one paddle. Set the other. He was precise about it — the placement of a man who was certified and competent and extremely unhappy. "This isn't a slow heartbeat I'm telling the scanner to ignore. This is a stopped heart."

"I know."

"If your hardware doesn't do whatever it does —"

"I know."

Moss's jaw worked. He looked at the paddles, at Kyle's chest, at the monitor. His face had the expression of a man who has spent a long time learning not to ask certain questions and had just been asked one.

"I should throw you in the trash," he said, "you son of a bitch."

"You will," Kyle said. "Aim for the heart."

Kyle's hand moved toward the chest lead, checking placement. Moss's palm came down across his forearm, flat and final. "Keep your hands down," Moss said. "I've run this a thousand times." Kyle kept his hands down.

The world went white.

Then it went specific: the taste of metal on the back of his tongue, and Pixel's two fingers on his wrist in 2E at 0247, the way her hand had felt over his pulse that time — still, professional, counting. Then the clamp came down.

Cold folded him.

He was labeled. Tagged with the case number Moss had prepared three days ago — male, sixty-one kilos, cause of death coronary, pending harvest review. He was put in a drawer. Bay four, third tier. The drawer was exactly as narrow as advertised and smelled of refrigerant and antiseptic and something deeper underneath that he did not catalog.

The last thing in the drawer with him was the count.

Eight seconds, maybe ten, where the NeoCortex was the only system running and everything else had simply stopped and he had been, without complication, a man somebody stopped.

Then the hardware did whatever it did.

It was not dramatic. No moment of awareness that snapped back, no sudden breath, no cinematic resurrection. There was only the thermal log resuming its recording and the cortical mesh running a location check and his hands, in the dark, finding the bag seam without consulting anything above them. His hands worked before his eyes did. His eyes caught up shortly after.

The drawer was cold. He was in it.

Four hours.

He began to move.

[Beat 3]
Northpoint's cooler ran in numbered bays on a grid that read left to right, low to high, intake order. The lighting was maintenance-level — enough to read labels, not enough to see faces. Kyle had memorized the floor plan from Hua's file, but the file was three months old and someone had shifted the grid numbering at bay six. He found the discrepancy with his hands before he found it with his eyes.

Bay seven was where it said it was.

The tag on the bag read SCHEDULED HARVEST, which was Northpoint's vocabulary for a living person they had administrative custody of and had decided not to waste. Kyle cut the seal at the top and worked the reversal agent through the carotid with two fingers and a syringe he'd kept sealed against his left palm for the last four hours. He had loaded it before Moss put him under. He had not been confident about the loading until his hands confirmed it.

Nadia Park woke the way scientists wake from sedation — in layers, each one claiming itself back from the drug in order of priority. Orientation first. Then assessment. Her hand went to her throat before her eyes had finished finding the room. Then she looked at the thermal display on Kyle's chest port.

He had not thought about the chest port. The display was still running — the NeoCortex's thermal log, live, which meant she was looking at the last four hours of his arrest interval in data format: core temp floor, autonomous oxygenation curve, suspension depth, the specific fingerprint of hardware performing a function it was only theoretically capable of.

She read it for eleven seconds.

"They actually built one," she said.

The delivery was flat. Not wonder — the thing a researcher says when a result she'd suspected for years and never expected to hold simply arrives and has to be acknowledged. The room smelled of refrigerant. Somewhere in the grid, a compressor cycled.

Kyle pulled the port cover closed. "Built what."

She looked at him. Not at the port — at him. The distinction was legible.

"The Atlas program had a specification ceiling." Her voice had the flatness of someone still finding their way back to language. "A theoretical maximum — autonomous function under zero perfusion. Not low perfusion. Zero." She stopped. Took the breath. "The ceiling was a number on a slide in a presentation. Nobody in the program believed it was achievable in a living system. The hardware that would hit that number was a boundary condition. Something the math permitted."

She stopped.

Kyle waited.

She did not finish the sentence. She had answered the question he hadn't asked — the one underneath the one about what she'd built — and answered it by stopping, which was its own kind of answer. He filed it the way he filed the togishi's verdict on Silence: in the drawer with everything he hadn't decided about yet, where it would wait in the cold until he was ready to open it.

"Can you walk?" he said.

She sat up. She tested her legs. "Yes."

"Two minutes," he said. "Then we move."

[Beat 4]
The supply dock at the north end took disposals around the clock. At 0340, bus 31 backed up the ramp with its running lights on and its manifest loaded. Moss came out in coveralls and moved two sealed medical-waste bags on a flatbed dolly with the economy of a man who had done this specific job eleven thousand times.

The gate clerk was a heavyset woman in a booth who had been working overnights long enough to treat it as a time zone. She ran the manifest, got to the bag count, and stopped.

"Says three bags," she said. "I'm looking at two."

Moss didn't look at the bags. He looked at the manifest on his slate and turned it toward her.

"Number transposed at dispatch," he said. "It's two. The three is a typo on the routing carbon — see the correction here, the initial." He produced a second manifest from the inside pocket of his coveralls and held it out. "I had dispatch resend it at 0330. This is the clean copy."

The clerk looked at the first manifest. Looked at the second. Looked at Moss.

Moss waited. He had the face of a man who had nowhere else to be and nothing more interesting to do and had been making night-window manifest corrections since before she was licensed.

She logged the second manifest and waved him through.

Bus 31 cleared the outer fence at 0351.

Three klicks south, Moss pulled into the shadow of a dead gantry and came back and opened the bags. Both bags. He stood over the two of them — the lunatic and an old woman the manifest swore was forty kilos of biohazard — and after a long moment set the empty bags back on the dolly with the care of a man putting something down he doesn't intend to pick up again.

"Told you," he said, "I'd throw you in the trash."

"You did," Kyle said. "Best ride I've had all year."

Nadia was already on her feet, brushing residue from her sleeve with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had woken up in a body bag before and had opinions about the experience.

Kyle gave her the burner and a sector address on the West Side. She took both without looking at them and moved to leave.

She stopped at the edge of the gantry's shadow. Turned her head, not her body.

"Marrow," she said.

Not a sentence. Not instruction. The way a researcher leaves a receipt on a workbench — a single artifact confirming the work happened, for the record, for whoever found it next. She didn't wait for a response. She was already moving.

Kyle let her go. In the overlay, the NeoCortex flagged the name and held it.

He climbed into the cab. Moss drove. The NeoCortex's thermal log was still running in the overlay — he hadn't closed it, and it occurred to him now that he wasn't going to. The arrest interval read 53 seconds. Full neuretic function. No heartbeat. The curve was clean and impossible and clearly his, running from the moment Moss set the paddles to the moment his hands found the bag seam in the cold without being asked.

He watched it the rest of the way to the depot.

The city came up through the floor.
