# M-101

_The origin story of Soren Rowe — the man whose mind was stolen from Earth 1919 by Verlaine, installed in a disposable automaton shell, and conscripted as Myrmidon M-1018883. His service at the Verlaine-Atrament front line, the refusal at Turret #34, and his eventual desertion. The events that precede his appearance in Vigil's End._

[Beat 1]
The bottle had cost him money he did not have, which was its own kind of joke, since the money he did not have was the reason he was drinking it.

Two years home, and the ranger posting had gone to another man - one who still had a job to leave when Declan's finally came through. So Declan Doyle went back down into the Unaka seam, because his hands still knew the work even if nothing else about him did. That had lasted until the company let three shifts go rather than one, and his had been the newer face in the room, home from the war or not.

Since then there had been Marte's counter in the mornings, coffee he mostly didn't finish, and evenings that ran long. Tonight he'd filled one with a truck, a two-lane road out past the county line, and a bottle riding the bench seat, three-quarters gone. Hartner sat beside him, same as he always did, for reasons Declan had never asked him and did not intend to start asking tonight.

"Doyle." Hartner's voice came flat, the calm gone thin at its edge. "You're taking that curve faster than you took the last one."

Declan corrected without answering. The truck came back off the shoulder gravel and found the center line again, more or less, and the radio had gone to static a while back on a station he hadn't bothered fixing.

He was thirty-one years old. He had fought in France for reasons a government had explained to him once, briefly. He had come home to a mountain that had not moved an inch in his absence, and a mine that took him back without asking where he'd been. The mine let him go anyway, eventually, the way jobs did. He had hands that knew coal and did not know how to do anything else, and a body that had learned, somewhere in a French winter, how to keep moving after most of what made it worth moving had already gone quiet.

The tree came up on the outside of the next curve, same as it had a hundred times before on nights he'd taken this road too fast and always made it. He did not see it soon enough this time. He would have said, if there had been time to say anything, that the whiskey was not the reason - that the reason was older than the whiskey, older than the mine losing him, older than France - but there was not time to say anything, and the tree's bark went white and enormous in the high beams the half-second before it would have mattered, and after that there was no more room in the night for reasons of any kind.

[Beat 2]
He did not feel the impact.

He felt, instead, the specific and appalling sensation of being drawn - not thrown, because a crash has a direction, a windshield or a ditch waiting somewhere ahead of it to be arrived at eventually, but drawn, sideways and up and through, the way a hook set deep in a trout's lip takes the whole fish out of the river in one motion the fish never sees coming. He had lived thirty-one years inside his own skin without once understanding it could be taken from him. Something reached into the warm dark he had occupied since before he had a word for himself, into the whiskey-loose hand still closing on a wheel that was no longer there, and lifted him clean out of it.

He went out entirely. Not into a darker room. Out of the having of a room at all.

Then there was warmth, and the warmth was the first thing he understood after the drawing had finished its work - a warmth that was nowhere specific, that had no skin to be felt through, that existed in the same place he did, which was not a place. There was a light that was not quite yellow. There was no sound. He thought of water. He would learn the word later, from a Verlaine handler speaking it in a tone of professional boredom, and the translation faculty would hand it to him along with the rest of the language before he'd asked for it: Amnios, the medium between what was and what came next. He did not know it would not last. He let himself be in it, because there was nothing else on offer, and he tried to hold onto the smell of the Hartner truck's cracked leather seat, the smell of the mountain in October, and found that he could not - that the warmth was already taking the edges off everything that was not itself.

There was wreckage in it, before the end. The bottle, turning end over end, whiskey and glass both. The truck's dash light, the tree's white bark, receding. France came past in pieces that hurt worse than anything else in the gold - chalk mud sucking at a boot, a flat crack close enough to feel in the teeth, a hand that had been Corbett's hand and then was not a hand any longer. And his mother's kitchen, oilcloth worn pale where her elbow went every evening, bread cooling under a cloth on a morning that had nothing to do with any of this - turned slowly out of reach, and he understood, with a clarity that had nowhere left to be afraid in, that he was watching himself come apart, and no part of it would wait for him to say goodbye to it properly.

He was taking the last of the warmth when it ended.

The ending was not like the drawing had been. The drawing had been slow enough to feel arriving. The ending was sudden - something seized the thing he was and pulled, and the warmth went, and the light went, and he had a fraction of a moment to understand he had been very wrong about the nature of where he was going.

Then he arrived.

[Beat 3]
He was cold.

Not as a man is cold - not the cold of winter against skin, which has its own texture, its own relationship to the body's response, the raising of small hairs, the slow migration of warmth away from the extremities. This was the cold of an enclosure that had no regulation of temperature, because regulation implied a system that had decided temperature mattered. It did not matter. He was in a body that had no way to feel cold. It only registered, distantly, that the air around him held no warmth worth mentioning.

He knew this because the moment he could think, he could see the log. He did not know why he was seeing it, or what the oval field of vision in which he saw it corresponded to, or why the proportions of the room he was in were slightly wrong - slightly too tall on the left side, as though his left eye was mounted differently than his right, except that these were not eyes. They received light. They transmitted it. The language available to him for the experience was the language of eyes and he used it because he had no other.

Someone spoke to him.

He understood what they said. This was a surprise. He had not known he would understand. The language was nothing he had spoken before - its sounds were those of a different family entirely, with a grammar whose architecture he could feel without being able to explain - and yet when the sound arrived in whatever served him for hearing, it arrived with meaning intact. He would think about this later. He did not have the luxury of thinking about it now, because the person who had spoken to him was waiting for an answer.

"Unit processed and activated," said the voice. "Designation: M-101. Catalog entry: M-1018883. Record acknowledged. Unit to processing bay three."

He said, in the same language: "I am not a unit."

The person with the voice - a woman, he thought, though the decision was based on very little - made a brief notation. "Vocal module is functional. Good." She moved away.

He considered what had just happened. He had protested and she had heard the protest as information about his functionality. The protest was a test result. He would need to think about this more carefully.

He looked at the room. A long low chamber, walls of worked stone, lit by a light source he could not locate - a uniform pallor that came from nowhere and fell on everything equally. Along one wall, other shells. Small infantry automatons like the one he was presumably inside, standing in rows. They were not active. He could not tell if they were occupied.

He tested his limbs. The arms moved. The legs moved. His sense of where they were in space was off - he kept correcting for a weight that wasn't there, a balance point that was lower than it should be, a reach that was shorter. He was smaller than he had been. He was lighter. His hands, when he raised them - the things that served as his hands - were flat plates with articulated sections. They could grip. They could not feel what they gripped beyond force and resistance. The calluses were gone - the ones the pick handle had raised at the base of each finger, the thickened web between thumb and forefinger where he braced against the shaft when a seam ran hard. The scar was gone too, the smooth one along his left heel where a slipped roof-bolt had found him three winters past. Nine years of record, erased.

He lowered his hands.

A different person arrived. A man, older, who held a board on which there were papers, and who studied him the way an inspector studies a delivery against its manifest.

"M-1018883," the man said.

He said: "M-101."

The man looked up from his board. "Your catalog entry is M-1018883."

"My name," he said, "is M-101. That is what I was assigned. The longer number is your paperwork. You may use it for your records, but M-101 is what I am called."

The man held his gaze - or whatever served as his gaze, the oval receptors that received and transmitted - for a moment. He wrote something on his board without a word of explanation. Then he moved on to the next shell in the row.

He looked at the room again. He memorized it. He did not know why. His memory was perfect now - everything he perceived was retained with a fidelity no human mind had ever had - and so the memorization was not a precaution. It was the thing he did when he was trying to stay in possession of himself. He fixed his attention on the physical facts of the place and held them.

He thought: this is a coma.

The thought arrived whole, and it was a comfort, and he was careful with it the way he had learned to be careful with anything that felt too good to examine closely. He had gone off a road he had taken too fast a hundred times before. A man went off a road, and a man's mind, starved of whatever a mind needs and does not get in the last moments before an impact, might build something like this - a room, a designation, a war that was not his war - while the actual him lay somewhere with tubes in his arms and a machine keeping count of his heart. That was the real world. This was the dream a man built to keep from lying in it awake. He held onto the distinction carefully, the way he held onto anything that felt too good to examine closely. It was not proof of anything. It was only the shape a hope takes when a man has nothing else to build one out of.

The smell of yggdra smoke was not here.

He noticed the absence. It sat wrong in a way he could not name yet, and he was careful not to think about what it meant, or about the sound he thought - for one half-second, gone before he could be certain he had heard it at all - had come from very far away and very close at once: something rhythmic, and metallic, and even, like a small bell tolling a number instead of an hour. He did not have a word for it yet. He would.

[Beat 4]
The front was louder than anything he had known, and he had known the Unaka seam on a bad day, when a distant shot-fire ran the whole drift's air pressure sideways and every man on the section felt it in his teeth before he heard it. He had known artillery, once, in France, close enough to put men down in the mud who had not been hit by anything at all. He had known sound as a physical fact, as a thing that made claims on the body.

The front made a different claim. It made no room for interpretation. The sound arrived and the body took it as a fact of significant weight and left the response to him, and what he found was that the right response was to move toward cover - that sound meant incoming fire, and incoming fire meant damage to the shell he wasn't ready to answer for.

He moved toward cover. The shell moved efficiently. He had expected the gap between what he knew how to do and what the shell could do to be a problem - in the mine, every task had been learned through the body, through the accumulated correction of repeated error, through the hands learning ahead of the mind - but the shell did not require that learning. It performed the physical tasks available to it without the learning curve. He told the limbs to move and they moved. He told the hands to grip and they gripped.

The problem was that the mine had also required judgment - when a prop would hold and when it wouldn't, when the seam was running true and when it was fixing to let go - and judgment was not something the shell supplied. He would have to supply it. This was clarifying in a way that the intake chamber had not been. In the intake chamber he had been processed. Here, he was being used. The distinction had a practical weight.

He reached cover - a section of ruined wall, worked stone like the intake chamber but broken, exposed, smelling of something he had no word for. Burned organic material. He crouched behind the wall and watched the field beyond it, where two parties were engaged in destroying each other with tools designed for that purpose, and some part of him that had crossed an ocean and a war already before any of this recognized the shape of it immediately, the way you recognize a voice before you place the face.

He made himself look at it properly, because looking was the only inventory he had.

The trench line ran to both horizons, and it was full of shells like his own - hundreds of them, maybe more than he had numbers ready for, all built to the same pattern, oval receptors and flat-plated hands and the same economical height, so that from any distance greater than a few yards a man could not have told one from the next even if he had known which one to look for. He thought of the drift at shift-change, the miners coming up out of the black in their identical coats with their identical lamps, indistinguishable in the half-light until you knew a particular walk or a particular way of carrying a lunch pail. This was that, with the walk and the lunch pail both taken away. He was one of several hundred identical bodies in a trench, and the trench did not care which several hundred, and neither, he understood, did anyone giving the orders.

Atrament's side of the field was a different kind of manufacture. Where Verlaine had built small and many, Atrament had built large and few, and what came out of the smoke on the far treeline was not a shell at all in the sense his was a shell - it moved on tracks the width of a house, a slab-sided thing three stories at the shoulder with a hide of riveted plate, and when it fired, the ground he was crouched behind carried the report up through the stone and into whatever passed, in him, for bone. Behind it came something bipedal, twice a man's height, walking with the wrong number of knee-joints in each leg, and he understood without being told that whatever crewed it, if anything did, was not going to be a mind that had volunteered for the privilege. Verlaine stole minds and put them in bodies built for the purpose. Atrament, the briefings said, stole bodies. He looked at the walking thing on the far treeline and thought he understood, for the first time, exactly what that distinction was going to look like from the outside.

He identified the factions from the briefing information that had been installed in the shell before deployment. He had not known the information was there until he needed it and found it waiting. Verlaine's forces wore identifiers he could cross-reference. Atrament's forces wore different identifiers. He would need to fire at Atrament's forces and not at Verlaine's forces. That was the job.

He found this task straightforward to understand and difficult to want.

He fired at Atrament's forces because the alternative had consequences he had been briefed on, and those consequences involved the shell being taken offline and the question of what offline meant for the thing currently occupying the shell was not one he had yet resolved. He fired accurately, because the shell's targeting systems were precise and he could let them be precise without the kind of involvement that would require him to be present in the act.

The engagement concluded. Atrament's forward line withdrew. Verlaine's forces advanced to the ground they had held, and the ground was not remarkable - broken, churned, littered with the evidence of what had been done on it. He advanced with the line. He walked across the churned ground. He did not look at it more than he needed to navigate.

He did not panic. This was the shell's gift, if it could be called that: the body did not panic. The body took in what happened and did what it was told and waited for more. The panic was in him and had nowhere to go because the body would not carry it, and so it accumulated. He was careful to note that it was accumulating. He was careful to maintain his attention on the physical facts.

The yggdra smoke was not on the air at any point during the engagement. He would have noticed.

[Beat 5]
He was crossing a staging area between engagements when it happened.

There was no warning that he recognized as such. Later, replaying the sequence, he identified a momentary resonance in the shell's signal processing - a thing that had no name in any briefing, a frequency or something that occupied the space where frequency would be if the phenomenon were a wave phenomenon. He did not know what it was. He knew that it happened.

Then he was somewhere else.

Not teleportation - he understood the difference, or thought he did, which in the absence of certainty was the same thing. He was not in a new location. He was in a different machine. A larger machine, roof-mounted, with a far greater field of perception and the low, resonant sensation of significant mechanical mass. He could feel the weight of it - an impression, not a physical fact, transmitted somehow through whatever mechanism allowed him to feel the shell at all. The machine was a field piece of some kind. It was dormant. It was waiting for an operator.

He was its operator, suddenly and without decision.

He stayed very still. Stillness was appropriate when he did not understand what was happening. He surveyed the expanded field of perception - wider, higher, the staging area visible below him at a different angle than he was accustomed to. He could see the line where he had been walking. He could see his own shell, standing in the middle of the path, motionless. His own shell looked small from here. He had not expected it to look small.

He returned.

This too had no mechanism he could explain - he thought return the way he would think any word and the return happened. He was in his own shell again. The small proprioception, the narrow field, the particular weight of this specific tin body. He stood in the staging area path and took account of what the shell's systems provided and held all of it.

He had been in a different machine. He had left himself and occupied something dormant and then returned. He did not know how. He did not know what the limits of it were. He did not know if it was repeatable or if it had only happened once and would not happen again.

Thirty feet away, one of Verlaine's handlers was looking at him with an expression that indicated something notable had been observed.

"M-1018883," the handler said. "Stand by."

"M-101," Declan said. A reflex, by now.

The handler made a notation and spoke briefly into a device that carried his voice somewhere else. The handler's expression was not concerned. It was the expression of a man who has found an additional use for a piece of equipment that was already doing its job.

Declan understood, watching that expression, that he had just become more valuable. He held this alongside the other facts. He did not decide yet what it meant. He suspected it meant that whatever choice he had imagined he retained about how he was used had become, in this moment, somewhat smaller.

[Beat 6]
He had been at the front for an interval he could measure precisely if he chose - the shell's logs were complete and accurate - but chose not to, because counting the days meant counting what the days had cost him, and that was a thing he could not make himself do. Long enough that the operational patterns had become familiar, that he knew which handlers expected verbal acknowledgment and which only wanted the task completed, that he knew the names of the terrain features on his section of the line.

He had not spoken to another Myrmidon.

He had known there were others. The intake chamber had made that plain - the rows of dormant shells, the administrative efficiency of the designation system. He was M-1018883 and there were numbers before and after his. He had not sought them out because the thing he feared most was finding in them something that would require him to feel something he had no way to act on, so he had kept his eyes off them.

He found the other one at a water resupply point between engagements, which was not a thing he needed - the shell did not need water - but which he had learned was a place where the operational pace slackened and it was possible to stand still for a few minutes without a handler noticing.

The other one was standing at the edge of the resupply point, also motionless, also not requiring water.

They looked at each other. The oval visual receptors, the flat plates of the hands, the particular posture of a mind that is managing a body it did not choose. He recognized it because it was his own. Whatever signal read as occupied-shell versus dormant-shell, he was reading it in the other one and the other one was reading it in him.

He said: "You are from somewhere else."

The other one said: "Yes."

"Not this Sphere."

"No."

He waited to see if the other one would offer more. The other one did not. He respected this. He said: "I am M-101."

"I know your designation," the other one said. "I have seen you on the line." A pause. "My designation is M-774. My name is not M-774."

"No," M-101 agreed. "Mine is not the number they gave me either." He said it without elaboration. The other one received it without elaboration. This was already a more efficient form of communication than he had experienced with anyone since the intake chamber.

The other one was quiet for a moment. M-101 looked at the field beyond the resupply point - gray-green, broken, the smell of burned organic material that he had stopped noticing until moments like this, when the operational pace slowed and the background information reasserted itself.

He said: "Where are you from?"

The other one said: "Somewhere with a river."

He thought of the creek that ran cold under the tipple even in August. He did not say: I am from somewhere with a river also, though it would have been true. He did not say anything for a moment, and then he said: "Is it worth naming it."

The other one considered this. "No," it said. "I do not think it would survive the naming."

"We cannot help each other," he said. This was also something he knew without needing to reason toward it.

"No," the other one said.

"But I am glad you exist," he said.

He had not planned to say this. It was true and so he said it, because he had learned that a true thing left unsaid did not go away - it just went nameless, and a nameless weight was heavier than one you had said out loud.

The other one was quiet. Then: "Yes. I am also glad."

A handler moved through the resupply point. They separated without agreement - simply moved in different directions, as two men who have finished a conversation move when the conversation is finished. He walked back toward his section of the line and was careful not to think of the other one as the other man. There was a reason for this that he did not examine. He would examine it later, when he had to.

[Beat 7]
The nature of Verlaine's war clarified over engagements.

He watched an airship work the far side of the valley on the third of these quiet mornings - Verlaine's, by the identifier bands along its flank, moving low and unhurried the way a man moves who is in no danger of being answered. It did not aim so much as it arrived over a place and then that place stopped being a place. He watched the bomb-fall pattern walk a line down the length of a ridge that briefing had marked, an hour before, as harboring elements in support of Atrament's forward position, and he watched the ridge afterward, and there was no way, from where he crouched, to separate what had been elements in support of Atrament's forward position from what had been a hillside. The airship came back around, unhurried, and did the next ridge, because there was a next ridge, because there was always a next ridge, and the operational tempo of the war seemed to consist mostly of there always being a next ridge.

He thought about it now. He had time between engagements, when the handlers were occupied with logistics and the line was holding its position and he was standing at the edge of it watching the contested ground and not being required to do anything with it.

He had access to the briefings. The strategic framing was consistent: a defensive operation, the preservation of Verlaine's territory against Atrament encroachment, the protection of the Spheres under Verlaine's custody. He had read this framing when it was first installed and had not thought about it carefully because careful thought about the framing was not relevant to the immediate operational requirements.

He thought about it now.

He thought: who was on that ground, in the last engagement.

He thought: what they were doing on that ground.

He thought: what they were told, about why they were there.

He could not answer these questions because he did not have the information. But he could observe patterns. Defense involved holding positions. Defense involved absorbing pressure and responding to it. Defense did not involve Myrmidons being sent forward into contested ground while Verlaine's regular forces held back, while Verlaine's officers directed the engagement from positions of safety, while Myrmidon losses were logged in the same administrative way that supplies were logged when they were consumed.

He was a consumed supply.

This was not a discovery that carried the weight he might have expected. He had suspected it since the intake chamber, since the man with the board who had looked at him the way a man looks at an entry in a ledger. The confirmation was not a surprise. What surprised him was the way it settled in him - not as outrage, not as grief, but as sediment. It accumulated. It did not have a feeling. It had a weight.

He watched the contested ground and he thought: the Atrament forces on that ground are also consumed supplies. They are not defending anything either. They are being used the way he is being used, by someone who has decided that using them is acceptable, and the thing they are fighting for is the continuation of the arrangement that allows them to be used.

He did not know what the arrangement was. He did not have access to that information. But the pattern was legible.

He thought about M-774, at the resupply point. He thought about the rows of shells in the intake chamber. He thought about how many there were and how many designations preceded his, and what that number came to if you followed it back far enough.

He turned away from it. He watched the contested ground - the wind direction, the light quality, the disposition of Verlaine's forward positions - all the things that were immediate and required no reckoning to be terrible.

The sediment was there whether he attended to it or not.

[Beat 8]
The order came through a standard briefing channel, three days after an engagement in which Myrmidon losses had been significant. He was accustomed to orders arriving through this channel. He was accustomed to executing them.

He had, by this point, developed a practice. The practice was this: he received the order, identified the operational parameters, executed with precision, and marked the execution as a completed fact. He did not allow the moment between reception and execution to be longer than it needed to be. He had learned that the moment was where accumulation happened, and he was already carrying enough.

This order was different in a way he could not articulate and did not try to. The target was legitimate by every operational definition he had access to. The action was within the scope of what he had been assigned. The execution would cause harm to persons who were, by the framing available to him, opponents of the interests he was coerced into serving.

He received the order. He identified the parameters. He closed the moment between reception and execution.

He executed with precision.

He was very good at this. He had understood this about himself - or about the shell, or about the combination of the shell's capabilities and his management of them - since the first engagement. The shell's targeting systems were precise and he could let them be precise without the kind of involvement that would make the precision cost him anything in the execution. He had used this. He had let the shell be precise while he was somewhere else in himself, attending to the yggdra smoke that was not on the air, attending to the weight of a pick handle that he no longer had hands to feel.

The execution concluded. He moved to the next operational position.

He noticed, two hours later, that something had changed in the way he was moving.

Not the shell - the shell moved exactly as it had before. But the thing inside the shell was moving differently, in the way that a man moves who is carrying something in his hands and does not want to look at what it is. A very small adjustment, barely perceptible, that he would not have noticed if he had not been carefully attending to the physical facts of himself as a discipline.

He stopped.

He was in a staging area, alone. He stopped and was still and he named what he found: I have done something I cannot put down. It is in my hands - or in what serves as my hands - and I am not looking at it because if I look at it I will have to know what it is.

He looked at it.

He knew what it was.

He stood very still in the staging area for a measured interval. Then he resumed moving. He moved precisely and functionally and he completed the tasks assigned to him for the remainder of that day's operations.

He was very good at this. He had already known he was very good at this. He had known it the way a carpenter knows the quality of their own work - not pride, just fact. He was precise and hollow and functional and he could do this for a long time.

That was the problem.

He had thought, in the first engagements, that the accumulation would reach a threshold and something in him would refuse - that he had a capacity and when the capacity was exceeded the refusal would come naturally, organically, as a consequence of the mechanism. He was learning that this was not how it worked. The mechanism did not refuse. He was the part that refused. He was the only part capable of refusal. The shell would comply as long as he allowed it.

The sediment was thick now - not a clean layer but a compound thing, made of engagements and orders and the precise hollow efficiency of his own execution.

He had the practice. He could still keep his eyes off what he was carrying.

He was good at compliance.

He was good at compliance and good at keeping his eyes off it and good at the practice, and he thought, with the part of himself that was still telling the truth, that he was not going to be able to stay good at all three of these things simultaneously for much longer.

[Beat 9]
The arc cannon fired for the first time on a morning three weeks into what he had stopped trying to count as his tenure at the front.

He had heard of it before he saw it - a weapon class the briefings referenced obliquely, as though even the documentation preferred not to dwell on it, and the handlers spoke of it the way men speak of weather they hope not to be caught out in. He learned what it was the way he learned everything now, by proximity to its effect rather than by explanation.

It did not fire a shell. There was no report he could correlate to an impact, no arc of a projectile his targeting systems could track from origin to terminus. There was a sound like the sky tearing along a seam it had been meaning to tear along for some time, and then there was a line of white light connecting a distant Atrament emplacement to a section of Verlaine's forward trench two hundred yards to his left, and where the line touched, there was, very briefly, an outline - men, and Myrmidons among the men, indistinguishable in the half-second the light held them, both kinds of soldier reduced to the same silhouette - and then there was no outline, and there was no trench, and there was a smell in the air that had no organic register at all, ash with nothing left in it that had ever been a person.

Man or myr. The weapon had not required an answer to the question. It had simply removed the ground the question was standing on.

He did not move for longer than was operationally advisable.

He began, sometime in the following hours, to lose the thread of where he was.

It did not happen as a single event. It happened as a slow failure of the boundary he had been maintaining since the intake chamber - the boundary between the facts he held and the self doing the holding - and the first sign of it was that he found himself standing in the Unaka seam, three winters back, checking a prop that was fixing to let go, and it took him a full four seconds of shell-time, which he could measure afterward with perfect and useless precision, to understand that he was not in the Unaka seam, that there was no prop, that he was standing in a staging area on a front that did not exist when his father was alive, and that some part of him had simply elected, without asking the rest of him, to be somewhere else for those four seconds because somewhere else was survivable and here was not.

He caught it. He named it: I was not here for four seconds. He held onto it.

It happened again an hour later, and this time it was not the mine. This time it was nothing at all - a gap, a silence in his own continuity that he could not afterward account for, that had no memory in it the way every other second of his existence since the Taking had a memory in it, perfect and total. He came back into himself mid-stride, crossing ground he did not remember beginning to cross, and for the length of that return he entertained, with a clarity that frightened him more than the gap itself had, the possibility that he had never been anywhere at all - that Aldenvast was the coma-dream, that the seam and his mother's kitchen and the smell of fir at first light were the thing a dying mind built to keep from a truth too large to hold, and that this - the front, the shell, the arc cannon and its silhouette of ash - was what remained when the dream ran out of material and the actual, only, entire fact of him was a small numbered thing standing in churned ground with nothing behind it and nothing ahead of it and no proof, none, that it had ever been anything else.

He held that thought for longer than he had held any thought since the Taking. He did not know how much longer. The shell's logs would tell him precisely, later, and later he chose not to look.

What brought him back was not reasoning. Reasoning had already failed him - every argument he could construct for which version of himself was real used facts supplied by the same disputed mind, and a mind that cannot trust its own witness cannot argue its way out of the dock. What brought him back was smell, or the memory standing in for it, because the shell had no olfactory receptor worth the name and never had: yggdra smoke, somewhere on the wind from a cookfire behind the line, and under it, insisted on rather than perceived, balsam and Fraser fir at first light above the Unaka seam, close enough to reach for and wrong enough to hurt, and the wrongness was the thing that saved him, because a coma does not build a smell that specific and that flawed. A dying mind reaching for comfort builds the thing it wants. It does not build the gap between the thing it wants and the thing it is given. Only the actual world was careless enough to leave that gap in.

He came back all the way into the staging area on that reasoning, such as it was - not proof, he understood, only the shape hope takes when a man has nothing else to build one out of, same as it had always been, but a shape he could stand on.

He said his own name to himself. Not M-101. The other one.

He stood in the churned ground for a measured interval, and then he resumed moving, and he did not tell a handler, and he did not tell M-774, because M-774 had troubles enough of his own that were not made lighter by adding this one to them, and because some of what a man carries he carries alone or he does not survive carrying it at all.

He was more careful, after that, about which four seconds he let himself spend elsewhere. He had learned there was a bottom to the well he had been managing his attention out of, and that he had come near enough to it to hear the sound the bucket makes when there is no more water to draw.

[Beat 10]
The order came on a morning that was gray and still, with no wind, the contested ground visible in the early light as a thing that had been worked over many times and still had not produced anything.

He was assigned to Turret Thirty-Four for that morning's operation. A fixed emplacement - machine of significant mass, roof-mounted over a fortified position, with a wide field of engagement and targeting systems considerably more sophisticated than the ones available to the small infantry shell he usually occupied, built around the same arc-cannon principle he had watched erase two hundred yards of trench three weeks before. He had been in it before, briefly, in those first accidental transfers before Verlaine's handlers had noted the capability and begun directing its use. He knew its weight, its field, its systems.

He entered the turret at the designated time. He crossed into the larger machine with the moment of resonance he had taught himself to produce, settled into its systems and its weight and its much larger field of perception.

He received the operational briefing. The target sector. The timing. The authorization codes.

He received the fire order.

He did not fire.

He did not decide not to fire. There was no decision, not in the sense of weighing and choosing. What happened was: the fire order arrived, and in the microsecond between its arrival and the execution that his operational parameters dictated, he understood what the target was.

He had not understood before. Small infantry shells were not given target information beyond identifier and location. The turret's systems provided the fuller picture. The target sector contained not Atrament forces but Atrament civilians - a settlement of workers and families in the contested zone, who were in the contested zone because the contested zone had been their home before it became contested. The operational logic was clear: the settlement provided support to Atrament forces, the removal of the settlement removed the support, the turret had the range and the authorization.

The fire order arrived and he understood all of this in the microsecond before execution.

He jumped.

He did not choose this. He did not decide. The jump happened the way the refusal happened - as physics, not philosophy, as a thing that occurred in the space where compliance would have been. He was back in his own shell, somewhere on the forward line, three hundred meters from the turret. Small again. In the particular narrow field of the infantry shell.

Behind him, in the direction his records told him the turret lay, there was silence.

The turret had received a fire order and fired nothing. He did not know what the handlers' systems would tell them about why. He knew he had done something that the operational parameters did not accommodate.

He stood on the forward line and attended to the physical facts. The gray morning. The still air. The contested ground unchanged. The silence from the turret's position, which would not last.

He thought of M-774. He thought: if I told him, he would understand. And then: he cannot do anything with the understanding. And then: that is not the point of it.

He thought of the settlement in the target sector. He thought of the families there the way he thought of the camp at the drift mouth and the smoke that rose from it every morning before shift - as a specific thing, as people who had a routine, as the kind of thing that was easy to reduce to a target coordinate if you let yourself. He had not let himself. The shell's targeting systems had the coordinates and he had the rest of it.

He waited.

The silence from the turret's position held for a long moment, and then there was noise - not the noise of ordnance, but the noise of handlers moving at operational speed, the noise of a problem being identified and routed to the appropriate level of authority.

He stood on the forward line and was very still. He thought, with the part of himself that was still telling the truth: I did not fire. I was asked to fire and I did not fire. It was not a decision because it happened in a space too small for decision. But it was me, because nothing else in the system could have done it.

It was the cleanest fact he had gathered since the Taking.

The machine and the man had divided here, and he was on the right side of it.

[Beat 11]
They did not tell him what they were going to do.

He had expected that the response to Turret Thirty-Four would be immediate - handlers, briefings, operational review, the machinery of accountability that he had observed working on other matters. What he received instead was a period of normal deployment. Operational as usual. Handled without notable difference. He was assigned engagements. He completed them. His performance logs were unremarkable. He waited for the other thing to arrive and it did not arrive.

It arrived on a day when nothing operational was scheduled. A handler he had not seen before - younger than most, with the particular expression of someone doing a task they understand to be administrative but would prefer not to understand too precisely - directed him to a bay in the rear of the fortified position.

There were instruments.

He could not identify all of them. Some were clearly calibration tools of the kind used for shell maintenance - he had seen their equivalents applied to other Myrmidons when there was time for upkeep. Some were not. Some were things that had no referent in his operational logs.

"This will not damage the shell," the handler said.

Which was useful information in that it told him the shell was what they were concerned about.

He said nothing.

He did not know what they did. This was the thing he was left with afterward: he had been present and the instruments had been applied and he had experienced something that he would not describe as pain - the shell did not transmit pain, only input - and when it was finished he was still himself in all the ways he could check. The handler left. He was returned to his operational post.

Somewhere in the middle of it - he could not afterward place exactly when, because the instruments did something to his sense of afterward as well as before - he heard, very clearly, a door. Not a door here. A door with weight to it, a door that swung on a hinge that needed oil, and a voice on the other side of it that he had not heard since the Unaka seam, saying his name the way she said it when she meant to be heard through a wall. He held onto it exactly as long as it lasted, which was not long, and did not tell the handler, because the real world was the one thing in this entire process he had not yet let them put an instrument to.

He noticed the change on the second day after.

He was crossing the staging area when he noticed it - not in the shell's movement, which was unaltered, but in the thing doing the moving. He was compliant. He had always been compliant in the sense that he had executed orders, but what had given the compliance its texture before - the sediment, the accumulation, the weight of what he was doing - had lost some of its resolution. The weight was still there. The sediment was there. But both were quieter, the way a sound is still audible after you've been in a loud place for a long time - present, but washed out.

He tested this carefully. He thought of the turret. He thought of the fire order and the microsecond between its arrival and his jump. He could recall it with perfect fidelity because his memory was perfect. He could not locate the same quality of the thing that had moved from knowing to acting.

It was there. It was quieter.

This was what they had taken from him. Not the knowledge. Not the memory. Not the record. The volume.

He stood in the staging area and took account of what remained. The argument - all the sediment of accumulated engagement, the facts, the patterns, the reckoning of Verlaine's use of Myrmidon lives, the civilian settlement, the operational meaning of what he had been ordered to do at Turret Thirty-Four - all of it was present and complete and perfectly recallable. The part that moved from accounting to action was quieter.

He knew, with the part of himself still capable of knowing things clearly, that the door with the weight to it and the voice on the other side had not gone quiet at all. Whatever the instruments had reached, they had not reached that. It was the one thing in him Verlaine's calibration tools apparently had no notation for, because it had never come from the shell, and had never come from the accounting either. He held this alongside the other facts, and was careful with it the way he was careful with anything that felt too good to examine closely.

He completed his operational tasks. He was precise. He was functional. He was empty in a new way - a cleaner emptiness than before, almost comfortable, the way a room is almost comfortable when everything that was in it has been cleared out. He moved through the days. The handlers were satisfied with him.

He thought, on a night when the front was quiet: this is what I let them take. The first thing they took from me I did not give them - they came and seized it. This one I gave them by being too close to the instruments and not having decided yet what I would do.

He decided then.

He did not act on the decision immediately. He was waiting for M-774.

[Beat 12]
M-774 was not at the resupply point.

This was not unusual - operational tempo varied, assignments shifted, the geometry of the line changed. He checked the sectors adjacent to his and did not find M-774 there either. He held onto the absence the way he held onto all things that required further information: he kept it without immediate interpretation, continued his operational tasks, and waited.

On the third day he asked a handler: "M-774's status."

The handler checked their board. "Offline. Sector four engagement, two days prior."

"Recovery status."

"Non-recoverable." Already moving on, because this was not information that required extended discussion.

He continued his operational tasks.

He thought, that night, of M-774 saying: somewhere with a river. Of M-774 saying: I do not think it would survive the naming. Of the moment at the resupply point when two occupied shells had stood in a field and recognized each other as something more than matériel, and had not been able to do anything with the recognition, and had known they couldn't, and had been glad anyway.

He thought: the other man is dead.

He used the word. He had been careful with it - had kept his distance from calling M-774 the other man, had known the reason without examining it, and now he examined it. The reason was that man carried weight. Man made a claim about what had been lost when M-774's shell became non-recoverable. He had been withholding the word because he had not wanted to carry the weight of it.

He carried it now. The other man was dead. Whoever he had been, wherever the river was, he was dead - the mind as permanent as the body, because death in this Sphere was permanent, and the shell was not a protection, only a location.

The option of M-774 - the option of not being entirely alone on the line, of having something to stay adjacent to that was worth staying adjacent to - was closed.

Three days later the order came.

He was assigned to a reconnaissance insertion - deep into Atrament-controlled territory, behind their lines, to carry and detonate an accelerant device at a specific structural target. The briefing was thorough. The target coordinates precise. The operational logic sound, by the framing available to him.

He was to carry the device himself. He was to detonate it himself, at the target. The briefing did not discuss recovery or extraction, because the operation did not include recovery or extraction. The briefing described the charge threshold of the device and its effective radius and the operational parameters for detonation.

He read the parameters.

He calculated the effective radius.

He understood what the operation required.

He thought: I cannot refuse this, because my refusal will be handled and the handler will send another Myrmidon in my place. He thought: I cannot comply, because I know what I know about the settlement in sector four, about M-774 in sector four, about what Verlaine's use of Myrmidons has been and will continue to be, and if I comply I will have given them everything that remains. He thought: the recalibration turned down the volume on the thing that moves from knowing to acting, but it did not silence it, and this is the moment when it either speaks or it does not.

The logic closed around him. He sat with it for the length of time it took him to understand there were no doors in it except one.

He thought of Aldenvast. He held the name the way he had held the yggdra smoke - as a fixed point, as something still his, as the proof that there had been something before all of this.

He said, to no one: I am going.

He did not say where. He did not know where. He knew it was not here.

[Beat 13]
He walked off the line at first light, when the morning watch was focused on the contested ground.

He did not run. Running implied urgency, and urgency implied destination, and he did not have a destination. He had a direction: away. Away from the line, away from the fortified position, away from the handlers and their boards and the rows of shells in the intake chamber and everything Verlaine kept count of. Away was not a destination. It was the only thing left when all the other options had been examined and closed.

The ground behind the line was broken and gray in the early light, churned where it had been crossed too many times by the movements of the war's logistics. He walked through it. The shell moved efficiently and he let it move. The fog was beginning to come off the interior valleys as the temperature differential between the ground and the air began its morning work, and the fog was the color of milk, and the small automaton walked through it and was not visible from any significant distance.

He thought about whether they would come after him.

Yes, they would. When the absence was noted, the handlers would route it to the appropriate authority, and the appropriate authority had a procedure for non-compliant matériel, and the procedure involved retrieval or deactivation. This was a fact, flat and unavoidable. He did not walk faster.

He had the wireless transfer. They knew he had it. They would know that retrieval was complicated by the wireless transfer, because he could be somewhere else when they arrived for the shell. He was not certain it would save him. It was the only thing he had.

He thought of M-774. He thought: you could not come. He thought: I know. He did not say it aloud.

The fog thickened as the light grew and he moved deeper into the interior, away from the front and the fortified positions. The air in the interior was different. Not clean - nothing here was clean - but a less compound thing, a smell that was only itself.

He walked.

He did not know where he was going. He knew where he could not go. He could not go back - not to the mine, not to the truck, not to a body that he had left in a ditch two years and a war ago, or however long it had actually been; he had stopped trusting his own count of it. He did not know if the body was dead the way the shell's own doctrine understood dead, permanent and complete, or dead the slower way - a heart still keeping its own count in a room he had heard once, briefly, through an instrument that was supposed to be doing something else to him entirely. He did not know which. He had decided, walking, that not knowing was the only mercy left available to him, and he was going to keep it for as long as the walking let him.

He walked and the fog moved around him and there was no sound from the line.

He had been going for a long time when he said it.

He said it not because he had decided to and not because the moment was weighted with intention. He said it because he was alone and the fog was quiet and the name was still his and he was not willing to arrive at the place he was going - wherever that was - without having said it once, to someone, even if the someone was only himself.

He said: Aldenvast.

He said it once.

He did not say it again.

The road went forward and the fog was thick and the small automaton moved through the gray light. There was nothing behind him that he wanted to carry forward. There was the road ahead and the fog and somewhere at the end of a walk he could not yet measure, something he had not yet found a name for.

He was going toward it.

## The Real World

[Beat 14]
He was three days out from the line, sleeping - if that was still the word for what the shell did instead of sleeping - in the lee of a stone wall that had once belonged to something, when he heard the voices clearest they had ever come.

Not one voice this time. Several, low, taking turns, the particular rhythm of people who have said everything there is to say to each other and are now only keeping each other company through the last of it. He knew one of them the way a man knows his mother's voice through a floor, before language, before the words resolve into words at all. He did not catch what she said. He caught that she was crying while she said it, and that someone else - a man, older, careful - was answering her in the tone reserved for the thing you say because silence would be worse, not because you believe it.

He heard the machine before he understood he was hearing a machine. Even, patient, indifferent to the room it was keeping time in - the same small bell tolling a number instead of an hour that he had first noticed in the intake chamber and had never found a word for, because he had not let himself look for one. He had a word for it now. He had heard the real thing, once, waiting outside a room while his own father breathed on the other side of a door too thin to keep the sound out, the year before any of this. He had not thought of that in months. He thought of it now, and understood, with the specific dry clarity of a man finally permitted to know something he had spent a long time refusing to, that the sound had been in every one of these visions since the first one, underneath the voices, patient, and he had simply never once let himself listen past the voices to hear it.

A doctor's voice, unfamiliar, procedural, said something about time and comfort and what the family wanted, in the tone of a man who has said this exact sentence to a large number of families and will say it to a large number more.

His mother said something he still did not catch.

The machine's rhythm did not change. That was the thing he would keep, afterward, of all of it - that nothing about the sound itself announced what was coming. It simply, at some point he could not mark with any more precision than he could mark the compression that had first taken him out of the mine, changed its character: the even tolling drawing out, and out, past where the next beat should have landed, into a single unbroken note that did not resolve into anything else.

He knew what the note was. He had known, distantly, buried and unexamined, since the first time he had heard the machine at all and decided not to look for a word for it. He looked now. There was nowhere left not to.

The real world, on the other side of whatever thin membrane had let him overhear it in fragments for the better part of a year, had just finished being real. Whatever his mother was doing with her hands in that room, whatever the doctor wrote on his board, whatever they did next with the bed and the tubes and the space where a body had been kept alive on behalf of a man who was not in it anymore - none of it would ever reach him again, because the last channel it had ever reached him through had just gone the one direction a held note can go, and stayed there.

He had told himself, walking away from the line, that not knowing was the only mercy left available to him. He had not known, at the time, that the mercy came with an expiration on it he had no say over.

He felt it the way a man feels a rope go slack that he did not know, until that moment, he had still been holding.

Something in him that had been reaching - quietly, underneath every operational task, every engagement, every recalibration, in a direction he had never let himself name because naming it would have meant admitting he still expected an answer - stopped reaching. Not with relief. Not with the clean grief a man is owed for a thing like this, either, because grief wants a body to stand over and he had none, had never had one since a July night on a county road two years and one lifetime ago. What he had instead was smaller and worse: the specific, total absence of a sound he had spent nearly a year learning to listen for without admitting he was listening.

The fog moved around him the way it had moved every morning since the desertion. He sat with his back against the old stone wall and did not move for a long while, and the shell, which required neither rest nor comfort, gave him nothing to do with the not-moving except be present inside it, entirely, for the first time since the Taking.

He got up, eventually, because the shell did not tire and staying down had stopped serving any purpose the moment the purpose left with the sound. He walked on. There was a river ahead, and beyond it a city large enough to disappear into, and beyond that, though he did not know it yet and would not for some months, a woman on a road who would ask him, kneeling, to come out of a hiding place with her hand open and her blade sheathed.

He walked toward the river because the river was there, and because walking was the only verb the shell had ever required of him, and it required it still.
