# The Interview

## The Interview

[Beat 1]
The rain had been intermittent for three hours and was now consistent — the fine kind that didn't fall so much as accumulate, a GLMZ spring rain that found every gap between collar and skin and settled there, the lake two kilometers east pushing cold ahead of it and the towers overhead cutting the sky into corridors. Kyle was on West Town cross-streets at 02:14 because the job had run long and the direct route back would have taken him through Lotus Syndicate carrier territory two nights after he'd turned down their standing offer, and he had decided that the indirect route was the more efficient one.

The block before this one had taken eleven steps to cross when the geometry said nine, and there had been a half-second where the streetlight read at a frequency that wasn't quite the frequency streetlights read at, and a low hum under the ferrocrete that arrived a beat before he could have named it as the Pulse running deep beneath West Town. He'd noted it and filed it the way he filed weather. West Town had a few stretches that did that now. You learned them or you didn't.

He read the six positions at twenty meters.

Not confirmed at twenty meters — triangulated. A window reflection at the east end of the block showed the wrong shape of nothing. The particular quality of quiet from the delivery alcove on the left meant someone was trying to make a delivery alcove sound like a delivery alcove. The dog drone's thermal signature two floors above was running a holding pattern that no courier drone held. He committed to ten more steps because the spacing mattered: six positions could be a cordon or a bracket, and the difference was geometric.

It was a bracket. He took the ten steps.

[Beat 2]
Mira called *Now* from somewhere he still couldn't place, and Kyle drew Silence.

The draw was one motion — the saya clearing, the blade arriving on line, a length of matte black that the cross-street's wet light touched and could not hold. No flourish. Fourteen years of the same draw, fitted to the body the way the body had been fitted to the work.

Kyle moved into the six of them.

He moved the way he moved when no one was watching, which was the only way he moved. The wet cross-street was a stage he hadn't booked. None of that changed the work.

[Beat 3]
KT came first, the way augmented arms always came first — the confidence of a limb that moved faster than the CNS that authorized it. Chrome-chased, industrial housing, the knuckles reinforced in a way that was decorative on a person who already hit hard enough to make decoration redundant. She swung wide and high.

Kyle stepped into it, caught the wrist at the soft joint where the housing met the biological sleeve, and turned it — a lever worked through the interface gap, the arm's own torque steered eight degrees past what the housing allowed. The limb's safety clutch tripped. KT's arm stopped mid-extension and she stumbled into it. Kyle put her into the cross-street's central barrier with her own momentum and kept moving.

Behind him, Mira called something to Nines.

The call was late.

In a real ambush — in the kind of ambush these six people were capable of running — Nines would have been moving already, keyed off KT's attack, no call required. The call was a coordination cue, not an emergency response. Someone was running a protocol. Kyle filed it and kept moving.

[Beat 4]
Rook closed from the left — large, a head taller than Kyle, the kind of build that translated well into close work. BCI port at his left temple pulsing blue-amber in the rain. He closed without a weapon drawn, which was either confidence or instruction.

The dog drone swept in from the right at the same moment. Standard security chassis, the stun emitter on the underbelly already charged and aimed. Kyle read the trajectory and kept moving.

The emitter fired.

It discharged at threshold — technically a lethal-threshold-safe setting, enough power to put a person down without damaging their hardware. It also discharged approximately forty centimeters short of Kyle's position, at an angle that required the drone to have deliberately miscalculated its own targeting solution.

An audition crew throttled its stun emitter. A crew trying to incapacitate a target did not throttle its stun emitter. He had two tells now and he had not yet changed anything about his approach.

[Beat 5]
Rook threw a body block — no weapon, deliberate — and Kyle redirected it, using Rook's mass and vector to carry himself into the space that opened behind it. The maneuver put Kyle against the median planter, Rook momentarily tangled in his own momentum.

He came around the planter edge at a low angle, caught KT's augmented arm at the elbow housing, and drove a thumb into the recessed emergency lockout the manufacturer had built in for industrial accident scenarios — the hard shutdown every licensed limb carries and almost no one expects a stranger to know the location of. The arm seized into a neutral position and KT, already prone, was now effectively secured by her own hardware.

She was alive. She was not going anywhere. That was all that had been required.

He stood.

Mira's voice came from somewhere above and left, calling a status check on KT. The voice was tight. Kyle tracked the specific quality of it: not the tightness of distress, not a captain watching her crew taken apart. The tightness of a rubric being updated.

That was the third tell.

[Beat 6]
He went over the planter in two moves — left foot on the edge, right hand on the rail, momentum carrying him up to the fire escape landing before Rook had completed his turn. The fire escape gave him vertical height and geometry Mira hadn't planned for. From the landing he could see the spotter now: a sixth position, three windows up in the building across the street, watching through what he'd been told was a one-way thermal overlay. Kyle filed the face without looking at it directly.

Nines was already coming up the stairs.

The wrist brace on Nines was the kind issued to workers with repetitive stress injuries in GLMZ's industrial corridor — adaptive, pneumatic, the locking mechanism triggered by impact. Kyle caught the brace at full extension on Nines's downswing, reversed the pressure in the joint, and held it there until the brace's own emergency lock engaged. The weapon in Nines's hand transferred to Kyle's in the same motion. Kyle set it on the railing without looking at it.

Below and south, Dex broke from the alcove and ran.

Kyle watched Dex go. There were always one or two whose job was to carry the information out if the engagement went wrong, and Dex had the look of someone whose job that was — younger, lighter boots, the lateral speed that served running better than fighting. Kyle had no particular interest in the information Dex was carrying. The information was that this had happened. He was comfortable with the city knowing that this had happened.

[Beat 7]
Rook was at the base of the fire escape, looking up.

The BCI port at his left temple was cycling through a sync pattern — active, seeking, the drone repositioned to a hold position that Rook was apparently trying to recall. The sync light pulsed amber-blue in the rain like something that had not yet decided what it was.

Kyle came down the fire escape at a pace that was not hurrying and was not slowing.

He moved around Rook's guard — the guard was large and honest and entirely legible — and drove the butt of Silence's tsuka into the BCI port's external housing at the temple: a short, measured strike, weighted to jar the sync interface into its own fault state without touching the skull beneath it. Hardware protects itself. You only have to know where it keeps its panic.

The sync light went out. Rook went down with the particular bonelessness of someone whose voluntary motor system had just lost its coordinator — not hurt, not dead, the BCI's own fail-safe routing his body into a controlled fall.

He'd be functional in twenty minutes. Kyle stood over him in the rain for a moment.

He was choosing, on this street, at 02:14, in a wet April. He was choosing each time he put someone on the ground and not in the ground.

[Beat 8]
Mira was at the planter.

She had not run. This was either discipline or professional pride or both, and Kyle respected it as the thing it was without being moved by it. She had a sidearm out, two-handed grip, the weapon aimed at the center of his chest as he walked back across the cross-street.

He had eight paces on her.

She let him take them.

At pace eight she fired — once, a single round into the subdermal mesh that covered his upper thorax. The impact registered in his hardware as a blunt-force event, nothing structural, the mesh doing exactly what the mesh was installed to do. He let the breath go. Kyle kept walking.

Silence was in his right hand, point low, matte black and giving the street nothing back — not the sodium lamps, not her muzzle flash, nothing. There was nothing to read on the blade because the blade offered nothing; there never had been. What there was to read was the man: a single round absorbed center mass, the pace unchanged, the point held low and patient through all eight paces, six minutes of work behind him done clean and precise with nothing in it wasted — and Mira had been watching all of it, and she could do the arithmetic from where she stood.

She dropped the weapon. Not a surrender — a data point. She had everything she needed.

[Beat 9]
"That was an audition," Mira said.

She said it professionally. She was standing, not on the ground, because she had not lost this fight in the sense that the fight had been designed for any particular outcome other than the one that had occurred. The rain came down on both of them. There was a hairline bruise deepening under her left eye — glass fragment, probably. She hadn't mentioned it.

"The Bucktown stretch," she said. "Two months ago. Fourteen crew. That was the first one." She wasn't reading off a report. She'd had the numbers long enough that they'd become sentences. "Eight didn't walk home. This was confirmation."

Kyle stood with that number for a moment.

"Lotus Syndicate wants you on payroll," she said. "I have a rate."

"No," Kyle said.

[Beat 10]
"Freelance roster," she said. "Lotus-territory contracts, routed through your fixer. Carrier rates. No further crews dispatched on you."

Kyle looked at her. The math was straightforward: the alternative was a better crew, at a time of Lotus's choosing, on a street he hadn't yet decided to avoid.

"Verbal arrangement," he said.

"Provisional," she said. "Sixty days, then we reassess."

"Until it isn't," Kyle said. "We'll know when."

Something shifted in her expression — recognition, or the specific acknowledgment of a point well made. Then it settled.

Kyle stood still for one more second. Then he nodded once and turned and walked west. By the time he reached the far side of the cross-street, the planter was empty.

[Beat 11]
Kyle walked the length of West Erie home.

The rain worked at the saya's lacquer and found nothing to keep. Silence carried the way Silence always carried: easy on the back, the weight familiar enough that his body had long since stopped accounting for it separately. He had been carrying it for fourteen years and he no longer knew what he weighed without it.

West Erie ran him out of West Town and into the blocks before the Pivot, the residential walk-ups with their vertical gardens and their climate-managed lobbies and their shared infrastructure that smelled of recycled air and other people's cooking. At this hour the street was quiet in the way streets were quiet when the people who filled them during the day were all accounted for — in beds and apartments and the spaces they had organized their lives around.

The Pivot's front door was unlocked for residents. He went in. He went up.

[Beat 12]
He sat on his bed with Silence across his thighs.

He ran three fingers along the flat, the morning check arrived early, and the edge gave him back the only reading it had ever supported: no new fractures. He sat with his hands resting on either side of it and the room quiet around him and the freelance arrangement settling somewhere in his architecture with the particular weight of a decision made.

The next route Sable sent him would be a Lotus route. He didn't know the name of the contract. He knew it would come through the cracked terminal behind the water recycler three blocks east, or through one of the other three terminals Sable used, and it would arrive the way they all arrived — flat, specific, the rate and the window and the minimum information required for the decision. He would decide with the information available, the way he always decided.

The last one that hadn't been a Lotus route had arrived too clean. No listed operator, a client that resolved to a shell when Sable ran it, the rate calibrated to the work down to the third decimal in a way no human fixer bothered with. Sable had flagged it the way you'd flag a smell you couldn't place. Kyle had noticed the shape of it and not concluded anything. That was all.

Kyle sat with the blade across his legs and the city outside the window doing what the city did at 03:00.
