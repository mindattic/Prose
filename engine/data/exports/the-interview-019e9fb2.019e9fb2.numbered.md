# The Interview

_Kyle walks a defecting clerk out of Lotus Syndicate territory and learns what it costs to be noticed: a city that closes door by door, an invitation under engineered cherry trees, the best bowl in the GLMZ, and a six-person audition on a wet cross-street. The test is a door opening._

[Beat 1]
Two months earlier there was a corridor in Bucktown, and the corridor was where the trouble started, although Kyle would not learn whose trouble it was until the invoices came due.

The client was a small woman with flour on her sleeves who had spent nine years running somebody's counting room and had decided, on a Tuesday, to stop. The contract said escort, dockside, one night, double rate for any hour past midnight. It had come through clean. What the contract had not said was that the somebody was the Lotus Syndicate, and that the syndicate's position on retiring counting-room clerks was under active review by fourteen people in the corridor between her safehouse and the dock.

The corridor was service infrastructure, two meters wide, no light worth the name. They came in fours. There was no room to read anyone, no floor to give ground on, no half-second to find a clutch or a lockout or a seam — there was the client's breathing going high behind him and the dark coming in waves with knives in it, and Silence answered the way Silence answered when the question was put that way.

He got her to the dock. She sailed at 03:40 with the flour still on her sleeves. He rode home wet to the bone, racked the blade without the morning check, and slept eleven hours. He did not count the corridor. The corridor had not left him the time to count it.

[Beat 2]
The pay cleared at dawn. By the end of the week, the city had begun, in small ways, to close.

The parts man on Ashland was out of stock — out of stock of everything, suddenly, for him specifically, with an apology that did not survive eye contact. A carrier who had run his packages for two years stopped answering. The second week, a door that had always been open at the Damen lift was locked when he reached it, and the man who kept it watched him try the handle through the glass and did not move.

Kyle took inventory the way he took inventory of wounds: pressed each one once, catalogued what it gave back. Somebody with reach was spending money to make him feel the size of the city. Reach like that came from exactly three places, and two of them would have just shot him.

[Beat 3]
Sable said it flat, through the cracked terminal, in the voice she used for line items.

"You're flagged. Lotus paper, syndicate-wide, two weeks old. No contract that touches their territory routes to you — and before you ask, their territory is a third of the working city. The third with the money in it."

"The Bucktown job was clean."

"The job was clean. The client was theirs. A counting-room clerk walks out with nine years of their arithmetic in her head, and the man who walked her out is either a rival's asset or the most expensive accident of the quarter." A pause, the length of a decision. "Here's what I'd want you to notice. When a syndicate decides you're a fly, the paper says kill. This paper doesn't say kill. It says hold."

"Hold for what?" he asked.

"That," Sable said, "is the interesting part."

[Beat 4]
The invitation came on the eighteenth day, and it was not a hit, which by then was itself information.

A kid in a dry coat — dry, in that week's rain, which said car, which said money — stood at the mouth of the alley behind The Pivot at a polite distance and held out a card with both hands, the way you hand over a thing that belongs to someone more important than you. Heavy stock. A single blossom stamped in the corner, five petals, no name. An address in the Narrows. A time, two days out, early evening.

And one line, brushed by hand: BRING THE SWORD.

The kid waited until Kyle took the card, bowed eight centimeters, and left without selling anything. Kyle stood in the alley and read the line twice. Every crew in the city would have written come unarmed. He had just been invited by the only people who considered the sword part of the man.

[Beat 5]
Lotus territory announced itself by subtraction. The streetlights all worked. The gutters ran clear. The walls carried no tags, the boys on the corners stood like staff instead of weather, and nobody begged. Kyle catalogued how much enforcement it took to make a neighborhood this quiet, and kept his hands easy at his sides.

Then the arcade opened ahead of him, and the trees made their argument.

Cherry trees, eleven of them, planted in steel collars down the arcade's spine, in full bloom in the wrong month — engineered stock, the blossoms running white to a pink with blood in it, lit from below so the canopy floated on the dark. Petals came down with the rain and lay on the wet concrete like the receipts of some enormous purchase. A vendor's grill smoked under the third tree. Two children ran a circuit around the trunks, screaming on the inhale, and nobody hushed them.

It was the most beautiful street Kyle had seen in eleven years of working streets, and someone had built it on purpose, with money that had blood in it the same way the pink did, and both of those things stayed true at once all the way down the arcade.

[Beat 6]
The address was a ramen-ya at the arcade's end — eight stools, a cedar counter gone gold with handling, a noren curtain the rain had not been allowed to touch. The smell arrived before the door did: tonkotsu run long past patience, char off the burners, something green cut fresh in the last minute.

Three of the eight stools were taken. Kyle read the room on reflex: a heavyset man with a cleaver-arm conversion, eating with the careful manners of the recently dangerous; a woman whose coat was worth more than guns; a courier still wearing her route. Crews from rival ledgers, eating in a row, because the counter was the counter. Weapons worn openly. Nothing checked at the door but behavior.

Kyle sat at the empty end. Nobody asked him to surrender anything. Nobody pretended not to see the saya. The cook looked at him once, the way Mrs. Chen looked at him, made a decision involving extra char siu, and built the bowl without taking an order.

It was, he admitted, around the fourth mouthful, the best bowl in the city. The admission felt like a small betrayal. He ate it anyway, and the betrayal got smaller with every mouthful, which was probably the point of the bowl.

[Beat 7]
The man came in at the hour on the card, alone, shook the rain off an umbrella and left it furled by the door. Mid-sixties. A suit without one concession to fashion in it. He took the stool beside Kyle with a small exhale, and the cook had tea in front of him before the exhale finished, and everyone at the counter got four centimeters taller without looking up.

"Branch Manager for the Narrows," he said, by way of a name. "You cost us eight people and a counting room."

"The contract was an escort. I escorted."

"Yes. We reviewed it. The contract was legitimate, your conduct was correct, and our crew engaged first." He drank his tea. "The eight are our failure — we sent fourteen to do a thing we had not priced. The clerk is our failure twice over; people do not leave rooms they are treated well in. You are the only part of that night that performed to specification."

Kyle set his chopsticks across the bowl. "And the flag? The locked doors, the carrier, the parts man?"

"An assessment period. Concluded." The Branch Manager turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "You will find the doors open on your way home, and the parts man restocked, with our apologies — we will cover what the delay cost you, at list." He said it the way Kyle said standard rate: an architecture, not a kindness. "We pay for what we break. It is most of what we are."

[Beat 8]
"Payroll," the Branch Manager said. "House rate, house colors, a chair at this counter with your name on the wood in five years, if the wood agrees."

Down the counter, an old man Kyle had taken for a customer had unrolled a cloth and was cleaning the cook's spare knives with the attention of a surgeon at a christening — a togishi, a polisher, the trade older than the syndicate and the reason half the syndicate's steel held an edge. He had not looked at Kyle once. He had looked at the saya several times.

"No," Kyle said. "I don't wear colors."

The Branch Manager nodded, unsurprised, the way a man nods at weather that confirms the forecast. "The clerk who left us said that word in this seat, nine years ago. Then she sat down anyway." He stood and reclaimed the umbrella. "The offer stands. Standing offers are how we say please."

At the door he paused, as if remembering a detail of no importance. "The polisher will look at your blade, if you wish. No charge — no." He corrected himself with the first visible amusement of the evening. "You would insist. One Φ. He has been wondering about it since you sat down."

Kyle paid the one Φ. The old man took Silence with both hands, read the matte black flat under the counter light for a long minute, and handed it back the way you return someone else's letter.

"Kept correctly," the polisher said. "No maker's mark. No file history. A blade with no parents." He went back to the cook's knives. "It is in good order. It is also the loneliest piece of steel I have handled in forty years, and I have handled funeral swords."

Kyle seated it home and said nothing, and walked out under the trees, where the petals were still coming down like receipts.

## The Interview

[Beat 9]
The rain had been intermittent for three hours and was now consistent — the fine kind that didn't fall so much as accumulate, finding every gap between collar and skin and settling there. The lake two kilometers east pushed cold ahead of it, and the towers overhead cut the sky into corridors. Kyle was on West Town cross-streets at 02:14 because the job had run long — a contract Sable had flagged on intake for arriving too clean, and which had then gone exactly as written — and because the direct route back would have taken him through Lotus Syndicate carrier territory two nights after he'd turned down their standing offer. He had decided that the indirect route was the more efficient one.

The block before this one had run eleven steps when the geometry said nine. He'd noted it and moved on. West Town had a few stretches that did that now.

He read the six positions at twenty meters.

Not confirmed at twenty meters — triangulated. A window reflection at the east end of the block showed the wrong shape of nothing. The quiet from the delivery alcove on the left was wrong: someone in it was trying to make a delivery alcove sound like a delivery alcove. The dog drone's thermal signature two floors above was running a holding pattern that no courier drone held. He committed to ten more steps because the spacing mattered: six positions could be a cordon or a bracket, and the difference was geometric.

It was a bracket. He took the ten steps.

[Beat 10]
Mira called *Now* from somewhere he still couldn't place, and Kyle drew Silence.

The draw was one motion — the saya clearing, the blade arriving on line, a length of matte black that the cross-street's wet light touched and could not hold. No flourish. Fourteen years of the same draw, fitted to the body the way the body had been fitted to the work.

Kyle moved into the six of them.

He moved like a man with somewhere to be and no interest in being seen getting there. The wet cross-street was a stage he hadn't booked. None of that changed the work.

[Beat 11]
KT came first, the way augmented arms always came first — the confidence of a limb that moved faster than the CNS that authorized it. Chrome-chased, industrial housing, the knuckles reinforced in a way that was decorative on a person who already hit hard enough to make decoration redundant. She swung wide and high.

Kyle stepped into it, caught the wrist at the soft joint where the housing met the biological sleeve, and turned it — a lever worked through the interface gap, the arm's own torque steered eight degrees past what the housing allowed. The limb's safety clutch tripped. KT's arm stopped mid-extension and she stumbled into it. Kyle put her into the cross-street's central barrier with her own momentum and kept moving.

Behind him, Mira called something to Nines.

The call was late.

In a real ambush — in the kind of ambush these six people were capable of running — Nines would have been moving already, keyed off KT's attack, no call required. The call was a coordination cue, not an emergency response. Someone was running a protocol. Kyle marked it and kept moving.

[Beat 12]
Rook closed from the left — large, a head taller than Kyle, the kind of build that translated well into close work. BCI port at his left temple pulsing blue-amber in the rain. He closed without a weapon drawn, which was either confidence or instruction.

The dog drone swept in from the right at the same moment. Standard security chassis, the stun emitter on the underbelly already charged and aimed. Kyle read the trajectory and kept moving.

The emitter fired.

It discharged at threshold — technically a lethal-threshold-safe setting, enough power to put a person down without damaging their hardware. It also discharged approximately forty centimeters short of Kyle's position, at an angle that required the drone to have deliberately miscalculated its own targeting solution.

An audition crew throttled its stun emitter. A crew trying to incapacitate a target did not throttle its stun emitter. He had two tells now and he had not yet changed anything about his approach.

[Beat 13]
Rook threw a body block — no weapon, deliberate — and Kyle redirected it, using Rook's mass and vector to carry himself into the space that opened behind it. The maneuver put Kyle against the median planter, Rook momentarily tangled in his own momentum.

He came around the planter edge at a low angle, caught KT's augmented arm at the elbow housing, and drove a thumb into the recessed emergency lockout the manufacturer had built in for industrial accident scenarios — the hard shutdown every licensed limb carries and almost no one expects a stranger to know the location of. The arm seized into a neutral position and KT, already prone, was now effectively secured by her own hardware.

She was alive. She was swearing into the wet ferrocrete — young, furious, a voice that hadn't yet learned to keep fear out of anger. She was not going anywhere. That was all that had been required.

He stood.

Mira's voice came from somewhere above and left, calling a status check on KT. The voice was tight. Kyle tracked it: not the tightness of distress, not a captain watching her crew taken apart. The tightness of a rubric being updated.

That was the third tell.

[Beat 14]
He went over the planter in two moves — left foot on the edge, right hand on the rail, momentum carrying him up to the fire escape landing before Rook had completed his turn. The fire escape gave him vertical height and geometry Mira hadn't planned for. From the landing he could see the spotter now: a sixth position, three windows up in the building across the street, watching through what he'd been told was a one-way thermal overlay. Kyle filed the face without looking at it directly.

Nines was already coming up the stairs.

The wrist brace on Nines was the kind issued to workers with repetitive stress injuries in GLMZ's industrial corridor — adaptive, pneumatic, the locking mechanism triggered by impact. Kyle caught the brace at full extension on Nines's downswing, reversed the pressure in the joint, and held it there until the brace's own emergency lock engaged. The weapon in Nines's hand transferred to Kyle's in the same motion. Kyle set it on the railing without looking at it.

Below and south, Dex broke from the alcove and ran.

Kyle watched Dex go. There were always one or two whose job was to carry the information out if the engagement went wrong, and Dex had the look of someone whose job that was — younger, lighter boots, the lateral speed that served running better than fighting. Kyle had no particular interest in the information Dex was carrying. The information was that this had happened. He was comfortable with the city knowing that this had happened.

[Beat 15]
Rook was at the base of the fire escape, looking up.

The BCI port at his left temple was cycling through a sync pattern — active, seeking, the drone repositioned to a hold position that Rook was apparently trying to recall. The sync light pulsed amber-blue in the rain.

Kyle came down the fire escape at a pace that was not hurrying and was not slowing.

He moved around Rook's guard — the guard was large and honest and entirely legible — and drove the butt of Silence's tsuka into the BCI port's external housing at the temple: a short, measured strike, weighted to jar the sync interface into its own fault state without touching the skull beneath it. Hardware protects itself. You only have to know where it keeps its panic.

The sync light went out. Rook went down with the particular bonelessness of someone whose voluntary motor system had just lost its coordinator — not hurt, not dead, the BCI's own fail-safe routing his body into a controlled fall.

He'd be functional in twenty minutes. Kyle stood over him in the rain for a moment.

He was choosing, on this street, at 02:14, in a wet April. The disarming line was always slower than the killing line — tenths of a second spent finding a clutch, a lockout, a port, paid out in range of people who were not returning the courtesy. He paid them each time he put someone on the ground and not in the ground.

[Beat 16]
Mira was at the planter.

She had not run. This was either discipline or professional pride or both, and Kyle respected it as the thing it was without being moved by it. She had a sidearm out, two-handed grip, the weapon aimed at the center of his chest as he walked back across the cross-street.

He had eight paces on her.

She let him take them.

At pace eight she fired — once, a single round into the subdermal mesh that covered his upper thorax. The impact registered in his hardware as a blunt-force event, nothing structural, the mesh doing exactly what the mesh was installed to do. He let the breath go. Kyle kept walking.

Silence was in his right hand, point low, matte black and giving the street nothing back — not the sodium lamps, not her muzzle flash, nothing. There was nothing to read on the blade because the blade offered nothing; there never had been. What there was to read was the man: a single round absorbed center mass, the pace unchanged, the point held low and patient through all eight paces, six minutes of work behind him done clean and precise with nothing in it wasted — and Mira had been watching all of it, and she could do the arithmetic from where she stood.

She dropped the weapon. Not a surrender — a data point. She had everything she needed.

[Beat 17]
"That was an audition," Mira said.

She was standing, not on the ground, because she had not lost this fight in the sense that the fight had been designed for any particular outcome other than the one that had occurred. The rain came down on both of them. There was a hairline bruise deepening under her left eye — glass fragment, probably. She hadn't mentioned it.

"The Bucktown stretch," she said. "Two months ago. Fourteen crew. That was the first one." She wasn't reading off a report. She'd had the numbers long enough that they'd become sentences. "Eight didn't walk home. This was confirmation."

Kyle seated Silence back into the saya. His thumb stayed on the guard a half-second past the click, the way it didn't usually.

Eight. He could replay the Bucktown stretch in order — every position, every line he'd taken, a corridor fight in the dark with no time to find the lines that put people on the ground instead of in it. He had not known the number until now. He stood with it while the rain came off his jaw, and the night reorganized itself around a simpler arithmetic: Lotus had spent eight people on a question, and sent six more tonight to check the answer.

"Lotus Syndicate wants you on payroll," she said. "I have a rate."

"No," Kyle said.

[Beat 18]
"Freelance roster," she said. "Lotus-territory contracts, routed through your fixer. Carrier rates. No further crews dispatched on you."

She crouched and picked her sidearm up off the wet ferrocrete and holstered it, unhurried, letting him watch her do it. Then she stood with her hands empty at her sides.

Kyle looked at her. The math was straightforward: the alternative was a better crew, at a time of Lotus's choosing, on a street he hadn't yet decided to avoid.

"Your crew's hardware goes on Lotus's account," he said. "The arm, the port, the brace. List price."

"You broke them," Mira said.

"You billed them to an audition. They walk home whole or there's no arrangement."

"They'd have shot you for free, you know." It was the first thing she'd said all night that didn't sound like a rate.

Kyle let it stand there. It was probably true. KT was still swearing somewhere behind him, young and loyal to people who had spent her, and he gave the line the half-second it had earned.

"Done," Mira said.

"Verbal arrangement," he said.

"Provisional," she said. "Sixty days, then we reassess."

"Until it isn't," Kyle said. "We'll know when."

She took something from her breast pocket and set it on the planter's rim where he could take it or leave it: a lacquered chip the size of a bottle cap, deep red, a single blossom stamped in gold, five petals, no name. The twin of the card that had come to the alley, rendered in a material that said the assessment period was over.

"Roster marker," she said. "Any Lotus counter in the city seats you. You pay carrier rates like everyone else on the roster." A beat. "The bowl's better than the politics."

He picked it up. It weighed what good lacquer weighs, which is more than it should.

The corner of her mouth moved — a millimeter, nothing professional in it — and was put away.

Kyle stood still for one more second. Then he nodded once and turned and walked west. By the time he reached the far side of the cross-street, the planter was empty.

[Beat 19]
Kyle walked the length of West Erie home.

The rain kept on, steady enough now that it no longer needed his attention. Silence carried the way Silence always carried: easy on the back, the weight familiar enough that his body had long since stopped accounting for it separately. He had been carrying it for fourteen years, long enough that his balance assumed it.

The night kitchens on the Erie blocks were doing their last hour, steam standing over the stalls like it had been paid to hold the corner. He passed the autokitchen first — clean glass, no queue, the menu cycling its colors for nobody — and then the human stall four doors down, where a cook with graft-glassed palms pulled a steel pan off open flame barehanded and six people stood in actual rain to wait for what was in it. The air off the autokitchen window was the scrubbed, mineral nothing of a box wiped down on a schedule and fed no one, and it thinned as he came level with the human stall and gave way to scorched oil and the particular sweetness of something nobody had wiped in years. The machine was faster and cheaper and its window was empty. Nobody on the street would have been able to invoice the difference. Everybody on the street was standing in it.

Mrs. Chen's stall was banked for the night when he passed it, the burner down to its pilot ring, and she was working the counter with the long slow strokes she saved for the end of a shift. She looked up before his footsteps could have told her anything — she always did — and what crossed her face was not a greeting. It was the look she gave a bowl that came back wrong: something off in the composition, nothing she could name, nothing she was going to say across four meters of rain. She held it for exactly as long as it took him to pass out of the stall's light. Then she went back to the counter, and the strokes were shorter.

West Erie ran him out of West Town and into the blocks before the Pivot, where the walk-ups slept behind their vertical gardens and the only thing moving was the rain. The Pivot's front door was unlocked for residents. He went in. He went up.

[Beat 20]
He sat on his bed with Silence across his thighs.

He ran three fingers along the flat, the morning check arrived early, and the edge gave him back the only reading it had ever supported: no new fractures. He sat with his hands resting on either side of it and the room quiet around him and the freelance arrangement settling somewhere in his architecture. A decision, made.

The next route Sable sent him would be a Lotus route. It would come through the cracked terminal behind the water recycler, or one of the other three Sable used, and it would arrive the way they all arrived — flat, specific, the minimum information required for the decision. Tonight's job was already behind him, gone exactly as written, every variable where the contract said it would be. Sable had flagged it for that, the way you'd flag a smell you couldn't place, and Kyle had noticed the shape of it and not concluded anything. That was all.

On the ledge outside the window a crow had settled, larger than a crow needed to be, the rain beading off a sheen in its feathers that the dark could not quite flatten to black, and it had angled itself to face the cracked terminal and the standby light it kept. It did not move. It was attending to the terminal the way a clerk attends to a number. Kyle had seen them on this ledge before, on others; he let it watch, and pulled the check back to the blade.

Kyle sat with the blade across his legs and the city outside the window doing what the city did at 03:00.

[Beat 21]
He put Silence on the rack. The marker he took out last — red lacquer, a gold blossom, five petals, the door to the only room in the city that had not needed him explained — and he stood with it a moment in the dark before he put it in the drawer where he kept the things he had not decided about. The drawer was not crowded. It was not empty either.

The next bowl he ate was at Mrs. Chen's, at the usual stool, at the usual hour. She set it down and looked at him a half-second longer than the bowl required — whatever had been off in his composition two nights ago, she checked for it, and the strokes of her ladle came back to their usual length. He paid the standard rate. She took it without looking down.
