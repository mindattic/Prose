# Testament

_Someone reopens the Cortland Engagement case. Bear has been paying a quiet, sustained rate to sixteen families for eight years — a ledger Halcyon Civil Security needs closed. Dual timeline: Bear in the present taking work that requires nothing of him, and Bear in the past watching a Halcyon commander bury forty-three people in a corporate report. The villain is not the person who reopened the case. It is the institution that needs Bear to have been wrong so it can stay clean. Arc 4 (CorpoNation cover)._

[Beat 1]
Third night of the same contract. The client wanted something on the loading dock that didn't move, and Bear was good at being something that didn't move — better than most things, because most things couldn't hold a position for six hours without their body registering a complaint somewhere in the stack. Bear's body registered no complaints. He had paid a great deal for a body that did not register complaints: the augmented frame, the Strix Harvest Frame woven through his torso — piezoelectric filament and kinetic accumulator lattice that drank in every step and every breath and banked it as charge. He could feel it at low rest: a warmth against his sternum, steady as a pilot light.

He had woken at 1800 from the same dream — a light on all night in a second-floor window, and a shadow behind the curtain — and come to work.

The dock was in the Ironbend district, a converted freight terminal that still smelled like the sulfur-processing plant that used to share the block. Bear had been noticing the smell since night one. The dock workers had stopped noticing it years ago; he could tell by the way they held their lunches over it without adjusting. He kept noticing it on principle. There was probably a lesson in that — what you stop clocking when something is always there — and he had decided the lesson was for other people.

A turret cat had taken up residence on a high shelf of empty pallets and was running its own overwatch on the dock floor. Eight kilograms, maybe. It sat without moving for four hours, adjusted its position three times, each adjustment covering a new sector. Bear watched it for a while. It had better noise discipline than half the operators he had shared a perimeter with.

His neuretics flagged an incoming message at 0217 — the in-skull hardware queuing it behind his eyes, text ready the moment he blinked for it. He read the sender and put it back.

He did not open it until the shift ended.

[Beat 2]
The comm flagged colonel at 0541. Not Halcyon Legal — the line was personal, not routed through any division. Bear took the call.

"Boris." The voice had the same register as eleven years of morning briefings: precise, unhurried, already past the part where it needed to establish anything.

"Colonel."

"I imagine you know what today is."

"I do."

"I made some calls last night." A pause — the specific kind that preceded the relevant information in a briefing, not the kind that was looking for a response. "The sub-license review committee convenes Thursday. Not November. The authorization suspension protocol runs forty-eight hours after the committee sits."

Bear said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

"I'm not asking you to reconsider." Another pause. "I know the Ironbend workers named it. I know what that is."

Bear let him have it.

"I appreciate the courtesy, Colonel. Take care of yourself."

He ended the call.

The turret cat had moved twice while he was on the comm. He put on his jacket — the heavy one, the one that looked like he'd bought it somewhere reasonable, but was eleven centimeters too wide in the shoulders for a man who hadn't paid for those shoulders. His beard needed attention. It did not get it.

He ran the Manowar status through his neuretics: accumulation at thirty-four percent, authorization green. It would be ready.

He left.

[Beat 3]
*Eight years earlier.*

The Cortland compound had a laundry line inside the perimeter fence. Bear counted sixteen items during the approach assessment — work clothes, civilian mix. At the far end: a child's yellow rain jacket. He stopped the count there. He did not want to be wrong about that jacket. The light in the second-floor window on the east side was already on.

His unit was eight operators, eastern perimeter, support role for a Meridian-sector civil security action. Bear was the senior NCO. Two of the eight were within their first six months. The younger of the two kept touching her sidearm in a non-draw motion — palm to grip, releasing, palm to grip — a habit Bear recognized. She was managing something. He didn't say anything about it. You don't name a thing like that before an operation. You watch it, and you make sure you know where she is.

The compound smelled like cold concrete and machine lubricant. The light was low, late-day gray. There was a ventilation fan running somewhere inside the building — a tick-and-whir every eight seconds, regular enough to count by. Bear felt the Manowar warm on cycle against his sternum — the equipment ready, the charge holding. He counted the fan for a while.

[Beat 4]
The message was from a Halcyon Civil Security Internal Review Board notification system. Automated. It advised him that a petition had been filed under Civil Accountability Code 14.2, requesting a status audit of Case CE-0217, the Cortland Engagement. It noted that his name appeared in the petition as a material witness. It noted that a response was not required at this time but that he might wish to contact his legal representative.

Bear read it twice on the walk back to the transit stop, then put his neuretics to background. He didn't need it a third time. He knew exactly what CE-0217 held.

The streets in Ironbend at 0300 were quiet in the way industrial corridors are quiet — not empty, just occupied by people who had agreed nobody else was there. A Behemoth cargo unit moved along the far lane, lights off, running on its routing logic. A woman at a corner kiosk was selling something in paper bags and not meeting eyes.

The temperature had dropped. He could feel the warmth of the Manowar along his chest — the cycle running on whatever he had put into it. The dock. The walk. The cold. He had stopped noticing the warmth years ago, the same way you stop noticing a familiar smell. He noticed it now.

He had not had a legal representative for eight years. He had not needed one. He had been very clear, when he walked off the base at Cortland, about what he was and was not going to do.

Hana Morimoto-Ferreira had found him. Which meant she had been looking. Which meant someone, somewhere in two years of civil accountability filings, had decided it was worth looking.

He ran the math on that at the transit stop. Then he went home and paid for the next month's rent.

[Beat 5]
Colonel Brandt ran the briefing standing. He had a portable display and he moved through the items at a pace that communicated: this is a formality.

There were three flagged items in the intelligence packet. The first was a confirmed identification: a cell of four operators with documented history, known weapons profile, last confirmed position inside the compound perimeter. The second was an unknown: a group of twelve unverified individuals, insufficient data for classification. The third item was nine lines long. It was flagged yellow rather than red. It read: *possible non-combatants, unverified — a combination of labor consortium affiliation and the presence of family units, including minors; source reliability moderate; no confirmation of weapons; recommend further assessment before engagement.*

The colonel moved to logistics before Bear had finished reading.

Bear looked at the display after the colonel moved on. The logistics chart had superseded the intelligence packet, but Brandt had not dismissed it — the yellow flag was still visible at the corner of the screen, behind the slide. Bear sat with his hands on his knees. He knew what the colonel had left behind. He listened to the rest.

The briefing closed. Bear saluted with the others and filed out.

[Beat 6]
He did not think of what he owed as guilt. Guilt was about the person who felt it. What Bear owed was about the forty-three, and he had a word for it, and the word was obligation.

Sixteen names. Sixteen account numbers — three had been too young to manage funds when he started, so he had opened trust accounts, which they had since taken over directly. The amounts were fixed: a rate he had calculated in the third month after walking off the base, based on his best estimate of what a sustained, non-interest-bearing obligation looked like over twenty years. He had not adjusted it since.

One account had no living recipient — filed against an estate, no family found. He sent the transfer to a civil victims' endowment on the twenty-first. He had done this for eight years. He did not know her name. He knew the child's jacket had been yellow.

Account eleven — the trust he had opened for a nine-year-old — had a standing redirect on forty percent of the monthly transfer, routing it to a bursary fund at Meridian Community College. Bear did not know what they were studying. He noted the redirect cleared correctly and moved on. The other two trust accounts belonged to people who were now seventeen and fourteen. He did not know their given names. He had decided it was cleaner not to.

Eight years of the twenty-first of every month. He had never missed one.

[Beat 7]
0300. Bear held the eastern perimeter position. The compound was quiet.

A light was on in the second-floor window. It had been on since 2200 — five hours, someone working late or not sleeping. He watched it. He did not look away from it. He knew what was in that building.

At 0215, a shadow had moved behind the curtain — slow and deliberate, the particular weight-shift of late pregnancy. She had crossed the room and returned. He had logged it: civilian, mobile, non-combatant. Second floor. He had kept the count. She was in it.

The ventilation fan ran its cycle. Tick-and-whir. He had been here long enough to know when it was coming.

The radio crackled with a single encrypted burst at 0311, and the stillness came back. Bear held the position. He held it the way he held all positions, which was completely.

He checked on the younger operator at midnight and again at 0300. By the second check, the palm-to-grip habit was gone. Whatever she had been managing, she had managed it.

At 0317, the operation began.

[Beat 8]
She found him the week before the IRB panel date.

She had his building address, which wasn't listed anywhere — she had found it through transit records and a forwarding arrangement he had stopped updating three years prior. She mentioned this without emphasis, the way a person describes a route they've walked a hundred times.

Her name was Hana Morimoto-Ferreira. She was a civil accountability investigator. She told him in the first two minutes that she had built the case without him and that it would proceed with or without his testimony — the warehouse registration was in the manifest, the manifest was in the procurement archive, and she had the procurement archive. She was not asking for the thing that would win the case. She was asking for the thing that was true.

She was wearing a gray field jacket with a pocket over the left breast that was frayed at the corner — the kind of fraying that comes from years of sliding a stylus in and out. The case file came from that pocket, folded, sealed with a plain adhesive tab. She said he could read it or not. She said she would be in the same place for another hour if he had questions.

He read it. The case file had the organization of something maintained, not assembled — years of incremental access, structured by someone who had been building toward this conversation for a long time. She had been building toward it without assuming he would be there at the end.

He came back after forty minutes with the file and two questions. The first was about the warehouse registration filing date — whether it preceded or postdated the Cortland operational planning period. She gave him a date and a document reference number.

The second question: "Why didn't you depose someone from the unit?"

She looked at him for a moment. "I found everyone who would have been in your position," she said. "You were the only one who kept paying on the twenty-first."

He nodded. He went upstairs. She went wherever she went.

[Beat 9]
The operation took eleven minutes.

Bear held the eastern perimeter. He had line of sight on two of the three building exits and a partial on the third. His orders were hold perimeter — prevent egress, document contacts. He held it. He listened.

He counted forty-three distinct voices in the eleven minutes. He stood twelve meters from the building. He knew the count was imprecise — voices overlap when people are afraid, the same voice registers twice at different pitches. He pressed his back teeth together at thirty-one and held them there. The number was forty-three.

The second-floor window was quiet. He noted that too.

The radio crackled at 0328: *eastern perimeter, stand down, objective secured.*

He stood down.

He has never adjusted the count.

[Beat 10]
Bear stood down at 0328 and walked the perimeter one more time because his orders were hold perimeter and the order was still technically in effect and nobody had told him explicitly that it wasn't.

Nobody told him to stop, so he walked it. He completed the eastern circuit at 0342. The compound was quiet. The ventilation fan had stopped. The light in the second-floor window on the east side was still out.

He filed the perimeter documentation at 0351. Eastern perimeter clear. No egress contacts. Count documented. He filed the count at forty-three and saved the file and looked at the file for a while and did not open it again.

At 0400 they moved to the consolidation point, a field two hundred meters north. Bear sat on his kit and waited. The younger operator was thirty meters away, back against a vehicle, not sleeping, not looking at anything in particular. He checked on her once. She said she was fine. He let it go. There are things you don't name before an operation, and there are things you don't name after one either.

He watched the compound from the consolidation point until 0700. He counted the ventilation fan when it came back on at 0419. He watched the light in the second-floor window and noted that it did not come back on. He held all of it the way he had been trained to hold intelligence: precisely, without editorial, available for later processing.

Later processing took eight years.

[Beat 11]
Bear knew how this ended before he sat down. He sat down to work through the how, not the whether.

The leverage was real — the license, the fourteen months of interface debt, the maintenance he had deferred. The Manowar's operational authorization ran through his Halcyon sub-license; the moment the license moved to suspension, the chassis compliance lock engaged and the system went dormant. Halcyon had always known that what they held over him was the right to take that away.

He found his hand at the back of his neck, where the Manowar met the base of his skull. Warm in cold weather. He left his hand there.

He was going to testify. He had known it since 0317, eight years ago, in a compound that no longer existed. Hana Morimoto-Ferreira had arrived with the means; that was the only new thing.

The how was going to involve teams. He was almost certain of that. He took his hand away from the back of his neck and thought about the route.

He had been thinking about it for three years. The identities were ready. The variable was the window — how long between the handshake and the moment Halcyon resolved the transponder lag. He had a reasonable estimate. He had been carrying it long enough to trust it.

[Beat 12]
The morning after the Cortland Engagement, Colonel Brandt assembled the NCOs in the forward briefing tent at 0700. The tent smelled like diesel and cold canvas. The light inside was gray and flat, the kind that made everything look slightly deader than it was.

He was direct. He said the Cortland Engagement had produced forty-three enemy combatants deceased. He said the after-action report would reflect this. He said the intelligence packet was under classification review pending security assessment and would not be available for reference until that review was complete.

He looked at each NCO in turn. Left to right, unhurried.

Bear was last. The colonel held eye contact for a long time. Bear looked back. He knew exactly what they were not saying. So did the colonel. Brandt had a scar under his left eye — four centimeters, old, the kind that takes a long time to go smooth. Bear had never asked about it. He noticed it again here, in the silence, the way you notice small things when the large things cannot be said.

Four seconds is long enough to hold a complete conversation between two people who understand what they are not saying.

The colonel dismissed the assembly. Bear saluted — the same motion, the same angle, the same eight seconds it had always been. He dropped it and walked out into the Cortland morning — gray, cold, smelling like everything that had burned. He felt the Manowar warm on cycle against his sternum — the device running, accumulation from the previous twelve hours. He had been noticing it all night. He noticed it again now. He was carrying a count and a long silence and the memory of a yellow flag, and he had decided, walking out of that tent, that carrying them was going to be his job from here on.

[Beat 13]
The fourth year in, he had looked up the Meridian Cross registry.

Not his entry. That was gone — he had known it would be gone, because the Cross was a Halcyon decoration and Halcyon no longer had a file on Boris Johansen that could be found. He looked up the registry itself: the list of criteria, the list of prior recipients, the dates and commands and engagements that had generated each commendation.

He read through it. He did not linger. He was checking whether the criteria said what he remembered them saying.

They did.

He had the Cross for the eastern corridor campaigns, fifteen years prior. He had held a perimeter at Brannach Station for nineteen hours under sustained contact. He had brought two NCOs out of a position that had stopped being defensible at hour seven. The panel had cited the nineteen hours and both NCOs and the specific decision he had made at hour seven. He had the Cross and the citation because the criteria were met. The criteria had not changed.

The registry said none of that had happened.

He closed it. He went back to work. He checked again four years later — standing at a public terminal in Meridian Station, a Tuesday in November. Not to confirm the erasure, but to confirm that knowing about it was still something he could carry. He could, both times. That was the information he needed.

[Beat 14]
The call came through his neuretics two days before the review panel date, flagged as business communication, Halcyon Legal Division. The liaison introduced himself as Declan Iyengar-Marsh. He had a warm voice and he used first names.

The architecture was standard: full cooperation, Bear's record spoke for him, support available if Bear needed it as he prepared his submission. Also: where was Bear working these days, and did he have any plans around the panel date.

Bear heard the questions inside the questions. He told Declan he was between contracts and hadn't decided about the panel yet. Both of these were not quite lies.

Declan Iyengar-Marsh was very good at his job.

"Thank you, Declan," Bear said. He did not use Declan's first name, because Declan had not earned that. "I'll be in touch."

He terminated the call. Sat with it for a moment. Then laughed — a short, real one, not at Declan personally but at the precision of the whole architecture: the care it had taken to build a conversation out of exactly the right silences.

Then he made an appointment for augmentation maintenance at the Halcyon-certified facility on Brecker Street. The appointment was for the day after the review panel. He paid for it in advance. He was still smiling when the payment cleared.

[Beat 15]
The charge was insubordination. Bear had refused a follow-on operational order issued seventy-two hours after the Cortland Engagement. The order was to provide security support for a warehouse clearance in the Cortland sector — same district, different building. He had been given the address and a timeline and nothing else.

He had not been given a complete intelligence packet. He had looked at the address, and the district, and the timeline — seventy-two hours after an operation that had produced forty-three enemy combatants deceased — and the building registration, which he had seen in the compound's internal logistics manifest two weeks before the engagement: a humanitarian staging facility under Meridian Compact emergency protocols, listed for temporary dependent housing for labor consortium families displaced by the security action. He had cross-referenced the manifest entry and the address and the timing and declined.

He was right about the warehouse. This was not entered into the record.

The court-martial room was a prefab administrative space on the Meridian annex: six fluorescent panels, a long table for the board, and a single chair on Bear's side that had been designed for someone considerably smaller. He sat in it anyway, rested his hands on the edge of the table, and kept his eyes on the center board member. There was an overhead ventilation duct with a tick on its cycle that was not quite regular.

The board reviewed the charge for four hours. Bear told them what he had been ordered to do and what he had refused and why. He was precise. He told the truth. He was not dramatic about it, because the facts didn't require it.

In the second hour, the senior board member asked him if there was additional contextual information he believed relevant to his decision.

Bear understood the question. He understood the intelligence item was classified. He understood what mentioning a classified item without authorization in this forum would add to the charge. He understood that the item was the only thing in the room that actually explained his decision — and that saying it would hand the board everything they needed to make the charge cleaner and the institutional record tidier.

He thought about a child's yellow rain jacket on a laundry line.

He said no.

He stated his refusal and the reason: operational judgment. Grounds for a reprimand under standard terms. Grounds for a court-martial under the enhanced operational classifications the charge carried.

He did not expect to win on the merits.

He did not win on the merits.

He walked off the base at 1430 on a Tuesday. He kept the augmentation — purchased against his salary advance, his personal property under Halcyon's own contracts. He had paid for it.

They did not pursue him. He thought he understood why: he had been right about the warehouse and had kept it to himself, and an institution that needed things kept had decided he was more useful loose than in a cell. He hadn't verified this. He didn't feel the need to.

[Beat 16]
He worked a freight security detail for the three days before the review panel. The client was a forwarding company he had worked for twice before. They did not ask him questions. He held the freight and they paid him by the hour.

On the second night, a lumen mouse ran the full length of a container rack.

Floor to ceiling, the whole way — eight kilograms of bioluminescent animal, moving with the specific confidence of something that had never once considered whether a surface wanted to be climbed. Bear watched it the whole way. It reached the top shelf, lit the rack up like a small lantern, and looked at him.

Then it went through the wall.

The dock workers were playing cards at the far end and didn't see it, and without the mouse they wouldn't have seen the wall either: the corrugated end panel had a seam in it, fold-concealed, maybe eighteen inches wide, reading as solid from any angle until something small and lit passed through and left a four-second glow on both sides of nothing. The light diminished evenly.

Bear stood at his post and looked at where the wall was not. He was six feet eight. The gap was eighteen inches. He started thinking about sideways — about what eighteen inches wanted from him, and what he could bring through it.

He confirmed the dead-drop location with Hana through the routing she'd set up. He had a route now that appeared in no Halcyon analysis of his known movements. An eight-kilogram animal had shown it to him.

[Beat 17]
The panel was at 1300. Bear was up at 0500.

He ran the sixteen transfers manually — the automatic date was the twenty-first, four days out, and he wasn't waiting. They cleared before 0600. He closed the screen.

He put together what he was bringing: three sheets of folded paper, handwritten, no neuretics trace. The warehouse address. Its registration in the compound logistics manifest — what the building had been listed as, and who it had been listed for.

He checked the apartment. There was nothing in it he needed to come back for. He left the door unlocked.

Halcyon had been running surveillance on his building for nineteen days. He had mapped the rotation through his neuretics — three cells, two-person, covering all four building exits in the first week. By day ten they'd dropped to three exits. By day fourteen, two. They had concluded he wasn't going to move. He had been making sure they concluded that.

He went to Ironbend first. The day shift was cycling up — cranes running, the transit slugs accelerating on the north track. He stood at the loading edge for a few minutes. A crane operator on the north dock lifted two fingers off the control at him, not quite a wave. Bear nodded. He pressed a palm flat against his sternum, felt the warmth of the Manowar on cycle, and walked to the transit platform.

[Beat 18]
The dock was open — his rotation badge still worked, nobody had pulled it. He went in at 0600, before the day shift, and found the wall where the lumen mouse had shown him the wall was not. In daylight, without bioluminescence, it was a seam pressed tight in the corrugated metal, invisible at standard distances — the kind of thing a building inspector might flag and a dock manager would defer through six fiscal quarters.

An eighteen-inch gap.

Bear is six-foot-eight. Tier 5 chassis, Manowar, Helix pressure suit under the skin. He went through sideways, carefully, and did not get stuck, which was a marginal outcome he had assigned a thirty-percent probability.

The maintenance corridor on the other side smelled like standing water and old freight. It connected to the distribution center's loading dock by a service passage that did not appear in any Halcyon route analysis of his known movements — an eight-kilogram animal had used it. He left what he had brought: three sheets of folded paper, handwritten, no neuretics trace, in the agreed location.

He came out the far side onto a service road. Halcyon's network didn't index this road. He had six hours until the panel.

[Beat 19]
They called it in at 1159.

Bear had turned away from Ironbend at 1158 — wrong direction for a freight shift, wrong transit, and Brandt's surveillance team would have had the deviation logged in under a minute. He'd given them everything they needed: a clear break from the pattern they'd been watching for nineteen days, heading toward the transit lines that ran past the IRB building. They had thirty-eight minutes before he reached the freight bay. Brandt was good at using thirty-eight minutes.

The gap was eighteen inches. Bear was not eighteen inches. He had given this discrepancy careful thought over three days and arrived at the same answer each time: sideways, committed, don't stop.

He went through the freight bay at 1237. He did not get stuck, which had been his primary objective. Three flights of service stairs. The fire door on the third level that the building's system flagged as alarmed had not been tested recently enough to know it wasn't.

He was inside at 1247. He had thirteen minutes.

He pressed a palm flat against his sternum. The Manowar was warm on cycle, accumulation nominal.

He straightened his jacket and walked toward the main corridor. The maintenance side of the building smelled like climate control and nothing else — the particular blankness of a space that had been air-managed into neutrality. His footsteps were the loudest thing in it. He noted that.

[Beat 20]
The service corridor inside the freight level had two of them.

Bear read the kit before the door finished swinging: Atlas II chassis, Halcyon Civil Security sub-license plates on the shoulder rigs, the particular way of standing that the training program put in your feet and didn't let out again. Former operators, both of them. Eight months out of active service, maybe. The stance was still there. These weren't the first names Brandt would have called — these were from somewhere below the active roster, the sub-license tier, the list you worked down when the better calls were already spent.

Brandt had been making calls since 1159.

Bear opened the vent arrays.

They were the tell — a click, distinctive, the kind that anyone who'd worked alongside him would recognize in the dark. The lead went for his sidearm. He was fast, exactly as fast as years of practice made you, which meant he was exactly as fast as Bear had planned for.

The charge released in a radius. Both operators went down. The warehouse ambient went quiet — the particular silence of running electronics that stop.

There was a third person behind a freight cart: one of the forwarding company's actual security staff. No chassis, no plates. A walkie-talkie and a lanyard badge. She was looking at Bear with the expression of someone who had signed up to check manifests.

Bear held up a hand. "Not your problem," he said.

She pressed herself against the freight cart and did not argue.

He stepped over the two operators and kept moving. Behind him, the walkie-talkie went live. That was fine. He had eleven minutes.

[Beat 21]
Declan Iyengar-Marsh was standing outside the IRB building entrance at 1247. He was wearing a suit. He did not have a weapon, as far as Bear could determine at distance.

"Boris." He smiled when he said it — the particular smile that meant he had prepared something careful. "I want you to know that the division takes no position on the substance of what you're going in to say. That's not our role. What I want to flag — and I'm flagging this as a professional courtesy — is that your license renewal comes before committee in six weeks. Separate track, different personnel. But those processes tend to be sensitive to the atmosphere at the time." He let that sit. "Whatever you decide today, I'd welcome ten minutes after. Boris."

Bear had always respected how Declan did this. Everything above-board, everything true, everything a different shape for *you're going to lose your work authorizations.* He had probably spent the night preparing it.

Bear said: "Thank you, Declan."

He walked past him into the building. The panel was at 1300. He was nine minutes early.

[Beat 22]
Brandt was in the lobby.

Not Legal. Not another operator. The colonel himself — civilian clothes, the same posture. Seventy-two, maybe. Still precise about where he stood. The scar under his left eye had gone smooth with the years.

Bear came through the entrance at 1253. Brandt saw him and moved to intercept — not quickly, just directly, the way a man moves when he has spent a long time deciding what he is going to say.

"Boris."

Bear stopped. "Colonel."

"I need two minutes."

"I have seven."

Brandt looked at him. Up close the scar was smaller than it looked at distance — the way scars usually were. Bear's neuretics ran a passive ambient sweep at arm's length: Halcyon Neural Services, medical-class, continuous-active. An NS-7 stem relay. The scar and the hardware were from the same incident. Fifteen years of the device keeping the lower brainstem functional.

"The committee date was a courtesy," Brandt said. "Not a reversal."

"I know."

"If you walk into that room —"

"I know," Bear said.

Brandt stopped. He was a man who respected precision, and there was nothing imprecise about what Bear had just said. He held the pause for a moment. Then — the old reflex, older than either of them — he straightened, chin coming up, weight shifting to the balls of his feet. The posture of a colonel addressing a subordinate. Bear had seen it ten thousand times.

He did not move his right hand. He nodded once, held Brandt's eyes, and waited.

Something moved in Brandt's face. He put it away. "The forty-three," he said. "I have thought about that number. We operated on the intelligence we were given."

"I know."

Brandt looked at him for a long moment. He had been going to say something after that — Bear could see the setup of it, the slight forward weight-shift of a man preparing a clause. He dropped it. "Take care of yourself, Boris."

"You too, Colonel." Bear meant it. He always meant it. "Take care of yourself."

He walked past him to the elevator.

[Beat 23]
The service corridor on the third level had three of them.

Not Atlas II. Atlas III — the same chassis configuration as the panel's own security detail, which meant these weren't building contractors. These were active-credential operators, and Brandt had gotten them here in fifty-three minutes. Bear did the math on what that cost. Not money. Relationships. The specific kind of favor you couldn't call in twice from the same person.

The lead had positioned to cover the stairwell exit the way the training manual said, and also the way the manual didn't say but that you learned after the manual stopped being enough. Someone had taught him that specifically — someone Bear recognized by the technique, if not the face. Brandt was spending things he couldn't replace.

The service conduit ran the length of the corridor — exposed aluminum pipe, three-centimeter diameter, bolted at every meter. Bear walked toward them with his left hand trailing the wall.

The lead put his hand on the conduit at shoulder height to cut off the approach.

Bear closed his left hand around the pipe at the same moment.

He discharged through it. Through the aluminum, up the conductive path, into the Atlas III chassis interface at the lead's palm. No vent arrays. No click. No display. The lead went down without a sound — the way a building goes dark when the main circuit breaks, not a collapse, just an absence.

The second operator had enough training to read what had just happened. She took a very careful step backward. Her hand was away from her sidearm.
