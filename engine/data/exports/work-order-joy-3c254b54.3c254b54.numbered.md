# Work Order

[Beat 1]
Pixel knocked on his door, which had happened four times in four years and meant the world had changed shape in some way she intended to explain.

She had a tablet under her arm and her field coat on — the one she wore to jobs, not to benches — and she walked past him into 2F and put the tablet on his kitchen counter where the kettle should have been.

"I have a contract," she said. "I'm crewing it. I need muscle that listens, and you're the only muscle I know whose listening I've personally calibrated."

On the tablet, in somebody's shaky handheld video, a machine the size of a four-story building was eating a row house in the Ashgrave fringe. Eating was the word. It took the structure down a bite at a time, unhurried, and behind it the rubble of the last block stood in three neat sorted piles — ferrous, mineral, everything else.

"That's an Axiom D-class," Pixel said. "Demolition and reclamation automaton. It woke up nine days ago, and it is very good at its job, and its job is now four hundred meters from two thousand people who live in the direction it's chewing."

Kyle watched the machine take a chimney off a brownstone with the care of something intending to file the bricks separately. "Whose is it?"

"Nobody's. Axiom Industrial died thirty years ago. No owner, no kill-codes, no support line." She tapped the tablet. "Arcturus left a cone. The residents pooled what the fringe could pool: 312Φ, held in a biscuit tin by a woman named Auntie Som, who sent the video to a parts man, who sent it to me — because everyone in the salvage trade knows the only person in the GLMZ who still speaks Axiom bus protocol like a living language works at a bench in West Town."

"Three hundred and twelve," Kyle said.

"Split six ways it's insulting. I'm taking it anyway, and so are you."

"I know."

"You haven't heard the plan."

"I've heard the pay," Kyle said. "The plan's not going to make it worse."

[Beat 2]
The crew's morning log arrived under the door at 07:00:

07:00 — SHIFT CHANGE. NO INCIDENTS. NOODLE CART (CONFIRMED NOODLE).
07:04 — PIXEL LEFT WITH THE FIELD COAT. BOSS'S DOOR OPEN A LONG TIME. FILING AS: DEVELOPMENT.
07:45 — LOG SUSPENSION REQUESTED. REASON: "WHAT JOB?" — B.
SUPPLEMENTAL (07:46): WE WILL HOLD WEST TOWN. THIS LOG IS THE RECORD. — B.

Kyle folded it into the drawer of things he had not decided about, and went downstairs to find the rest of his morning.

[Beat 3]
He ate at Mrs. Chen's at 08:00 — two bowls, schedule kept — and his work terminal chirped between the first and second.

A new posting on the standing contract. Not a task. Nobody had filed a report about the D-class; the entity had found it on its own.

STANDING CONTRACT — ADDENDUM.
CLIENT ADVISES THE UNIT KEEPS A SHIFT. 0600 TO 2200.
CLIENT RESPECTS THIS.

No scope, no fee, no direction. Kyle set the terminal face-down and finished the bowl while he thought about both readings, found them both sound.

"You're thinking loud," Mrs. Chen said, setting down the change. She put a fish ball in his pocket without comment, and that was that.

[Beat 4]
"You're not going near—" Kyle started, and Pixel set her cup down with a click that ended the sentence.

"Say the rest of that word and my rate doubles," she said. Not heated. The voice she used on a fastener that thought it was load-bearing. "The D-class runs a pre-2200 Axiom control bus. No remote interface — Axiom didn't trust radio, they were right, they died before anyone retrofitted one. The bus has one physical maintenance spur, on the chassis, where I will be standing, because the alternative is nobody closes this thing down and the city loses a neighborhood at the speed of careful chewing."

She turned the tablet around. A schematic, hand-annotated in her tight engineer's print — she had owned this before the job existed.

"I have wanted to talk to one of these since I was nine," she said. "You are not protecting me from it. You are buying me ninety seconds in front of it. That's the job, samurai. Price it like one."

Kyle looked at the schematic and at his neighbor of four years in her field coat, and did the thing the moment called for.

"Standard rate," he said. "Yours is double. Specialist on site."

"Correct," Pixel said.

[Beat 5]
The crew came together in thirty hours, because crews came together fast when the job was real and the pay was a joke.

Echo took eyes and comms — high ground, voice in five skulls, never wrong about a sightline. Stash took transport: a flatbed he refused to explain, cases packed for every door in the brief.

The new hands came from the parts man and one of Echo's old ledgers. Gantry was a rigger out of the lakefront cranes — big, slow-moving, maybe forty words a day, addressed suspended loads in the second person like they had opinions worth respecting. Furnace was a demolition op with a thermal lance and the cheerfulness of a man whose tool solved most arguments; he had burned scrap in the Ashgrave yards for a decade.

Five of them and Pixel, in the back of Stash's flatbed, on a contract worth less than the fuel.

"For the record," Furnace said, happily, "I've never worked a job this stupid."

"Stick around," Echo said, from everyone's skull at once.

[Beat 6]
They walked the eaten blocks first, because Pixel wanted to see its work.

The thing was tidy. That was the part that climbed into Kyle and stayed. The rubble of nine days stood in sorted piles at exact intervals — ferrous, mineral, organics — each pile compacted square, each street swept after, the lamppost bases unscrewed and laid in rows like cutlery. A city block taken apart with the intention of accounting for every piece.

In the middle of the fourth block, the piles stopped mattering, because the machine was there.

Eleven meters at the shoulder. Four legs, two boom arms, a hull of sintered gray monocoque printed in one piece — no plates, no rivets, no panel lines, no seams at all. Studding the hull every meter sat low black discs, and around each disc the rain steamed faintly off the surface.

"Arc-fence," Pixel said. "Anti-tamper. Capacitive plates, the hull's one big charge bank. Anything conductive touches the skin, the skin touches back. Forty thousand volts of touches back."

She looked at Silence's saya, then at Kyle.

"Your sword is a key for doors, samurai. That thing has no doors. No seams, no cable runs, nothing surface-routed — Axiom buried it all a meter deep in structure. There is nothing on that hull for an edge to mean. Steel and carbon — conductive. You touch it, you're not cutting, you're grounding. It will cook your neuretics inside your skull, and the burn scar will be the shape of your own blade."

"So I don't touch it," Kyle said.

"You don't touch it. Tonight your job is everything that isn't it."

[Beat 7]
It kept a schedule. The D-class worked a shift: 0600 to 2200, and at 0300 it walked back to the dead Axiom yard and docked against a transformer pylon to charge, the way it had every night for nine days, the way — Pixel said, quietly — it had probably been doing in 2196, before somebody powered it down mid-rotation and walked away.

"It docks, the arc-fence drops to maintenance levels, the spur goes live," she said. "Axiom units safe themselves for service. Ninety seconds on the spur, handshake with the bus, find out why it's awake."

"And if the handshake goes wrong?" Echo asked.

"Then it objects," Pixel said. "And the five of you get to be more interesting than I am."

[Beat 8]
The Axiom yard at 0250 was rust and sodium dark. They took positions: Echo on the crane cab; Stash at the gate, engine warm; Gantry in the rigging of the old loading frame, where two hundred tons of abandoned shipping container hung from cables she had spent the afternoon talking to; Furnace at the transformer with his lance dark; Kyle on the ground, where the geometry said trouble would have to pass.

At 0301, the ground learned a rhythm. Four legs, each footfall arriving through the boots a half-second before the sound.

At the yard's edge, the D-class stopped. A dead pedestrian crossing — two lamp poles, the ghost of paint — and the machine stood at it and waited, exactly as long as a signal cycle nobody had powered since before Kyle was born. Then it crossed.

Nobody on comms said anything. There was nothing to say that the waiting hadn't said.

[Beat 9]
It docked. The boom arms folded. The hull's black discs dimmed one by one, a contactor closed somewhere inside, and the transformer pylon began, gently, to sing.

"Maintenance state," Pixel said, already moving. "Fence is down to tickle. Ninety seconds. Count me."

She crossed the yard at a walk — running hands shake — stepped over Arcturus's cone, and put her bare hand flat on the hull the way she put it between Kyle's shoulder blades before trusting a readout.

"Hello, you absurd old thing," she said, too quiet for the channel, and found the spur.

Kyle counted. The whole yard counted. The handshake chattered in Pixel's earpiece, Axiom bus protocol going both ways for the first time in thirty years, and at second forty-one of ninety a power flicker rolled through the dead grid — the pylon's note wavered a quarter-tone — and the D-class's watchdog, which had kept this machine alive through three decades of abandonment, made the only decision a watchdog knows.

Every disc on the hull lit at once.

"Off the chassis—" Echo said, and the machine stood up out of its dock with Pixel's spur cable still live, and the night went to work.

[Beat 10]
Hatches the monocoque had not admitted to opened along the lower hull, and the skirmishers came out: knee-high yard-keepers, six-legged, the maintenance swarm repurposed by the watchdog into a perimeter, two dozen flowing off the chassis like spiders off a kicked log.

"Pixel's clear of the envelope," Echo called. "Cable's still in the spur — if she gets back on it she can finish. Kyle—"

"I see them."

The skirmishers were everything the hull was not: seamed, jointed, cabled, built to be repaired — built, in other words, to be taken apart. Kyle drew Silence and went into them.

It was geometry in the dark. Hip actuator on the first, where the cable loom crossed the joint; the second hooked low at his ankle and he gave it the boot it wanted, took the weight onto the planted leg, opened its lid along the dorsal service line. Two down. Third and fourth came together and he made them arrive separately — a half-step of timing that cost a slash across his forearm from a manipulator claw, shallow, hot, catalogued.

"Four," Echo counted. "Twenty left."

Gantry's voice arrived for the first time all night, slow as a load swinging: "Heads down in the south lane."

The container came off the loading frame on released cables, two hundred tons landing where Gantry had been promising it all afternoon, and the south half of the skirmisher line stopped existing as a line.

[Beat 11]
Furnace tried the hull once, because professionals confirm.

The lance ran eleven thousand degrees at the tip. He laid it against the D-class's foreleg for a full three-count, and when he pulled it away the monocoque wore a glassy scorch the size of a dinner plate. Not a hole. A polish.

"That's me out," Furnace said, packing the lance. "Structure's sintered the whole way through. You'd want a week and a mining permit. Also it's a third rail."

The foreleg turned. A boom arm came around, unhurried — everything the big machine did was unhurried, which was somehow worse — and swept the slag barrier away with Furnace already running, and Kyle was there, between, doing the thing his body had decided before his neuretics could file an objection.

He parried.

Steel-CNT met monocoque for a quarter-second, and the arc-fence did exactly what Pixel had priced. The world went white and sideways. The discharge took him off his feet, through the bones, out the heels, and the ground arrived like an invoice. His neuretics browned out whole: the overlay gone, the counts gone, the array's quiet arithmetic gone, eleven years of second heartbeat slapped silent, and Kyle lay in the rust mud as just a man, numb arm, blade still locked in his fist, the smell of his own scorched glove going up his nose.

Silence was unmarked. Steel has no opinion. The burn across his palm was the exact shape of the wrapped grip, printed in.

He got up, because the count in his head still worked even with the hardware down, and the count said Pixel needed thirty more seconds.

[Beat 12]
"Kyle's hit," Echo said, voice going flat the way trusted crews go flat. "He's up. Moving. Pixel, status."

"Back on the spur." Her voice had the bench in it. "Handshake's holding. It remembers me from forty seconds ago. Watchdog's split — half on perimeter, half on me. Keep the perimeter busy."

Kyle kept the perimeter busy. Without the overlay he read the skirmishers with his eyes alone — the settle before a lunge, the way the swarm's flank packed when it meant to push — and Silence went through service seams and actuator looms by feel and training, the discipline carrying the body the way it had been built to when the body was all there was. Five more came apart. Stash's flatbed came through the gate at second seventy, plow edge down, and herded the remnant. Gantry's second container landed where the survivors had agreed to regroup.

"Seventy-five seconds," Echo said. "Pixel."

"Almost. It's showing me something."

[Beat 13]
"It's not broken," Pixel said, and her voice had changed — the bench gone, something younger underneath. "Nobody corrupted anything. Listen: work order 7741-C, opened March 2196. Block demolition and reclamation, eleven structures, Ashgrave reclamation phase two. It completed ten. Then the yard lost power and somebody walked away and never closed the ticket."

The D-class stood over its dock, boom arms half-deployed, two dozen of its children dead in the mud, and the watchdog watched her read its homework.

"It's been asleep on an open work order for thirty years," she said. "Nine days ago a scavenger crew lit the yard transformer to strip it, and the wake-on-power did what wake-on-power does. It's not eating the city, Kyle. It's finishing its shift. The eleventh structure is gone — demolished in '08, built over. It can't find it, so it's working the survey grid block by block. The geofence isn't corrupted." A breath. "The city grew into its permit."

"Can you close the ticket?" Kyle asked. His palm had begun, distantly, to inform him about the rest of his week.

"I'm a certified Axiom maintenance bus," Pixel said. "Twenty years of self-certification. Watch me."

[Beat 14]
She closed the work order the way the standard demanded: completion codes, reclamation tallies — she entered the sorted piles, all nine days, because they were real work and the ledger should say so — sign-off in a dead corponation's maintenance dialect, down a spur cable, ten meters from the thing's foreleg with her hood thrown back.

The D-class went still in stages. Discs dimmed. Boom arms folded with the care of something stowing tools it expected to need again. The hatches took the surviving skirmishers back aboard. Then it walked — past Kyle, past the flatbed, footfalls arriving through everyone's boots — to the center of the yard it had been built in, turned once in the survey habit of a machine confirming its ground, and folded itself down onto its knees and shoulders.

The pylon's note died. The yard went dark and merely dark.

"Shift's over," Pixel said, to the machine, too quiet for the channel, and coiled her cable.

Kyle's terminal chirped.

STANDING CONTRACT — ADDENDUM.
CLIENT NOTES THE UNIT COMPLETED ITS CONTRACT.
CLIENT APPROVES.

CLIENT RESPECTS THIS had been the first posting, eight hours ago, before the job had a name. CLIENT APPROVES was the close. Both readings true at once. He put the terminal away and went to see about his hand.

[Beat 15]
The accounting happened at dawn, at the edge of the fringe, out of a biscuit tin.

Auntie Som counted the 312Φ onto a folding table in front of the whole street, because money counted in front of everyone stays counted. Kyle took the crew's shares the way the crew had set them: standard rate five ways, Pixel's doubled. Furnace laughed at his and took it. Gantry bit hers, which may have been a joke; nobody had the data to confirm. Echo's went into a dead drop. Stash's went into the flatbed's tank, approximately.

Kyle took his 26Φ. The fee can be the size of a gesture; it is never nothing. Auntie Som watched him pocket it with the expression of a woman recalibrating a rumor, and put two foil containers of food on top without asking — not payment, so acceptable without arithmetic.

"The big one," she asked. "It's dead?"

"It's off shift," Kyle said. "It finished."

[Beat 16]
Pixel took her salvage fee in kind, with the residents' blessing and a crowbar: the D-class's master clock — Axiom-sintered, thirty years drift-free — and the work-order seal module, which she held to the dawn like a jeweler examining a stone.

"Every Axiom unit in the lakes answered to one of these," she said. "There are maybe four left that still validate. Now there are maybe five." She wrapped it in the cloth she saved for things that mattered.

Kyle stood beside her, bandaged palm, neuretics rebooting in slow stages — the overlay assembling back across the world like frost growing in reverse — and looked at the folded machine in the middle of its yard.

"You talked to it for ninety seconds," he said. "Worth it?"

"Twenty years of wanting to," Pixel said. "Ninety seconds of getting to. That's a better rate than most wants ever pay." She shouldered the clock. "You shook its hand."

"It shook back."

"I told you it would." She started toward the flatbed. "Next time, samurai, when the specialist says don't touch it—"

"Standard rate," Kyle said. "I touched it at standard rate."

[Beat 17]
The crew's log arrived under the door at 07:00, waterproof cover performing under conditions:

03:12 — D-CLASS CROSSED AT THE DEAD LIGHT. STOOD AND WAITED. (RAFTERS: THIS IS THE PART THAT KEEPS ME UP.)
04:01 — BOSS PARRIED IT. SPECIALIST TOLD HIM NOT TO. HE DID IT ANYWAY. HE GOT UP. — B.
04:18 — PIXEL CLOSED THE TICKET. (THE MACHINE FOLDED. DENTS SAID IT WAS LIKE WATCHING A BUILDING GO TO SLEEP. DENTS IS RIGHT.)
06:00 — WEST TOWN HELD. LOG CLOSED. — B.
SUPPLEMENTAL (06:07): WHAT DOES IT MEAN THAT IT WAITED FOR THE LIGHT? WE HAVE BEEN DISCUSSING. — RAFTERS.

Kyle read it at Mrs. Chen's at 08:00, hand wrapped, neuretics still sorting themselves into order. He had no answer for the supplemental that wasn't more question, so he filed it in the drawer with everything else.

[Beat 18]
A week later the laundry came.

Somebody on the fringe ran the first line from a ground-floor window to the D-class's folded foreleg, and by the weekend there were nine lines, sheets and work shirts and a yellowed wedding tablecloth drying in the lake wind off eleven meters of the tidiest demolition in GLMZ history. Kids dared each other to slap the dead arc-fence discs and ran away screaming on the inhale. A vendor moved her cart into its shade on hot afternoons.

Kyle rode past it once, the long way, three fragments of skirmisher manipulator still working their way out of his forearm and his palm wearing his own grip in scar tissue, and did not slow down, and did not need to.

The piles stayed sorted. Nobody touched the piles.

Cacophony's count: five rounds. None spent.
