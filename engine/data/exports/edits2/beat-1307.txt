The team is good. They don't bunch and they don't panic; they peel to the SUVs and start working angles, and good is exactly the problem, because precognition against one trigger is a gift and against five is a budget. Kyle spends it carefully. An electric-bola round takes the flanker breaking left — the man drops into a private argument with his own nervous system. A breaching slug goes through the door an operator is crouched behind and folds the steel into him. Then Chorus runs dry on the fourth, the hammer coming down on an empty chamber with a click he feels in his teeth, and there is no time to swing the cylinder out and feed it, so he quits solving the problem with the gun. He draws Silence off his back. The katana is a matte-black length of steel-CNT meta alloy — no edge that takes the light, no trick to it but the hand that holds it, and the hand has had a long time to practice. He crosses the open ground inside the half-second the lattice buys him, under the muzzle before it can track, and takes the rifle out of the fifth man's hands at the receiver — the man left disarmed, intact, and loudly amazed to still have his fingers. Steel for steel's problems, Chorus for the rest: the whole of Kyle's philosophy of violence, and it has kept him alive longer than the actuarial tables like. Behind his eyes the overclock runs hot, spending him the way it always spends him when he asks it to do this, the cost gathering like weather he will settle up with later. Five down or out. One left — the leader, who has not moved, because the leader has been waiting.
