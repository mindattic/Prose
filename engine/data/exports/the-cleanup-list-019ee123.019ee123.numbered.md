# The Cleanup List

*Seto breaks into a dead relay annex. He finds the manifest. 847 names. Two of them are people he knows.*

The server cabinet smelled of hot aluminum and something older — sealed air, the smell of a room whose ventilation had been running the same loop for months with no human body to metabolize. The cooling fan held a continuous low pitch, the sound of hardware kept alive for reasons it did not know. He cracked the panel without ceremony.

The hardware inside was denser than he expected for legacy infrastructure: four drives and a routing board with dedicated local processing. Not a transit relay. A holding system, built to receive and accumulate without routing anywhere. He had found the node in month four of the silence mapping — not by accident, but because he had been looking for a relay that received and never forwarded, and this was exactly that. He plugged in his reader.

Eleven months of Cellvault contractor records, accumulating in a sealed box on the third floor of a building running 2219 firmware. The access had taken eleven minutes.

He pulled the batch records first.

Batch 44-C appeared on the fifth file. The document header called it a maintenance coordination file. The contents were not maintenance coordination. He recognized what he was looking at before he finished the first column.

Eight hundred and forty-seven rows.

Each row: a name, a neuretics batch code, a facility assignment, a calibration schedule running out eighteen months. The classification field, buried in the metadata, read *Managed remediation. Ongoing. See secondary protocol.*

He kept his back against the cabinet and worked through the list by facility. Zone 6: twenty-two entries. He found Amara's name in the nineteenth row. He had known she was inside the scope since the Monday before last. Eight days of carrying the match. The name made it specific.

He kept reading.

*Banda, Nuru. Batch 44-C. Zone 6, Facility C-9. Current status: active.*

He stopped.

He knew C-9 — it was four blocks from her clinic. She had mentioned, once, that Orison ran a subsidized calibration program for community healthcare workers. He had not thought about it since. He had not thought to think about it.

He read the entry three times: name, batch code, facility, status. He took his eyes off the screen and put them on the wall. The server cabinet was warm against his shoulder blades. The cooling fan ran its continuous pitch. Outside, the Seam was doing what it always did — relay workers on rotation change, the canteen exhaust, a two-stroke engine idling at the corner. He had been in this room six minutes.

He kept her outside his professional world deliberately. Six years of it. Every decision about how he worked — the sealed neuretics, the routes he never named, the people he never introduced to clients or contacts — was built around the fact that she had a clinic with fixed hours and people who expected her at specific times. He had constructed a wall between his work and her life and maintained it with the same precision he maintained his routes.

The list didn't know about the wall.

He forced himself back to the manifest. Zone 7: eighty-four names across six facilities on his routes. He had been delivering packages past Facility Z-3 on Tuesday mornings for two years — past the checkpoint and the receiving bay, the flat managed light that never varied. He had moved through the landscape of this thing without knowing what the landscape was.

He pulled one name at random from the Zone 7 block. *Eze, Theodora. Zone 7, Facility Z-3. Active.* He did not know Theodora Eze. He had probably passed her building. That was the shape of what eight hundred and forty-seven meant, rendered into one specific row. And his sister in another.

He copied the manifest. Encrypted the copy, split it across three drives. Two into his kit bag, third into the inner pocket of his jacket. He locked the cabinet, replaced the panel, and left.

---

The Seam between Zone 7 and Zone 6 ran its morning shift below him. Four days. Ekow Ato had given him seven at the Seam crossing — *walk away from the Orison work, close out the inquiry* — and three were already spent. He had noted, at the time, that Ekow had paused before saying "seven." Not hesitation — calculation. As if the number had been derived on the spot. The offer had been reasonable. That was the shape of a warning before the shape changed.

He reached his block and went upstairs.

The transfer protocol he had been building for six weeks was ready. The relay path ran through Seam beacon infrastructure — legacy firmware, off the main monitoring grids, low traffic. He committed to the move, opened the relay path, and watched the handshake sequence run.

The timing came back wrong.

He had run this path forty times in testing. The Zone 5/6 beacon registered at 340 milliseconds under normal load. Tonight it came back at 340, then at 1,200 as the transfer sequence opened — the gap that appeared when an additional process was monitoring the channel and hadn't finished loading when the first registration fired. The subsequent beacons came back at consistent intervals. Whatever was watching had found its footing.

He aborted before the first real packet cleared. Certain enough.

He sat with the drives in both hands. Three locations on one body, the relay path burned, three days on Ekow's clock. He had spent six weeks building the path and thirty seconds reading the timing before he shut it down.

He set the drives on the table and messaged Amara before he worked out what came next. *I've had your name for eight days. I held it.*

He didn't explain why. The explanation was true and incomplete and she would know both things when she read it. He did not tell her about Nuru. He didn't know yet whether telling her would help or just widen the thing he needed to contain.

Her reply came while he was still looking at the drives. The message started, broke, restarted: *Eight — how did you — okay. How long have you been inside the Zone 7 facilities?* No period at the end. He had never seen her skip punctuation before.

He answered. Two years of routes, six facilities, the Tuesday morning window at Z-3.

His right hand registered something before he caught it — the heel of the palm and three fingers, the specific torque of a calibration probe withdrawing. Her hands, not his. The way it arrived in the muscles before the mind sorted it. She was somewhere she hadn't finished reading. He did not reach further through.

He looked at the drives. The relay path was burned. Three days on the clock. He needed a different route, possibly a different decision, and the githeri on the counter had gone cold while he was in the annex.

He ate standing at the counter. He had the name of a journalist in Zone 3 — a gray-zone contact he had moved material for twice before, nothing of this weight, nothing that couldn't be disavowed. He looked up the contact while he ate. He had not worked out what to do about Nuru by the time he finished.

The window above the counter looked north toward Zone 6. He stood there after he was done eating, longer than he needed to.
