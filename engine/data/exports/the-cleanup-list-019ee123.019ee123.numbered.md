# The Cleanup List

*Seto recovers the Batch 44-C manifest from a dead corridor. 847 names. Amara's is one of them.*

The dead drop sat on the third level of a relay annex that had stopped relaying anything three months ago.

He had found it in month four of the silence mapping — one of seventeen nodes in the crescent where information flow had changed character rather than stopped. The others had gone genuinely quiet. This one had gone quiet in a specific way: outbound traffic logged and routed, everything arriving correctly on the far end. The silence was inbound only. Someone had configured the relay to stop accepting traffic from a class of source addresses wide enough to cover most of the gray zone without advertising what it filtered.

That kind of silence had a shape. It meant someone had something to keep still.

He had watched the annex for three months and had not touched it. Now he was inside it.

Amara was already at her console when he reached the building entrance — he could tell by the texture in the channel, the specific compression of someone who had been working for an hour and had found something they were not yet ready to name. He filed it and went in.

The access took eleven minutes. The building management system ran firmware that hadn't been patched since 2219, and the legacy auth token he carried had been good for seven years on systems that old. He was through the tertiary annex door and up the utility stairwell before lobby security finished its rotation.

Third level. A corridor that ended in a locked server cabinet the size of a wardrobe. The space smelled of hot aluminum and something older — sealed-air stale, the smell of a room whose ventilation had been running on a loop for months with no human body to metabolize. The single cooling fan ran at a fixed pitch, low and continuous, the sound of something kept alive for reasons it did not know. The cabinet itself ran hot, not fan-cooled hot but the sustained heat of hardware on a continuous draw nobody had serviced. Whatever lived here hadn't been touched since the person who set it up stopped coming back.

He cracked the panel with the probe from his inner kit pocket. Four drives and a small routing board with a local-only interface. No external connection. No egress. This was where the filtered inbound traffic had been landing, accumulating in a sealed box with nowhere to go.

He plugged the reader in.

Eleven years on gray zone routes had taught him what different kinds of silence sounded like, and this one sounded like a system someone intended to come back to. You didn't build a sealed accumulation box, configure a filtering rule, and patch the auth to expire in 2026 unless you expected to still need the contents in 2026. This was not a dead drop. It was a holding pattern.

The reader loaded. He ran the directory.

Eleven months of accumulated inbound traffic from source addresses inside Cellvault's contractor network. Administrative records, calibration session logs, batch certification updates — the traffic that moved between a corporate administrative tier and its contractor facilities, routed through relay infrastructure neither party fully controlled. The gray zone's preferred channel for anything you needed moved without audit.

He pulled the batch records first.

Batch 44-C appeared on the fifth file. He opened it. The document header named it a maintenance coordination file — a standard title. The contents were not standard. He read the recipient table and understood what he was looking at before he finished the first column. Not a maintenance schedule. A list.

Eight hundred and forty-seven rows. Each row a name, a neuretics batch code, a facility assignment, a maintenance visit schedule running out eighteen months.

*Cleanup list.* Not his word — the document's own classification field, buried in the metadata, the kind of thing that sat in administrative files until someone ran the right search. *Managed remediation. Ongoing. See secondary protocol.*

He knew the secondary protocol. He had read it two weeks ago, nested inside an Orison access tier Amara had given him the path to. It targeted associative memory nodes — not the memories themselves, the index that connected them — and reshaped the surrounding architecture until the degradation stopped appearing in standard reads. The degradation did not stop. The evidence did.

The list ran through him in a way that had nothing to do with the number. The number he had already worked out — the model, the geographic scope, the calibration windows. Seeing the names was different. These were specific people who had received specific hardware and were currently being managed through specific facilities, and the word *cleanup* sat at the top of the document the way a job description sat at the top of a file.

He scrolled.

The names were sorted by facility, not alphabetically. He worked down the Zone 6 block. The Zone 6 Cellvault facilities held twenty-two entries between them.

He found it in the nineteenth row.

Osei, Amara. Batch 44-C. Zone 6, Facility C-7. Visit schedule: quarterly, beginning March 2224. Current status: *active.*

He read it again.

His hands had gone flat on the floor. He noticed it without moving them.

He had known, from the model, that she was inside the scope. The batch number had been in his correlations for three weeks — since the week she told him which installation center she had used, and he matched it to the facility list in the protocol document, and the result came back the same result he was looking at now. He had been carrying the knowledge since the Monday before last.

He sat on the floor of the annex, his back against the cabinet, and read the list.

Eight hundred and forty-seven people. Not projected. Not modeled. Named.

He worked through the Zone 7 entries, because Zone 7 was his and he wanted to know how many of his routes had passed facilities on the list. The answer was six. He had run past Facility Z-3 on his Tuesday mornings for two years. He had dropped packages for people whose names he didn't know and whose neuretics were on this list, and the calibration visits he had sometimes seen posted on the facility schedule boards had been, on some percentage of those visits, the secondary protocol. He had moved through the landscape of this thing for two years without knowing what the landscape was.

The annex was quiet except for the drive fan. The stairwell outside was quiet. Down at street level the Zone 7 Seam ran its morning shift: freight operators, early-rotation relay workers, the canteen two addresses over already pushing food smells into unfiltered air. Here the air tasted of exhaust particulate and cooking fat and the faint ionized sting off the relay mast.

He copied the document. He encrypted the copy, split it across three of the drives he had brought, and set the recovery to require all three. Two drives went into separate compartments of his kit bag, the third into his jacket's inner pocket. Three locations, one body — redundancy against loss, but not against someone taking the body.

That was the best he could do.

He locked the cabinet, replaced the panel, and left the way he had come.

On the stairwell his right hand registered — briefly, the heel of the palm and three fingers — the specific torque of a calibration probe withdrawing. Clean extraction from a maintenance seat, the give of a securing clip releasing. Her hands, not his. He knew the sensation by now: the way it arrived in the muscles before the mind sorted it, the two-second window before it left. He let it pass.

She was working. In the Cellvault server room, he thought, from the texture of it — concentrated, careful, the kind of attention that meant she was inside something she hadn't finished reading. He did not reach through the channel. She did not need a signal that he had found what she was on her way to finding.

He was back at the table by 08:30.

The githeri had gone cold again. He hadn't eaten since the night before, but the appetite hadn't returned, and he didn't force it. He set the three drives out and looked at them. Eight hundred and forty-seven names across two hundred and four grams of storage hardware, sitting on a table in Zone 7 beside a pot of cold food.

The Ekow problem. *Walk away from the Orison work.* Seven days, Ekow Ato had said — the contract window. He had pushed Amara's schedule, rerouted his own Zone 7 stops, done what he could to move them both out of the crescent's visible footprint. Four days left. He didn't know whether the window was a genuine offer or a test of whether he would comply. The man had been reasonable. Reasonable was the shape of a warning before the shape changed.

Three options.

Move the data now, through the Seam relay path he had been building for six weeks, before the four days ran out. The path was ready. The risk was that moving it would register somewhere — the relay beacons ran legacy firmware off the main monitoring grid, but off-grid was not invisible, and an unusual pattern on a channel nobody official watched was still a pattern.

Wait for Amara to finish and move together. Her archive access plus the physical manifest was more than either alone. But waiting spent days he might not have.

Or give her the manifest and let her decide.

He sat with the third one. Giving her the manifest meant she would know he had looked at the list and held it for two days before telling her.

He had not chosen this. He reached for the ambient. *Someone is watching the Orison queries. I need to know how much you've accessed through the Cellvault system.*

He sent it and waited.

The response came back with the scope, and he read it, and his hand came off the table without a decision being made. The scope was large — larger than he had given her access for. She had gone further in than he had material for, which meant further than anyone at Orison or Cellvault had intended her to go. It also meant she had been inside their system long enough, and deep enough, that if they were watching, they had seen her.

Four days.

He folded the drives into his kit bag, ate the cold githeri standing at the counter, and started building the transfer protocol.

She was not quite there yet — he could tell from the channel, the texture of someone still working, not yet arrived. He would wait. When she got there, she would know that he had known, and he would tell her why he had waited.

He had promised her nothing, and he had started looking anyway. He let it be.
