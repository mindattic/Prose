# The Move

*Amara prepares to leave Orison Zone 6 for the Shen crossing. She attempts to document Theodora's calibration record before leaving. The system stops her mid-attempt.*

She woke at 05:40 and did not go back to sleep.

The ceiling had the same gray-white quality it always had. She lay looking at it long enough to think about the three-year figure she'd noted the morning she visited Theodora. She had never thought about the ceiling before then. Now she would probably think about it every morning for the rest of the time she lived here.

She dressed in the dark: field jacket, the kind tech leads wore to external facility visits. No reader case. The case would read as purposeful. She had nothing she was allowed to bring.

At 07:15 she opened her terminal and pulled Theodora Eze's calibration record. The file opened the way it always did — patient header, facility assignment, calibration window, visit history. The drift-in-motor-pathways data should have been in the second panel. The column was there, the header was there, the data field was gray. *Access level insufficient for this record type.*

She opened her phone's camera and pointed it at the terminal screen.

A soft alert tone. A banner across the terminal: *Screen capture attempted — personal device detected near active clinical record. This attempt has been logged.* The banner was still visible when the camera app closed itself.

She set the phone face-down on the desk. She waited three seconds to see if anything else happened. Nothing did.

She closed the terminal.

Ciro had filed both compliance entries as inadvertent policy misapplication. He had closed them. What he had also done, at some point between her leaving his office and this morning, was change her read permissions on the clinical panel and trigger the device-proximity flag she had not known existed. The file existed. She could see the header. She had a third log entry now.

She put on her jacket and left her apartment at 08:12.

The checkpoint between Zone 6 residential and the Seam access corridor was staffed by a guard whose name she didn't know and a biometric reader she had passed through four hundred and thirty-some-odd times. She pressed her hand to the reader. The light went amber. The guard looked up.

"Early start," he said.

"External facility follow-up," she said. "Zone 7 side. Client prefers mornings."

He held her eyes for one beat before going back to his screen. Not suspicious. Taking note. The biometric reader logged the time — 08:19 — and cleared her with a soft double-tone. She stepped through into the access corridor and did not look back.

The Seam hit her the way it always did: the managed warmth of Zone 6 giving way to air that moved. She smelled diesel from the transit loop three blocks east and cooking fat from the vendors setting up before the commuter peak. The relay beacons overhead were cycling through their hourly test.

She found the third relay west on her second pass — a gray-painted junction box on the side of a food stall selling rice porridge to transit workers. The stall owner kept her eyes on the porridge. Chalked in the lower corner of the box was a handwritten number she memorized without slowing.

She bought a cup of porridge and stood eating it, watching the foot traffic thin as the commuter flow peaked and subsided. Hot and plain. The kind of place where people ate quickly because they had somewhere to be.

The bleed arrived without announcement: a specific muscle fatigue across her upper back, the kind that came from sitting low on a bicycle for too long. She had never owned a bicycle. She shifted her shoulders and it didn't ease.

She looked up and he was already looking at her.

He was not what she had expected, which she recognized was her own failure — she had spent two weeks receiving his cognitive states without having clearly pictured the person who produced them. He was standing thirty feet away at the edge of the transit lane, carrying nothing, watching her with a patience that didn't read as waiting. He already knew she was there.

She set the cup down on the stall's counter and crossed the transit lane toward him.
