# Neon & Rust

_The message arrives at a dead-drop that Rook was certain no one else knew about.

It says: *There is a second thing in my head.*

Eighteen months before the last job, Dr. Halina Soraya walked twenty-one registered Reads off a barge that nobody officially acknowledged — and locked their names and locations into a cognitive partition in her own neuretics, behind a passphrase she then deliberately erased. She locked it against herself. She made herself the only security that mattered.

Now PEREGRINE has reconstituted under new paper and new funding, and what they want isn't her research. The Class V formula went public. What they want is the survivor list.

The partition is Atlas-grade hardware, eleven years old, two generations behind current standard, and rusting shut in real time. The only person in the GLMZ who has cracked one from the outside did it once before and watched it go wrong. They've been thinking about the failure mode ever since.

Rook broke her own rule to take this job. She is beginning to understand why the rule existed.

NEON & RUST is a story about twenty-one lives kept hidden in the only place they could survive — and the window, rusting shut, that is the only way to reach them._

[Beat 1]
The note was paper. Rook turned it over before she read it. She checked the weight, the grain, the crease — pressed flat with a thumbnail, not a machine. Paper meant a person had touched this and chosen to. Nobody used paper anymore unless they wanted no copy to exist. No cached draft, no send-receipt, nothing a forensic sweep could peel off a dead channel.

And nobody who wanted no copy would use the drop under the loose tile at the back of Mrs. Chen's. Because that drop was hers. She'd set it in 2206, pried the tile up with the flat of a knife and seated it back so the grout line stayed whole. She had told four people about it. All four were dead.

The name at the bottom was *O.*

She had never told Ohara about the tile. She'd told no one by that name. The tile was twenty years old. Ohara was newer to her than the grout was dry. So Ohara had found it on her own. Which meant she had gone looking.

Rook read the rest standing. Eight lines. Ohara wanted a meet. She named a time and a place — the Gray Zone, close enough to the Arcturus line that it was either a confession or a trap. Rook didn't care which. The part that mattered was the first line. Ohara had used the tile. She had reached into the one channel that should have been cold for two years, and she'd done it knowing exactly what that would say to whoever found it. You didn't spend a thing like that unless what you were holding cost more than the channel did.

Mrs. Chen's was warm the way places are warm when they run the grills past the point of profit, because shutting them down costs more than the gas. The air was thick with pork fat and the burnt-sugar bite of chili oil gone too hot in the wok. Under it sat the wet-concrete smell of a floor mopped every night for nineteen years and never once dried all the way through. Neon came through the front glass and lay across the formica in long stripes — magenta, a sick lake-green, the cyan of the noodle sign two doors down. From the street the light looked like warmth. Up close it was only color.

Her bowl sat on the counter where the boy had set it. Bone broth, the fat beading gold on top, the egg split and weeping into the dark of it. She'd paid before she sat. She always paid first. It was one of the small rules that kept her from owing anything to a room she might need to leave fast.

She folded the paper back along its crease — the same crease; you don't improve on a fold a thumbnail already set — and slid it inside her jacket, against the ribs, where her own warmth would dry whatever the night had put into it.

She was three steps toward the door before she noticed the steps.

That was the thing. Not the note. Not Ohara. Not the meet by the Arcturus line. The three steps. She had stood, crossed half the floor, her gloved hand already reading the cold of the door's push-bar — and back at the counter was a decision she hadn't made yet. A bowl going cold under the neon. Her legs hadn't waited for her.

She made herself stop. Weight even on both feet.

She had built the rule so this couldn't happen. No repeat clients. No repeat crew. No reaching back into a closed channel. She'd built it the year after Wennick, after one wrong call came back as a look she still couldn't shake — the way a person looks at you when they understand the job has moved under them, and you're the last one who saw the whole board. That look doesn't leave. The rule was how she made sure she'd never have to see it again.

It was maintenance. And she'd let it slip once already, on the last job, because slipping had been easy. The note had found the spot where the rule was already rusting and leaned on it, and the metal gave. A little at a time, quietly, until the thing holding the door shut was just the shape of a door.

She let herself look at that once, straight. Then she went.

She left the noodles. The egg still bleeding gold into the broth. Behind the counter Mrs. Chen watched her go and said nothing. Rook didn't look back.

[Beat 2]
At that hour the GLMZ was all lit surface. The signs ran through the wires like people who'd stopped listening to each other — red cycling to magenta, magenta bleeding into the sick lake-green of the noodle house, the cyan of an atelier nobody had walked into in four years still advertising an appointment no one had cancelled. The light ran off the wet streets and threw itself at the buildings above. The buildings took it, dimmed it, and handed it back. They didn't keep any of it.

Over the commerce ran the civic layer, and it didn't care that the street was empty. Waxwing Neuromedia's crowd-flow arrows paced the pedestrian lanes in regulation amber. A holographic stop sign floated above a junction where nothing was stopping. The infrastructure ran its protocols for an empty street exactly the way it ran them for a full one.

She was twelve blocks from Mrs. Chen's before she thought about where she was going.

Not Boiler first. That was the easy read — start with the one who came easiest — and easy was where the rule had slipped last time. Convenience threaded itself into the approach until the approach was just habit, and habit was what you had when you'd stopped thinking. She needed to think. She needed the route to cost her something before she started collecting people.

Her feet found the vendor before she did — a stall wedged into the dead angle between two closed storefronts, its sign in no language she read. She was at the window before she'd decided to go. She paid. She took the container. Fermented barley broth, a float of something she couldn't name.

The warmth came up through her palms first. Her hands settled around the container before she'd formed an opinion of it. She drank while she walked. A few blocks on, the rest of her caught up: a sign in no known language meant a vendor who'd chosen a good spot over being seen, and a good spot was the whole point. Her hands had known before she did.

Vox was the right first call. Not Boiler — Vox. Vox could tell her what the network had been saying while the crew was scattered, whether PEREGRINE had gone quiet or only quiet-sounding. Vox could run the four channels she'd worked since the Lake Platform and hand Rook a read with real ground under it. But Vox read the board the way Rook did, from her own squares, and two people reading the same board in separate rooms only made two rooms to square up later. So — not first, even so.

Her right hand had come up to her collar while she worked it through, an old adjustment her body ran when her mind was busy. She left the collar half-turned and kept walking.

The city wasn't complicated at this hour. The complicated parts were asleep. What stayed awake was the version they left running. Gray-zone crews pulled double shifts on the Pulse station four streets east, the vacuum tube carrying a low drone through three levels of ferrocement — you felt it in the backs of your knees before your ears found it. To the south, above the rooflines, the Thread ran its pods down through the dark, each one barely moving. A pair of ArcSec sat on a cart at the Z1 line and didn't look at her, because she walked at the speed of someone expected somewhere and wore the clothes of someone who belonged there. The Greymark Unseen jacket filed no proximity contact with their readers. That was what you paid for — not the fabric. Two scavs traded something out of a bag at a loading bay. She saw them and let them go, because they saw her and made the same call: not worth the trouble.

She'd spent years making herself into both not-a-threat and an asset. The pair let her walk where she walked, at the hour she walked it, and arrive with no record of the route.

She stopped at a junction box rusting along its lower bracket and laid her palm flat on the metal. The rust told her how long since city maintenance had come through this block, which told her whether the substation two streets over was on the same neglect cycle. It was. She moved on.

The thing she was carrying wasn't complicated either, underneath. Ohara had reached. She'd spent a channel to do it, which meant not reaching had come to cost her more than reaching would — and only one kind of problem pushes a person that far. A problem she couldn't solve alone and thought Rook could. That was the job. It was always the job: a person in a room that was closing, a crew that could open it, and the window between.

The rule existed because the job was seductive in exactly that shape. You found the closing room and the person in it, and you told yourself the crew was how you set it right. Then you spent someone you hadn't counted and called it the work. Six months later you were in a booth over the lake, in a diner nobody maintained, adding up numbers that had no column, and the broth in the bowl was cold.

She hadn't looked at the noodles since she left Mrs. Chen's. She looked now, the way you look at something no longer in the room, and it cost her nothing. She wouldn't know for a while whether that was good news.

She walked the rest of the way to the condemned building without hurrying and without slowing.

[Beat 3]
They weren't hard to find, which told Rook how badly they'd scattered — which was not far at all. Real distance leaves no forwarding address. These three had left whole streets of it.

Boiler hadn't gone anywhere. He was four blocks off the lake, in a building that had been scheduled for demolition six years ago and outlived the schedule, walking the third floor with his head tipped back. He was reading a crack in a load-bearing column the way another man reads a letter he's carried too long. He heard her on the stairs and didn't turn.

"I haven't agreed to anything," he said, cheerful, to the column.

"I haven't asked you anything," Rook said.

"That's the ask." He ran a thumb along the crack — slow, the touch you give a thing you're trying to understand before it decides for you. "This column's held on pride for fifteen years. You come up my stairs, it's either a job or the building finally went to tribunal — and only one of those has you walking up instead of calling." He pulled his hand back and looked at the pale dust of old concrete on his thumb. "What are we cracking?"

"A locked room inside someone's head."

He patted the column once, the way you say goodbye to something you respect, and went for his kit.

He'd found a condemned building to stand in while he waited to be asked. It was the least surprising thing he had ever done.

Outside, the rain had started — the fine kind that hangs more than falls. Z2's signs hung in it: two dead storefronts and one live noodle counter, all three rotting on the same brackets. The live one buzzed pink against the wet. The dead ones held the shapes of letters that no longer lit. The light coated everything — the standing water in the gutter, the back of her own hand, the rust creeping up a junction box the city had stopped maintaining a decade before it stopped pretending it would. Warm from a distance. She knew the trick of it. She walked the eleven blocks to the Gray Zone, because a taxi leaves a record.

Two so far. Both had said yes before she finished asking. She was keeping count of who was easy.

Scout she found on a Gray Zone rooftop, eyes rolled white, four crawlers walking the parapet and mapping the block in vectors and gaps. She was in four places up there, and not one of them was the roof her body sat on — alone, unguarded, eyes white to the rain. She didn't take the folded paper when Rook held it out. She came back into her own eyes by degrees and looked at Rook's face instead of the note, and for a long moment Rook wasn't sure which way she'd land. Her body held its position: knees on the roof membrane, spine straight, still as someone who's already done the math and is only waiting for the last number to come in.

"I'm asking," Rook said.

"The place is a kill-box. Angles wrong on three sides, worse on the fourth." She reached for one of the crawlers and drew it back against her palm without looking at it. "I'm coming anyway." She stood, the crawler in her closed hand the only thing she lifted off that roof.

Someone two jobs back had called that one Gerald. She'd never corrected it. She'd stopped correcting it. But her grip tightened on it for a second, small and unmeant, and Rook set it beside the rest of what this job was going to cost more than she'd planned for.

Nobody asked how Rook still had the rooftop, the kit, the unlisted door. That was the courtesy. You didn't ask the woman who built the crew how she kept its keys. You only saw that she did, decided what that was worth to you, and came or you didn't. Three years she'd held them — the lease, the dead-drop, the door nobody scanned. The rain found the back of her collar on the way down. She let it.

Lace came last, on her own, an hour later, in out of the rain.

She came in the way she came into everywhere — sideways to the doorframe, never square to it, coat still on and still buttoned, because a buttoned coat is a thing you leave a room in faster than an open one. The rain hadn't touched her face. She'd kept to the awnings, or timed the gaps. Either way she came in dry to a room the wet had gotten to, which was Lace all over: present where the cost was lowest, the small prices already paid before anyone else thought to pay them. She didn't sit. She put her back near the wall that gave her the door and the table both. Only then did she look at the paper on the wood — once, the way you confirm a thing you already know.

Then she ran the room.

Rook watched her do it, because Rook had taught her half of it, and the worse half Lace had brought from wherever she'd been before. Her eyes went to Boiler, then Scout, then Rook, then the exits, then back across the three faces in the same order — a circuit, not a glance. Who holds authority here. Where compliance gives. The exit she was already standing in. It took under two seconds and cost nothing you could see, which was the part Rook had never managed to teach anyone else, and the part that made Lace worth the door.

But Rook wasn't reading the room. Rook was watching Lace read it, and the circuit hitched. Not on the exits, not on Boiler's hands, not on the angles Scout had already condemned. On Rook's own face. The eyes stayed a fraction past their interval, a half-beat too long. Something moved low in Lace's jaw — a small tension banking and letting go, the work of a person swallowing a sentence on its way up the throat. The weight came most of the way to the surface of her face and stopped. Then she set her shoulders back against the wall, and her face closed over the spot the way water closes over a dropped stone.

Whatever she'd found in their faces, she kept it, and said nothing.

From Lace, that was a finished sentence. But Rook had just watched the sentence cost her something to keep shut, and that was new. Not a secret — a secret is clean. This was load. Carried since the safe house, by the look of it. A brace that old doesn't fail loud. It fails when you lean on it.

Boiler owed Ohara nothing and came. Scout had a reason no one could name and came. Lace had read the cost on the first day and came anyway, and her not-saying was the only thing holding the four of them at one table. Every one of them had a different reason, and all of them were Rook's. She wasn't going to name a single one out loud. The moment one of them did, somebody would leave.

[Beat 4]
Ohara's current address wasn't in any record Rook could reach, which meant Ohara had stayed clean since the Lake Platform — a person-shaped hole in the city's data, the gap that opens when someone is very careful for a very long time about what the infrastructure gets to know. Rook found her through the kind of evidence that never reaches a record. In a two-block stretch of the Gray Zone's Z8 edge, three pieces of maintenance equipment were registered to a shell cooperative set up the same year Axiom's Class V research division folded. And a third-floor unit was drawing power in a pattern that matched the cooling profile of an Atlas-grade neuretics rig idling on low.

She hadn't shared the method with the crew.

The building had been split six times since it went up, each split adding a wall and a lease and taking a window from whoever had been there longest. The third-floor unit got light from one direction, and that direction was the alley. The door carried two physical locks and entry monitoring that cost more than the door. Whoever lived there had put security above comfort, and was probably right to.

She knocked with a knuckle, twice — the way she knocked on anything she wasn't sure she was allowed through. A request, not a statement.

The door opened with no sound before it. No footstep, no approach. Ohara had already been at the door. She'd been watching the alley from somewhere in the room and had put herself there in the time it took Rook's hand to come off the wood.

She looked different, in a way Rook couldn't name at once. Two years showed in the expected places — lighter at the temples, a new economy in how she held herself, the posture of someone who'd learned to take up less room and found it easier to keep than she'd feared. What she didn't look was afraid. She looked like a woman who'd been managing fear on a tight budget so long that fear had become one more thing with a line and a limit, like power and time. Not gone. Accounted for.

She stepped back from the door to let Rook in, which wasn't the same as welcoming her, but was the right call. You don't welcome someone who might be there to audit you into a room you're living in carefully.

The room smelled of cooling equipment and the flat chemical stillness of a place where the vents never stop, because stopping them costs more than running them. A worktable held the rig Rook had tracked by its draw — an Atlas-grade neuretics interface in a custom housing, three generations old, still good at its job precisely because it was old enough that security had stopped watching for it. Beside it lay a handwritten log. Pages of it. The kind that logs to nothing and can't be edited after the fact.

"I wasn't sure you'd use the tile," Rook said.

"I was sure you were still checking it." Ohara's voice matched her posture — arranged, careful, with something underneath that the arrangement was holding down. "You built it the way you build things you don't want to need but know you will."

That was accurate. Rook didn't argue it.

"Tell me what's in the partition," she said.

"I don't know what's in the partition." Ohara crossed to the worktable without making it look like she was leading. She moved through the room the way you move through a space you've crossed a thousand times, the furniture arranged around the work until you've half become furniture yourself. "I know what I was working on when I made it. Eighteen months ago. The first time the east wall showed a hairline." She set a hand near the rig's housing, not quite touching — measuring its heat, the way you check on something you're worried about. "I had a second research thread Axiom didn't know about. I was protecting it from them, I told myself. Moving it somewhere they couldn't reach even if they reached me." She paused, and the pause had weight. "I told myself that. I'm not sure it was the reason."

Rook looked at the hand near the housing. The knuckles were careful — the care of someone who'd spent years on hardware that punished a rough touch and carried the habit into everything. Stave's hands had the same habit. The same precision. The same rust in the joints from years in the wrong room. Two people who'd each built something carefully and then sealed the door on it.

"What's the working theory?" Rook asked.

"That I locked something I was afraid of." Ohara took her hand back and glanced at it. "Not afraid Axiom would take it. Afraid of what it said about the work I'd been doing — where the Class V pathway was heading before I sealed the formula and broadcast it." She looked at Rook directly, the first full look since the door. "I've been right about Axiom's plans three times in ten years. I've been wrong about my own work twice. I don't know which this is. I know it's sitting in hardware that's closing around it, and when the hardware closes, the data goes with it. And whoever's running PEREGRINE now has a reading on the wall that says the same thing." She sat. "So I used the tile."

Rook looked at the log — pages of it, handwritten, no revision history. She didn't touch it. She looked at Ohara instead: at the two years between the woman she'd pulled out of Axiom and the one sitting in this careful, arranged room. At what it costs to spend a channel you've held shut that long. At a note that had come in a hand she'd known in one sentence.

"Who knows the partition exists?" Rook asked.

"You," Ohara said. "Stave. And whoever paid Stave to sell a door." She looked at the log. "I didn't tell anyone else, because anyone else is a record. Records are what I've been living without." She touched the edge of the log without opening it. "I've been very good at it. Until it stopped being enough."

Rook let the room hold that a moment.

"We'll need three days before we go in," she said. "Two to find Stave, one to set the site. If the wall's reading the way you say, we don't have slack." She moved toward the door, because staying wasn't what the job needed. "Lock the rig before you sleep. I don't want the draw reading any higher than it is now. If PEREGRINE has a monitor on it, I don't want them seeing it spike."

Ohara nodded once — the nod of someone already doing exactly that, waiting for someone to confirm it was right.

"Rook," she said, before the door closed.

Rook stopped.

"I wouldn't have sent the note if I'd found another way." She said it without apology, which made it honest. "I know what it costs. I know what channel I used."

Rook thought about three steps toward a door, and a rule she'd built the year after Wennick, and the way a body moves before the decision catches up to it. "I know what you used," she said. "That's why I'm here."

She closed the door behind her.

[Beat 5]
Ohara sat last, because Ohara had asked, and someone who asks for a table doesn't take it before everyone else has decided to sit. She took the chair across from Rook and set her hands flat on the wood — palms down, fingers spread, nothing of yours under the table where it can't be seen. Rook had taught all four of them to sit that way in a room they didn't trust. Ohara laid out the shape of it with no preamble, which was the only mercy she had left to give. She was thinner than the lake had left her. Two years of living carefully had gotten into her hands. They didn't shake, and the not-shaking was work — the way a person holds a full glass level across a crowded room.

"The partition's failing," she said. "The one I left up. Not failed — failing. There's a difference, and the difference is the only reason I used your tile instead of running."

She turned a salt cellar a quarter-turn with one finger, giving her eyes somewhere to be that wasn't a face. "When I sealed it eighteen months ago, I built a slow death into it. Corrosion across the gate hardware. Eighteen months to brick, give or take. No alarm, no inheritance, no question of what was sealed behind it." She let that sit — eighteen months had, in fact, now passed. "It should have bricked itself by now. That's what I built it to do. I checked the curve last week, out of a habit I should have broken." Quarter-turn. "It's not corroding on my numbers. It's not corroding at all. Something's pulling current off the gate to keep itself warm past its own deadline. Drawing power through a partition that's supposed to be a tombstone — and a tombstone doesn't get cold feet." She set the cellar down square and stopped fidgeting with it. "There's something on the other side of the wall I built. It's been spending the wall's own power to outlive the schedule I gave it, and I don't know how much longer that buys it. I stopped trusting the maybe two days ago."

She said all of it in one level register, the sentences laid end to end like a checklist read aloud to an empty room until the words went smooth and lost their teeth. That was the part that landed wrong. Not the content — the delivery. She had rehearsed the worst day of her life until it sounded like a maintenance report. That wasn't calm. That was a person who'd stood in front of the same fear long enough to take it apart and put it back together in a shape that couldn't hurt her. It could still hurt everyone else.

And the table heard it, each of them their own way, because Rook had built a crew of people who didn't take a room the same way. That had always been the point.

Boiler took it as load. His broad hand went flat on the wood, the way it had gone flat on the cracked column an hour before, palm reading for the line of failure, and the cheer went out of him all at once. He didn't ask anything. Men who read columns for a living know what a thing holding on pride looks like from the inside, and he'd just been told one of them was a person — and the person was sitting across the table turning a salt cellar.

Scout heard the word warm and came most of the way back into her own eyes for it. She'd been half-gone into the overlay even here, one foot on the parapet the way she always kept one. But a thing pulling current to stay warm past its deadline was a thing that wanted feeding, and a thing that wanted feeding was, by her count, alive enough to matter. She didn't say it. She just stopped reading the angles of the room and looked, for a second, at the wall behind Ohara — as if the partition were something you could find an angle on.

Lace read the faces. That was what Lace did with horror: she watched it travel. She ran the room again, slower than when she'd come in, watching Boiler's hand go flat and Scout come back from the parapet and Rook not move at all. Something in her jaw set. She'd known a piece of this since the safe house, carried it the whole time like a stone in a coat she'd forgotten she was wearing. Rook saw the weight shift in her and shift back, held a moment longer, and not come out. Not yet, the look said. But it was getting heavy where she held it.

Four reasons, all of them Rook's, none of them said.

Ohara had built the thing to die on schedule, and it had decided to keep living. So she'd come to the one person who told her, back in 2206, that you can't seal a thing you don't understand — you can only put off the conversation. This was the conversation.

"You don't know what's in there," Rook said.

It wasn't a question, and she didn't aim it at Ohara. She aimed it at the table, at all of them, at the fact that not one of them had ever opened the door they'd spent six weeks welding shut.

Nobody filled the gap after it. That was the discipline Rook had drilled into them across a dozen worse rooms: when a thing that heavy goes onto the table, you let it sit. The first hand to reach for it tells you what they're afraid of. So the four of them let it lie there between the salt cellar and Ohara's flat hands. The bar's bad cabling ticked behind a crooked panel. The neon from the window laid its useless warm color across the table and warmed nothing. Ohara held still inside the silence the way she'd come prepared to hold still inside everything.

"No," Ohara said, when she'd let it cost her the full measure. "I know what I put in. I don't know what it's become while we weren't listening. Those aren't the same thing anymore." She turned her hands over, palms up, empty — the oldest gesture there is for someone with nothing left to hide and no way to prove it. "I have a window and I don't have a crew. You have a crew and you stopped having a reason. I came to trade."

[Beat 6]
Vox hadn't sat down. She stood at the wall where the bar's cabling ran behind a panel somebody had screwed back on crooked years ago, two fingers resting on the seam, and when Ohara finished, she let the room hold for exactly as long as it took everyone to look at her — not a beat longer. That was her courtesy and her arrogance both. She didn't announce the Tap. She just stopped feeling the room and started feeling the four channels that ran under it: the bar's own dirty line, the Gray Zone municipal trunk, the Arcturus relay throwing its hard clean signal off the line six blocks east, and under all of it the thin starved thread Ohara's tile had told her to look for — the one drawing current it had no right to.

Rook watched her work, because watching Vox work was the closest thing the crew had to a clock. The bar gave the hour back in its own terms: the long quiet of a Gray Zone dive at the dead end of the night, when the dawn shift hasn't come and the night people have thinned to the ones with nowhere better. Behind the counter a cooler cycled on, off, on, the compressor chasing a temperature it would never hold. Somewhere a ceiling fan ticked once a revolution where a blade had bent. The whole place ran the way Z-anything ran — on the parts nobody had gotten to — and the silence Vox stood inside wasn't silence, just the absence of anyone left who could afford to be loud.

Three of the four channels were ordinary. The fourth was somebody breathing through a straw and calling it the wind.

"Your curve's not wrong," she said, eyes half-shut, voice flat the way it went when most of her was elsewhere. "It's stale. Somebody bought your trash. There's a query pattern hitting the gate hardware on a paid relay — not corponation, not Arcturus prime. A subcontract shell pushing fresh capital through a name I've seen twice this month. PEREGRINE. New paper, new funder. And they're not poking the wall to understand it. They're poking it to time it. They've already decided what's behind it is worth more cracked than studied. The funding chain ends at Helix Biosystems." She opened her eyes. "Your eighteen months died the day they signed. They're moving fast, and they're moving like people who've done this before."

She took her fingers off the seam, and the four channels went out of her like a held breath let go — back to the ordinary deafness of one room instead of the whole electrified spine of the city. Then, for a stretch, she said nothing, and let the first half of the verdict finish landing before she set down the rest. The cooler picked that second to cycle off, and the quiet that dropped after it had weight, the kind the body feels before the mind decides what it means. Rook felt the table go still — Boiler's hand flat on the wood again, Scout surfaced far enough to be present, Lace watching the faces instead of the wall. Nobody reached for the gap. Vox let it come down on its own. Then she looked at Ohara, not unkindly, which was worse.

Ohara had come here with weeks. She'd come here proud of weeks. She'd turned a salt cellar a quarter-turn all night to keep her hands from saying so.

"You said weeks," Vox said. "You don't have weeks. PEREGRINE's draw curve and yours cross inside nine days — and that's if they don't speed up, and people who buy a tombstone don't slow down to admire it."

Nine days. Rook set it against the job. Two days to Z2 and the broker, minimum, even if Scout's crawlers had already found the door. That left seven. Stave would want three of them to model the hardware before touching anything, and she couldn't give three, and the three she couldn't give were the exact margin that kept Ohara breathing. Without Stave there was no procedure — only a corpse with the answer still locked inside it. So the whole risk of the job had just slid off the schedule and onto the surgeon's hands. And the surgeon, by every account she had, did not like weight put there.

She watched the number reach each of them. Ohara didn't flinch — she'd spent two years not flinching — but the hand on the salt cellar stopped, the quarter-turn going dead still, and her thumb pressed once against the glass corner hard enough to whiten, then let go, then went flat to the wood again.
