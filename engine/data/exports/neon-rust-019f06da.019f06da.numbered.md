# Neon & Rust

_Sequel to Magenta & Gunmetal. Adalemo survived the lake. Ohara's neuretics hold a locked partition — older than she remembers, worse than she theorized. Rook reassembles the crew to crack it before PEREGRINE collects her._

[Beat 1]
The note was paper. Rook turned it over once before she read it, checking the weight, the grain, the crease pressed flat with a thumbnail and not a machine. Paper meant a body had touched this and chosen to. Nobody used it anymore unless they wanted no copy to exist — no draft saved somewhere a forensic sweep could peel up later, nothing on any wire — and nobody who wanted no copy to exist would also use the drop under the loose tile at the back of Mrs. Chen's, because that drop was hers. She had set it in 2206, pried the tile up herself with the flat of a knife and seated it back so the grout line stayed unbroken, and she had told four people. Three of them were dead.

The fourth had signed it. *O.*

*He went into the wall I built and he left a door open behind him.*

She read the rest standing — eyes moving, hands already deciding. Eight lines. Ohara wanted a meet, named a time, named a place in the Gray Zone close enough to the Arcturus line that it was either a confession or a trap, and Rook did not care which, because the part that mattered was the first sentence. Ohara had used the tile. She had reached into the one channel that should have been cold for two years, and she had done it knowing — this was the thing, this was the part that landed somewhere under the sternum before Rook could file it — knowing exactly what it would mean to the person who found it. You did not spend that. Not unless what you were holding cost more than the channel did.

Mrs. Chen's was warm in the specific way of places that ran their grills past the point of profit because turning them off cost more than the gas. The air sat thick with pork fat and the burnt-sugar edge of chili oil gone too hot in the wok, and under it the wet-concrete smell the whole back room carried, the smell of a floor that had been mopped every night for nineteen years and never once dried all the way through. Neon came in through the front glass and lay across the formica in long stripes — magenta, a sick lake-green, the cyan of the noodle-house sign two doors down — light that looked like warmth from the street and turned out, up close, to be only color. It picked out the steam off her bowl and made it look like something worth staying for.

The noodles were on the counter where the boy had set them down. Bone broth, the fat beading gold on top, the egg she always asked for split and weeping into the dark of it. She had paid before she sat. She always paid first; it was one of the small protocols, the ones that kept a body from owing anything to a room it might need to leave fast.

She folded the paper back along its crease — the same crease, she did not make a new one, you did not improve on a fold that someone had set with a thumbnail — and slid it into the inside left of her jacket, against the ribs, where the warmth of her own body would dry whatever the night had put into it.

And she was three steps toward the door before she registered the steps.

*That.* That was the thing. Not the note, not Ohara, not the wall or the door someone had left open in it. The three steps. She had stood, she had crossed half the floor, her hand was already reading the temperature of the door's push-bar through the leather of her glove — and somewhere back at the counter was a decision she had not made yet, a bowl going cold under neon, and her legs had not waited for it.

She made herself stop. Weight even on both feet. *Count it.* She counted, because counting was the one thing she did when her body had decided ahead of her: two dead storefronts visible through the glass, four stools between her and the drop, three years since she'd built the rule precisely so this could not happen. No repeat clients. No repeat crew. No reaching back into a closed channel. She had built it the year after Wennick, because once a job had moved under a man's feet on her say-so, and she had been the one holding everything he didn't know, and she had watched him understand that in the last second he had. That look did not leave. The rule was the wall she built so she would never have to stand on the wrong side of that look again — and a wall, she of all people knew, was only ever as good as the last day someone maintained it. She had let this one slip once, on the last job, because the job made the slip convenient. The note had found the place where the metal had already started to go, and leaned on it, and the metal gave. Quietly. A little at a time. Until the thing holding the door shut was just holding the shape of a door.

She registered that, set it aside, and went.

She left the noodles. The steam was still climbing off them, the egg still bleeding gold, the whole bowl paid for and abandoned. Behind the counter Mrs. Chen watched her go, and Rook felt the weight of the watching between her shoulders — not concern, not the flat nod she gave a stranger, not the *come back, the broth keeps* she'd have aimed at a regular who'd had bad news. Something older than any of those. The look of a woman who had run a drop under her own floor for nineteen years, who had never once asked what the tile was for, and who knew — had always known, Rook understood now — that the day somebody left food cooling it would be a day the person who left it had already decided. That silence was its own kind of mercy. Rook took it the way she took everything offered free, which was warily, and on her way out she did not look back, because looking back would have meant deciding what the watching meant, and she had decided enough for one night.

[Beat 2]
They were not hard to find, which told Rook everything about how badly they had scattered. People who actually wanted to be gone left no thread to pull. These three had each left a loose end where a hand could find it, and pretended that was an accident.

Boiler had not gone far at all. He was four blocks off the lake in a building that had been scheduled for demolition for six years and outlived the schedule, walking the third floor with his head tipped back, reading a crack in a load-bearing column the way another man reads a letter he's been carrying around too long. He heard her on the stairs and didn't turn. "I haven't agreed to anything," he said, cheerful, to the column.

"I haven't asked you anything," Rook said.

"That's the ask." He ran a thumb along the crack's length — slow, diagnostic, the kind of touch you gave something you were trying to understand before it decided for you. "This column's been holding on pride for fifteen years. You come walking up my stairs, it's either a job or the building finally went to tribunal, and only one of those has you walking up instead of calling."

"A locked room inside someone's head."

He pulled his hand back and looked at the residue on his thumb, the fine pale dust of old concrete. He patted the column once, the way you say goodbye to something you respect. "Whose head?"

"Ohara's."

That moved something. Not much — Boiler's face mostly stayed where he left it — but he looked at the column a second longer, the way he looked at a thing he'd just learned was carrying more than its rating. "She came back," he said. Not a question. He went to get his kit.

*He found a condemned building to stand inside while he waited to be asked. That is the least surprising thing he has ever done.*

Out of the building the rain had started, the fine kind that didn't fall so much as hang, and Z2's signage hung in it — the neon of two dead storefronts and one live noodle counter, all three rotting on the same brackets, the live one buzzing pink against the wet and the two dead ones holding the shapes of letters that no longer lit. The light coated everything: the standing water in the gutter, the back of her own hand, the rust-bloom creeping up a junction box that the city had stopped maintaining a decade before it stopped pretending it would. Warm from a distance. She knew the trick of it. She walked the eleven blocks to the Gray Zone on foot because a taxi left a record.

Scout was the one she wasn't sure of.

She found her on a rooftop in the Gray Zone with her eyes rolled white and four crawlers walking the parapet, mapping the block in vectors and gaps — four places at once on that parapet and not one of them the rooftop her body was actually sitting on, alone, unguarded, eyes white to the rain. Rook held the folded paper out and Scout did not take it. She came back into her own eyes by degrees, looked at Rook's face instead of the note, and said nothing, and the nothing went on long enough to be an answer forming.

"The place is a kill-box," Scout said finally. "Angles wrong on three sides and worse on the fourth. I ran it twice." She pulled one of the crawlers in against her palm and held it there, not looking at it, and Rook watched her weigh it — watched the no sit right there in her jaw, fully built, a breath from being said. Scout had the better read of anyone on the crew of exactly how a room killed you, and her read on this one was that it would. "Whose head," she said, the same question Boiler had asked, except Scout asked it because the answer was the only thing that could move her off the no.

"Ohara's."

Scout was quiet. Then her grip closed on the crawler, a small unconscious thing, and she stood, the crawler the only thing she picked up off that roof. "I'm coming," she said, like a person conceding an argument she'd had alone and lost. One of the crawlers had been called Gerald by somebody two jobs back. She'd stopped correcting it. She didn't correct it now. But Rook filed the way her hand had closed, and the two seconds it had taken her to lose to herself, with the rest of what she hadn't budgeted for.

Nobody asked how Rook still had the rooftop, the kit, the unlisted door. That was the courtesy. You did not ask the woman who built the crew how she kept the keys to it; you only noted that she did, and decided what that was worth to you, and came or you didn't. The rain found the back of her collar on the descent. She let it.

Lace came last, on her own, an hour later, in out of the rain.

She came in the way she came into everywhere — sideways to the doorframe, never square to it, the coat still on and still buttoned because a buttoned coat was a thing you left a room in faster than an unbuttoned one. The rain hadn't touched her face. She'd kept to the awnings, or she'd timed the gaps; either way she arrived dry into a room full of people the wet had gotten to, and that was Lace all over — present where the cost was lowest, having already paid the small prices nobody else thought to. She didn't sit. She put her back near the wall that gave her the door and the table both, and only then did she look at the paper on the wood, once, the way you confirm a thing you already knew the contents of.

And then she ran the room.

Rook watched her do it because Rook had taught her half of it and the worse half she'd brought with her from wherever she'd been before. The eyes went first to Boiler, then to Scout, then to Rook, then to the exits, then back across the three faces in the same order — a circuit, not a glance. Authority load-bearing here. A threshold of compliance there. The exit she was already standing in. It took her under two seconds and it cost her nothing visible, which was the part Rook had never managed to teach anyone else and the part that made Lace worth the door.

But Rook was not reading the room. Rook was reading Lace reading the room, and the circuit hitched. Not on the exits, not on Boiler's hands, not on the angles Scout had already condemned — on Rook's own face, where the eyes stayed a fraction past their interval, a half-beat too long. Something moved in Lace's jaw, low, a small tension banking and releasing, the work of a person swallowing a sentence on the way up the throat. The weight shifted in her — Rook saw it come most of the way to the surface of her face and pause — and then she put her shoulders down against the wall and her face closed over the place it had been the way water closes over a dropped stone.

Whatever she'd found in their faces she filed, and said nothing.

From Lace that was a finished sentence. But Rook had just watched the sentence cost her something to keep finished, and that was new. Not a secret — a secret was clean. This was load. Carried since the safe house, by the look of it. A brace that old didn't fail loud. It failed when you leaned on it.

Boiler owed Ohara nothing and came when he heard the name; Scout had condemned the room and come anyway, against her own read, when she heard it; Lace had read some cost on the first day none of them could see and come carrying it. Three different reasons. All of them Ohara's name, and under Ohara's name, Rook's. The not-saying was the only thing holding the four of them at one table, and Rook intended to keep it that way, because the second one of them named a reason out loud, somebody would have to decide whether it was good enough, and people who started deciding that left.

[Beat 3]
Ohara came last, because Ohara had asked, and someone who asks for a table does not sit at it before everyone else has decided to. She took the chair across from Rook, set her hands flat on the wood the way Rook had taught all four of them to do in a room they didn't trust — palms down, fingers spread, nothing of yours under the table where it can't be seen — and laid out the shape of it without preamble, which was the only mercy she had left to offer. She was thinner than the lake had left her. The carefulness she'd been living inside for two years had gotten into her hands; they didn't shake, and the not-shaking was a thing she was doing on purpose, the way a person holds a full glass level across a crowded room.

"There's a room inside my head I built and locked," she said, "and the lock is rusting open."

She let that be the version a person could hold, and then she gave them the rest. "Eighteen months ago I walled off a piece of my own neuretics — sealed it behind a passphrase, the way you'd brick up a doorway you didn't want anyone using. Mine included. When we walked off the Axiom job I set it to die quiet: slow corrosion across the hardware that holds the seal, eighteen months to brick, give or take. No alarm. No question of what was behind it." She turned a salt cellar a quarter-turn with one finger, not looking at it, giving her eyes somewhere to be that wasn't anyone's face. "I checked it last week out of a habit I should have broken. It's not dying on my numbers. Something behind the wall is pulling current off the seal to keep itself warm — drawing the wall's own power to stay alive — and a thing I built to be a tombstone does not get cold feet." She set the cellar down square, the way you set down a thing you've decided not to fidget with anymore. "There's something on the other side of the wall I built, it's been spending the wall's own power to stay alive, and the wall has weeks left. Maybe less. I stopped trusting the maybe two days ago."

She said all of it in one register, level and unhurried, the sentences laid end to end like a checklist read aloud to an empty room until the words went smooth and lost their teeth. That was the part that landed wrong. Not the content — the delivery. A person did not get a voice that flat off two years of careful living. A person got it off standing in front of the same fear long enough to take it apart and reassemble it in a register that couldn't hurt her. It could still hurt everyone else.

And the table heard it, each of them in their own frequency, because Rook had built a crew of people who did not process the same room the same way and that had always been the point.

Boiler took it as load. His broad hand went flat on the wood the way it had gone flat on the cracked column an hour ago, palm reading for the line of failure, and his cheerful went out of him all at once. He didn't ask a question. Men who read columns for a living know what a thing that's holding on pride looks like from the inside, and he had just been told that one of them was a person, and that the person was sitting across the table turning a salt cellar.

Scout heard the word *warm* and came most of the way back into her own eyes for it. She'd been half-gone into the overlay — the spread of feeds her crawlers fed her, the room rendered as angles and gaps instead of faces — the rooftop in her never quite coming inside, one foot on the parapet the way she always kept one. But a thing that pulled current to stay warm was a thing that wanted feeding, and a thing that wanted feeding was, by every read she had, alive enough to count as a body in the room. She didn't say it. She just stopped mapping the exits and looked, briefly, at the wall — the literal wall behind Ohara — the way she'd look at any structure she suddenly needed an angle on.

Lace read the faces. That was what Lace did with horror — she watched it travel. She'd come in out of the rain an hour ago and run the room for exits and authority and threshold, and now she ran it again, slower, watching Boiler's hand go flat and Scout come back from the parapet and Rook not move at all, and what she filed this time she did not file away. Something in her jaw set. She had known a piece of this since the safe house, carried it the whole time like a stone in a coat she'd forgotten she was still wearing, and Rook saw the weight shift in her and shift back, held a moment longer, and not come out. *Not yet,* the look said. *But it's getting heavy where she's holding it.*

Four reasons, all of them Rook's, none of them said.

"You don't know what's in there."

It was not a question and she did not attribute it to Ohara; she attributed it to the table, to all of them, to the fact that not one of them had ever opened the door they'd spent six weeks welding shut.

Nobody filled the gap after it. That was the discipline Rook had drilled into them across a dozen worse rooms — when a thing that heavy goes onto the table, you let it sit, you do not rush to lift it off, because the first hand to reach for it tells you what they're afraid of. So the four of them let it lie there in the middle of the wood between the salt cellar and Ohara's flat hands, and the bar's bad wiring ticked somewhere behind a crooked panel, and the neon from the window laid its useless warm color across the table and warmed nothing, and Ohara held still inside the silence the way she'd come prepared to hold still inside everything.

"No," Ohara said, when she'd let it cost her the full measure. "I know what we put in. I don't know what it's become while we weren't listening. Those aren't the same noun anymore." She turned her hands over, palms up, empty — the oldest gesture there is for someone with nothing left to hide and no way to prove it. "I told you once, in 2206, that you can't seal a thing you don't understand. You can only delay the conversation." Her eyes finally came up to Rook's and stayed. "I have a window and no crew. You have a crew and you stopped having a reason. I came to trade. This is the conversation."

[Beat 4]
Vox had not taken a chair. She stood at the wall where the bar's wiring ran behind a panel somebody had screwed back on crooked years ago, two fingers resting on the seam, and she had been there the whole briefing — close to the wall because the wall was where she did her listening. When she worked, she stopped feeling the room and started feeling the lines that ran under it: the live wires, the network trunks, the signal traffic, all of it. She called them channels. The graze on the Lake Platform had cost her two of the six she used to run, and she had never gotten them back, so now she felt the building through four instead of six, the way you'd map a house with half its lights out. She let Ohara finish, and then she let the room hold for exactly as long as it took everyone to look at her and not a beat longer. That was her courtesy and her arrogance both.

"Your curve's not wrong," she said, eyes half-shut, voice flattened to the register she used when most of her was somewhere other than her own mouth. "It's stale. Somebody bought your trash." Her two fingers pressed the seam. "There's traffic hitting the seal hardware on a paid line — not corponation, not Arcturus prime, a subcontract shell pushing fresh money through a name I've seen twice this month. PEREGRINE. New paper, new funder. And they're not poking the wall to understand it. They're poking it to time it." She opened her eyes. "They've already decided what's behind it is worth more cracked than studied. Your eighteen months died the day they signed."

She took her fingers off the seam, and the four channels let go of her the way a held breath lets go, the whole electrified spine of the block dropping back to the ordinary deafness of one room. Then she looked at Ohara, not unkindly, which was worse.

"You budgeted weeks. You came in here proud of weeks." The cooler behind the counter chose that second to cycle off, and the quiet that dropped after it had a weight the body felt before the mind named it. "You don't have weeks. Their draw and yours cross inside nine days — and that's if they don't accelerate, and people who buy a tombstone don't slow down to admire it."

Nine days. Rook felt the number land in the room and watched it travel — felt the whole table go still around her, Boiler's hand flat on the wood again, Scout surfaced far enough out of the overlay to be present for this, Lace watching the faces instead of the wall. She did not run the schedule out loud. She didn't have to. The shape of it settled the way a load settles onto a column, and she could feel where it was going to take the weight before she saw the crack: nine days, and most of them spent reaching a surgeon who would want time she could not give him to do the one thing the whole job turned on. The careful margin was the first thing the clock took. Which meant the job's whole risk had just slid off the schedule and onto a stranger's hands — and the stranger, by every account she had, did not like weight put there.

She watched the number reach each person in turn. Ohara did not flinch — she had spent two years not flinching — but the hand on the salt cellar stopped, the quarter-turn it had been keeping up since she sat down going dead-still, and her thumb pressed once against the glass corner hard enough to whiten, then released, then went flat to the wood again. Forty-seven years of careful, and the tell was not that the careful failed. It was that it had to be reapplied at all. She had walked in with a budget she'd built herself, a clean eighteen-month death with the rounding off, and she sat there now watching it shrink to a span she could hold up on her fingers. What crossed her face before she put it away was not fear. It was the specific exhaustion of a person who builds things for a living being told, again, that the build was never going to hold the way she'd drawn it.

Vox saw it too. Vox saw everything; it was the part of her the graze hadn't taken. She gave Ohara the rest without softening it.

"Whatever you sealed in there, somebody's already paying to dig it out — and they didn't come asking the woman who built the wall. They didn't have to. They just need it open." She let that sit, one revolution of a ceiling fan ticking where a blade had bent, the cooler holding its breath. "We're not the only crew that read your curve, Ohara. We're just the only one that knew you."

[Beat 5]
*Present day. Nine days, and they spent the first one driving.*

Z2 came up the way decay always did in this city — not a border but a tax the eye paid in increments. The neon thinned first. The bar-front saturation of the inner Gray Zone gave way to signage that had outlived its own businesses, half-lit, a vowel dead in every third word, whole letters gone dark and never replaced because nobody was left to file the complaint that would have gotten them replaced. A noodle sign blinked the same two characters out of phase with itself, on, off, never both. Under it a launderette's window still ran its loop of a smiling family folding sheets, the colors bled pink with age, the storefront behind the glass empty to the bare studs. The light kept advertising things the buildings had stopped being.

Then the rust showed. Not the picturesque kind operators liked to photograph for their feeds — the orange bloom that read as texture and meant nothing. The structural kind. The kind that meant a stair rail would take your weight or wouldn't, and you found out which on the way down. It came up out of the wet pavement in the support columns of an overpass that hadn't carried traffic since the lakefront subsidence, the rebar showing through the concrete like bone through a wound that had stopped trying to close.

Vox sat in the back of Boiler's hauler with two fingers laid flat against the door frame and felt the channels go sparse and ugly. The municipal trunk frayed out here to a thread, a single overtaxed line carrying what four should have carried, the packets arriving late and out of order like mail to an address the post office had quietly stopped believing in. The Arcturus relay was gone entirely — not down, gone, the mount on the tower picked clean and sold for the copper. Nothing clean left to lean on. Even the Pulse had given up on Z2. The tube bed ran through the district on a bypass spur that never stopped, no berth, no bench, the slug windows blurring past every four minutes at a speed that had nothing to do with anyone here — a reminder, four times an hour, that the city moved fastest through the parts it had already written off.

*Out here the Read gets quiet because there's less to overhear. Fewer minds, fewer machines, fewer wet little broadcasts to filter. I should like that. I don't. A loud room you can navigate — everybody's talking, nobody's listening, you ride the noise. A quiet one means the only signal left is the one that's hunting you, and it gets to hear you breathe.*

Vox closed the unused channels one at a time, the way you'd shutter rooms in a house too big to heat, and held the four that were left a little tighter for it.

At a corner where two dead storefronts shared a wall, a woman sat on a folding stool with a case open across her knees. Chrome instruments laid in foam, a clamp-lamp on a gooseneck angled down at the wrist of a man in the other chair. A Splicer working the gray zone rate — no clinic, no record, no anaesthetist, just the stool and the lamp and the rain coming down a meter from the open case. The man's sleeve was rolled to the elbow and his forearm was open from the heel of the hand to halfway up, the skin folded back in two neat flaps and pinned, and inside it the dull gleam of a tendon-bridge being seated by hand. He was reading something on his phone with his other thumb and not looking at his arm. The Splicer's hands moved without hurry, without ceremony, the motions of someone who had done this on this corner in this rain a thousand times and would do it a thousand more.

Rook watched them through the rain-streaked glass and felt something tighten in her chest before she could name it — the involuntary clench of a person recognizing something about herself in an image she had not expected. *Same trade.* Then, lower, before she could file it: *Wennick let me work on him once. Different corner, different rain. He didn't look at his arm either.* She closed that before it could go further. Filed it. Looked away.

"You've been quiet," Vox said, from the back.

Rook didn't turn around. "Counting."

"What are you counting?" Not a challenge. Vox's real questions always sounded like observations.

"I'm counting the number of people in this hauler who agreed to this job before they finished hearing what the job was." She watched the Splicer's hands in the mirror. "It's four."

The quiet that followed had a texture to it — not empty, not comfortable, just the sound of people who had all said yes to something they hadn't fully priced and who were, each in their own way, accounting for it now. Boiler's eyes in the rearview, easy, unbothered, the cheerful that had never quite left him. Scout's face angled toward the window, jaw set, watching vectors. Vox in the back not answering, which was its own answer.

"I'd do it again," Vox said finally.

"I know," Rook said. "That's what I'm counting."

Scout's crawlers had been in the district since the night before — palm-sized things she'd seeded off the back of a freight crawler, walking the gutters and the dead conduit, mapping current the way she mapped a heartbeat: not by listening to it, by feeling where it pooled and where it had stopped. Scout came back over the line not in words first but in a position — a single fixed point dropped onto the shared overlay, clean and certain. Then the words, dry and unbothered: the broker, Stave, ran their paper out of a substation conversion four blocks deep. An old municipal step-down building the grid still fed because nobody had ever gotten around to telling the grid it could stop.

That mattered. A building with its own dirty power was a building you could not starve out, could not blind by cutting a line, could not walk up on while it slept. Stave had chosen it the way Ohara had chosen her tile — because it was load-bearing.

Boiler had the wheel, and he watched the substation come up gray and squat through the rain with the frank affection he gave anything built to carry a load. "Somebody chose this the way you'd choose a corner," he said, approving.

Then, because to him it was the same thought: "Stave sells access. Whoever PEREGRINE is, they bought a map off Stave to find Ohara's wall — which means Stave sold them a door and sold us a chance, and Stave'll do it again to the next crew that walks in Q-dry and desperate."

Rook turned that over once. They were walking in owing Stave nothing and wanting everything. *PEREGRINE is new paper over an old hand. Somebody bankrolled this who went into the lake on the last job and walked back up the ramp dry — and you don't pay twice for a tombstone unless the first one didn't take.* She filed it where it could be used, and watched the substation come up through the rain.

[Beat 6]
Stave's office had been a transformer room and still smelled like one — ozone with a copper finish, the kind of air that put a film on the back of the throat. They sat behind a desk made from a sheared-off section of bus duct, and they did not get up when Vox came in alone, which Rook had told her to. Rook stood outside in the rain with the rest of them, because Rook's whole craft was being where the thing was before the thing knew it wanted to be found, and a broker's front room was not where the thing was. The back was. So Vox had gone around.

She came in through the load corridor, past the cabinet Stave thought was dead and Scout's crawler had told her was only sleeping, and she put the negotiation on the table before Stave finished registering that the table had two sides. "You sold a door to PEREGRINE," she said, and her voice did the thing it did out here, flattened to the register where most of her was listening to the building instead of the broker. "I'm not buying the door. I'm buying the lag. Whatever they paid you for live updates on that wall — the draw curve, the query timing, all of it — I want the channel that feeds them, and I want it to keep saying everything's fine for nine days while it stops being true."

*They're going to name a number and the number isn't the price. The price is the thing they ask for after I say yes too fast.*

"Money's easy," Stave said, and they smiled the way people smile when they've already won the part that mattered. "You're Q-dry, all of you, it's written on the hauler. So it isn't money." They tipped two fingers at the half-shut eyes, the stillness, the part of Vox that was three feet inside Stave's own wiring without permission. "It's that. PEREGRINE's people come back through here on their way out — and when they do, I want a name and a face on whoever's listening to my lines from the outside. You. I sell them you. Not now. After. A favor I haven't called yet." They spread their hands, the same empty-palmed gesture Ohara had made, and on them it meant the opposite. "Lag's yours. Cost is your face goes on a board I don't control."

Vox heard herself say yes before she finished hearing the terms, because the clock was nine days and the door was here and the crew was outside in the rain, and *that* was the thing she hadn't priced — not the favor, the speed of her own mouth, the way she'd built a whole life out of being the unseen channel and had just agreed to be the one named aloud to people who buy tombstones. Stave's line went quiet and obedient under her fingers, a fourth channel breathing the lie she'd bought.

*I sold the one thing I never put a number on. And the worst part is I'd do it again in eight days when there's one left.*

She walked back out into the redistribution coming down sideways off the substation's bad gutters, and Rook watched the door before Vox cleared it, because watching the door was the part of the job nobody thanked her for. The wall stopped pushing back at exactly the point she'd expected its hardest resistance, and the missing push was its own kind of vertigo, a stair-not-there in reverse: she'd leaned her whole weight into the loud place expecting it to be the keystone, the brick the whole lie was built to protect - and the loud place folded, not because she'd beaten it but because it had been waiting eighteen months for someone to lean on it from this exact angle. It didn't fail forward into the room she'd been positioned under, the live cache PEREGRINE had bought a clock to stand beneath. It failed backward. Into itself. The seam unsealed along the line of the original patch, peeling the way a label peels when you finally catch the corner that was always meant to lift, and the thing behind it came up cold.

Cold-stored. Not the live data, not warm with use, not the kind of thing a person keeps because they're still deciding what to do with it. Time-stamped - and the stamp was the part that stopped her, a date the operational log had never once carried, a date that sat before the partition itself, before the lie, before the eighteen months. Older than the wall that hid it. And under a credential the wall surrendered the instant she leaned on the loud place - not a credential she had to break, not one Stave had priced, but one the wall handed over the way a tired person hands over a thing they have been clutching too long: Ohara's own credential. Ohara's own hand. The lock she'd come to crack was not a lock against the world. It was a door Ohara had shut on something and then built eighteen months of false-live over the shutting, layer on layer of a wall pretending to be empty, until even she would read the place as already gone.

That was the thing Vox had not seen coming, and it arrived in her body before it arrived as a sentence - a drop, low and physical, the particular cold of finding a second floor under the floor you thought was the bottom. She'd cracked a hundred walls. She had never once cracked one and found the architect on the inside of it, locked in with the secret, locked in *by* the secret, the maker and the made-against the same person.

*She didn't pay for a door she forgot she had. She paid for one she forgot she'd locked. And I'm the only one inside it now, reading her reason in the cold.*

On the floor Rook's lamps held their meters, the readouts steady in their cradles, indifferent, counting a distance that had nothing to do with what the wall had just turned out to be. Ohara stood where Lace had put her against the cabinet, mouth kept where she'd been told to keep it, hand flat on the steel of a thing she'd sealed and paid Stave to open and stood inside the door of - not knowing, could not have known, that the open was already past the data she'd come for and into the data she'd hidden it behind. A version of herself eighteen months gone had stood somewhere with her hand on a different surface and decided that the safest place to put this was inside herself, behind a wall she would teach herself to forget. That version had left the cold like a letter, and the present one was standing on top of it with her palm flat against the steel, waiting for a stranger's hand on the loud place to remember it was there - and the stranger's hand had just remembered.

[Beat 12]
What was inside was a manifest. That was the first thing, and it landed wrong, because Vox had built the whole reading around the shape of a data-cache — a slab of formula-adjacent research, dense and dead and exactly the size of PEREGRINE's purchase order — and a manifest had a different texture under her hand entirely. Not a slab. A list. Rows. Something written to be read off by a person standing at the foot of a ramp, and a list felt different in the cold the way a column felt different under Boiler's hand: weight distributed in entries, not in mass.

Not corporate. Not a data-trade ledger, nothing PEREGRINE had a name for on their purchase order. A personnel manifest, cold-stamped two years before the job that had sent her into this wall ever existed. She read the header first, the way you always read a header first, and it was a transit code — six characters, the kind of routing string the city stamped on anything that moved through a berth — and she read it once and parsed it as freight, because that was what transit codes were, and started down into the rows.

Then she stopped, and went back up to the header, and read it again.

*A transit code is a thing that moves. It moves on a barge. The barge has a manifest. The manifest has names.*

She made herself take it in order, because out of order it was just dread, and in order it was evidence, and the order of things was the only floor she had left to stand on in here. The transit code was a barge; she'd crawled enough port traffic to read a hull out of six characters. The barge had a date, stamped in the second field, machine-flat, and it took her a full second to place it because it was an ordinary night two years back with nothing on the public record to mark it — and then she placed it, and the second went long.

The date was the night Old Harbor lost a whole tenement of registered Reads to a relocation order nobody had signed.

Everyone who worked the channels remembered the gap — a building's worth of registered sensitives current in the rolls one week and flagged *reassigned* the next, no destination filed, no receiving district, the kind of hole in a ledger you learned to read past because reading into it cost more than you had. She knew that gap. She'd had six channels then, and she'd felt the absence like a band of the signal she'd been calibrated to and then lost overnight, gone, no explanation, no body, only the *reassigned* in the column and the silence where sixty-one people's broadcasts had been. She'd never said it aloud, not even to the crew, because saying it meant sitting inside it, and the column was cleaner.

*Relocation is the word you write so the form clears. The word for what it actually was does not go on a form, because the people who sign forms do not write harvest where an auditor might one day read it.*

Sixty-one names. She felt the length of the row-stack the way she felt everything in here, not by reading each but by the weight of the whole, and it came back sixty-one — sixty-one registered Reads loaded onto a hull on a night the city had agreed not to remember. And then she felt the second column, and the second column was worse than the first, because the first was a crime and the second was a tally of it.

Forty of the names carried a second mark. A small flat notation set beside the entry, the kind of glyph that meant the same thing in every ledger language Vox had ever crawled a wall to find — receipt, reconciliation, the line item closed against its delivery. *Delivered.* Forty names with the books squared beside them. Forty people moved off a barge and onto whatever the relocation order had actually meant, and a clerk somewhere had ticked the column and the column had balanced.

The other twenty-one had no second mark at all.

She read that column three times, and the three reads were not the same read done over. The first was just the count — forty marked, twenty-one bare — and she ran it looking for the error that would let her put the number down. There was no error. The second was a read for a reason: she went down the bare entries one by one, looking for a reconciliation tucked small, set faint, where a tired clerk might have under-stamped it. The cold gave her nothing to find. Clean white space where forty of its neighbors carried a glyph, and clean white space does not get less clean for being read.

It was the third read that did it, because some facts will not go into the body on two passes. The count was right, the searching was done, the column was still empty, and on the third pass there was nothing left to do with it but believe it — and the believing arrived the way it always arrived for her, not as a thought but as a place going wrong in the meat of her: a tightening low under the sternum, the cold of the wall reaching past the hand-span of partition and into her own held breath two floors down, where her real throat caught on a real intake of air she hadn't told it to take. Twenty-one entries that went onto the barge and never reconciled against a delivery. The absence stopped being a number missing a mark and became the size of twenty-one people.

And outside the wall, a hand-span from where she knelt, Lace's two fingers were still on the cold. Still steady. Still not knowing that the silence they were keeping watch over was sixty-one names on a barge and twenty-one columns of empty space. The steadiness felt unbearable from the inside, not because Lace was failing her but because Lace couldn't know what she was steady for, and Vox was the only person in the room who understood the full weight of what those two fingers were holding place over. Lace had sat on eleven days and called it nine; here was the inverse — Vox sitting inside the whole of it, the only witness, with Lace's warmth fading into the patch a hand-span away.

*Twenty-one she walked off the manifest before it could square. Twenty-one she papered over with eighteen months of a wall pretending it was empty. That's not a secret she sat on to protect herself. That's the only good thing she ever did, and she built a year and a half of lying just so nobody could thank her for it and nobody could hang her for it — and she built it so well she forgot she'd done it.*

And PEREGRINE was forty meters out and closing — Rook's knock still in the bones of her hand, the patient vector folded into the last of the floor — having bought a clock to stand under a wall they thought held a data-cache, and that actually held the one document that put forty delivered names and a barge and a date in the same buried hand. The instant the seam finished unsealing, the manifest stopped being Ohara's secret and became the thing in the room, readable off the far face by anyone with a hand on it the second the wall stopped lying.

The decision was a verb and the sentence had been waiting all morning for it. She could finish the open — let the seam fail the way she'd half-built it to fail, hand PEREGRINE the live data and the buried data both in one clean break — and let the change clear the way she'd priced it back in Stave's transformer room. Or she could do to the wall what Ohara had done. Reseal the loud place behind her on the way out. Leave the twenty-one in the cold where Ohara had left them. Walk out of the seam carrying a thing nobody on the floor knew she carried, including the woman whose two fingers were still on the steel a hand-span from hers, steady and waiting and unable to know what they were holding watch over.

*I priced this against a data-cache. I never once priced it against this. And the change clears in eight days either way — except now I'm the part of the wall that decides whether twenty-one people stayed lost on purpose, and the meters don't stop for that, and Lace's two fingers are still on the cold a hand-span from mine, and I have to decide before PEREGRINE puts up a forty-first mark in a column that doesn't have a glyph for what they'd be writing.*

[Beat 13]
She resealed the loud place.

Not all the way — she couldn't, not with the reference gone and PEREGRINE's clock chewing the last forty meters, but she folded the seam back over itself the way Ohara had, leaned on the loud place a second time to make it lie a second time, and felt the manifest sink back under its own credential like a stone going down through water that closed over it without a mark. The live data she left where it lay, clean and shallow and exactly the size of the purchase order. PEREGRINE would get the clock they paid for. They would stand under the wall and read a data-cache and never know they'd been forty meters from sixty-one names and a barge.

*That's the job done and a wall lied to twice in one morning, both times by someone who didn't want a thing found. Ohara built me a door I was supposed to open and I'm walking out having locked it behind me, and she'll never know I was in the room with her reason, and that's the only way it stays the good thing she made it.*

Her hand was on the far face when the floor changed. Not a sound — a pressure dropping out of the room the way the reference tone had, except this one had a name on it, and the name came up through the cold reading on Lace's two fingers before it came up through anything she'd have called hearing. Adalemo. Standing in the doorway Rook's lamps didn't reach, having walked the loud room's eighteen months of complaint same as she had, except Adalemo hadn't come for a data-cache and hadn't come for PEREGRINE's clock. PEREGRINE had sent him to confirm what was behind the wall before they spent the last forty meters standing under it — and to confirm it, he'd needed her reference gone, so he'd put his hand on the feed that fed Stave's cabinet in Boiler's building and killed the hum at the source. He hadn't done it to blind her. He'd done it to watch her read a wall with nothing left to lean on but herself, because a Reader with no instrument reads true or reads nothing, and Adalemo had wanted to see which.

*He didn't come to read the wall. He cut my reference so he'd be reading me instead — and the only man who'd burn his own confirmation to watch me choose is one who already knew what twenty-one names in the cold were worth before he ever put his hand on that switch.*

Adalemo crossed the floor the way Rook crossed it — knowing where the lamps didn't reach and using it — and stopped close enough that the cold reading on Lace's fingers logged him as a second body, a second temperature standing off the patch.

"You sealed it," Adalemo said. Not a question. He had watched the wall stop lying and start again; he knew the shape of a reseal from the outside the way Vox knew it from in.

Vox said nothing, which was the only answer that didn't hand anything over.

"I'm not paid to open it," Adalemo said. "I'm paid to confirm what's behind it. PEREGRINE wants a data-cache. I just watched you build them one." He let it sit. "I read the strain on the loud place from out here. Forty marked, twenty-one bare. I can count, same as you." Then he did the thing that fit on no one's purchase order: he turned, put his own body between the seam and the vector Rook had marked, and faced out, the way you stand when you've decided which side of a wall you're on. "Forty meters. Walk your crew out the load corridor. I confirm a data-cache and I never read a forty-first name."

*He counted the same twenty-one I did, off the strain in the loud place, from the outside. And he's spending his own standing to let them stay lost — which means somewhere on the last job he stopped being only the thing that walked back up out of the lake, the one the crew said came back wrong because the man who'd weigh twenty-one strangers against his own fee doesn't walk this body between them and the meters. I'll never get to ask him about it. The corridor is right there and the meters don't stop for it.*

So she went, because there was nothing left in the room worth the time it would cost to feel anything about it, and the feeling could wait in the corridor where it didn't have a clock on it.

The load corridor was the building's spine before the building had been anything anyone lit — concrete poured for freight, ceiling low enough that the neon someone had strung along it decades ago had been strung sideways, dead tubes and live ones alternating so the light came at her in bands, warm, warm, dark, warm, none of it reaching the floor. Rust had taken the channel struts where the damp got in and left them the color of dried blood, and the whole length of it smelled of cold iron and standing water, the GLMZ underneath the GLMZ, the part that held the rest up and got no maintenance for it. Her steps fell into the dark bands and out of them. Behind her, Adalemo's body stayed where he'd put it, a shape she could feel on the patch sensor going small the way a held note goes small, and she did not turn around to confirm it, because confirming it would have meant deciding what it was, and she had decided enough in that room for one morning.

She walked out of a room where a man had just chosen a side, and the thing she carried out with her was not relief. It was the specific weariness of having watched someone do an expensive thing for a reason they would never be paid for and never explain, and knowing she was the only witness, and knowing the witnessing was the whole of what she'd ever get. The four channels she had left ran quiet in her skull the way they always ran now, and for once she let them stay quiet. There was nothing out there she needed to hear over the sound of her own steps in a corridor that should have been demolished six years ago and was, instead, the only clean way out.

Rook was at the mouth of it.

She had the door cracked the width of a forearm and her back to the jamb, watching the street side, and she turned her head when the light bands caught Vox coming up the spine and read her in the half-second it took — read the channels running quiet, read the hands empty of a transfer brick, read whatever showed on Vox's face that Vox could not see herself. Rook's eyes went past Vox's shoulder, down the dark of the corridor, to the place where a second temperature was standing off a sealed wall, and came back. She did not ask who was back there. She did not ask why the wall was sealed instead of opened, why the brick was empty, why Vox had walked out of a recovery without the thing recoveries were for.

For a moment Rook's hand, the one that had been flat on the grating all morning, came up and pressed, briefly, against her own sternum — a small involuntary check, as though confirming a thing she'd needed to confirm. Then it came back down. The not-asking was the most Rook thing she did all morning — she had built a crew out of people whose silences she trusted, and she paid the trust now by spending none of it on questions whose answers she could see Vox had already filed somewhere they both knew she'd never reopen.

"Boiler's got the truck running on the lake side," Rook said, which meant *I see you, the count is closed, we go.* "Load corridor was clear?"

"It was," Vox said, and stepped past her into the neon coming off the water, where the light meant nothing and looked like everything, and let the door close on the dark behind them.

[Beat 14]
*Present day.*

Rook took a booth at the Sojourn because it wasn't Mrs. Chen's and wasn't the place by the cabinet and wasn't anywhere Vox would think to look for the count to get done. The Sojourn was a corner diner that had been a corner diner long enough to have stopped advertising it — half the booths torn down to the foam, the menu board missing the prices it used to carry, a coffee machine that worked because someone had decided once, years ago, that it would keep working and then walked away. It suited her. Nobody maintained it and it held anyway, out of habit, which was the only kind of holding she trusted at the end of a job.

The window faced the lake, which did what it always did, which was nothing. It held the neon off the towers in a long broken smear on the water and gave none of it back — not the color, not the heat, not the meaning. In the dark glass she came back to herself laid over the lake, a woman the window held as indifferently as it held the neon and gave back just as little. She looked a second longer than she had to, the way you check a reflection not for vanity but to confirm the body is still doing what you told it to. Then she let the glass have it. Six lamps, accounted for and bagged in the duffel under the table, the weight of them against her boot the cleanest fact in the room. She set the ledger on the laminate and did not open it yet.

The number was different this time. Eight days to clear, Vox had priced it, and the change had cleared, and the duffel under the table was the proof of it. But the line that wouldn't leave the bottom of her mind wasn't quanta. It was twenty-one. Twenty-one wasn't a fee. There was no column for it. And she was the only one at this booth — the only one anywhere, now that Adalemo had decided to never read a forty-first name — who knew the column existed at all.

*She sealed it back and the change cleared and the lamps are in the bag, and the books say we got paid. The books always say we got paid. They have never once said who didn't.*

The coffee came. She hadn't ordered it. The woman behind the counter had put it down without being asked, without looking at her, with the specific non-attention of someone who had been keeping a place running long enough to know when a booth needed something warm in it whether or not the occupant knew they did. Not Mrs. Chen's watching, that careful leveled look that knew what the tile was for. Simpler than that. Just a cup, handle turned toward her hand, and the woman already walking back.

Something moved behind Rook's eyes before she could stop it — a tightening, low and quick, the particular kind that didn't have a name because she had never given it one. Wennick had done that, once. Turned the cup. Not because it was kindness, or not only — it was the small operational habit of a person who paid attention to which hand you favored, who built the room around you without making a performance of it. She had not thought of that cup in years. She thought of it now, in a booth in a diner nobody maintained, looking at a handle turned toward the hand of a woman who would never know she'd done it. The tightening released, slowly, the way a held thing releases when you stop deciding whether to hold it.

*He would have known what to do with the twenty-one. He'd have found a place to put them — a column, or made one. That's the thing she built the rule to avoid feeling again: not the loss. The certainty that he'd have found the column, and she just closed the books instead.*

She looked at the water and let the number sit where it was. She had carried numbers out of rooms before. You logged them, filed them, and the next morning the weight was gone. She reached for that now and found nothing there. The twenty-one had no heading. *Paid* didn't cover it. *Lost* wasn't hers to write — the names were Ohara's, and Adalemo's now, and the lake's. *Done* was a lie she could feel the shape of before she finished forming it. So the twenty-one stayed. Level, unmoving, occupying a place in her she had no column for, the way the duffel stayed against her boot — weight, real and specific, with nowhere to set it down.

The neon off the lake was a different number of colors than the last time she'd sat at a window doing a count — fewer towers lit, the Spire dark for some scheduled reason that didn't reach down to a booth at the Sojourn. Different smear, different night, different job sealed behind a wall. The lake was the same. It had been here before the barge and would be here after the manifest went cold a second time, and it did not care that twenty-one names had no second mark. The not-caring was almost a comfort. She let it be one for as long as it would hold.

It didn't hold.

She took out a square of paper. Real paper — weight and grain, cold at first against her fingers and then not, the kind nobody used unless they wanted no copy to exist, nothing that could be pulled or indexed or read by anyone she hadn't handed it to. She had carried it a long time without knowing what it was for. Now she knew. She wrote the number down. Just the number. Twenty-one. No names; she didn't have the right to carry the names. Only the count, which was the one thing that was hers to keep and the one thing the ledger on the table would never hold.

She folded the paper once, and along the crease she ran her thumbnail — slow, the way you press a thing you mean to keep. She had pressed a fold like this once before, at the start of all of it, on the note that brought her back. The same motion. The same pressure of the nail against the grain. She understood, doing it a second time, that she had not invented this gesture. She had been keeping it for someone who no longer needed it, and she was spending it now on a count he would have known what to do with, and the spending was not grief, exactly. It was closer to owing. She put the paper where she kept the things that were only hers.

The lake went on giving nothing back. She let it.
