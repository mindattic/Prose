# Neon & Rust

_Sequel to Magenta & Gunmetal. Adalemo survived the lake. Ohara's neuretics hold a locked partition — older than she remembers, worse than she theorized. Rook reassembles the crew to crack it before PEREGRINE collects her._

[Beat 1]
The note was paper, which was the first wrong thing. Rook turned it over once before she read it, checking the weight, the grain, the way the fold had been pressed flat with a thumbnail and not a machine. Nobody used paper anymore unless they wanted no copy to exist, and nobody who wanted no copy to exist would also use the drop under the loose tile at the back of Mrs. Chen's, because that drop was hers. She had set it in 2206 and told four people, and three of them were dead.

The fourth had signed it. *O.*

*He went into the wall I built and he left a door open behind him.*

She read the rest standing, the way she read everything she did not intend to keep — eyes moving, hands already deciding. Eight lines. Ohara wanted a meet, named a time, named a place in the Gray Zone close enough to the Arcturus line that it was either a confession or a trap and Rook did not care which, because the part that mattered was the first sentence and she had stopped reading after it. Ohara had used the tile. She had reached into the one channel that should have been cold for two years and she had done it knowing Rook would know exactly what it meant.

She folded the paper back along its crease, slid it into her jacket, and was three steps toward the door before she registered that her noodles were still on the counter, steaming, paid for. She left them. Mrs. Chen watched her go and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

[Beat 2]
They were not hard to find, which told Rook everything about how badly they had scattered. Real distance leaves no forwarding address. These three had left whole streets of it.

Boiler had not gone far at all. He was four blocks off the lake in a building that had been scheduled for demolition for six years and outlived the schedule, walking the third floor with his head tipped back, reading a crack in a load-bearing column the way another man reads a letter he's been carrying around too long. He heard her on the stairs and didn't turn. "I haven't agreed to anything," he said, cheerful, to the column. Then he told the column it had been holding on pride for fifteen years, patted it once like the flank of a horse, and went to get his kit.

*He found a condemned building to stand inside while he waited to be asked. He'd call that a coincidence. It is the least coincidental thing I have ever seen.*

Scout she found on a rooftop in the Gray Zone with her eyes rolled white and four crawlers walking the parapet, mapping the block the way she mapped everything — in vectors and gaps, never in walls. She didn't take the folded paper. She came back into her own eyes by degrees, looked at Rook's face instead of the note, and said the place was a kill-box, the angles wrong on three sides and worse on the fourth, and she was coming anyway. One of the crawlers had been called Gerald by somebody two jobs back. She didn't correct it. She'd stopped correcting it.

Nobody asked how Rook still had the rooftop, the kit, the unlisted door. That was the courtesy. You did not ask the woman who built the crew how she kept the keys to it; you only noted that she did, and decided what that was worth to you, and came or you didn't.

Lace came last, on her own, an hour later, in out of the rain. She didn't look at the paper. She looked at Rook, then at each of them in turn — the quick read she ran on any room she meant to stand in, authority load-bearing here, a threshold of compliance there, the exit she was already standing in — and whatever she found in their faces she filed and said nothing, which from Lace was a finished sentence. Boiler owed Ohara nothing and came; Scout had a reason nobody could name and came; Lace had read the cost on the first day and came anyway, and the not-saying was the only thing holding the four of them at one table.

*Everybody at this table has a different reason and all of them are mine. I will not name a single one of them out loud, because the second one of us does, somebody leaves.*

[Beat 3]
Ohara came last, because Ohara had asked, and someone who asks for a table does not sit at it before everyone else has decided to. She took the chair across from Rook, set her hands flat on the wood the way Rook had taught all four of them to do in a room they didn't trust, and laid out the shape of it without preamble, which was the only mercy she had left to offer.

"The partition's failing," she said. "The one we left up. Not failed — failing. There's a difference and the difference is the only reason I used your tile instead of running."

*She says "we" like it's load-bearing. She knows it is.*

"When we walked it off two years ago I left a degradation curve so it would die quiet," Ohara went on, turning a salt cellar a quarter-turn with one finger, not looking at it. "Slow corrosion across the gate hardware. Eighteen months to brick, give or take. Clean. No alarm, no inheritance, no question of what was sealed behind it. I checked the curve last week out of a habit I should have broken. It's not corroding on my numbers. Something's pulling current off the gate to keep itself warm. Drawing maintenance power through a partition that is supposed to be a tombstone, and a tombstone does not get cold feet." She stopped turning the cellar. "There's something on the other side of the wall I built, and it has been spending the wall's own power to stay alive, and the wall has weeks left. Maybe less. I stopped trusting the maybe two days ago."

She let the silence do its work around the table — Boiler's broad hand going flat on the wood like he was feeling it for load, Scout half-gone into the overlay even here, the rooftop in her never quite coming inside, Lace reading each face in turn the way she read a room she already meant to leave by a particular door. Four reasons, all of them Rook's, none of them said.

*She built it to die and it decided to keep living, and she came to the one person who told her in 2206 that you cannot seal a thing you do not understand — you can only delay the conversation. This is the conversation.*

"You don't know what's in there," Rook said. It was not a question and she did not attribute it to Ohara; she attributed it to the table, to all of them, to the fact that not one of them had ever opened the door they'd spent six weeks welding shut.

"No," Ohara said. "I know what we put in. I don't know what it's become while we weren't listening. Those aren't the same noun anymore." She turned her hands over, palms up, empty — the oldest gesture there is for someone with nothing left to hide and no way to prove it. "I have a window and I don't have a crew. You have a crew and you stopped having a reason. I came to trade."

[Beat 4]
Vox had not sat down. She stood at the wall where the bar's cabling ran behind a panel that somebody had screwed back on crooked years ago, two fingers resting on the seam, and when Ohara finished her sentence she let the room hold for exactly as long as it took for everyone to look at her and not a beat longer. That was her courtesy and her arrogance both. She did not announce the Read. She just stopped feeling the room and started feeling the four channels that ran under it — the bar's own dirty line, the Gray Zone municipal trunk, the Arcturus relay throwing its hard clean signal off the line six blocks east, and underneath all of it the thin starved thread that Ohara's tile had told her to look for, the one drawing current it had no right to.

*Four channels and three of them are normal and the fourth is somebody breathing through a straw and pretending it's the wind.*

"Your curve's not wrong," she said, eyes half-shut, voice flattened to the register she used when most of her was elsewhere. "It's stale. Somebody bought your trash. There's a query pattern hitting the gate hardware on a paid relay — not corponation, not Arcturus prime, a subcontract shell pushing fresh capital through a name I've seen twice this month. PEREGRINE. New paper, new funder, and they're not poking the wall to understand it. They're poking it to time it. They've already decided what's behind it is worth more cracked than studied." She opened her eyes. "Your eighteen months died the day they signed. They're moving fast and they're moving like people who've done this before."

She took her fingers off the seam, and the absence of the four channels came back into her like a held breath let go, the ordinary deafness of one room instead of the whole electrified spine of it. She looked at Ohara, not unkindly, which was worse.

*She budgeted weeks. She came here proud of weeks.*

"You said weeks," she said. "You don't have weeks. PEREGRINE's draw curve and yours intersect inside nine days, and that's if they don't accelerate, and people who buy a tombstone don't slow down to admire it. Whatever you sealed in there, somebody else is already paying to dig it out — and they didn't come asking the woman who built the wall. They didn't have to. They just need it open. We're not the only crew that read your curve, Ohara. We're just the only one that knew you."

[Beat 5]
*Present day. Nine days, and they spent the first one driving.*

Z2 came up the way decay always did in this city — not a border but a tax the eye paid in increments. The neon thinned first, the bar-front saturation of the inner Gray Zone giving way to signage that had outlived its own businesses, half-lit, a vowel dead in every third word. Then the rust showed. Not the picturesque kind that operators liked to photograph; the structural kind, the kind that meant a stair rail would take your weight or wouldn't and you found out which on the way down. Vox sat in the back of Boiler's hauler with two fingers on the door frame and felt the channels go sparse and ugly — the municipal trunk fraying out here to a thread, the Arcturus relay gone entirely, nothing clean left to lean on.

*Out here the Read gets quiet because there's less to overhear. I should like that. I don't. A loud room you can navigate. A quiet one means the only signal left is the one that's hunting you.*

Scout's crawlers had been in the district since the night before — palm-sized things she'd seeded off the back of a freight crawler, walking the gutters and the dead conduit, mapping current the way she mapped a heartbeat. Scout came back over the line not in words first but in a position, a single fixed point dropped onto the shared overlay, and then the words, dry and unbothered: the broker, Stave, ran their paper out of a substation conversion four blocks deep, an old municipal step-down building the grid still fed because nobody had ever gotten around to telling the grid it could stop. That mattered. A building with its own dirty power was a building you could not starve out, could not blind by cutting a line, could not walk up on while it slept. Stave had chosen it the way Ohara had chosen her tile — because it was load-bearing.

Boiler had the wheel, and he watched the substation come up gray and squat through the rain with the frank affection he gave anything built to carry a load. "Somebody chose this the way you'd choose a corner," he said, approving. Then, because to him it was the same thought: "Stave sells access. Whoever PEREGRINE is, they bought a map off Stave to find Ohara's wall — which means Stave sold them a door and sold us a chance, and Stave'll do it again to the next crew that walks in Q-dry and desperate."

*And we walk in there owing Stave nothing and wanting everything, which is the worst hand to play and the only one we've got.* She took her fingers off the frame and let the ugly thin channels go. *And PEREGRINE is new paper over an old hand. Somebody's bankrolling this who went into the lake on the last job and walked back up the ramp dry — and you don't pay twice for a tombstone unless the first one didn't take.*

[Beat 6]
Stave's office had been a transformer room and still smelled like one — ozone with a copper finish, the kind of air that put a film on the back of the throat. They sat behind a desk made from a sheared-off section of bus duct, and they did not get up when Vox came in alone, which Rook had told her to. Rook stood outside in the rain with the rest of them, because Rook's whole craft was being where the thing was before the thing knew it wanted to be found, and a broker's front room was not where the thing was. The back was. So Vox had gone around.

She came in through the load corridor, past the cabinet Stave thought was dead and Scout's crawler had told her was only sleeping, and she put the negotiation on the table before Stave finished registering that the table had two sides. "You sold a door to PEREGRINE," she said, and her voice did the thing it did out here, flattened to the register where most of her was listening to the building instead of the broker. "I'm not buying the door. I'm buying the lag. Whatever they paid you for live updates on that wall — the draw curve, the query timing, all of it — I want the channel that feeds them, and I want it to keep saying everything's fine for nine days while it stops being true."

*They're going to name a number and the number isn't the price. The price is the thing they ask for after I say yes too fast.*

"Money's easy," Stave said, and they smiled the way people smile when they've already won the part that mattered. "You're Q-dry, all of you, it's written on the hauler. So it isn't money." They tipped two fingers at the half-shut eyes, the stillness, the part of Vox that was three feet inside Stave's own wiring without permission. "It's that. PEREGRINE's people come back through here on their way out — and when they do, I want a name and a face on whoever's listening to my lines from the outside. You. I sell them you. Not now. After. A favor I haven't called yet." They spread their hands, the same empty-palmed gesture Ohara had made, and on them it meant the opposite. "Lag's yours. Cost is your face goes on a board I don't control."

Vox heard herself say yes before she finished hearing the terms, because the clock was nine days and the door was here and the crew was outside in the rain, and *that* was the thing she hadn't priced — not the favor, the speed of her own mouth, the way she'd built a whole life out of being the unseen channel and had just agreed to be the one named aloud to people who buy tombstones. Stave's line went quiet and obedient under her fingers, a fourth channel breathing the lie she'd bought. She walked back out into the redistribution coming down sideways off the substation's bad gutters, and the crew read it off her before she said a word, the way you read weather.

*I sold the one thing I never put a number on. And the worst part is I'd do it again in eight days when there's one left.*

[Beat 7]
The crew didn't ask and Vox didn't give it to the crew clean. She gave them the building first — Stave's front, the dead-not-dead cabinet, the lag bought and breathing — and watched Boiler turn the thing over the way he turned everything over, looking for where it carried load and where it didn't. He asked the only question that mattered, which was not *what did it cost* but *what did Stave know about the wall that we don't*, and that was when Stave's voice came back over the line they'd left open, uninvited, because a broker who has sold you a door likes to stand in it.

"You want to know why PEREGRINE's curve and Ohara's meet inside nine days?" Stave said, and the copper-ozone in the air seemed to lean in with them. "Because the wall already cracked once. Eighteen months back. Small — a hairline, a query that got an answer it shouldn't have, sealed inside a day. Ohara papered it and went quiet and everyone who'd been counting wrote it down. I wrote it down. The crack's been widening at a rate you could set a clock by ever since, and PEREGRINE bought my clock. The thing you're racing isn't their dig. It's the wall failing on its own. They just want to be standing under it when it does."

*Eighteen months. Ohara budgeted weeks because Ohara has been lying to herself longer than she's been lying to us.*

Stave gave Vox the rest the way people give bad news they aren't carrying — lightly, palms up. The degradation had a knee in it, a point past which the curve stopped being a line and became a drop, and Scout's crawlers could read where they were on it but not whether they'd already passed it, because the only instrument that could tell her that was a person going in through the seam Ohara had sealed and listening to the wall from the inside. The procedure was survivable. They said it like that, flat, the word *probably* doing all the work they weren't going to do for free â€” and in the half-second before the word, something crossed Stave's face and was filed before it finished, not fear so much as a person checking a scar they had stopped looking at on purpose. Someone had gone in on Stave's read once and come back out less than they went in with. Stave did not say so. The pause said so, and then they put it away, palms up, nothing in the hands. Reads had gone in before. Most of them came back out. They didn't say what came out of the ones that didn't, and Vox didn't make them, because she could already feel the shape of it sitting in the quiet between the channels — the loud room she could navigate and the silent one that meant the only signal left was the one going in after her.

*Most of them came back out. Stave built a whole quiet life out of the ones that didn't — and now they're handing me the same odds with their palms up, because out here a person is just one more piece of infrastructure nobody got around to maintaining.*

[Beat 8]
Lace had been quiet against the hauler the whole walk back, the way she got quiet when she was deciding how much of a thing to spend, and she spent it now. "I knew about the crack." She said it to the gutter, not to any of them, watching the redistribution sheet off the bad eave and split around her boots. "Safe house in MGUN. The night before we crossed the Gray Zone. There was a man came through selling a degradation log on Ohara's east wall and I read it and I priced it and I didn't buy it, because the number was eleven days and we had nine and I thought nine was enough margin to not pay for the truth." She wiped rain off her face with the flat of her hand and left it there a second, over her mouth, like she could put the sentence back. "Stave's clock and the MGUN log are the same clock. I just didn't want it to be."

*I sat on it because buying it meant admitting we were already late, and admitting we were already late meant Rook runs the numbers and the answer comes back don't go.*

Boiler didn't say anything this time. He just looked at Lace the way he looked at a column that had been carrying more than its rating for years, and the looking was worse than arithmetic, because the cost he was reading wasn't on any curve he could point to.

Rook came around the front of the hauler from wherever Rook had been standing — out at the seam of it, where the thing was — and said nothing, which was Rook's whole position stated in full: the wall wasn't eleven days out anymore and it wasn't nine. It was however long it had already been failing while two people who knew kept it folded in their pockets for two different prices.

"Then we're not racing PEREGRINE's dig and we're not racing Stave's clock," Vox said, and she heard the flatness come back into her own voice, the listening register, except there was nothing left in the room to listen to except this. "We're racing the one in MGUN, which is the earliest reading any of us had, which means the knee Stave wouldn't price — we might already be past it. And the only instrument that tells us is me, inside, against a wall that's been lying about how close it is for eighteen months." She looked at Lace, and she didn't make it a question, because Lace had already answered it by going quiet for nine days. "You owed me that one in the safe house. I'd have gone in either way. But I'd have gone in knowing." And then Vox let it lie, because she of all of them had no standing to press it â€” she had sold her own face to Stave and told no one until the terms were already signed. Lace had sat on eleven days; Vox had sat on a name. The not-pressing was the closest the two of them came to saying it out loud: same animal, paying in the one currency each had sworn she never would.

*And that's the thing about all of us at this table — every one of us has a number we sat on, and mine is the lag I bought with my own face, and hers is eleven days she let read as nine, and Ohara's is eighteen months, and none of it changes the seam I climb into in the morning. It only changes what I carry in with me.*

[Beat 9]
Morning came as a degree of difference in the dark, not a sunrise — the substation had no east wall to give them one, just the slow lift of the rain from sideways to vertical, which meant the storm had moved off the higher tiers and they were getting it straight down now, their share.

Stave's people were gone by then. They'd left the cabinet breathing its bought lie on the fourth channel and a clean seam where the dead-not-dead door read live to anyone counting from the outside, and that was the whole of what they'd left, because Stave sold doors, not entrances.

Boiler walked the floor twice with Scout's crawlers trailing him like something he'd been assigned to feed, reading the wall's near face for the place Ohara had papered eighteen months ago, and found it where the log said it would be — a hairline you'd have to know was there, sealed and resealed, the patch slightly the wrong temperature against the back of his hand.

*The patch is colder than the wall around it. Whatever's behind it has been pulling heat for a year and a half and nobody noticed because nobody put a hand on it.*

Ohara came in last and ungraciously, the way people come to the thing they paid for and then resent paying, her face doing the empty-palmed arithmetic she'd done at the table except there was no spread of hands now, just the standing in the door she'd bought a stake in. Lace put her against the cabinet and told her where to keep her mouth, and she kept it.

Rook set up out at the seam in the floor where PEREGRINE's vector came in low and patient on Scout's read — not a dig anymore, Stave had been right, just a crew positioning to be standing under the wall when it went, their approach the slow confident line of people who'd bought a clock and trusted it. Rook marked the vector with two cold lamps and said the only thing Rook said all morning, which was the distance, in meters, and let the number be the warning.

Vox sat against the wall with the patch and let the channels go down one at a time the way you turn off lights leaving a house — the crew's chatter first, then the cabinet's bought hum, then Stave's open line they'd left in the door, last.

Lace crouched in front of Vox and didn't say *be careful* and didn't say *I'm sorry* and didn't say the eleven days again, just put two fingers flat against the patch beside Vox's head so the two of them would be reading the same cold, and held them there.

Then Vox put her own hand on the seam, the silent room on the far side of it, and went in after the only signal that was left.

Vox went quiet. On the floor the lamps held their distance, and the rain came straight down now, and her breathing slowed and stopped being a thing the crew could hear.

[Beat 10]
Inside the wall was the loud room first, and it arrived as pressure before it arrived as anything else — a weight that set behind her eyes and sank into her jaw, eighteen months of the patch's own complaint pitched at a frequency she felt in her molars more than heard. The cold came next, not a temperature but a direction, a steady pull through a seam that read live and wasn't, the wall lying to its own near face the way it had lied to Ohara's log. Her body was somewhere behind her — knees on the substation grit, hand flat on the patch, Lace's two fingers a hand-span from her own — and she made herself feel the floor through her kneecaps once, a tether, before she let it go and went past the loud room into the quiet, the silent room where the only signal was the one she carried. She started reading the wall the way you read a held breath: where it was thin, where it was thinnest, where the knee Stave wouldn't price had already bent. She found the bend. It was behind her, not ahead. They were past it.

*Past it. Not at it, not near it — through it, the way you're through a stop sign before your foot finds the brake.*

Rook's number reached her sideways, not through any channel — Rook had crossed the floor and put a boot to the cabinet's frame twice, a flat physical knock that traveled the steel into the patch into the bones of her hand, and she counted the gaps and read the meters out of them. Closing. PEREGRINE's slow confident line had stopped being slow. They'd felt the same bend she had — somebody on their side had a hand on the far face — and the patient vector folded into the last fifty meters all at once, people who'd bought a clock and just heard it stop ticking.

*They're not waiting for it to go anymore. They're going to be the reason it goes, and they want to be standing inside when it does, and the wall doesn't care which of us is the excuse.*

She had the reading half-built — the thin places mapped, the order she'd have to take them in to walk the failure backward instead of forward — and half-built was a sentence with no verb. To finish it she needed the cabinet's bought hum on the fourth channel, the reference tone Stave had sold them, the clean false-live she was supposed to read the real wall against. She reached for it the way you reach for a wall in the dark, and it wasn't there. The hum was gone. Stave's cabinet had stopped breathing its lie — brownout, or a hand on a switch fifty meters out, or the wall itself finally pulling more than the line could feed — and the silent room got one degree more silent, the reference tone she'd been standing on dropped out from under the reading, and she was inside a wall she could no longer measure against anything but herself.

*Survivable, probably. Except probably was priced against an instrument that just went dark, and now it's just me and a wall, both of us lying about how much time is left.*

[Beat 11]
She walked the failure backward without the reference, which meant she walked it against the one wall she had left — the loud room's eighteen months of complaint, the patch's own held breath, the cold's steady rate of pull. She used the lie as the measure for the lie. The wall had been telling its near face it was live for a year and a half; if she could find where the lie ran thinnest, where the patch had to work hardest to keep saying live, that was where the real seam was widest. So she stopped reading for the silent room and started reading for the strain in the loud one, and the order came up out of it inverted — not the thin places first but the place that lied loudest, because that was the place most afraid of being read.

*Ohara papered this. Eighteen months ago, with her own hand, and the patch is the shape of whatever she didn't want found. So the wall isn't lying to me. It's keeping her secret, and it's tired.*

The open came when she took the loud place first instead of last. It didn't fail forward into the room she'd been positioned under; it failed backward into itself, the seam unsealing along the line of the original patch, and what was behind it wasn't the live data PEREGRINE had bought a clock to stand under. It was older. Cold-stored, time-stamped to a date the log had never carried, locked under a credential the wall surrendered the instant she leaned on the loud place — Ohara's own credential, Ohara's own hand, a door she'd shut on something and then built eighteen months of false-live over the shutting so that even she would read it as already gone.

*She didn't pay for a door she forgot she had. She paid for one she forgot she'd locked. And I'm the only one inside it now, reading her reason in the cold, and her reason is older than the job, older than the crew, older than the number any of us sat on at that table.*

On the floor Rook's lamps held their meters and Ohara stood where Lace had put her against the cabinet, mouth kept where she'd been told to keep it, hand flat on the steel of a thing she'd sealed and paid Stave to open and stood inside the door of, not knowing that the open was already past the data she'd come for and into the data she'd hidden it behind — her own younger handwriting in the cold, waiting eighteen months for a hand on the loud place to remember it was there.

[Beat 12]
What was inside was a manifest. Not corporate, not a data-trade ledger, nothing PEREGRINE had a name for on their purchase order — a personnel manifest, cold-stamped two years before the job existed, sixty-one names under a header Vox read once and then read again because the header was a transit code and the transit code was a barge and the barge had a date and the date was the night Old Harbor lost a whole tenement of registered Reads to a relocation order nobody had signed. Relocation was the word on the order. The word for what it actually was did not go on orders, because the people who put their names to those did not write harvest where an auditor might one day read it. Sixty-one names. Forty of them with a second mark beside them, a small flat notation that meant the same thing in every ledger language Vox had ever crawled through a wall to find. Delivered. The other twenty-one had no second mark at all.

*Twenty-one names she didn't deliver. Twenty-one she walked off a manifest and papered over with eighteen months of a wall pretending it was empty. That's not a secret she sat on. That's the only good thing she ever did, and she built a year and a half of lying just so nobody could thank her for it or hang her for it.*

And PEREGRINE was forty meters out and closing, having bought a clock to stand under a wall that they thought held a data-cache and that actually held the one document that put forty names and a barge in the same hand — and the instant the seam finished unsealing, that document stopped being Ohara's secret and became the thing in the room, readable by anyone with a hand on the far face the second the wall stopped lying. The decision was a verb and the sentence had been waiting all morning for it. She could finish the open and hand PEREGRINE the manifest whole, the live data and the buried data both, and let the change clear the way she'd priced it. Or she could do to the wall what Ohara had done — reseal the loud place behind her on the way out, leave the twenty-one in the cold, and walk out carrying a thing nobody on the floor knew she was carrying, including the woman whose hand was flat on the steel of it right now.

*I priced this against a data-cache. I never priced it against this. And the change still clears in eight days — except now I'm holding the part of the wall that decides whether twenty-one people stayed lost on purpose, and the meters don't stop for that, and Lace's two fingers are still on the cold reading whatever I read, and I have to decide before the second mark goes up next to a forty-first name.*

[Beat 13]
She resealed the loud place.

Not all the way — she couldn't, not with the reference gone and PEREGRINE's clock chewing the last forty meters, but she folded the seam back over itself the way Ohara had, leaned on the loud place a second time to make it lie a second time, and felt the manifest sink back under its own credential like a stone going down through water that closed over it without a mark. The live data she left where it lay, clean and shallow and exactly the size of the purchase order. PEREGRINE would get the clock they paid for. They would stand under the wall and read a data-cache and never know they'd been forty meters from sixty-one names and a barge.

*That's the job done and a wall lied to twice in one morning, both times by someone who didn't want a thing found. Ohara built me a door I was supposed to open and I'm walking out having locked it behind me, and she'll never know I was in the room with her reason, and that's the only way it stays the good thing she made it.*

Her hand was on the far face when the floor changed. Not a sound — a pressure dropping out of the room the way the reference tone had, except this one had a name on it, and the name came up through the cold reading on Lace's two fingers before it came up through anything she'd have called hearing. Adalemo. Standing in the doorway Rook's lamps didn't reach, having walked the loud room's eighteen months of complaint same as she had, except Adalemo hadn't come for a data-cache and hadn't come for PEREGRINE's clock. Adalemo had come because Boiler's building was on the same line that fed Stave's cabinet, and the cabinet's hum had gone out when Adalemo cut the feed at the source — which meant Adalemo had been standing in Boiler's building with a hand on the switch the whole time, killing her reference, watching the wall stop lying, waiting to see which way she'd seal it.

*He didn't come to read the wall. He came to read me reading it. And the only person who'd cut Stave's hum to watch me choose is somebody who already knew what twenty-one names in the cold were worth — which means I just made the decision in front of the one person on this floor who had a reason to make me make it.*

Adalemo crossed the floor the way Rook crossed it — knowing where the lamps didn't reach and using it — and stopped close enough that the cold reading on Lace's fingers logged him as a second body, a second temperature standing off the patch.

"You sealed it," Adalemo said. Not a question. He had watched the wall stop lying and start again; he knew the shape of a reseal from the outside the way Vox knew it from in.

Vox said nothing, which was the only answer that didn't hand anything over.

"I'm not paid to open it," Adalemo said. "I'm paid to confirm what's behind it. PEREGRINE wants a data-cache. I just watched you build them one." He let it sit. Then he did the thing that fit on no one's purchase order: he turned, put his own body between the seam and the vector Rook had marked, and faced out, the way you stand when you've decided which side of a wall you're on. "Forty meters. Walk your crew out the load corridor. I never read a forty-first name."

*He counted the same twenty-one I did, off the strain in the loud place, from the outside. And he's spending his own standing to let them stay lost — which means somewhere on the last job he stopped being only the thing that walked back up out of the lake. I'll never get to ask him about it. The corridor is right there and the meters don't stop for it.*

*Present day.*

Rook took a booth at the Sojourn because it wasn't Mrs. Chen's and wasn't the place by the cabinet and wasn't anywhere Vox would think to look for the count to get done. The window faced the lake, which did what it always did, which was nothing — held the neon off the towers in a long broken smear on the water and gave none of it back, not the color and not the heat and not the meaning. Six lamps, accounted for and bagged in the duffel under the table. She set the ledger on the laminate and did not open it yet.

The number was different this time. That was the thing she kept circling, her hands flat on the closed ledger the way Ohara's hand had been flat on the steel — eight days to clear, Vox had priced it, and the change had cleared, and the duffel under the table was the proof of it. But the line at the bottom read a count it never had before, and it wasn't quanta. It was twenty-one, and twenty-one wasn't a fee, and there was no column for it, and she was the only one at this booth who knew the column existed.

*She sealed it back and the change cleared and the lamps are in the bag, and the count says we got paid. The count always says we got paid. It has never once said who didn't.*

She looked at the water a while. The neon off it was a different number of colors than the last time she'd sat at a window doing a count over something that gave nothing back — fewer towers lit, the Spire dark for some scheduled reason that didn't reach down to a booth at the Sojourn. Different smear, different night, different job sealed behind a wall. The lake was the same. It had been here before the barge and would be here after the manifest went cold a second time, and it did not care that twenty-one names had no second mark.

She did. She found that out the plain way, the way you find out your own weight on a stair you thought would hold: she reached into her jacket and took out a square of paper — real paper, weight and grain, the kind nobody used unless they wanted no copy to exist — and with the pen that had no column for it she wrote the number down, just the number, twenty-one, no names she didn't have the right to carry. Then she folded it flat along a crease pressed with her thumbnail and not a machine, and she put it where she kept the things that were only hers, and she found she could not make herself care less than the lake did, and had stopped trying somewhere around the fourth meter of the load corridor.
