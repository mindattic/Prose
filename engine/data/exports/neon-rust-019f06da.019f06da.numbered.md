# Neon & Rust

[Beat 1]
The note was paper. Rook turned it over once before she read it, checking the weight, the grain, the crease pressed flat with a thumbnail and not a machine. Paper meant a body had touched this and chosen to. Nobody used it anymore unless they wanted no copy to exist — no cached draft, no send-receipt, nothing for a forensic sweep to peel up off a dead channel — and nobody who wanted no copy to exist would also use the drop under the loose tile at the back of Mrs. Chen's, because that drop was hers. She had set it in 2206, pried the tile up herself with the flat of a knife and seated it back so the grout line stayed unbroken, and she had told four people. Three of them were dead.

The fourth had signed it. *O.*

She read the rest standing — eyes moving, hands already deciding. Eight lines. Ohara wanted a meet, named a time, named a place in the Gray Zone close enough to the Arcturus line that it was either a confession or a trap, and Rook didn't care which, because the part that mattered was the first sentence. Ohara had used the tile. She had reached into the one channel that should have been cold for two years, and she had done it knowing — this was the thing, this was the part that landed somewhere under the sternum before Rook could name it — knowing exactly what it would mean to the person who found it. You didn't spend that. Not unless what you were holding cost more than the channel did.

Mrs. Chen's was warm in the specific way of places that ran their grills past the point of profit because turning them off cost more than the gas. The air sat thick with pork fat and the burnt-sugar edge of chili oil gone too hot in the wok, and under it the wet-concrete smell the whole back room carried, the smell of a floor that had been mopped every night for nineteen years and never once dried all the way through. Neon came in through the front glass and lay across the formica in long stripes — magenta, a sick lake-green, the cyan of the noodle-house sign two doors down — light that looked like warmth from the street and turned out, up close, to be only color. It picked out the steam off her bowl and made it look like something worth staying for.

The noodles were on the counter where the boy had set them down. Bone broth, the fat beading gold on top, the egg she always asked for split and weeping into the dark of it. She had paid before she sat. She always paid first; it was one of the small protocols, the ones that kept a body from owing anything to a room it might need to leave fast.

She folded the paper back along its crease — the same crease, she didn't make a new one, you didn't improve on a fold that someone had set with a thumbnail — and slid it into the inside left of her jacket, against the ribs, where the warmth of her own body would dry whatever the night had put into it.

And she was three steps toward the door before she registered the steps.

*That.* That was the thing. Not the note, not Ohara, not the wall or the door someone had left open in it. The three steps. She had stood, she had crossed half the floor, her hand was already reading the temperature of the door's push-bar through the leather of her glove — and somewhere back at the counter was a decision she hadn't made yet, a bowl going cold under neon, and her legs hadn't waited for it.

She made herself stop. Weight even on both feet. *Audit it.* She had built the rule precisely so this couldn't happen — no repeat clients, no repeat crew, no reaching back into a closed channel. She had built it the year after Wennick, when the arithmetic of one wrong call had come back as a count she still couldn't carry clean: the way a person looked at you when they understood the job had moved under them and you were the last person who had all the numbers. That look didn't leave. The rule was the thing she had built so she would never have to see it again. It was maintenance, and she had let the protocol slip once before, on the last job, because the job had made the slip convenient. The note had found the place where her rule had already started to rust, and leaned on it, and the metal had given. Quietly. A little at a time. Until the thing that had been holding the door shut was just holding the shape of a door.

She registered that, set it aside, and went.

She left the noodles. The steam was still climbing off them, the egg still bleeding gold, the whole bowl paid for and abandoned. Behind the counter Mrs. Chen watched her go, and Rook felt the weight of the watching between her shoulders — not concern, not the flat nod she gave a stranger, not the *come back, the broth keeps* she'd have aimed at a regular who'd had bad news. Something older than any of those. The look of a woman who had run a drop under her own floor for nineteen years, who had never once asked what the tile was for, and who knew — had always known, Rook understood now — that the day somebody left food cooling it would be a day the person who left it had already decided. That silence was its own kind of mercy. Rook took it the way she took everything offered free, which was warily, and on her way out she didn't look back, because looking back would have meant deciding what the watching meant, and she had already spent her quota on deciding for one night.

She walked the remaining distance to the condemned building the way you walked toward something you had already decided to approach: without hurry, without lingering, with the specific attention of a person who has committed to the choice and is now only interested in the execution.

They weren't hard to find, which told Rook everything about how badly they had scattered. Real distance leaves no forwarding address. These three had left whole streets of it.

It took her under two seconds and it cost her nothing visible, which was the part Rook had never managed to teach anyone else and the part that made Lace worth the door.

But Rook wasn't reading the room. Rook was reading Lace reading the room, and the circuit hitched.
