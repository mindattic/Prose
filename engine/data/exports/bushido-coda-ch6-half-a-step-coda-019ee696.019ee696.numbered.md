# Chapter 5: Half a Step

_Kyle returns from Bucktown with taser ribs. Pixel tends the damage._

[Beat 1]
He knocked at eleven, which was late even by his standards, and Pixel opened the door without the soldering iron this time — which meant she'd heard the stairs and put the work down before the knock came.

"Ribs," she said. Not a question; she'd seen him favor the left side coming up.

"Two, probably. The taser found the gap between the dorsal plates at the lat — the seam the subdermal mesh doesn't cross."

She stepped back from the door. He came in and set Silence — the flat-black katana — against the wall by the stool, quiet the way he set it down anywhere he meant to stay more than a minute. Then he sat where she pointed, without ceremony, and she put her hands on either side of the damage and felt for what the mesh had stopped and what it hadn't. Her hands were cold from the bench work. She didn't warn him, and she didn't apologize for it, because this was the understanding between them: these hands, this cold, this room, the work that needed doing.

"Not displaced," she said. "Hairline on the lower one. It'll hurt for a week and then it'll decide it's done." She taped it with the same efficiency she gave any structural problem — the question was whether it held, not whether it hurt. When she finished she did not step back.

She stayed where she was, a hand still flat against his side. The room was the room it always was at this hour: the lamp on the bench, the smell of flux and something she'd been eating, the half-assembled arm laid out at the far end of the worktable, the soft cycling of the bench coolers between jobs. And nothing happened — her hand still flat against his ribcage where the tape ended.

He started to stand. Her hand, flat against his side, stayed — not pressing, just present, the weight of it between him and standing. His heel found the floor an inch back. He stayed. She looked at the floor for one beat, and then she was done looking at it.

"Friday," she said, which was when Oziel was coming for the arm. A reminder about the morning; an exit from the edge; the ordinary thing she always offered him when the other thing got too close.

He was quiet for a breath. "Friday," he said.

She turned back to the bench. He took Silence — the weight of it familiar, unchanged — up from the wall, left the door the width she always left it, and crossed the four steps home through the taser burn's ongoing report.

[Beat 2]
The taser burn would be worst in the morning. He knew that from the ledger he kept in his head — the shoulder's recovery curve, the rib tape interval, the approximate duration of any given mesh-seam failure. He ran his thumb over the tape, the hairline beneath it, the line where the mesh stopped and the decision to stop it there had started.

The gap was by spec. The engineer who'd laid the mesh in had traded coverage for articulation at the lat — a trade Kyle had not been asked about. He had been sixteen. Not a client, not yet — something closer to a project on the bench, a variable that someone was still solving for, one of the decisions made before anyone thought to ask. The engineer had done clean work. The mesh had stopped two center-mass rounds and a follow-through stave. What it hadn't stopped was exactly what it was designed not to stop, which was the cost of being able to reach.

He lay back in the dark, the burn finding its schedule. He thought about Pixel's hand flat against his side where the tape ended, and the word "Friday" which was the ordinary thing she offered when the other thing got too close, and how he'd said it back without deciding to, and what that said about what was already decided. Then he let it go and slept.

[Beat 3]
The burn woke him at six, as promised. Kyle sat at the kitchen window with his coffee and took inventory: two hairline ribs, tape holding, the pull of the lab-grown skin over the taser contact at the lat. The burn reported itself with each breath, a clean specific signal. He ran his thumb along the tape. He'd made peace with the spec years ago.

He pulled up the account on his terminal while the coffee was still hot. The Axiom relay showed the retainer deposit: exact rate, no gratuity, processed at 02:14. Eleven years and the tip had been reinstated as goodwill sometime after the job — he'd seen it for about four minutes before the line cut. Now the entry read clean: standard rate, no additional. The client was silent.

Not the usual silence, which had a texture — a held breath before the next posting. This silence was closed. The tip: he noted the four-minute window. In eleven years, the shell had never moved off stated rate. Not once. He'd taken that as professionalism — the kind of operation that didn't incentivize, just paid clean and filed clean. The tip had lasted four minutes, which was exactly long enough to mean something. Something had extended goodwill and then withdrawn it, which was a different kind of communication than never having extended it at all.

He thought about what kind of entity decides that. Then he washed the mug and went to find his jacket.

[Beat 4]
He didn't plan to stop at the carousel park. He rode past on Leavitt and his hands slowed the bike anyway.

The park stood the way it had always stood — tucked between a rezoned printshop and a wall of second-growth sumac that had come up through the old parkway concrete. The crown bulbs were dead. The animals held their suspended positions: the palomino mid-rear, the lake sturgeon banked into its turn, the rooster at the top of its leap with its beak pointed at something it would never reach. The junction hatch in the carousel's base was locked. Kyle tried the handle, which told him what he expected.

He stood there in the cold. His neuretics were logging the frequency — 18.7 Hz, type unclassified — and what was unusual was that they kept logging it. They'd normally tag and file a signal in two, three seconds. This one was returning UNCLASSIFIED on each pass, the same notation repeating in the background like a task that couldn't complete. The frequency wasn't loud. It didn't announce itself. It sat in the chest the way a sound sits that the body hears before the ears do.

He looked at the palomino for a moment. Mid-arrival — front hooves lifted, still in the step it had been making for however long the park had been running. He tried the hatch one more time and came up with the same answer.

He got back on the bike and rode north. The notation was still running when he got home. He had to close it manually.

[Beat 5]
The relay logs went back eleven years, which was longer than Kyle had lived anywhere. He'd been reading them for two days. Not auditing — reading the shape. Every posting had arrived on the same Axiom feed. Every scope was exactly what he would have taken. Not coercion; just fit, the way a calibrated instrument fits the hand that uses it.

He'd sent half a Φ back up the line after the Leavitt job — standard habit, the way he'd close a tab. The response had come back in the usual format.

*received. filed under: firsts.*

He read it four or five times. Not a typo. Not a system category. Something had put that word there with intent, which meant something had a folder called firsts, which meant something had been waiting to fill it. Eleven years of clean pay and not one word between them — and the first word was a filing note on his half-Φ closing a tab.

He put the kettle on. He opened the relay and moved the message into a new folder. He labeled the folder: *Open.* He hadn't replied. He had done something else instead.

[Beat 6]
He told Pixel about the frequency the way he told her things — adjacent, while she was busy with something else. Not the carousel specifically. Just: there was something at the park on Leavitt, and his neuretics were logging it at 18.7 Hz and couldn't classify it.

Pixel was at her bench with a prosthetic shoulder in the truing jig, making minute corrections to the rotation lock. She did not look up.

"Something's emitting in that range," she said. "Your hardware's logging it as unclassified because it doesn't know what to do with it. Seventeen to nineteen Hz — below conscious hearing, above infrasound." She set the probe down. "The body reads it as something alive. Something nearby."

She looked at him for the first time.

"Where exactly is the park?" she asked.

He gave her the cross-streets. She wrote them in the margin of her work notes, in the small mechanical handwriting she used for things she intended to find again, and went back to the shoulder.

He left when she went back to work. In the hall, the four steps between his door and hers, he thought about the filing note — *received. filed under: firsts* — and about the small mechanical handwriting in the margin. He didn't know what to do with either of them. He went inside.
