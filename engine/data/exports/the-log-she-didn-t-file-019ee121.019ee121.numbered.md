# The Log She Didn't File

_Amara flags anomaly drift across four clients in her private log. She doesn't file it._

[Beat 1]
The fourth client was a freight scheduler named Daud. Forty-three, second-generation neuretics, the tissue grown in situ when he was eleven — standard for working-class kids in the transit corridors, back when Orison Neuretics still subsidized pediatric installs in Zones 5 through 7. His implant had the cellular density of something well-maintained: no scarring, healthy vascularization, an associative mesh with the even conductance of a system nobody had ever pushed too hard. A careful man, his tissue said, before he'd said a word.

Amara finished the calibration in forty minutes. She was good at her job, which mostly meant she was quiet during it. People with neuretics didn't like to be talked at while someone read their tissue. It felt, they told her, like being watched think.

She packed her portable reader into its case — Cellvault-issue gray foam, the latch that stuck on the left side — and told Daud he was clear for another six months. He thanked her and offered her tea, which she declined. She had two more visits on the books.

In the van she pulled the standard report and set it beside her own read.

The standard model showed nominal. Her numbers showed a 3.2-point drift in the posterior associative cluster, localized in a way that age-related degradation never was. Ordinary drift spread outward from load-heavy nodes like a bruise — radial, predictable. This kept to one neighborhood: the nodes that governed contextual memory integration. Not the memories. Not what he remembered. The way memories were fitted together.

She looked at the number for a while.

Then she saved the raw read to a local partition on her own device and started the van.

---

Her anomaly archive lived on a thumb-encrypted partition, no cloud sync, no Cellvault routing. She'd started it eight months ago with no theory in mind. A technician's habit: when the numbers don't match the model, you keep the numbers.

The format was plain. Client code, calibration date, region of anomalous drift, delta from expected baseline, any co-presenting markers. She didn't editorialize. She didn't have enough to editorialize.

This was her thirty-first entry.

She added Daud in the station's parking bay while the van's cooling system ticked down in the heat. *Client 31. Z6-DAK. Calibration 19 Jun 2225. Posterior associative cluster, bilateral. Delta: −3.2 from model baseline. No hardware variance (no hardware). Vascularization clean. Pattern: Type C.*

Type C was her own designation. She'd given it to a clustering pattern that first appeared in Client 19, then 24, then 28, and now here — four clients in eight months, the same localized drift in the same cognitive neighborhood, in a way no maintenance error and no ordinary degradation produced. Maintenance errors introduced noise. This wasn't noise. This was signal without a source she could name.

She read the four Type C entries again. The deltas varied — 2.8, 3.1, 2.9, 3.2 — but the location held to a margin too tight to be coincidence.

The compliance protocol was clear. An anomalous finding went to Cellvault's review queue, which tripped a flag in the Orison portal. Orison Neuretics held the master maintenance contracts for every sub-contractor in GLMZ, which meant Orison evaluated everything Cellvault flagged. A review team. A follow-up audit. Her raw reads, leaving her partition for theirs, attached to four names that were doing nothing wrong.

She thought about that for the length of time it took the station's climate unit to cycle on.

The finding wasn't urgent. Daud was not in distress. None of the four were — their neuretics functioned, they were living their lives, and nothing in the data said *this person needs intervention*. Filing a speculative pattern off four cases with no working hypothesis generated review overhead for no outcome. A responsible technician verified before she escalated. She wanted to understand it first.

That was the reason she gave herself, and it was not entirely false. The part she left out was that filing was not neutral. Orison evaluated everything, and once four names were inside its system, what happened to them was no longer a question she got to ask.

She closed the archive and went back inside to finish her paperwork.

---

The Zone 6 freight grid ran elevated above Meridian Corridor — mag-lev cargo rails with a narrow passenger accommodation bolted to the south side of each car: a row of fold-down seats, a handhold bar, a view of the city through scratched polycarbonate. The working grid. Most of the commuters were dock techs and warehouse staff who smelled of machine oil and recyclable packaging and stood with the easy balance of people who rode this route every day. Amara had ridden it four years. She stood without holding the bar.

The city below was dense the way only freight made cities dense: warehousing and fabrication, the great articulated arms of the cargo loaders moving in their careful arcs over the depot yards, and between them the street life of a corridor that fed itself — repair shops, canteen windows, a noodle stand she'd eaten at since she moved to the zone because it was cheap and good and the owner knew her order. Late-summer heat shimmered above the yards. The grid smelled of ozone and warm concrete and, faintly and always, of the lake — a mineral coldness under everything, the smell of a body of water big enough to make its own weather.

She was not thinking about the delta column. Her surface mind had moved on to supply: she needed calibration solution, she was behind on her quarterly training hours, the left latch on the case had to be requisitioned before it failed for good.

The delta column ran underneath, the way unresolved data always ran underneath. She didn't have to attend to it. Her mind was methodical the way a good system was methodical — it kept processing whether she watched or not.

The grid swung east toward the Lakeview spur, and she watched a cargo loader pivot six stories down and thought about Client 19. The first Type C. Eight months ago, when she hadn't known it was the first of anything.

---

Her apartment occupied the upper third of a converted freight office: high ceilings, a single long window facing west, a smell of metal she'd stopped noticing years ago. Zone 6 had a particular evening light, amber filtered through industrial haze, the sun going down behind a skyline of distribution centers and rail. She found it beautiful the way functional things were beautiful. She had tried once to explain this to her mother, who lived in Zone 3 and found Zone 6 depressing, and the explanation had not gone well.

She made egusi soup. The recipe was her grandmother's, transcribed into the back of a maintenance log when she was nineteen and had no paper, kept now on a card tacked inside a cabinet door. The melon seeds toasted first — you had to watch them, they went from perfect to ruined in under two minutes — then the palm oil, then the tomato base she kept in a jar in the refrigerator. The apartment filled with the smell of it. She added the spinach last.

The lumen mouse moved in the wall behind the stove. She heard it before she saw it: a small percussive scrabbling, then a faint phosphorescent leak at the seam of the baseboard, blue-green. They came in through the building's original cable conduits, which the landlord had sealed badly. She'd thought about reporting it, but the mice were harmless and the extermination fee was Φ340 she didn't want to spend. They were a nuisance, technically. Mostly they were just there, the way the metal smell was there.

She ate at the kitchen table, which was also her work table, which was also where she kept her maps.

The maps covered the north wall: Zones 1 through 8, official Meridian Authority charts on heavy stock, annotated in her own hand in three colors. Blue for routes she used. Red for infrastructure she'd catalogued on client visits. Green for the things she'd noticed that weren't on any official chart — the informal settlements at the Zone 4/5 border, the freight access roads the grid maps didn't show, the three buildings in Zone 7 where she'd done maintenance for clients whose addresses weren't in Cellvault's database and who had paid in cash.

She studied the wall while she ate. Zone 6 was dense with red and blue. Zone 3, where she'd grown up, was nearly bare. She hadn't had reason to go back, and the green told the truth about that more plainly than she would have.

The delta column was still running.

*Four clients. Same cluster. Same neighborhood.* Nine hundred and twelve calibrations in four years at Cellvault. Thirty-one anomalous findings — a normal rate for high-density work. Twenty-seven explained themselves: equipment drift, tissue variability, one client whose metabolic event between visits accounted for the whole conductance change. Four were Type C. Four she could not explain.

She washed her bowl. She made tea.

---

At 23:07, something moved through her signal.

She was at the table with her tea and her maps and her laptop, not looking at anything in particular, when it arrived: a fragment of someone else's evening, clean as a recording but not a recording, textured the way lived experience is textured. A freight route — she knew the topology before she knew anything else, the particular arc of an elevated grid, the sound of mag-lev deceleration, the way a city looks from inside a moving car at night. But the zones were wrong. The skyline was wrong. The smells were wrong in ways she couldn't have named and knew at once: not her corridor, not her lake-cold ozone, something more industrial, more southern, a district she had never entered and recognized only from maps.

Twelve seconds, near enough. She held still while it lasted, the way she held still during a read, and then it resolved and she was at her kitchen table in Zone 6 with her tea, the lumen mouse working behind the baseboard, the west window full of dark skyline.

She sat with it for four seconds.

It had not been frightening. It had not been like anything she had a category for. It was data.

She opened the archive.

She added a column at the rightmost edge of the spreadsheet. The header took her a moment; she drafted it twice before the language matched the precision of the others.

*Unidentified signal — first receipt, 23:07. Duration: ~12 sec. Origin: unknown.*

She gave it its own row, dated it, and placed it beside the four Type C patterns in a way that asserted no connection she could not yet demonstrate.

Then she closed the laptop.

The lumen mouse moved again, its glow leaking blue-green at the seam of the wall, and she noted it the way she noted everything — a thing that was there — and went to bed.
