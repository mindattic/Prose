# Two Mirrors

[Beat 1]
The logbook came under the door at 07:00 on a Tuesday, same as always, but this one had an addendum on the last page in Dents's handwriting — tighter than Rafters's, looser than Boost's, the tilt of someone writing fast enough that the hand couldn't apologize for the content.

ADDENDUM (D.): TOOK A SIDE JOB. NOT A CREW JOB. PERSONAL. PENDANT THING. PAYS 2Φ/DAY. WELLNESS STUDY. ASKED BOOST. BOOST SAID ASK PIXEL. PIXEL SAID FINE. EVERYBODY ON IT. FOLLOW-UP: RAFTERS STILL OWES ME A DUMPLING.

Kyle read it twice with his tea and considered the phrase "wellness study" the way you consider a box you found inside a wall. Wellness, in this city, meant either something was selling your gait or something was testing a drug and needed twelve-year-olds to test it on, and both were different names on the same invoice.

He photographed it and sent it to Pixel. The reply came before he'd finished the cup — three seconds, then: "How many kids?"

He commed in.

"Dents plus whoever he recruited. He called it a wellness study. Two Φ a day, per child."

"Two Φ a day is a real wage." The precision of a woman who knew every wage in West Town. "That's not a study budget. That's a recruiting budget. Get me one of the pendants, samurai, and give me an hour."

[Beat 2]
Dents was eleven, narrow through the shoulders, waiting for the rest of his body to catch up. The pendant rode on a cord at his throat — a flat disc of matte resin the size of a coat button. His logbook entry on it read: PENDANT ISSUED BY WOMAN (TAN COAT? DIFFERENT TAN COAT). MUST WEAR DURING WAKING HOURS. MUST NOT REMOVE. FOLLOW-UP: IT GETS WARM SOMETIMES. NOT BAD WARM. JUST WARM.

"Different tan coat," Kyle said.

"There are multiple tan coats in this city." Boost was on the alley crate, arms folded, taking the news at six-in-the-morning volume, which meant quietly, which meant angry. "Where's it run, Dents? You didn't log the address."

"Building on Damen. Third floor. Lady takes your name, gives you the pendant, pays you. No medical stuff. Just wear it."

Dents dug out a pamphlet — good stock, not Vey's grade but a credible print run: MERIDIAN WELLNESS INITIATIVE, COMMUNITY SENSORY SURVEY. A logo Kyle didn't know, a shell he'd have bet resolved to a shell. He took it upstairs and slid it under Pixel's door without knocking.

[Beat 3]
She had it open in eleven minutes, not the hour — the music doing the thinking she'd otherwise spend narrating, so the bench ran fast.

The resin case lay split along its seam in two readable halves, and on the magnification plate sat a disc of optical mesh no bigger than a thumbnail.

"Tracking node," Pixel said. "Passive mesh, crowd-sourced topology. Seed these at density — one every forty meters — and they triangulate anything that crosses them. The warm Dents felt is the uplink: every four hours each node phones home what it logged." She set the magnifier down with the flat attention she gave a patient's chart. "It's not a wellness study. It's surveillance infrastructure, and somebody hired children to be the infrastructure. At forty-meter spacing you'd want three hundred nodes to cover West Town." She met his eyes. "Dents has worn his eight days. The first week of everyone's movement already shipped. That part's gone — that's not a problem, it's a cost. We count it and move."

The way she put it filed the thing where it could be paid instead of relitigated. He noted that.

"Get them all out?" Kyle asked.

"Pull eleven pendants at once and enrollment craters and the buyer knows we found it — then they re-seed through a vendor we haven't met, and we're blind." She picked up the live node, still warm. "The collection point doesn't know what it's collecting. It just aggregates packets and ships a map." She turned the disc between two fingers. "So we don't pull the pendants. We make the map wrong."

[Beat 4]
The entity's posting arrived at 11:40, on the walk back with the breakfast she'd demanded — dumplings, not cart noodles, from the place she'd named and he'd already known.

CONTRACT — STANDING SCOPE UPDATE.
CLIENT: [SHELL — RESOLVES TO SHELL — RESOLVES TO NOTHING].
CLIENT NOTES THE MERIDIAN INITIATIVE IS A FUNDED PROGRAM IN ACTIVE COLLECTION.
CLIENT NOTES ITS PAYROLL STRUCTURE AND IS DISTURBED.
CLIENT'S HIRES ARE ADULTS, OR CREW WITH AN ADULT SPONSOR, PAID STANDARD RATE, ITEMIZED, ON TIME.
CLIENT NOTES: THAT IS HOW YOU PUT SOMEONE ON PAYROLL.
CLIENT'S OPINION OF THE ALTERNATIVE IS FILED UNDER: FORMAL INDIGNATION.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT CAN NAME THE RELAY'S MODEL AND FIRMWARE. CLIENT CANNOT TOUCH IT — THE BUILDING IS OFF-NETWORK BY DESIGN, WHICH THE BUILDER WAS RIGHT TO DO. THIS ONE IS THE ENGINEER'S.
ADDENDUM: MODEL AND FIRMWARE BELOW. FEE: DO NOT BILL.

Kyle stopped in front of the noodle cart, read it twice while the cart man gave him the look that meant an errand had become a religious experience, and forwarded the firmware string to Pixel.

It mattered, he'd learn at the relay, that the entity couldn't do this part. The building was air-gapped on purpose. Whatever the entity was, it lived in the wires, and there were no wires here. This was hands and feet and a kid on a stairwell.

[Beat 5]
Pixel read the firmware string and swore with feeling.

"It's got a watchdog. The relay re-checksums its outbound queue every ninety seconds — if the aggregate doesn't match what the nodes signed, it flags corruption and locks, and a locked relay is a buyer who knows. So I can't just swap the packet. I have to swap it AND re-sign it AND land both inside one ninety-second window, by feel, in a basement junction box, with the original lady's access key, which I do not have."

"What gets you the key?"

"She carries it. Physical fob, on the third floor." Pixel pulled her splice kit. "It pairs to the relay over a thirty-centimeter near-field handshake every time she's in the room. If somebody were near enough to her bag during a handshake with a cloned coil — close, brief, deniable — I'd have the key for exactly as long as her fob stays in range of that floor. After she leaves at nineteen hundred, the window's gone."

Kyle looked at the geometry of that and didn't like the only piece that fit.

"That's a kid's job," he said. "Small, ignorable, already enrolled, has a reason to be on that floor."

"It's Dents's job," Pixel said. "And I hate it, and it's the right call, and you're going to brief him like he's a professional because he is one now."

[Beat 6]
Dents took the brief the way he took the dumplings: like a thing he had not expected to be handed and intended to deserve.

"You go up at eighteen-forty to turn in today's pendant reading, which is a real thing you really do," Kyle said. "You stand where the log says you stand. When she runs her fob — she does it at the desk, you've watched her — your bag is on the desk too. The coil does the rest. Six seconds. Then you leave. You walk out before nineteen hundred no matter what, and if anything is wrong you leave faster and we eat the loss."

"What's the coil look like?"

"A pendant," Pixel said, handing it over. "Like yours. Wear it under the real one. If anybody asks, you found it, you thought it was a spare."

Dents hung it under the live one and looked, briefly, like a kid wearing two of something. "Logbook?"

"Logbook," Kyle said.

[Beat 7]
It nearly went wrong at 18:51, which Kyle and Pixel listened to over the open channel from the basement junction box, in a light the color of an old grudge, unable to do anything but listen.

The lady ran her fob at the desk. The coil caught the handshake — Pixel's screen bloomed green, KEY CAPTURED — and in the same second the lady said, conversationally, "You've got two cords on, hon," and the channel went very quiet, and Kyle's hand was already on the basement stairs.

"Found it on the ramp," Dents said, upstairs, in the bored flat voice of a kid who has decided being bored is the safest thing to be. "Thought it was a spare. Does it count for the two Φ if I wear two?"

A pause that lasted, by Kyle's count, four full seconds.

The lady laughed. "No, hon. One pendant, one wage." A rustle — the spare confiscated, the real one left. "Run along."

Dents ran along. He came down the back stairs at a walk, because Pixel had told him to walk, and the walk cost him more than running would have, and he did it anyway. He handed Kyle the coil at the basement door and his hand was shaking and his chin was up.

"She almost—" he started.

"You handled it," Kyle said. "Better than I would have. Go home. You're done."

"Does it count for—"

"It counts for logbook. Go."

[Beat 8]
Pixel had the key, and then Pixel had a fight.

The watchdog gave her ninety seconds a cycle and her swap-and-resign took, the first attempt, ninety-four. The relay locked. She held her breath; it auto-recovered on its own self-heal timer, thirty seconds of nothing, the longest thirty seconds of the night — a locked relay that stayed locked would have been the buyer's tip and the whole loss for nothing — and then it came back clean, having logged the lock as a power blip, and she had one more try before a second lock would stop reading as weather.

She made the second one in eighty-one seconds by cutting her own re-sign routine in half and trusting the cut.

At 20:14 the first forged packet went out: three hundred nodes, worn by eleven kids living their ordinary evenings across West Town, all faithfully reporting the position of a noodle cart making its slow devoted circle of Damen and Erie and the alley by The Pivot.

"Packet accepted," Pixel said, from the junction box, and he could hear her hands stop shaking through the change in her voice. "Forgery's cycling. The buyer's going to open a map of three hundred people who all decided to orbit a noodle vendor at walking pace." A breath. "Data this uniform reads as a hardware fault, not a result. Five, six days, the program writes off the sensor batch and pulls funding. The kids keep wearing pendants that now log a lie."

"Cost?" Kyle asked, because there was always one and she always knew it.

"The first week's real data still shipped before I had access. Eight days of where these kids sleep, sitting on a holding server somewhere. The forgery doesn't reach back and scrub it." Flat, precise, paid. "I'm logging it."

"Log it."

[Beat 9]
The entity's posting came at 21:00, as if it had been counting splice time.

OPERATION: COMMUNITY MESH FORGERY. DATA INTEGRITY: COMPROMISED (BUYER'S SIDE). INTACT (OURS).
CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE ENGINEER'S METHODOLOGY. CLIENT FINDS IT SUPERLATIVE.
CLIENT NOTES: ONE WEEK OF REAL DATA EXISTS ON AN EXTERNAL HOLDING SERVER. THAT SERVER IS ON-NETWORK.
CLIENT WILL NOT ACT WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION. CLIENT WOULD LIKE AUTHORIZATION.
REASON: THE SERVER HOLDS CHILDREN'S LOCATION HISTORY. CLIENT HAS OPINIONS.

So that was the division of the night, Kyle thought: the air-gapped room had needed a kid on a stairwell and a woman in a basement betting a cut routine on one breath, because the wires didn't reach there. The holding server was on the wires. That one the entity could carry — and had asked, instead of taken.

He typed: AUTHORIZATION GRANTED.

Four seconds.

SERVER: OFFLINE. FILED UNDER: RESOLVED.
GRATUITY: 1Φ — ITEMIZED: APPROPRIATE PERSONNEL PRACTICES.

[Beat 10]
Six days later the Meridian Wellness Initiative form-lettered every participant: SENSOR BATCH DEFECTIVE — STUDY SUSPENDED — COMPENSATION FOR FULL THIRTY-DAY TERM PAID IN FULL, IN ADVANCE. Whoever built the program had built it with enough real infrastructure that the payout clause was real, which was its own small horror and its own clean win at once.

Dents folded his receipt into the logbook: WELLNESS STUDY: COMPLETE. TOTAL: 60Φ. (CANCELLED, HARDWARE FAULT APPARENTLY. NOT OUR DEPARTMENT.)

Boost brought the envelope to Pixel's door personally and stood in it with the look she used when she was about to invoice somebody.

"Mirror two," she said. "You said pending performance review. We ran a stairwell key-lift with a near-miss handled clean, a basement intercept, and six days of pendant discipline without pulling a node. Is that the review?"

Pixel finished pulling her coat on. "Mirror two: approved. Rafters's optics cleared — they've been on my bench three weeks, tell him. And Dents gets the dumpling. That's not negotiable." She picked up her kit. "And tell Dents the thing the lady said — two cords, hon — he answered that exactly right. I've watched grown operators answer it worse."

"He'll pretend he didn't need to hear that."

"I know," Pixel said. "Tell him anyway."

[Beat 11]
Kyle found the geometry three nights later, in the relay logs Pixel had kept as a backup before she wrote the forgery — the first week's real data, mapped, printed to a single page, set on his kitchen table with one line under it: LOOK AT THE CENTER.

The page was a constellation: hundreds of tracked points spread across West Town, every one a child crossing a block, turning a corner, living an ordinary day. And when you drew the geometric center of all that ordinary motion, it landed on one block. Six trees. A swing set. A dead-bulb crown under a mossy canopy.

The carousel park. The hatch under the maintenance skirt. The junction room where an old hand-spliced line had been cut and now went dark into old glass.

Somebody had spent a funded program and eleven children to build a surveillance lattice, and its dead center was the entity's first address.

He put the page in the drawer — next to the buyers list and Boost's folded lock-spec file and the other things he had not finished deciding about. The drawer was getting full.

The logbook came under the door at 07:00, and at the bottom, in Boost's hand:

P.S. — BOSS. DENTS ASKS WHAT WAS IN THE PENDANT. HE ALREADY KNOWS IT WASN'T WELLNESS.
P.P.S. — HE SAYS THANK YOU. THE DUMPLING WAS VERY GOOD. (RAFTERS PAID UP. THIS LOG IS HONEST.)

Kyle wrote under it, in his own hand, and slid it back: DENTS — IT TRACKED POSITION. SOMEBODY WAS DRAWING A MAP. WE MADE THE MAP WRONG. YOU WERE NEVER ON IT. — K.

Through the thin floor he could hear the logbook get read, then two voices going fast, then a third, then Rafters saying something about the cart that made the other two laugh. He went down and bought everybody breakfast.
