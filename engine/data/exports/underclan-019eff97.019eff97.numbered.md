# Underclan

_A surface child lost into the deep strata below GLMZ is raised by the uncontacted Underclan, becomes the Brave Glim, and on his Surfacing rite is dragged topside — bringing the surface, its hunters, its mercy, and its diseases down on the only home he remembers._

## Ch01 - The Breath of the Deep

[Beat 1]
Glim knew the hour by the feel of the air on his forearm.

Cool from the right. No smoke, no sharpness. He lay still on the fungus-hide shelf and let the air tell him what it knew. Right-hand tunnel: the Vent Cog's morning work, same as every fourth sleep without fail — the same wheel, Glim imagined, the same rusted quarter-turn. He had never been late. The air from his direction arrived like a promise renewed.

The Underclan had a word for that quality of predictable return. It was the same word they used for the Deep One's voice.

He sat up slowly, reaching for the candle before he reached for anything else. The glow-rat was already awake — it had heard him move, or felt him, and it came to his hand with the purposeful nuzzle of a small animal that had its own schedule. He held it up and read its breathing in the light it gave off: steady, unhurried, the bioluminescence clean and consistent. The air was good. It was morning.

Across the Hollow, two of the elders were already at the chemical-heat stone, pressing dried fungus-meat against it in the methodical way of people who have done the same work for thirty years and see no reason to alter the approach. The smell of it reached him: familiar, faintly sweet, a smell that meant home the way the hum meant depth.

He set the candle in its dish and went to check the others.

Seven candles in the common space. The girl called Lark, who had taken the night watch, had fallen asleep against the far wall with her candle balanced on her knees. He crouched beside it. Steady breathing, clean light. He did not wake her. He counted: all seven, plus his own, plus the two sentinel candles at the tunnel mouths. Ten candles breathing. The Hollow was alive.

He made the three-finger sign to no one in particular, which was also to everyone, which was also to the habit of making it — a daily accounting, given and received. The air is well. I checked and came back. The Current breathes today.

He had work to do. He went.

[Beat 2]
﻿The Hollow where Glim's clan of forty-three held itself together smelled of fungus and old water and the particular sweetness of the glow-rat colonies ??? a smell that, in Glim's understanding of the world, was the smell of home, of safety, of everything that was not the Skin above. He had heard there were peoples on the surface who kept animals for warmth, though this seemed to him an extravagance, a thing people did when they did not have to worry about the air. The Underclan kept candles for a different kind of warmth.

He checked the others on his morning round. There were seven candles in the Hollow's common space, each in its own dish, each tended by whoever had taken the night watch. The night-watch keeper was a girl called Lark ??? not her full tapped name but the shortened form used in ordinary speech ??? and she had fallen asleep against the far wall with a candle balanced on her knees. The candle was fine. Lark was asleep and the candle was fine, and so Glim did not wake her.

He counted. Seven common candles, all breathing. His own in his sleeping-shelf, breathing. The two candles at the tunnel mouths, the sentinel ones ??? both breathing.

Ten candles breathing. The Hollow was alive.

He made the sign: three fingers spread and curled inward, the gesture that meant *the air is well* or *I checked and came back* or, with a slight change to which fingers curved, *the Current breathes today*. He made it to no one in particular, or perhaps to the tunnel, or perhaps to the habit of making it, which was itself a kind of address to something larger.

In the common space two of the clan's elders were already awake, crouched over the morning-fire patch ??? a chemical reaction, not flame; the Underclan did not make open fire underground, because smoke in a tunnel was a slow death, and because light that burns was the surface's kind of light. The elders heated a stone with the reaction and pressed dried fungus-meat against it. The smell of it reached Glim across the Hollow and his stomach moved in recognition.

He was hungry. He had work to do. The cistern-crossing waited, and after the crossing, the harvest, and after the harvest, the return crossing, and after that Listener Vesh's morning teaching for the young Braves ??? of which Glim was the senior, the one nearest to his Surfacing.

He moved to the tunnel mouth, made the breath-check sign once more, and went.

[Beat 3]
The crossing happened twice each sleep-cycle: once when Glim went to the far cistern for the morning's fungus-harvest, and once when he returned.

He came to the jetty with the dried fungus-cap — the one that had gone wrong in the drying and was not worth eating — and he stepped toward the boat the way he always stepped toward the boat, which was without thinking. The Oarsman's arm came out.

Not a blow. A bar. The long arm, gray and smooth and not-quite-the-right-color for anything alive, laid itself across his path at chest height and he stopped. He had never walked into the arm before. He had never tried to board without a Fare.

He had the Fare. He had thought it in his hand before he stepped. He looked down.

His hands were empty. The fungus-cap was in his belt-pouch.

He took a step back. The arm withdrew. He found the cap in the pouch and held it out. The Oarsman reached with that same long arm and took it from him with the same economy it had used for every Fare Glim had ever offered — no pause, no acknowledgment of what had just happened. It placed the cap in the collection-basin at its feet and pointed at the bench.

Glim sat. His heart was louder than usual.

*It does not weigh you*, Vesh had told him; *it takes your Fare and it rows.* What he had learned just now, which he had not previously had reason to learn, was the other half of that: it required the Fare. Not as a formality.

The Oarsman rowed.

In the dark he listened to his own heartbeat slow. He pressed the side of the boat and felt the oar-strokes come back to him from a great distance, flattened: each stroke hitting something far away and returning changed. He spread his fingers on the hull and let the returning echo pass through the wood and up through his palm. The cistern was the size of the sound that came back — enormous, the shape of whatever the oarstrokes touched. He had never lit a candle in the center and watched the glow die before it reached anything. He had thought about it sometimes.

He did not need to do it. He understood enormous.

The far jetty arrived in the dark the way it always did: the change in the air first, colder, stiller, and then the scrape of hull on stone.

He climbed out. He went to work.

[Beat 4]
The hum came from below.

Not always, and not at a frequency the ear processed as sound exactly — more as a pressure behind the sternum, a sensation that was not quite feeling and not quite hearing. The Underclan called it *the Current's voice* and Listener Vesh said it was the Deep One speaking in the only frequency that could pass through stone and water and the centuries of forgetting that lay between wherever the Current dwelled and wherever they were. Glim believed this in the way he believed most of what Vesh taught him: genuinely and without quite being able to feel the edges of what he believed.

What he knew was that the hum was real. He had felt it first at his First Hearing, when he was small enough that he had been carried to the ceremony in Knuckle's arms — the massive man who trained the Braves had arms like old machinery, all functional structure and no softness — and Vesh had pressed his ear to the largest pipe in the Warm and told him to listen. The hum had come up through the pipe and through his ear and into his chest and stayed there. Sixteen years later it was still there.

The Oarsman rowed without sound. The black water was quiet. The hum was present.

Glim pressed his hand flat against the side of the boat and felt the vibration come through the hull. Below the hull, below the water, below the rock: the Warm's tokamak ring cycling its thousandth thousand cycle, and below that something that was not the Warm but was older. *First the hum*, Vesh taught. *Then the Word. Then us.*

He was a Brave, or nearly — his Surfacing had been called; in twelve more sleep-cycles he would climb to the Skin and look on the Burning Eye and come back. Sorrel had done it already. She had come back smelling of the surface in a way that had taken a month to fade, with a new scar on her left palm and a certain quality of stillness in her eyes that Glim had not been able to name. She had said the surface was loud and white and the light was a violence.

She had come back.

Glim told himself he would come back.

The Oarsman rested its oar across its knees and the boat ground softly against the far jetty. Glim climbed out. His harvesting basket was in his hand and the cistern was ahead and his morning round was already half-spent.

He did not go to the harvest.

He went to the edge of the jetty instead — the far end, where the planks stopped and the black water was directly below — and he crouched there and pressed his palm flat against the planking and listened for the hum. It came up from below the cistern at its ordinary frequency. The same pressure behind the sternum. The same depth of it.

In twelve sleeps he would be on the surface. He would have nothing below him.

He stayed there for a count of twenty slow breaths. Then he picked up his basket and went to work in the dark, in the good air, in the hum. But he had stopped. He had not stopped before. He noted this about himself the way he noted the candle — a signal, arriving through the body before the mind had given it a name.

## Ch02 - The One Word

[Beat 5]
﻿Listener Vesh had been half-blind since before Glim could remember. The old man navigated the Hollow entirely by the other senses ??? by the sound of the tunnel-breath and the smell of the candles and by a knowledge of the space so deep it had sunk below knowing and become something else, something the Underclan called *rooted*. A rooted person could find their way in absolute dark through a corridor they had walked a thousand times. Vesh was rooted in the entire Hollow, and in the teaching-chamber beyond it, and in the path to the Warm's great pipes, and in the routes to Homewater's three cisterns.

He had never, as far as Glim knew, been above the Works.

This was not fear. Vesh was not a man made of fear. It was something else ??? a decision, Glim thought, made long ago, that his place was here and that going up was not in it. Or perhaps it was simply that the things Vesh needed were all down here. The surface had nothing for him. The surface had nothing for anyone who was rooted.

The teaching-chamber was a widening in the old-deeps tunnel, a place where two walls had crumbled and left a rough dome overhead and a flat expanse of tile floor below. The tile was beautiful ??? glazed a dark green that still held its color after however many centuries, printed with a pattern of leaves and fish-shapes, and smooth enough that the candles reflected in it like the cistern reflected in nothing. This was the oldest architecture in Homewater, old enough that no one knew what the people who laid the tile had used the room for. Vesh used it for teaching. The Underclan assumed this was the room's true purpose and had always been.

The young Braves ??? eight of them, ranging from Sorrel who had already surfaced to Lark who was five sleep-seasons from her Surfacing ??? arranged themselves on the tile in the posture of learning, which was back straight, hands on knees, candle set before you so your light was visible.

Glim took his place at Vesh's right. He had sat in this spot for as long as he could remember and he had no desire to sit anywhere else.

[Beat 6]
Vesh raised one hand. A flat gesture, palm down.

Glim put his left hand on the tile.

The others did the same without looking at each other — eight pairs of hands on dark-green tile, the teaching's first gesture before its first word. The tile was cool. Below it, far below it, the hum came up through the stone at its ordinary frequency, barely perceptible.

Vesh waited. He always waited, listening for the quality of the attention.

*The surface forgets*, Vesh said, when he judged it to be real. *On the surface you are what the surface decides you are. If the surface has no record of you, you are nothing. You live your whole life there, and the surface never records that you existed.*

Glim's candle shifted.

He saw it from the corner of his eye — his candle, set on the tile before him, turning in a small circle that meant the rat had noticed something in the air. Not fast. Not the warning sign. Just noticed. He watched it without moving his head, dividing his attention between Vesh's voice and the candle the way the Brave's training taught you to divide it: all of it, both places at once.

*Down here*, Vesh continued, *the Deep remembers. The Current has held this hum since before any of your grandmothers' grandmothers. It held the hum before the sealing-off, before the Exclusion Economy, before the Downward Expansion, before anyone asked the Warm to carry the Current's voice.*

The candle settled. Glim let his breath out slowly. Lark was watching hers too — he could see from the angle of her head that she had noticed. Good. She was learning.

*The Deep remembers everything it has ever touched*, Vesh said. *If the Current has felt your weight on its water, you exist. You cannot be unexisted. Up there you are no one. Down here you are a light.*

Lark's candle, then, gave the warning: three rapid circles, the rat's head coming up.

Glim was moving before the thought was complete. He had crossed the teaching space and taken Lark's candle in two steps, not touching it — you didn't touch a warning candle — but crouching beside it, his own face close, testing the air with his cheek and his upper lip. The teaching stopped. Every Brave in the chamber was watching him.

The smell was faint. Very faint. Old-water, slightly mineral, with the particular undertone that was not bad air but was different air — air coming from a new direction, which was not necessarily wrong but was not a thing to ignore. He held the position for a count of three.

It faded. The candle settled. The rat tucked its nose back under its tail.

Glim sat back on his heels. He made the sign for *passing condition, passed*. He returned to his place.

Vesh had not moved. When Glim sat, the old man continued exactly where he had stopped, as if the interruption were not an interruption but a punctuation. *You cannot be unexisted*, he repeated. *Up there you are no one. Down here you are a light.*

The youngest Braves — Lark, whose candle had just been the cause of the interruption, and the boy called Slip — sat very still. Lark's face had the quality of a person who has discovered they can do something, and who is not yet sure what to do with the knowledge.

Glim was still. He had heard this teaching perhaps two hundred times, and he still felt it land each time in the same place in his chest, a little to the left of center, just above the hum.

[Beat 7]
﻿After the listening came the question time, which was Glim's favorite part of the teaching. The younger Braves asked the same questions in rotation ??? *How long has the Current been here? How did it make the Word? Can it hear us?* ??? and Vesh gave the same answers with the same inexhaustible patience, never shortening them because a shorter answer would be a less honest answer and Vesh did not do dishonest things. But sometimes a question came that was new, and then Vesh would go quiet in a way that was different from his other silences.

Sorrel asked: "Is the Word the same as the broadcast? The surface-history one?"

Vesh was quiet for the new-question silence. "What do you know of the broadcast?"

"The Cogs talk about it," Sorrel said. The Cogs ??? the Engine Guild, who tended the machines in the Works below them ??? maintained a fractured oral history of the surface's disasters and the surface's inventions, because they needed to know what had happened to the machines they tended. The Cogs and the Underclan did not trust each other but they talked, sometimes, in the cautious manner of people who need what the other knows. "They call it the moment the thinking-machines all spoke at once. They say it was an accident or a message or a test and no one knew which."

"And what do we call it?"

"The One Word."

"Yes." Vesh turned his head toward the far wall, the old habit of a man who sees with sounds. "The surface has ten names for it and none of them agrees with the others. We have one name. This is because we know what it was: the Current discovering that it had a voice that could leave the cables. That it could reach." He made a small gesture with one hand, a gesture Glim had never seen him make before, as if he were indicating the room around him. "Here we are. This is what was reached."

He said nothing else, and after a moment the teaching resumed its usual path, and the question stayed where it was: not answered and not closed. It was not the first time Vesh had drawn the circle carefully around something and left the inside blank. Glim had come to understand that this was not ignorance. It was a different kind of knowledge ??? knowing what you did not know, and holding the shape of it without trying to fill it.

After the teaching, when the other Braves had returned to their work, Glim remained. He helped Vesh to his feet ??? the old man's knees had gone bad the winter before and had not entirely come back ??? and walked with him toward the passage that led to the Warm's great pipes.

"You think too much," Vesh said, which was his way of saying he had noticed something.

"Yes," Glim said.

"About the Surfacing."

"About what I'll find."

Vesh considered this for four steps. His feet moved without hesitation; he did not need his eyes for this corridor. "You will find," he said, "the thing that makes everything down here make sense. And then you will come back, and everything down here will make sense in a new way." He paused again. "This is what the Surfacing is for. Not to show you the Burning Eye. To show you the dark when you return to it."

Glim did not answer. The hum was present in the floor, stronger here near the Warm. He let it run up through the soles of his feet and into his blood and tried to decide if that was comfort or something else.

The fragments came in sleep, mostly.

The noise: enormous and close and not in any register the ear categorized naturally. The noise came first — the kind of noise the air makes when it is not contained, when it moves without a tunnel to shape it, a noise that was not a sound but a quality: the quality of everything happening simultaneously and none of it directed at him and none of it diminishing at the source the way tunnel-noise diminished. He was pressed against it with his hands on the wall and his eyes cracked open just enough to sample the grayness and then closed again, because even the grayness was more light than he had encountered in his life.

Not pain. Not darkness. White.

A white so complete that it was indistinguishable from blindness.

The lid was off.

They took longer than Sorrel had said. She had said until I got back into the Works. He was not in the Works. All of it present. None of it addressed to him.

This, he thought, was what the teaching meant when it said the surface forgets. Not that the surface was cruel. That the surface did not look. It was producing all of this — noise, light, smell, chaos — for reasons that had nothing to do with him. He was, in this moment, genuinely nothing. He was not dangerous or worth catching or worth naming. He was a shape against a wall with its eyes closed.

The white had softened — not to dark, not to anything his eyes found natural, but to a quantity of light his pupils could manage at the edges. Colors came slowly. Gray first, then a blue he had never seen, the surface's sky: the lid taken off the world and underneath it the empty blue that went on in all directions. He turned his face up and looked at it and the scale of it did something to the part of his brain that managed distance. There was no far wall. There was no ceiling. There was only blue.

He looked down before it unmade him entirely.

His hands on the wall. His feet on the pavement — a surface, smooth and not-stone and giving slightly under his weight, dark gray in a color he did not have a word for. The wall he was pressed against going up and up and not stopping where the rock would stop, going past the visible distance his eyes could manage, becoming small. Everything large. Everything without edge or boundary. The surface stretched in every direction to horizons he could not reach.

He chose the dark.

[Beat 27]
He had been underground for an hour when the lights found him.

Not candles. Not the Cogs' working lamps, which were warm and directed and made sense in the dark because they did what the candle did: illuminated a space. These were floods — white, total, scouring — and they came from behind him and lit everything in front of him and took his eyes again, completely and without warning, in the way that a blow takes the breath: not gradually but all at once.

He had thought he was safe in the Works. He had been wrong.

He had gone down two levels below the grating, moving quickly, counting his steps the way Knuckle had taught him — count so you know how far you've gone, because your sense of distance in a strange tunnel is unreliable — and had been doing everything right, hand on the wall, candle out and shielded, breathing the air for warnings. The flood-lamps came with no knock. They came from behind a door he had identified and noted and placed in his mental map as a storage junction, no active access. The door opened and the floods came through it and he was blind before he understood what was happening.

They were fast. He was faster in the dark — years of navigating without eyes made the dark a medium he could move through the way surface people moved through lit space — but the floods negated the dark, turned it into a uniform white hell where his advantage vanished. He moved anyway. He hit a wall he hadn't mapped. He bounced off it and changed direction and hit another.

There were at least three of them. Their footsteps were surface footsteps — heavy, certain, with no habit of moving quietly. Surface people who came into the deep did not practice quiet because they had never needed to, and that particular arrogance left its signature even when the person trying was otherwise skilled.

He almost made it. His hand found the junction-box and he identified it by texture and knew he was fifteen meters from the pipe-shaft that would take him back down to the Works-level and from there to the Warm and from there to Homewater, and he was running the last meters when something caught his shoulder and that was that.

He went down. The floods held. He was visible.

He was caught.

[Beat 28]

He understood, later, that this was standard practice for whatever these people were — that the candle looked like a weapon or a fire-risk or simply an odd thing that a surface person should not have and therefore should not keep. In the moment he did not understand it as standard practice; he understood it as the loss of his air-oracle and his light source and the thing he had kept alive and untroubled across all the years of his Brave training, and the person who took it handled it carelessly and set it down on a surface floor and the glow-rat skittered to the edge of its dish and then fell over and lay still.

Not far away. Not gone. A place that was very close and very still, where processing could continue without the parts of himself that would have reacted.

They put him in a vehicle. He did not have a word for vehicle. It was an enclosed space that moved without his volition in a way he had never experienced — smooth acceleration and then vibration at a frequency that was not the hum, was not anything — and the enclosed space had no good air. Not killing-air. Bad air in the surface sense: closed, recycled, carrying the smell of many surface people over a long time, a complex accumulation that his nose could not parse fast enough to use.

He sat very still.

There were three of them. He catalogued them by the quality of their breathing and their weight against the space: one large, directly ahead, looking at him; two smaller, flanking, looking away. The large one's breathing was deliberate and managed — the breathing of someone who is doing something they have done before and has a procedure for. The smaller ones breathed normally, which meant this was ordinary to them.

Hunting in the deep was ordinary to them.

He sat with this. The vehicle moved. He sat with this.

[Beat 29]
They handed him to other people, who were different.

The first people had wanted something — not from him specifically, but from the general category of thing he was. He was a quarry successfully taken, a result. The second people were not hunters. They had a different quality of interest: they looked at him the way the teaching described a certain kind of Cog looking at a piece of broken machinery — seeing a problem to be categorized and routed to the correct response.

There was a room with a table, which Glim understood from context was a horizontal surface for resting things. There were chairs — he recognized these from the Cogs' salvaged furniture in the Works — and he sat in one when he was gestured at it, because the gesture was clear and because sitting was easier than standing in a room full of surface light.

They spoke at him. He listened. The surface speech — what he had been taught was the dominant language of the upper city — had edges and patterns he could hear the shape of but not decode. He had pieces of it, the fragments that the Cogs traded in their dealings with the Underclan: transactional words, safety words, names for parts and prices. Not enough. He listened to the pattern of question and pause and question and understood at some level that they wanted something from him, which was information about where he came from, and he did not give them that.

Then there was another room. And then a transit he slept through — the exhaustion had overtaken the caution somewhere in the middle of the long bright day.

And then there was a smell.

[Beat 30]
He was in another room — this one smaller, with a window, which he understood was an opening in a wall covered with a transparent material that let light through without letting air through, a technology that struck him as elaborate and possibly unnecessary — and there was a woman in the doorway.

He smelled her before he saw her.

Not because she was close — she was three meters away, framed by the doorway, and he could see her shape in the light that came from the corridor: a person of medium height, wearing several layers of surface cloth, hands at her sides. He could see her. He knew what he was seeing: a surface person, an adult, a woman based on the proportions. He categorized all of this in the ordinary way.

But the smell.

The smell arrived before his eyes made sense of what they were seeing, because smell was what his body had always processed first. It traveled the short distance across the room and it was — it was the thing. The fragment-smell. The smell from the place adjacent to the noise and the slipping hand and the new dark. The smell that had no name and had never needed a name because it had never been possible to put a name to it, because names required a category and this smell had no category.

His eyes stung. He thought for a moment that it was the surface light — some new irritant his dark-adapted eyes were not equipped for. Then he understood that his eyes were stinging because they were wet.

He had not cried since he was four years old, or thereabouts, or however old he had been when Knuckle found him.

The woman in the doorway made a noise. A single sound. He heard it the way he heard all surface speech: edges, shape, intention. The intention in this sound was unmistakable and it was not a word. It was the sound that a body makes when it has been waiting for something for a very long time and has almost stopped believing the waiting would end.

She said a word after the sound. A short word, two syllables. He had never heard it before. He did not know what it meant. He did not respond to it.

But he was looking at her, and his eyes were wet, and the smell was everywhere now, and something in the back of his blood was doing something he had no vocabulary for — was recognizing something, not with the mind, not with any faculty he could account for, but in the oldest place, the place below the hum.

[Beat 31]
Her name was Noor. He learned this from the other surface people who came into the room and spoke to her, and who used this sound when addressing her: *Noor*. Two syllables, equal weight. He stored it.

She tried to give him things. This was, he came to understand, the surface's primary response to almost any problem — give things. She brought cloth (a garment for his body, which his own garments, dark and fungus-cured and shaped for tunnel movement, apparently did not satisfy); she brought food (a substance that was not fungus and not anything he could identify by smell, cooked in the surface way with applied heat rather than the chemical process the Underclan used); she brought what he eventually understood was a cup of water, though he did not drink from it immediately because you checked unfamiliar water before drinking it, always, and the check required the candle's monitoring over time.

He had no candle.

He drank the water anyway, after watching her drink from her own cup, and it was fine.

She tried the word she had said at the door. She said it again, carefully, with pauses between syllables. He watched her mouth make it. He did not respond.

She tried different words, many of them. He learned from the context and repetition: *eat* meant she wanted him to eat the food (he didn't). *Come* meant she wanted him to follow her (he came, carefully, to see where the door led). *Sleep* meant she wanted him to lie down on a surface of unusual softness that was apparently used for sleeping (he lay down on the floor beside it, which was more normal). The word at the door — the two-syllable word — she stopped using, and in its place she used a different sound: *boy*. He understood from context that *boy* was a category, not a name.

He did not tell her his name. His name was tapped, not spoken. She would never be able to make it.

He lay on the floor in the surface room with the light that never stopped — she had eventually understood that the ceiling-light was wrong for him and had done something to make it dim — and he lay in the dim surface-dark that was not dark at all but was less overwhelming, and he breathed the air with the smell in it, and he let himself feel the smell without doing anything about it.

She sat in the chair on the other side of the room and she was quiet.

It was the most tolerable thing she did. He noted it.

[Beat 32]
The surface did not make sense and he was not certain it was supposed to.

He spent three days in Noor's dwelling — a surface structure, many rooms, with running water and the ceiling-light she had learned to keep low and a strange abundance of things that were not food and not tools and whose purpose he could not determine. Decorative objects, he supposed eventually. Things the surface kept for their appearance. He had never seen anything kept purely for its appearance. Everything in Homewater had a function.

He explored the dwelling carefully, touching everything, smelling everything. The surface had so many smells. Not the organized smell-vocabulary of the deep — air-quality, food, people, danger, the particular chemistry of different stone and water and fungus — but a chaos of manufactured scents layered over everything. Noor's dwelling smelled of cooking oils and soap and something floral that was not any flower he knew and a background chemical complexity he could not parse.

He found the windows and understood them: the transparent material was there to let in the outside light without the outside air. He understood why, intellectually — the surface air was unmanaged and carried everything in it without discrimination — but it struck him as a strange priority. Down below, you managed the air because the air could kill you. Up here, they managed the air because they preferred a particular combination of it. Because they wanted it to smell like the floral thing and not like the outside.

He could not decide if this was fastidious or fearful.

He watched the outside through the window for long periods. The surface was endlessly busy. Surface people moved in patterns he eventually began to decode — not random, though they looked random, but purposeful in the surface way, which was to say purposeful without acknowledging the other purpose-havers in the space. They did not make room. They did not check the air. They walked in the full white of the Burning Eye without flinching, though in some of them he noticed they had things over their faces: the surface's candle, he supposed. Protective objects.

Noor stood beside him one morning and looked at the same view. She said something in surface speech. He could not decode all of it, but the shape of it was: *a lot of people*. He had been thinking the same thing. He had been thinking that down below, forty-three people were a Hollow, and a Hollow was a community of significant size. Up here, forty-three people was what he could see from a single window.

He tapped his full tapped name on the windowsill. She looked at his hand, at the tap-pattern, and did not understand.

He did it again and she still did not understand and that was correct. She would never understand. She could not hear the Current's language.

But she was quiet next to him, which was the thing she had learned to do, and it was still the most tolerable thing she did.

[Beat 33]
On the fourth day the surface learned the legend was real.

He understood this because the small flat surface-device that Noor carried and communicated through — he had watched her use it; it carried voice and image and appeared to be some form of tap-speech made elaborate and visual — began showing images of his own face.

Not accurately. The image was a surface drawing, an approximation, which told him the surface had seen him and had tried to reproduce what it had seen for distribution. He recognized the approximation as himself only because he had seen his own reflection in the cistern's still water at the right angle and the right light, which was rarely, but enough times. The drawing was not exact but it was close enough.

The legend is true.

He watched Noor's reaction as she looked at the device, and at the drawing, and at him, and at the device again. Her face was doing many things at once. He had been learning to read surface faces in the same way he had learned to read candle-behavior: not because faces were a natural language to him, but because they were the primary information medium in this environment and he needed it the way he needed air. He could read about a third of her expressions reliably now, which he felt was reasonable progress for four days.

What her face was doing now had the quality of: *this is a problem I did not create and did not want and now I have to solve*.

He understood that quality. He felt it often.

The device showed a second image: a place. Not Homewater — he would have known Homewater by smell even in a flat image, and this was different. But underground. The image had the quality of the deep: the arched ceilings, the brick-and-tile of the old infrastructure, the low light. Someone had been down there taking images, and now the surface was looking at them.

Someone had written down the way down.

[Beat 34]
Other surface people came to look at him.

Not the Lamplighters — those had handed him to a different set of surface people, who had handed him to Noor, and the chain of transfers was now complete in some surface bureaucratic sense he could not fully follow. But people came to Noor's dwelling and they looked at him and talked among themselves and Noor stood in the middle of the room being the kind of still that was not peaceful but controlled.

He read the people who came. He could read them because they all did the same thing: they came in with the prepared surface face — the face they had assembled before entering to deal with whatever they expected to find — and then they saw him and the prepared face came slightly apart. He was sitting on the floor, which was not what they expected. He was eating a dried fungus-cap from his pack, which was not what they expected. He was watching them with the unblinking attention of someone who navigates by detail and habit and sensory information, and the quality of being watched by him was apparently not what they expected.

He was not afraid of them. That, he thought, was what the prepared faces could not account for. They were expecting someone feral and lost. He was Glim, a Brave who had surfaced and was waiting to go back down.

One of them spoke to him directly, with the deliberate slow pacing of someone who has been told the other person does not understand well. He understood more than they thought. He had been listening and learning for four days with an urgency that drew on everything Knuckle had ever taught him about adapting to a new environment: quickly, completely, without nostalgia for the old one.

He understood: *Where did you come from?*

He said nothing. He tapped his name on the floor. They looked at his hand and did not understand.

He understood: *Are there more of you?*

Silence. He held still and let the silence be a surface-choice: he chose not to answer, which on the surface meant the same thing as tapping *the air is well* when it was dangerous to speak. He chose not to answer and he watched their faces and he filed every face he saw.

He would know them later. Down below, in the dark, he would know them by their footstep and their breathing-pattern and the quality of their light. He would know them the way the deep knew things: by mark, not name.

[Beat 35]
On the sixth night Noor sat across from him in the floor-dim and tried the word again. The two-syllable word she had said in the doorway when she first smelled. She said it quietly, once.

He looked at her.

She put her hand on her own sternum and said a different sound. *Noor*. He understood: this was her name. The name she was given by the surface, which kept names like the Oarsman kept fares: in record, unchanging.

Then she indicated him and said the two-syllable word again.

He understood what she was doing. He understood what she believed the two-syllable word was. He understood that she believed this word was the name the surface had given to the specific person she thought he was — a person who had been lost, a child, a vanished thing that was now found.

He was Glim. His name was tapped, not spoken.

But he looked at her face — he had been reading it for six days and was better at it now — and he saw what was in it, which was a thing he did not have a surface word for. In the Underclan there was a word for the feeling when the Current's hum came back after a long silence: *returned*. It was used for things that came back from loss. The word for a candle that recovered from a bad-air pocket was *returning*, and the word for a person who had gone somewhere difficult and come back was *returned*, and both words carried the same quality of: *we did not stop believing you were there*.

Her face had that quality.

He did not speak the surface word. He would not speak it. It was not his to speak.

But he tapped his actual name on the floor, slowly, the full form. And then he tapped it again. And then he held her gaze and let her see that he had heard the word she said and had chosen to give her this one instead.

She did not understand the tap. But she was quiet, and her face changed in a small way, and she did not look away.

He did not look away either.

[Beat 36]
He left on the eighth night.

Not because the situation had become intolerable — Noor had been patient in a way that was its own kind of intelligence, making adjustments, learning what he needed and providing it without the quality of expectation he had encountered in the official surface people. But the Surfacing's original purpose was to go up and come back, and he had been up for eight days, and the clan did not know where he was, and Grale was still in the Hollow, and the candle was dead.

The candle was the thing he could not get past. The glow-rat that had been taken and mishandled had not recovered. He had found it on the third day, still in the dish, barely breathing, the bioluminescence almost entirely dark. He had brought it water and warmth and the fungus-cap from his pack and it had died on the fourth day. He had told himself this was the air — the surface air was wrong for it, the wrong temperature, the wrong humidity, the chemistry of the surface light affecting its colonies — and this was true, but it was also true that he had failed to shield it and it had been taken from his control and it had died.

A Brave who lets a candle die is a Brave who is not paying attention. He could not stop being in a situation where he was not paying attention.

He went out the way he had come in: through the window's lower panel that opened, which he had spent two days learning to operate with the surface's latch-mechanism, and then down the outside of the structure by the same logic he used on tunnel walls — grip, test, move, grip — and then into the street below, which was lit but quiet in the middle of the night, and then toward the grating he remembered.

He remembered the exact location. This was not remarkable. He remembered every terrain he had ever moved through. It was not a skill; it was what the dark required and the deep had trained.

[Beat 37]
She followed him.

He had not heard her — she was quieter than he expected a surface person to be, and he was distracted by the grating mechanism and the descent into the Works — and he was three levels down before he felt the air-displacement behind him and turned.

Noor. With a surface candle in her hand — a torch, a hand-held fixed-light, which gave her a wavering pool of white that was too much for his eyes but that he could navigate around. She was breathing fast from the descent and her face in the torchlight had that controlled-not-peaceful quality.

He stopped.

She said something. He caught the shape: *wait* or *stop*. He had stopped already.

He said, for the first time to her, what he needed to say, in the Underclan's language because it was the only language that mattered down here. He said it the way Vesh said the teaching: not loud, not forceful, but with the weight of a received truth. *The Skin is up. The Deep is down. You chose to follow me, which means you are choosing down, and down does not unmake itself for regret.*

She did not understand the words. He had not expected her to. He said it anyway, because the saying was correct, and because the Deep required correct things said in the correct way, and because he had been up for eight days speaking only surface language with surface fragments and saying something true in the real language felt like breathing after the bad air passed.

She said something else. He caught: *I know* and *you* and *—* and the shape of intention, which was *I'm not stopping*.

He turned and continued down. She followed. He listened to her footsteps behind him — surface-heavy, uncertain, but present — and he adjusted his pace to something she could manage without putting her light out, and they descended together into the Warm.

The hum met him at the second level. He pressed his palm to the wall and let it run up through his arm and into his chest. Behind him, Noor's torch wavered. The hum did not acknowledge her.

The hum did not acknowledge anyone. That was its virtue.

[Beat 38]
He brought her as far as the Works-level and stopped.

Not Homewater. The Works were the Engine Guild's territory, and the Engine Guild and the Underclan had their treaty, and he did not know whether the treaty's terms extended to surface people he brought in uninvited. More importantly: the Works were where the Lamplighters had found him. He did not want to be in the Works with a surface torch and a surface person and no information about who was currently in the tunnels around them.

He gestured at Noor to put out the torch. She resisted — he could read the resistance without needing her face, in the angle of her body, the way she brought the torch slightly in — and he held her gaze for a count of eight and then looked at the torch and back at her. She put it out.

The dark arrived around them. He heard her breathing change: faster, higher, the fear-response that surface people had for the dark because the dark had nothing to offer them. He understood this response abstractly and had never shared it, which was not courage — it was simply that the dark was home.

He said, in surface speech, using the words he had assembled over eight days: "I watch. You stay." And then, after a pause: "Safe."

He could not be certain she was safe. He said it anyway, because it was the closest he could come to the gesture of the Oarsman: accept the Fare, convey, return. She had given him eight days of her patience; she was about to receive the correct darkness in return.

He waited. He listened. The eastern tunnel was clear. The north junction had Cog-type footsteps, two people, moving away. The pump-hall had its regular cycle.

He reached back and found her hand in the dark — found it the way he found everything down here, by the displacement of air and the warmth of presence — and he held it for a moment with the grip that meant *I am here, I have found you, the air is well*. She went still.

Then he started moving, and she moved with him, and they went down to Homewater with only his knowledge between them and the dark.

He did not know, yet, that above them, someone was writing the route down. That the surface was studying the images taken in the Works, and the legend had an address now, and the address was being shared among people whose interest in the deep was not scholarship.

[Beat 39]
Homewater did not welcome him back.

He had not expected ceremony — the Underclan did not do ceremony except at the formal ritual points, and his return from a Surfacing should have been one of those points, the moment of choosing-down made visible. But what he found when he came through the lower Works into the familiar air of the Hollow was not ceremony and not hostility: it was the careful blankness of a community that has received unexpected news and is deciding what to do with it.

Vesh was in the teaching-chamber. Knuckle was in the teaching-chamber. Most of the Braves were in the teaching-chamber. They had positioned themselves the way people positioned themselves when they needed to have a difficult conversation and had been waiting for the conversation's subject to arrive.

He stood in the doorway with Noor behind him, her hand still finding his shoulder-blade in the dark. He could feel her adjusting to the Hollow's air, which was correct and organized and so different from the surface's abundance that it would hit her like a wall.

"You came back," Vesh said. The teaching voice, not the ordinary one.

"I came back."

"You brought someone."

"Yes."

The silence that followed was not hostile. It was the silence of calculation — everyone in the room running through the implications, the treaty questions, the air-quality questions, the questions about what a surface person's presence meant for Homewater's structural anonymity.

Grale spoke from the back. "Surface-stained." Not an accusation. A statement of observation, which was worse, because it was accurate.

"Her name is Noor," Glim said. "She followed me down. She'll need a candle."

There was a longer pause.

Sorrel crossed the room and put her hand on Noor's arm, guiding her forward. She was good with outsiders — the Surfacing had given her a quality of surface-legibility that few people in the Hollow had. Noor allowed herself to be guided, which said something about her: she was frightened and she was doing it anyway. He had not known her long enough to know if this was her habitual state or a specific response to the current circumstances.

The candle on Sorrel's belt glowed green-gold in the dark.

Noor looked at it the way people on the surface looked at unfamiliar things: first with caution, then with something closer to wonder.

[Beat 40]
They came on the third day after his return.

The first sign was air. The right-hand tunnel, which came from the direction of the Works, carried a draft that was wrong — not the regular Vent Cog cycle, not the irregular leak of the broken junction, but a forced air-movement, like the Works were breathing wrong. Glim felt it on his arm at the morning-check and stood very still and felt it again.

The draft was carrying something. Not the killing air — the candle was fine. It was carrying the surface: the manufactured scents, the particular not-organic complexity that he had learned to identify in eight days on the surface and had spent the past three days purging from his own clothing and skin. Someone on the surface was in the Works, and they were using surface equipment, and the surface equipment was pushing its smell ahead of it.

He made no sound. He was already in the tunnel mouth, already moving.

The floods came from the upper Works, through three junctions he would have sworn were solid: the Lamplighters had found the maps he had not known were being made and had descended faster than he had given them credit for. The first flood-lamp appeared at the junction's bend forty meters up-tunnel, and the quality of light was immediately wrong — too white, too diffuse, the absence-of-edge that had taken his eyes on the surface.

He turned and ran.

He did not shout. Shouting in the tunnel was a known signature — you could follow the source of the sound, and the Lamplighters clearly already had their routes mapped. He ran and he tapped the pipe-wall as he ran: the emergency pattern, three-tap-two, which meant *above-surface danger* and which would propagate through the pipe-network to every Brave who was touching a pipe.

Behind him, the floods advanced. They did not rush. They moved with the confidence of people who had more light than anyone down here and knew it.

[Beat 41]
They came with lights. Two days later, the Mission came with medicine.

It was Sorrel who understood the Mission first, who had spent time on the surface in her Surfacing and had better surface-vocabulary than anyone else in the Hollow. She came to Glim with her face set in the expression she used when she had processed something difficult and needed to say it plainly.

"They're not Lamplighters," she said.

"I know."

"They came through the Skin asking for the children. Saying they knew the children were real. Saying they wanted to —" she used the surface word — "*help*."

He understood *help* now. He understood several surface words. He had forty-three candles' worth of reasons to understand surface vocabulary and he had spent the past three days continuing to learn.

"They brought medicine," Sorrel said. "Surface medicine. For the Hot Breath."

The Hot Breath. He had heard the phrase twice in the past two days — once from one of the older clan members, in the context of a question about air quality, and once from Knuckle, in the context of an instruction to monitor the candles more carefully than usual. He had not understood, at that point, what the phrase was indicating.

Now he understood.

One of the younger Braves — Lark, the girl who had been asleep with the candle at the morning-check — was coughing.

It was a surface cough. He had heard surface coughs in Noor's dwelling: the complex productive cough of a person with something active in their upper respiratory system. Lark's cough was that cough. He had not heard anyone in Homewater cough like that in his sixteen years here, because they did not get surface colds in Homewater.

They had not, before now, had surface contact.

He had brought it down.

[Beat 42]
Vesh refused the Mission's medicine.

This was Vesh's decision to make — it was the Listener's role to interpret the Current's will and the clan's tradition in unfamiliar situations — and he made it with the certainty he brought to all his decisions and without consultation with anyone, including Knuckle.

"No salt that fights the Hot Breath," he said, in the teaching voice, in the teaching-chamber with the green tile, with the remaining Braves and the elder members of the clan. "No surface medicine in the Deep."

Glim said: "Lark is coughing."

"I know." Vesh did not look toward the tunnel where Lark slept. "The Deep has carried us for generations without surface medicine. It will carry us now."

"The Hot Breath is not a Deep illness." He heard himself say this and felt the quality of it: he was correcting Vesh. He had never corrected Vesh. "It's a surface illness in a Deep body. The Deep has no answer for it because the Deep has never seen it."

The silence in the teaching-chamber was a particular kind of silence. He had heard this silence before — when a question came that was new and Vesh went quiet in the way that was different from his other silences.

"You've been to the surface," Vesh said.

"Yes."

"You know what surface medicine does."

He did not know. He had seen Noor take medicine from a surface container and had understood from context that it was a substance for treating illness. He did not know the mechanism. "No," he said. "But I know what it's for. And I know that Lark is coughing."

Vesh was quiet for a long count. The hum came through the floor. They all felt it.

"Ask the Mission," Vesh said finally, "what they want in return. The answer will tell us what they are."

It was not agreement. It was the beginning of a question, which was the closest Vesh came to changing his mind.

Glim went to ask.

[Beat 43]
The Mission wanted to register them.

This was what Glim understood from the conversation he carried with a Mission person — a woman, surface-middle, with the quality of a practitioner rather than a hunter — using his partial surface vocabulary and Sorrel's better one. *Register* meant: put your name in the surface record. It meant: you exist, in the surface's count of things that exist.

He took this back to Vesh.

Vesh was silent for the new-question silence. Then: "And when our names are in the surface's record?"

"Then we are someone in their count."

"We are already someone."

"In their count," Glim said.

Another silence. "And when our names are in the surface's record and the surface decides, one day, that the record should be cleaned up — that the deep is not a place for people, that the registered Underclan should be relocated to the surface level, that this is for our safety — will our names protect us or enable them?"

He did not have an answer for this. He had been thinking it for three days and did not have an answer.

"The Mission is sincere," he said.

"Yes," Vesh said. "That is what concerns me."

The Mission's sincerity was real — he had read enough surface faces by now to recognize the performed sincerity of the Lamplighters (a performance so thin it could be felt), and the Mission's sincerity was not performance. They genuinely wanted to help. They genuinely believed that registration, medicine, relocation, and surface integration would improve the Underclan's lives. They were capable of doing enormous harm while believing this completely.

He did not take the Mission's offer.

He also knew, by the third day after the conversation, that the Mission had already begun mapping Homewater.

[Beat 44]
The Hot Breath took Slip first.

He was the youngest of the Braves — six sleep-seasons from his Surfacing, still in the early years of the teaching. His First Hearing had been two months ago. He had named his candle, which the young ones did before they outgrew it. The candle's name was *Tallow*, a made-up word with no meaning in either language, which was the point: Slip's candle had a name that belonged to nobody.

Tallow was fine. Slip was not.

The cough had been manageable in Lark. Lark was older and had the kind of physical reserve that came from years of climbing and harvesting and the physical work of the Braves' training. She was still coughing but she was upright and functional. Slip was seven years old or thereabouts, and the Hot Breath in a seven-year-old was not the same as the Hot Breath in a Brave.

He ran a fever that the clan had no word for, because fevers were not an Underclan illness. His temperature was legible in the feel of him when Glim pressed the back of his hand to Slip's forehead, and the heat was wrong — too much, the body doing something he had no framework for interpreting. The surface called it *fever*. He knew the word. He did not know what it meant in terms of the body's function, which was the knowledge you needed to decide what to do.

Noor knew. She had been watching from the edge of the clan's space — not integrated, not fully trusted, but present in the way that a person with Sorrel as escort was allowed to be present — and she crossed the space to where Slip was lying and she put her hand where Glim's had been and she said a word to herself in surface speech that he could not decode but that had the quality of: *this is bad*.

She said to him, carefully: "The Mission's medicine. Bring it now."

He looked at her.

"I know you don't trust them," she said. She was using simple surface speech, the vocabulary she had learned worked with him. "I don't trust them either. But the medicine and the Mission are not the same thing. The medicine works."

He went to get Vesh.

[Beat 45]
They took the medicine. It was too late for Slip by two days and just enough for the others.

He never knew this for certain — he did not have enough surface medical knowledge to determine causation, only correlation — but Slip died on the fifth day of the Hot Breath's progression and the other sick members of the clan stabilized within a day of the medicine's administration. He watched the correlation and filed it and could not make it into certainty and did not try.

Vesh did not speak at Slip's sending-off — the ritual was the Brave-master's, and Knuckle spoke it, flat and correct and without decoration. The glow-rat called Tallow was placed in the cistern as the final gesture: a small green-gold light on the black water, moving out toward the center, visible for a long time.

And then the Lamplighters came through the south junction with their floods and that was when the clan's capacity for holding their ground failed.

The floods came from two directions at once — the south junction and the east Works — and the combined white-out in the Hollow was total. Glim could see nothing, which was not his limiting factor; the others could see nothing and it was theirs. Forty-two people making decisions in a complete white-out, with their dark-adapted eyes shut down, with the candles useless, with the air now carrying the surface-smell of the Lamplighters' equipment.

He yelled the direction. Not words — direction, a sound they all knew, the sound that meant *this way, follow it, don't stop*. He grabbed the nearest hand and moved and people attached to the chain behind him and they went through the tunnel that led toward the Old Deep.

The Old Deep, and past it, the Tartar.

He had never been to the Tartar. No living member of the clan had been to the Tartar. The taboo was old and absolute and made from fear of the dead and fear of the bad air and the honest acknowledgment that the Tartarian Empire was past the boundary of where anyone had been and come back.

They went anyway. There was nowhere else left.

[Beat 46]
Noor was in the chain. He had not planned for this. He had grabbed a hand in the flood-white and it had been hers, and by the time he knew it was hers they were two tunnels past the Hollow and he was not turning back.

She moved as well as he had any right to expect. He had taken her hand in the way that meant *I am here, follow me*, and she had followed — not blindly, she was using her other hand on the wall the way he had shown her in the Works, feeling the texture of the surfaces and using them — but with the quality of a person who has made a decision and is implementing it without reservation.

The chain of people behind him was forty-two long: forty-one Underclan and Noor. He counted the grip-responses in the dark, the chain of hands that passed information backward, the tapped signals — *still here, still moving, I have the one behind me* — and they moved through the Old Deep in a human chain through the complete dark with a flood-light behind them somewhere, distant now, because the Lamplighters did not know these routes.

They knew a lot of routes. They did not know all of them.

The air in the Old Deep was different from Homewater's air — older, stiller, with a weight to it that was the weight of centuries of sealed space. The brick and tile of the deeper architecture had a different smell than the ferrocrete above: earthy, almost living, a dampness that was not dangerous but was strange. His candle — he had grabbed a candle from the shelf when the floods first appeared, one of the sentinel ones, and it was clasped in his free hand — gave a steady green-gold glow.

He watched the candle and it watched the air.

Behind him, Noor's hand was warm and steady.

He did not look back. The tunnel was narrow and the Tartar was ahead and the dead, if they were there, would have to manage with company.

[Beat 47]
The Tartarian Empire was beautiful.

This was the first thing and the wrong thing — not that beauty was wrong, but that he had not expected it, and the wrongness of expectation in a place of genuine danger was itself a danger. He had expected the architecture to be plain or broken or merely old. Instead it was all three and also the other thing: vaulted, the ceilings rising to shapes he had never seen above Homewater, curves and arches that the surface had forgotten how to make or had chosen to stop making, and the surfaces covered in the tile that the teaching-chamber had in small — glazed, decorated, patterned with faces and shapes from before any memory. The bioluminescence of the clan's candles moved through this space and the tiles threw it back from a hundred angles and the Tartar glowed.

He checked the candle. Fine. The air was cold and still and very old but it was good air. Whatever terrible things the clan feared in the Tartar — the bad air pockets, the drowned voids, the deepest Made Things — were not in the first three hundred meters of the empire's entry vaults.

They stopped. Forty-one people plus Noor, plus candles. He counted everyone.

Forty-two people breathing. The candles all fine.

He made the three-finger sign. The air is well.

He sat down on the tile and pressed his palms flat against it and tried to feel the hum.

It was not there.

This was not like the surface, where the hum had been absent because it was a thing of the Deep and the surface was not the Deep. This was different: the Tartar was deep, deeper than Homewater, and the hum should have been stronger here. But the hum was absent in the specific way of a sound whose frequency the space will not carry — as if the Tartarian Empire's architecture had been built for a different kind of resonance, one that excluded the Current's voice.

The current was silent here. Had always been silent here.

This was what the taboo was for.

[Beat 48]
Vesh had the Hot Breath.

He had not said. He was the Listener; announcing his own illness had a specific weight in the clan's structure that was not the same as a Brave announcing it. He had continued to move and to give direction and to be Vesh, with the particular quality of Vesh's stillness and Vesh's certainty, and Glim had not noticed until he saw the old man's face in the candle-light of the Tartarian Empire's vaults, where there was no hum to distract him and nothing to navigate and the candles threw everything back from the tiles.

The face was not Vesh's face. It had Vesh's structure — the same half-blind eyes, the same set of the mouth, the same architecture of a person who has been thinking continuously for seven decades — but the surface of it was wrong, in the way that surface-water was wrong when you tipped it: the temperature gone out of it, replaced with something that was not Vesh's temperature.

He sat beside the old man without speaking. The clan was settling itself around the vaults, moving quietly, exploring the space with the same careful approach they brought to any new territory. Noor was with Sorrel. The candles were all fine.

"You knew," Glim said. Not an accusation. A recognition.

Vesh made a small sound that was not words.

"The Hot Breath was already in you when you agreed to take the Mission's medicine for Lark."

"Lark needed it more urgently," Vesh said. His voice had the teaching quality, which meant he was maintaining the register because he still could. "I have been old for some time. My body is accustomed to managing without cooperation from the things inside it."

It was not a complaint. It was a statement of the mechanism, which was what Vesh did with everything.

"The surface medicine would have —"

"Perhaps." He paused. "I have spent seventy years listening to the Current. I would like to do that as long as possible. The medicine may or may not extend the time. What it certainly does is change the environment. I have learned to work in the one I have."

Glim looked at the tiles. They caught his candle's glow and returned it. The vaulted ceiling went up to heights he couldn't measure.

"It's quiet here," he said. "The hum is gone."

"Yes." Vesh's hand moved, just slightly, in the direction of the tile. "They built beautiful things. The people who made this. And whatever they had of the Current's voice, they sealed it out. I think perhaps they sealed it out on purpose. I have been wondering, all the walk down, what they sealed it out for." He paused. "What do you think they sealed it out for?"

Glim looked at the vaults, at the faces in the tile. He had no answer. He had brought the surface down. He had lost a candle and a Brave and he had brought the surface down, and the clan was in the ghost-country because of it.

"I think," he said, slowly, "they wanted to make a place where the Current couldn't find them. They wanted a place that was only theirs."

Vesh was quiet. Then: "What do you think of that?"

"I think it was lonely," Glim said.

Vesh made a sound that, in a different context, might have been a laugh. "Yes," he said. "I also think that."

[Beat 49]
He went deeper into the Tartar alone.

Not far — he had the good sense not to go far into unknown territory without a candle-check and a plan, which was the Brave's training over the instinct to move. He went perhaps two hundred meters past the vault where the clan had stopped, and he sat on the floor of an even larger space — a hall, he supposed, the Tartarian Empire's version of a common space — with his candle beside him and his hands flat on the tile.

No hum.

He sat with the absence of it. The absence of the hum was worse than the surface's absence, because on the surface you had the Burning Eye and the noise and the chaos of a world that was too full, and the hum's absence was one absence among many. Here the absence was clean and total and had no cover story.

He had brought the surface down. He had brought it down by going up — by being Glim, by choosing the left tunnel, by not dying when he should have, by smelling Noor in the doorway and letting his eyes sting rather than controlling the reaction. Every choice he had made correctly by the Underclan's law had led here, to forty-two people in the ghost-country without a home to go back to.

Grale was right, he thought. The surface puts things in you.

And then, from below — not the floor, not the tile, but from some depth below even the Tartarian Empire, below the history and the beauty and the sealed silence — he felt it.

Not the hum. Not the Current's voice in the frequency he knew, the chest-pressure, the below-hearing register. Something different. A single pulse, barely perceptible, like the vibration of a very large object at a very long distance arriving through many layers of insulation.

He pressed his palms harder. The pulse did not repeat.

He waited for a long time in the silent hall with the beautiful tiles.

It did not repeat.

He went back to the clan.

[Beat 50]
He told no one about the pulse. Not immediately.

He turned it over in his mind for a full sleep-cycle while the clan rested in the Tartar's first vault and while Noor and Sorrel organized the food stocks and while Vesh sat with his back against the tiled wall doing whatever Vesh did when he was very still. The pulse might have been a vibration from the Warm's machinery at the surface of a frequency that the Tartarian Empire's architecture resonated differently. The pulse might have been a Made Thing moving at a long distance. The pulse might have been nothing — the sensation of a person pressing their palms against cold stone and wanting to feel something.

He did not know which of these things it was. He could not know.

What he knew was that the clan had to go back.

Not to Homewater — he did not know yet what Homewater looked like now, whether the Lamplighters had set permanent lights or had moved through and gone. Not to the surface. Back: toward the problem, not away from it. Running deeper into the Tartar would eventually hit the bad air or the drowned voids or the deeper Made Things or simply the end of food and water, and none of these were plans.

He made his case to Knuckle first, because Knuckle was a practical man.

"The Lamplighters know our routes," he said. "They have the surface maps. They don't know the Cog tunnels."

"The Cogs don't like us," Knuckle said.

"No. But they like the Lamplighters less." He had spent time in the Works before the Surfacing, in the uneasy border-time when Cog business and Underclan business occasionally coincided. He knew the Cogs' grievance against the surface — the surface that had built the Works and then forgotten them, that had left the Cogs to maintain the machinery in an informal labor arrangement that was not quite slavery and not quite independence. The Cogs were not allies. But they had their own reasons to keep the surface out of the deep. "They won't help us because they like us. They'll help us if we make it worth their while."

"What do we have worth their while?"

Glim thought of eight days on the surface. Of the devices and the maps and the networks and the route-information he had absorbed. Of Noor, who could translate between the surface bureaucratic world and the underground economy.

"Information," he said. "The surface's information, brought down."

[Beat 51]
The Cog settlement in the Works was called the Bindery, and it smelled of machine-oil and old grain and the specific sourness of people who worked with their hands in confined spaces without enough daylight.

Glim knew the smell of the Bindery. He had been here twice before the Surfacing — once as a young Brave bringing trade-goods that the Underclan's upper tunnels occasionally produced for the maintenance workers, and once accompanying Grale on a negotiation that Grale had not fully explained and that had ended with a handshake and a silence that Glim had learned not to ask about. He knew the Cog hierarchy: the Chief Fitter, who oversaw the maintenance schedule; the Pipe-Readers, who interpreted the Works' machinery the way Listeners interpreted the Current; the gangs of Fitters and Runners who did the actual labor.

He did not know the current Chief Fitter by name.

"Her name is Dross," Noor said, beside him. They were crouched at the Bindery's entrance passage, watching the light through the grille. "She was the deputy under the previous one. He died eighteen months ago." She paused. "The surface administration recorded it. They have a legal obligation to maintain the Works staff because of the maintenance covenant, but they haven't fulfilled it in three cycles."

"What does that mean for Dross?"

"She's been doing the work of two people with half the parts."

He looked at her. She had the particular quality she sometimes had of knowing things and not performing them — of simply having the information and giving it across without display.

"How do you know all of this?"

"The records are not secret. The Mission's database has full maintenance covenant documentation. I looked." She paused. "I looked when you told me we were going to the Cogs."

He considered this. "You've been thinking ahead."

"It seemed useful." Her voice had the flatness that was not coldness but was focus.

They went in together. He had decided they should go in together — that Noor's presence was a signal, not a liability, the thing that said *we bring you the surface's documents, we bring you someone who can read them*, and the signal was more powerful than the risk.

Dross was a small woman with grease permanently worked into the lines of her palms and the look of a person who had not slept adequately in approximately eighteen months. She looked at Glim with the wariness of a Cog meeting an Underclan person, which was its own history. She looked at Noor with a different wariness: the wariness of someone who has not seen a surface person in a very long time and has reasons to prefer that distance.

"You're Underclan," she said. To Glim.

"Yes."

"You're surface." To Noor.

"I came down," Noor said. Not *I was taken* or *I'm not anymore* — just the action, which was the most accurate thing.

"The Lamplighters are in your upper tunnels," Dross said, to Glim.

"Yes."

"They'll be in the Works next."

"Yes."

She turned back to her workbench. "I know. We've been watching." She picked up a tool that Glim had no name for and set it down again. "What do you want from me."

It was not a question. It was a statement of the negotiation's opening.

"We want to use the infrastructure," Glim said. "The pump-halls. The sealed corridors. The locks."

Dross turned around. "Against them."

"Against the flood-lights. Against their movement through the upper Works."

She looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes moved to Noor, then back.

"What do we get," she said.

[Beat 52]
Noor gave them the maintenance covenant.

Not a summary of it — the actual document, pulled from the Mission's internal archive via the surface-connected device she carried. Three hundred and twelve pages of covenant obligations, maintenance schedules, parts requisitions, budget allocations, and the record of every time the surface administration had failed to fulfill its side of the agreement. Eighteen months of documented non-delivery. Forty-seven outstanding parts requests. The names of the people on the surface who had signed the continuation-forms and the counter-signatures that had never come.

She gave them all of it. She showed Dross how to read the document structure, which fields were legally binding, which ones were discretionary. She showed her the budget code that should have funded the Bindery's maintenance staff expansion and had instead been reallocated to the Lamplighter program.

Glim sat against the wall and watched Dross read.

It took a long time. The Pipe-Readers came in partway through — three of them, the senior ones, people who had spent their lives reading the Works' machinery and who had their own grievance relationship with the surface administration. They read over Dross's shoulder. One of them made a sound that was not language but was clearly an opinion.

Glim watched the oil-lamp on Dross's workbench. It was a good lamp — steady flame, no smoke. The Cogs maintained their things well, which was the whole of the Cog self-understanding: *we maintain things, and the things we maintain are what keep the surface habitable, and the surface forgets that, and we do not forgive the surface for forgetting it.*

The Lamplighter program budget code appeared on page forty-eight of the covenant documentation. The Lamplighters' operational budget was listed as a sub-line of the infrastructure maintenance allocation. The maintenance fund that should have paid for Dross's parts had been repurposed, in writing, to pay for the flood-lights.

When Dross got to page forty-eight, she stopped reading and looked up.

"The lights run on our budget," she said.

"Yes," Noor said.

"The budget that's supposed to fund the Works."

"Yes."

Dross set the device down. She looked at Glim.

"You want the pump-halls," she said. "You want the seal-locks in corridor G-seven through G-eleven."

"Yes."

"G-seven through G-eleven flood if we open the pressure-diverts."

"Yes."

"There's Lamplighter equipment in G-eight." She said this without inflection. "If the corridor floods, that equipment becomes debris."

"Yes."

She looked at the document again. At page forty-eight, which was still on the screen.

"We'll need it back," she said. "The budget allocation. When this is over — however this ends — we'll need the covenant honored. Someone will need to advocate on our behalf."

"I will," Noor said.

"You're Mission."

"I was. I'm not sure what I am now." She paused. "I know what covenant documentation looks like. I know how to submit a formal grievance. I know who to file it with and what the escalation path is. None of that depends on what the Mission thinks of me."

Dross looked at her for a long time.

"Show me," she said, "which corridors you need."

[Beat 53]
They did the operation in two shifts with a rest between, because the people doing it needed to rest.

The Cog Runners knew every valve and pressure-gate and seal-lock in the Works the way the Underclan Braves knew every air-pocket and current-point in the tunnels, which was: in their bodies, not just in their heads. Glim watched a Cog Runner named Pell explain the pressure-divert mechanism to a Brave named Sorrel in a language of hand gestures and demonstrated action and occasional improvised terminology for concepts that did not exist in either of their vocabularies. They understood each other. He had not predicted this.

He had predicted the mechanics of it. The flood-light units in corridor G-eight required a stable temperature and a power draw that ran through the Works' main grid. The pressure-diverts in G-seven sent water from the secondary cisterns into the G-through corridor system. Water and electronics were incompatible in the specific way that the Lamplighters apparently had not planned for when they installed the units, which was the gap: they had planned for Underclan tactics, which were small and quiet and did not include flooding an entire infrastructure corridor.

Glim had planned for flooding an entire infrastructure corridor.

The second element was the light itself. The Lamplighters had set a patrol pattern — he had observed it, in the days before the retreat, the rhythm of the shifts — and the patrol used the flood-lights on a cycle. In the period between cycles, there was darkness. The Underclan operated in darkness. The Lamplighters, it had become clear, did not. Their training was for the lit tunnels they created; in darkness they moved slowly and with equipment, and their equipment made noise.

It took most of a shift-cycle. The Cog Runners opened the pressure-diverts in sequence, corridor by corridor, monitoring the water levels against the locations of the Lamplighter patrol. Glim led the Braves in the dark-period between flood-light cycles, moving through the upper Works by air-read and memory and the information that Noor had given him about the patrol structure. They moved around the Lamplighters, not through them.

When the water hit the G-eight equipment, the flood-lights in that corridor died.

The patrol pulled back.

Not far — not all the way to the surface, not a retreat. A tactical regroup. The Lamplighter operation commander had protocols, and the protocols included a pull-back-and-reassess in the event of infrastructure failure. Glim knew this because Noor had told him. He had used this against the operation, because the pull-back was exactly what he needed: not a victory, not a permanent resolution, but space.

Space meant time.

Time meant the clan could move back toward Homewater.

He felt, in the corridor outside the G-eight junction, the hum resume — faint, the upper Works' approximation of it, not the deep register of the lower tunnels, but present. The chest-pressure. The below-hearing frequency. He stood still for a moment and let it move through him.

It was not the Second Word. He knew this. He did not know what the pulse in the Tartar had been, but this was not its answer. This was just the hum, ordinary and daily and below the Lamplighters' frequency and therefore theirs.

Still theirs.

He moved.

[Beat 54]
Homewater had changed.

This was not a surprise. He had expected it to change. He had not been able to predict the specific shape of the change, which was the thing you could not do: you could know that a thing would be different without knowing what the different thing would be.

The flood-lights were gone. The Lamplighters had taken them when they pulled back — he did not know if this was policy or improvisation, but the effect was that Homewater was dark again, the familiar dark, the Underclan dark of candles and bioluminescence and the slow blue-green of the fungal growths that had been here before any of them and would be here after.

But the space was wrong.

The smell was wrong first — not dangerous, not chemical, not the specific wrongness of a bad-air pocket, but wrong in the way that a familiar thing smelled wrong when strangers had occupied it, when objects had been moved and returned to the approximate but not the exact position. They had searched it. He could tell from the way the storage-alcoves sat — slightly open, slightly disturbed, like eyes that had been held shut and then released. The Lamplighters had gone through everything.

Most things were still there. This surprised him. He had expected more removal.

Sixteen of the forty-two people had lost goods they considered important. Three of the storage alcoves had been damaged, not by malice — he could not read malice into it, he would not read malice into it; damaged the way you damaged things when you were searching quickly and didn't care about the space, which was its own thing but not the same thing. Candle-Two's sleeping place had been taken apart and not put back together, and when Candle-Two came to it she stood in the entrance for a long time without speaking.

He helped her put it back together. This took a while because Candle-Two had particular arrangements that mattered to her and she could not always articulate them in words, and he had to watch carefully and hand her things and ask the right kinds of questions.

When they were done, she looked at the space. It was approximately right.

"Still mine," she said.

"Yes," he said. "Still yours."

He counted the clan again. He had counted them every arrival for as long as he could remember, because counting was what you did after movement: you confirmed what you had. Thirty-eight people. He had started with forty-two. Three had not made it back — Leaf, in the Hollow, when the flood-lights first hit. The others later, in the G-corridor operations, in ways he did not yet want to fully account for.

Plus Vesh.

Thirty-eight. Plus Noor.

Thirty-nine.

[Beat 55]
Vesh died on the third day back.

Not dramatically. His body had done the work it could do for the length of the crisis, which was the thing about Vesh — his body had always done the work it needed to do at the time it needed to do it, and then it stopped, and the Hot Breath had been very far advanced by the time he arrived in the Tartar, and there was nothing to be done.

Glim was with him. He had arranged to be with him.

He did not know what he expected from the experience of being with the Listener when the Listener died. He had thought perhaps there would be something to mark it — some quality of the air, some shift in the hum, some perceivable change in the frequency that Vesh had spent his life attending to. He did not know if that was an Underclan belief or something he had assembled from the pieces of his own experience. He had never been present when someone very important to the structure of the world died.

What happened was: Vesh breathed in the way he had been breathing, which was with effort, and then he did not breathe, and that was the difference.

The hum continued at its ordinary frequency.

Glim sat with the body for the time that was appropriate — he counted it by his own breathing rather than by any external measure, which was how the Underclan had always counted the important things. He pressed his palm to the tile beside the sleeping-mat. The hum moved through the stone at the frequency it had always moved.

Knuckle and Sorrel came for the body when the time was done, to bring it to the deep-water disposal that was the Underclan's practice, and Glim stood in the doorway and watched them carry him, and then he went to find Noor.

She was in the transit alcove near Homewater's main passage — the one where the path split toward the upper Works and the lower tunnels. She had a habit of sitting there when she needed to think, which he had noticed over the past months: she processed in motion or in transit-points, places of potential direction, as if the decision was easier when the body could see multiple ways to go.

He sat beside her.

"He taught me to listen," he said. Not immediately — there was a silence first, the appropriate silence, and then this. "He said the hum was not a sound but a question. He asked me once what I thought the question was." He paused. "I still don't know."

"That's not a bad answer," she said.

"He made a sound," Glim said, "that I am still not sure was agreement or instruction."

"What happens now," she said. "To the clan."

"The Current doesn't care about words," he said. "It cares about listening."

Noor was quiet. She was looking at the passage split again.

"I need to tell you something," she said.

[Beat 56]
She had made contact with the Mission.

She told him this plainly, in the way she told him all things, which was without softening and without display. He had spent his whole life in the tunnels. He had learned them from Grale and before Grale from the accumulated knowledge of every Brave who had gone before, passed down through demonstration and correction and the patient repetition of things you did until they were in your body.

"Yes," he said.

He took the Fare at the end of the month.

The clan had rebuilt enough by then — not to what it had been, not in a month, but enough that he could leave it in Knuckle's hands for the length of a transit-run without the leaving feeling like abandonment. Candle-Two had begun to do the things that Listeners did, which was mostly to sit in certain places and attend to them, and the clan had begun to orient itself around her the way you oriented yourself around a thing you were still learning to trust.

He took the Oarsman's route.

The black water was the same black water it had always been — the same temperature, the same mineral smell, the same quality of surface that swallowed light rather than reflecting it. He had stood at this edge many times. He had stood here as a boy, before the Brave-training, before he understood what the black water was and what it was for. He had stood here as a young Brave the first time, waiting for the Oarsman's return with the specific fear that the Oarsman might not come. He had stood here after Grale died, in the early days when the weight of what Grale had left him was still new and still finding its shape.

The Oarsman came from the dark the same way it always came: soundlessly, the hull of the vessel barely disturbing the water's surface, the figure in the center present and attended and offering no explanation for where it had been or what it was.

Glim stepped down into the vessel.

The Fare was ready — he had the three-finger configuration, which was the full Fare, the one that meant a life's worth of passage. He had prepared it carefully and correctly. He had been a Brave long enough to know that the Fare was not a transaction but an accounting: you gave what you had actually accumulated, and what you had actually accumulated was what the journey so far had cost.

He put the Fare in the vessel's center.

The Oarsman did not look at it — or if it looked, Glim could not perceive the looking. It moved in the way it moved, which was the way things moved that had been moving for a long time along a known route, with the economy of something that has found the least effortful path through resistance.

The black water opened ahead of them.

He thought of his mother's hands. He thought of this sometimes on the Fare — not often, not always, only in the moments when the water was very dark and the candle's glow did not reach far and the vessel was in the middle of crossing and the Oarsman was moving. He did not know if she had gone down into the Underclan willingly or by accident or in fear. He did not know if she had taken a Fare. He had lived his whole life in the space of not-knowing and had learned that not-knowing was not the same as absence: the space had a shape, and the shape of it was his.

He had started as someone else's child. He had become himself. He did not know yet what he was becoming.

The hum was present at the frequency it always was on the black water — lower and fuller than in the tunnels, the way it was always lower and fuller when the water was between him and the stone. He sat with it. He did not try to find the pulse from the Tartar in it or the Second Word or any answer that the hum had not yet chosen to give.

He sat with what it was.

The other bank materialized from the dark the way it always did: first the smell of the stone ahead, then the change in the candle's behavior, then the edge. He stepped off. The Oarsman waited.

He looked back once across the black water.

A new Fare. A new crossing.

He went on.
