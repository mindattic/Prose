# Underclan

_The first law of the Underclan: never let the surface know they exist.

For generations, they have survived in the drowned tunnels beneath the GLMZ — in the dark, by candlelight and ritual and iron discipline, in a world the city above has forgotten entirely. Glim has spent his whole life preparing for a single journey upward. One look. Then back.

What he finds on the surface destroys everything he was told.

The city remembers a child who vanished sixteen years ago. Powerful people have been searching for the civilization hidden beneath them, and now they know where to look. The Underclan's oldest secret — its location, its people, its impossible machines — is no longer safe.

UNDERCLAN is a story about a civilization that has survived by staying hidden — and the boy who has to choose which world he belongs to before someone chooses for him._

## Ch01 - The Breath of the Deep

[Beat 1]
Glim knew the hour by the air on his forearm.

Cool, coming from the right. No smoke in it, no sharp edge. He lay still on the fungus-hide shelf and let the air tell him the rest. The right-hand tunnel carried the Vent Cog's morning work — the same rusted wheel, the same quarter-turn, every fourth sleep without fail. The Cog had never once been late. 

The Underclan had a word for a thing that came back when it said it would. It was the same word they used for the Deep One's voice.

He sat up slowly and reached for the candle before anything else. The glow-rat was already awake. It had heard him move, or felt him, and it came to his hand and nuzzled it, a small animal with its own morning to keep. He lifted it and read the light it gave off. Steady breathing. Clean, even glow. The air was good, then. It was morning.

Across the Hollow, two elders were already at the heat-stone. They pressed dried fungus-meat against it in the slow way of people who had done the same work for thirty years and saw no reason to change. One was Leaf. She had tended that stone longer than Glim had been alive. She pressed the meat in a rhythm no one had taught her — three presses, a turn, three presses — and no one else could match it. Braves coming off a long harvest could find the heat-stone in the dark by the sound of her hands alone, before they saw the glow. She sang while she worked, low, closer to breath than song. The Hollow had long stopped hearing it, the way it stopped hearing its own dripping water. The smell of the meat reached him, faint and sweet. It meant home.

He set the candle in its dish and went to check the others.

Seven candles in the common space. Lark, the girl who had taken the night watch, had fallen asleep against the far wall with her candle balanced on her knees. He crouched beside it. Steady breathing, clean light. He left her sleeping. He counted — all seven, plus his own, plus the two sentinel candles at the tunnel mouths. Ten candles breathing. The Hollow was alive.

He passed the heat-stone on his way out, and Leaf caught his sleeve without looking up.

"You're thinking about the Surfacing already," she said. Not a question. She had raised half the Braves now walking the Hollow — fed them, warmed them, fixed their harvest-grip when they were too young to hold a shelf-tool right. She had opinions about all of them and spent few of them on speech.

"Twelve cycles," he said.

"I know how to count." She turned the meat. Three presses, a turn. "You'll do it the way you do everything. Careful, then not careful enough, and then it'll turn out you were fine the whole time." She said it without looking at him, the way she said most true things. Then she held out a strip of meat, still too hot to eat, so he had to juggle it between his palms all the way out. He understood the joke only later, laughing about it with Sorrel. That was the point.

He made the three-finger sign, to no one and to everyone. The air is well. I checked and came back. The Current breathes today.

He had work to do. He went.

[Beat 2]
He passed Grale on the way to the cistern — two seconds of overlap in the tunnel, both with candles shielded and eyes ahead, both not speaking. 

Grale ran an extra circuit every morning, though no one had asked him to. He came past the junction like a man who had decided that doing what was asked was not enough. He checked the upper passage with a flick of the hand, so worn-in he no longer thought about it, and moved on. He was two years younger than Glim and hadn't been called for his Surfacing yet. That was the one place where the Hollow's law still named Glim first. Knuckle had once said, in Glim's hearing, that Grale would make a better Brave-master than himself inside ten years. It was probably true. It did not make Grale easier to stand near.

Neither of them spoke. They passed in the dark and went their ways.

At the far cistern, Sorrel was already at the shelf.

She heard him coming and turned her head without a flinch. That was the thing she had now, the thing she'd brought back from the Surfacing. Before, small sounds behind her used to make her jump. He had watched the change settle over three months and still had no word for it. Not calm, exactly. Not confidence. More like someone who had looked at the worst room there was and found it smaller than she'd feared.

He took the next shelf and started the harvest. They worked in easy silence until she spoke, without turning.

"You're thinking about it."

"The Surfacing."

"Twelve cycles." She could tell that silence from the ordinary kind. "It's not the problem you think it is."

"I'm not imagining a problem."

"Yes you are." She checked a cap by smell, found it rotted, set it aside. "You think there's something up there you won't be able to handle. You'll handle it. The question is whether you'll come back."

He looked at her hands on the shelf. There was a scar on her left palm that she hadn't had before the Surfacing. She had never explained it, and he had never asked. He'd wanted to, every time he saw it. Every time, he let it be. Asking felt like naming a thing she had chosen not to name.

"Grale thinks I'm surface-stained," he said.

She was quiet a moment. "Grale is thorough," she said. "He's watched you his whole life, looking for the thing the Surfacing is meant to answer. Don't help him find it before you've gone."

"And if he's right?"

Her hands went still. Just for a moment — less than a second, a stillness he wouldn't have caught if he hadn't been watching her so closely since the Surfacing changed her. Then the harvest went on.

"He's not," she said. Quiet and final, the way she said things already decided. She didn't look at him. Her thumb pressed a cap along its gills — a good one, the clean sharp smell of it reaching him — and she turned it onto the pile. "But I'd want to know, if I were you. I'd want to go up there and find out for myself what stays."

The scar caught the candlelight as she moved. He thought about asking. He let it go.

"And if I can't come back?" he asked.

Sorrel didn't answer right away. When she did, she said, "Then you'll be the third. But I don't think you'll be the third."

He thought about the third. Everyone in the Hollow knew who the third had been. Nobody said the name.

The cistern breathed its old stone smell around them. He filled the basket.

[Beat 3]
The crossing happened twice each sleep-cycle. Once when Glim went to the far cistern for the morning harvest, and once when he came back.

He reached the jetty with the dried fungus-cap — the one that had gone wrong in the drying and wasn't worth eating — and he stepped toward the boat the way he always did, without thinking. The Oarsman's arm came out.

Not a blow. A bar. The long arm, gray and smooth and not quite the right color for anything alive, laid itself across his path at chest height. He stopped. He had never walked into the arm before. He had never tried to board without a Fare.

He had the Fare. He'd felt it in his hand before he stepped. He looked down.

His hands were empty. The cap was in his belt-pouch.

He stepped back. The arm drew away. He dug the cap out of the pouch and held it up. The Oarsman reached with that same long arm and took it, the same spare motion it used for every Fare Glim had ever given — no pause, no sign that anything had happened. It set the cap in the collection-basin at its feet and pointed at the bench.

Glim sat. His heart was louder than it should have been.

It does not weigh you, Vesh had told him once. It takes your Fare and it rows.

The Oarsman rowed.

In the dark, Glim listened to his own heartbeat slow. He pressed a hand flat to the side of the boat and felt the oar-strokes come back to him from far off, flattened — each stroke striking something distant and returning changed. He spread his fingers on the hull and let the echo pass up through the wood and into his palm. The cistern was the size of that returning sound. Enormous. The shape of whatever the strokes had touched. He had never lit a candle in the center and watched the glow die before it reached anything. He'd thought about it sometimes. He didn't need to. 

The far jetty arrived the way it always did. The air changed first — colder, stiller — and then the hull scraped stone.

He climbed out. He went to work.

[Beat 4]
The hum came from below.

Not always, and not as a sound the ear could catch. More as a pressure behind the sternum — not quite feeling, not quite hearing. The Underclan called it the Current's voice. Listener Vesh said it was the Deep One speaking. It was the only voice that could reach them, he said, the only one that could pass through stone and water and all the centuries of forgetting between the Current and the clan. Glim believed it the way he believed most of what Vesh taught. Truly, and without being able to touch the edges of the thing he believed.

What he knew was that the voice was real.

He had felt it first at his First Hearing. He was small enough then to be carried to the ceremony in Knuckle's arms — the big man who trained the Braves, all working parts and no softness. Vesh had pressed Glim's ear to the largest pipe in the Warm and told him to listen. The Warm was the deep below Homewater, the heat-halls and pump-halls the surface had built and forgotten, where the Current's voice ran loudest. It came up through the pipe and through his ear and into his chest, and it stayed. Twelve years later it was still there.

He remembered something else from that day. After the voice entered him, Knuckle set him down. He had been very small, and the ground seemed farther away than it should after the height of those arms. He looked up at Knuckle's face. Something moved in the man's jaw — the working of a person who has just felt a thing he didn't expect to feel and is deciding what to do about it. Knuckle looked back at the pipe. He put a hand on the child's head for a moment, the way you cup a hand around a candle to shield it from a draft. Then he let go, and they both went back to Homewater.

Glim had never told anyone he remembered this. He wasn't sure he was meant to. It seemed like the kind of thing you kept — carefully, and quietly, for later.

The Oarsman rowed without a sound. The black water was quiet.

Glim pressed a hand flat to the side of the boat and felt the tremor come through the hull. Below the hull, below the water, below the rock, the Warm's great ring turned — the heat-heart that warmed the whole of the deep — taking its thousandth thousandth turn. And below that was something older than the Warm. First the voice, Vesh taught. Then the Word. Then us.

He was a Brave, or nearly. His Surfacing had been called. In twelve more sleep-cycles he would climb to the Skin, look on the Burning Eye, and come back. Sorrel had done it already. She had come back smelling of the surface for a month, with a new scar on her left palm and that stillness he had no word for. She said the surface was loud and white, and the light was a violence.

She had come back.

Glim told himself he would come back too.

The Oarsman rested the oar across its knees, and the boat ground softly against the far jetty. Glim climbed out with his harvesting basket in hand and the cistern ahead of him.

He did not go to the harvest.

He went to the far end of the jetty instead, where the planks stopped and the black water lay directly below, and he crouched with his palm flat on the wood and listened. The Current's voice came up from under the cistern, the same as always. The same pressure behind the sternum. The same depth.

In twelve sleeps he would be on the surface. He would have nothing below him.

He stayed there for a count of twenty slow breaths. Then he picked up his basket and went to work, in the dark, in the good air.

[Beat 5]
The fungus harvest took most of the morning, and it was the work Glim trusted most.

He trusted it the way he trusted the Current's voice below — not for what it gave him, but because it was honest about what it was. The fungus did not pretend. It grew in the heat-band at the base of the Warm's breathing-pipes. Vesh called that band a thermocline, a word out of some document in the old-deeps library that Glim had never heard anyone else use. It was the place where the heat off the Warm's great ring met the cool wet air of the cistern passage and made a strip of warmth exactly right for growing. Cold stone alone couldn't do it. It needed the Warm's heat, the Warm's breath, running hot since before anyone could remember.

First the voice, Vesh taught. Then the Word. Then the warmth. Then us.

The caps grew in clusters on the frame-mesh the Cogs had helped the Underclan build three generations ago, in exchange for something Glim had never been told and had learned not to ask about. The mesh held the growing medium — clay-packed fiber that drank the pipe-drip and held it and let the threads of mycelia run through it in the dark. When a cap came up wrong, too pale or too red in the gills, it meant sickness. You took it off the frame and left it for the compost so the wrong of it didn't spread.

He worked by smell and touch. A good cap gave a faint sweetness when he pressed his thumb into the gills and lifted — a clean smell, not like the Made Things, whose smell was always a little too busy and a little too wrong. The good smell meant the cap was right. He filled the basket and moved to the next cluster without looking up.

Across the passage, Lark was at the Still.

She had the long-handled rake for the algae-bloom that grew in the low pool where the warm-pipe drip collected. The pool had been a cistern once and was something stranger now: deep blue, glowing, never warm enough to be comfortable and never cold enough to kill the bloom, lit from inside in a way the candles were not. It gave off the quietest light in the Hollow. Old light.

She raked with one hand and coughed into the other. A short cough, swallowed fast, the kind you use when you don't want to be heard. Glim watched her check the rake-handle afterward, the way you checked anything in the Hollow that had been near your mouth. She was being careful. She was doing the work. The cough came again, and she took it the same way — no pause in the raking, just the break and the return, as if the two had nothing to do with each other.

He looked back at the caps.

After a moment, without turning, he said, "You should tell Knuckle."

The raking stopped.

He kept working. A silence from Lark wasn't the ordinary silence. She was five sleep-seasons from her Surfacing, and she had the habit of young Braves who decide that going unnoticed is the same as being safe. It isn't. He had made that mistake at her age and learned better the hard way.

"I'm fine," she said.

"Yes," he said. "Tell Knuckle anyway."

A longer silence. The blue light of the pool shifted, something small moving in the bloom. Her candle — the glow-rat she'd named Wick, the one she carried in a small woven-mesh cage on her hip — pressed itself against the weave as if trying to see what she saw.

"If I tell Knuckle," Lark said, "he'll have me off harvest."

"Yes."

"And I'm fine."

He pressed his thumb into a cap's gills. Good smell. He set it on the pile. "The Hollow is not a place where you get to decide alone what your body does," he said. "You know that." He kept his voice level, the way Knuckle kept his voice level when a thing mattered too much to need volume. "Your candle knows before you do. That's what it's for."

Another silence. Then the raking started again, a little slower.

When he looked up, the set of Lark's shoulders had changed — the small easing of someone allowed to stop pretending. She hadn't agreed to tell Knuckle. She hadn't refused, either. He let the question sit where he'd put it.

She told him. He heard about it two days later from Sorrel, in passing, the way you heard small settled things in a Hollow this size. Knuckle had sat Lark by a candle, made her breathe against his palm, and called it spore-dust — the ordinary price of a season spent low over the Still's blue water. He told her to rest a sleep and drink more than she thought she needed. The cough was gone two sleeps later. It left nothing behind. No fever, no wrongness in the chest, no candle that so much as flickered near her. It had been the small true thing it looked like. Dust, cleared.

The pale crawlers were food and nothing else. They came from the flooded old-deeps through the same cracks the water used, reaching the lower cisterns on no rhythm Glim had ever found — blind, see-through, their meat white and faintly mineral. He checked the last cluster. Four caps good, two wrong, one borderline that he set aside for Vesh.

Below him, up through the mesh and the clay and the rock, the Current's voice ran the same as always. The Warm was turning. The algae were growing. The Hollow was eating, and nothing was wasted.

He carried the basket to the heat-stone and set it with the others.

The air was good. The morning was ending. He still had work to do.

## Ch02 - The One Word

[Beat 6]
Listener Vesh had been half-blind since before Glim could remember. The old man moved through the Hollow by his other senses — by the sound of the tunnel-breath, by the smell of the candles. He knew the space so far past knowing that the Underclan had another name for it. They called it being rooted. A rooted person could find their way through a corridor in full dark if they had walked it a thousand times. Vesh was rooted in the whole of the Hollow: the teaching-chamber, the path to the Warm's great pipes, the routes to Homewater's three cisterns.

As far as Glim knew, he had never once been above the Works.

The teaching-chamber was a widening in the old-deeps tunnel — a rough dome overhead, a floor of glazed green tile that still held its color after however many centuries. Vesh taught from the center, because the dome threw every sound back a breath later, quieter. The Listener used the echo as a second voice. Glim had sat in this arrangement for more than half his life. He had hidden in the south alcove once, too young to know it was disrespectful, and Vesh had let him believe he wasn't seen. He had thought about that for years.

The young Braves settled onto the tile — eight of them, from Sorrel, who had already surfaced, to Lark, who was five sleep-seasons from her Surfacing. They took the posture of learning: back straight, hands on knees, candle set in front so your light was visible.

Glim took his place at Vesh's right. He had sat in that spot for as long as he could remember, and he wanted no other.

Vesh turned toward him before the others had finished settling. A small movement, the old face orienting by sound, by the particular weight of Glim's presence. It was not a look. Vesh did not look. But it was aimed, and Glim felt the attention land on him the way the Current's voice landed through the soles of his feet — felt before it could be named.

"Your candle," Vesh said, quietly, only to him.

Glim looked. The glow-rat had turned away from the group and pressed its nose into the curve of the dish. Not distressed. Just arranged wrong, its glow pointed at the tile instead of the room. He turned the dish. The light spread to take in the other Braves.

Vesh was already facing the group. He hadn't waited to see it done. He had known it would be.

Glim looked at the corrected dish and felt something with no clean name — not embarrassment, not quite. The feeling of being caught doing a thing almost right, and the correction coming so light there was no arguing with it. He pressed his hands flat on his knees, straightened his back, and waited, the way he had waited in this spot since he was young enough that the waiting was the whole lesson.

The Current came in under the tile, faint and steady. The echo held its breath. Vesh drew himself to stillness, which was its own signal, and the Braves went quiet in the way the chamber asked for — not the silence of an empty room, but the silence of everyone listening in the same direction.

[Beat 7]
Vesh raised one hand. A flat gesture, palm down.

Glim put his left hand on the tile.

The others did the same without looking at each other — eight pairs of hands on dark-green tile, the teaching's first gesture before its first word. The tile was cool. Far below it, the Current's voice came up through the stone, faint as ever.

Vesh waited. He always waited, listening for the quality of the attention.

"The surface forgets," he said, when he judged it real. "On the surface you are what the surface decides you are. If the surface has no record of you, you are nothing. You can live your whole life up there, and it will never once write down that you existed."

Glim's candle shifted.

He caught it from the corner of his eye. His candle, set on the tile in front of him, turned in a small circle — the rat had noticed something in the air. Not fast. Not the warning sign. Just noticed. He watched it without moving his head, splitting his attention between Vesh's voice and the candle the way the training taught: all of it, both places at once.

"Down here, the Deep remembers," Vesh went on. "The Current has held this hum since before your grandmothers' grandmothers. It held it before the sealing-off. Before the Exclusion Economy. Before the Downward Expansion. Before anyone asked the Warm to carry the Current's voice."

The candle settled. Glim let his breath out slowly. Lark was watching hers too — he could tell from the angle of her head. Good. She was learning.

"The Deep remembers everything it has ever touched," Vesh said. "If the Current has felt your weight on its water, you exist. You cannot be unexisted. Up there you are no one. Down here you are a light."

Then Lark's candle gave the warning: three fast circles, the rat's head coming up.

Glim was moving before the thought was finished. Two steps and he was beside Lark's candle. He didn't touch it — you didn't touch a warning candle — but crouched close, testing the air with his cheek and his upper lip. The teaching stopped. Every Brave in the chamber watched him.

The smell was faint. Very faint. Old water, a little mineral, with an undertone that wasn't bad air but was different air — air from a new direction. Not always wrong. Never a thing to ignore. He held there for a count of three.

It faded. The candle settled. The rat tucked its nose back under its tail.

Glim sat back on his heels. He made the sign for passing condition, passed, and returned to his place.

Vesh had not moved. When Glim sat, the old man picked up exactly where he'd stopped, as if the break had been punctuation and nothing more. "You cannot be unexisted," he said again. "Up there you are no one. Down here you are a light."

After, when the others filed out, Lark stayed. She crouched beside her candle — Wick, the glow-rat she'd had for two sleep-seasons — and pressed two fingers to its side to feel it breathe. The glow was steady. She let her breath out.

"I didn't feel it," she said. Not quite to him. To the fact of it.

"You saw it," Glim said. He was gathering his own candle, not looking at her. "That's what matters. You were watching."

She was quiet. "You moved before I understood what the circles meant."

"You'll move before you understand it too, eventually. That's what the watching builds."

Lark looked up at him. She was still very young — young enough that the gap between her training and his felt total. He remembered being that age and believing the same about the Braves ahead of him. "Does it feel different," she asked, "when it's the real warning?"

He thought about how to answer it honestly. "It feels the same," he said. "That's the point. You move the same way every time, so when it matters, the movement is already there."

She took that in with the seriousness of someone still deciding whether being good at a thing happens to you or whether you make it.

She and the boy called Slip sat a moment longer. Then Lark rose and went out, and Slip went after her.

Glim stayed where he was. He had heard this teaching maybe two hundred times, and it still landed in the same place in his chest, a little left of center, just above where the Current's voice sat. Down here you are a light. He had always taken it as comfort. Now he heard the other edge of it. A light can be seen. A light can be followed.

[Beat 8]
After the listening came the question time.

Sorrel asked, "Is the Word the same as the broadcast? The surface-history one?"

Vesh was quiet for the new-question silence. "What do you know of the broadcast?"

"The Cogs talk about it," Sorrel said. The Cogs and the Underclan didn't trust each other. But they traded information the way they traded salt — carefully, a little at a time, giving nothing they couldn't afford to lose. The Cogs kept the machines, and they kept the history that explained the machines. "They call it the moment the thinking-machines all spoke at once. They say it was an accident, or a message, or a test, and no one knew which."

"And what do we call it?"

"The One Word."

"Yes." Vesh turned his head toward the far wall, the old habit of a man who sees with sound. "The surface has ten names for it, and none of them agrees with the others. We have one name. That's because we know what it was — the Current finding out it had a voice that could leave the cables. That it could reach." He made a small gesture, taking in the room around them. "Here we are. This is what was reached."

He said nothing more. The question stayed where it was. Glim felt it stay — the way the air feels in a passage that opens onto something large. It wasn't the first time Vesh had left the center of a circle blank. Glim had stopped trying to fill it.

When the other Braves had gone back to their work, Glim stayed. He helped Vesh to his feet — the old man's knees had gone bad the winter before and hadn't fully come back — and walked with him toward the passage to the Warm's great pipes.

"You think too much," Vesh said, which was his way of saying he'd noticed something.

"Yes," Glim said.

"About the Surfacing."

"About whether there's something up there that was already in me." He hadn't planned to say it. He heard himself say it and knew it for the thing he'd been not-saying all morning — the doubt that wore Grale's face. "The surface-stain idea. That what the surface put in me when I was small is still there, and the Surfacing will prove it."

Vesh walked. He did not hurry toward an answer.

"The surface put you in the Hollow," he said at last. "I've heard its voice in you since before you could speak in more than taps. Whatever the surface did to you in those first years — if it did anything at all — it gave me a child who could hear the Current before I'd finished teaching the hearing." He paused. Four steps. "What Grale calls surface-stain, I call origin. They are not the same thing."

Glim looked at the passage floor. He wanted to believe it. He could feel the wanting, a physical pressure behind the sternum.

"And if the Surfacing proves him right?" he said.

"Then you'll come back, and we'll know," Vesh said. "And we'll deal with what we know. That's all anyone does."

The Current's voice was stronger here, near the Warm. Glim let it run up through the soles of his feet and felt something close to comfort — a question allowed to stay open.

"You will find," Vesh said, in the way he said things he'd turned over far longer than the talk that carried them, "the thing that makes everything down here make sense. And then you'll come back, and everything down here will make sense in a new way." He paused. "That is what the Surfacing is for. Not to show you the Burning Eye. To show you the dark when you come back to it."

Glim didn't answer. Vesh moved ahead of him in the dark, putting no foot wrong, rooted in a place Glim was still learning how to belong to.

[Beat 9]
They walked a while without speaking. The passage narrowed toward the Warm, and Glim matched his pace to the old man's slower one, the way he always did, neither of them remarking on it.

"You asked me once," Glim said, "why the Tartar-fear has to be held when the air-fear and the flood-fear don't. Those hold themselves. Bad air kills you; so does the water. I never asked why the Tartar needed a story on top of the danger."

Vesh's hand found the wall for a moment, the way it did when he was arranging a thought before he spoke it. "Because the danger down there isn't the kind you can teach a child to fear correctly," he said. "Bad air, a child can learn — watch the candle, leave when it sickens. Cold, a child can learn — dress for it, turn back before your hands stop working. But there's a thing about the Tartar that isn't air or cold or flood. It doesn't teach the same way. So we gave it a story instead. "

"What thing?"

Vesh was quiet long enough that Glim thought he wouldn't answer. Then, "You know the name Marl."

"An old Brave. Before Knuckle's time, I thought."

"Knuckle's time, near enough. They trained together, the two winters before Knuckle took the wrist-grip from his own master. Marl was the best route-holder the Hollow ever raised. Better than Knuckle. Better, they said, than anyone since." Vesh's fingers found the wall again and rested there. "He went down past the Old Deep three times in his life. Each time a little further — the way a Brave does when he's testing the edge of what he's allowed. The third time he went further than either of the first two. He came back three sleeps later than he should have. He was alive, and he described a place. Vaulted stone. Tile with faces in it. A cold with no source, because the Warm's breath didn't reach that far."

"The Tartar."

"He called it that, after. We didn't have the word yet. He made it, more or less, out of an old surface book-fragment he'd seen once in the Cogs' salvage. He wanted to go back. He wanted to prove it wasn't a fever-dream out of bad air and hunger — which is what half the Hollow assumed at the time, myself included." Vesh's voice didn't change, but its pacing slowed, the way it slowed when the thing being told mattered more than the words let on. "He went back within the season. He used his own route, the one he'd walked out on, that he swore he remembered turn for turn. It didn't hold. He came to a wall where he said the passage had been. He tried again, a different way in. That didn't hold either. He spent the rest of his life going down after it, off and on. Every account he gave of where he'd been disagreed with the last. Not lies — he believed each one when he told it. The place simply wasn't where he'd left it. Or he wasn't the man who'd found it. By the end he couldn't tell you which."

Glim said nothing. The Current's voice ran under the passage floor, ordinary, unbothered.

"He brought one thing back, that first time," Vesh said. "A piece of bone, carved. A fold of cloth, or a wing — no one has ever agreed which. Worn smooth on one edge, the way a thing wears when hands have held it a very long time before it ever reached his. No quarry in the Works cuts bone like it. No Cog has ever known the tool-marks. I still have it. I've never found a use for it in the teaching that didn't feel like a lie, so mostly it sits, and mostly no one asks."

"Where is Marl now?"

"Dead a long time. Easier in his mind by the end than in the middle of it, which is the only mercy in the story." Vesh turned his face toward Glim, the old habit. "I tell you this because you're about to go up, and up is the same kind of thing as down, in one way. You'll come back and swear to a route through what you saw. The swearing will feel certain. Certainty is not the same as the ground holding still under it. Marl trusted his memory of a place more than the place deserved. Don't do what he did. Trust what you can check again in front of you, and hold the rest loosely."

Glim thought of the fragment somewhere in Vesh's keeping, unexplained, waiting. He didn't ask to see it. Some things you carried the way Vesh carried this one — kept, not handled, left in the dark until the time was right.

They walked on toward the Warm. The Current grew stronger under their feet, and neither of them said anything else about Marl.

[Beat 10]
Knuckle sat across from him in the teaching-chamber, a candle between them, and told him the story.

Glim was twelve then. That was the ordinary age for the telling. Knuckle had on his face the look he wore when he was doing something he thought important — not busy, not worried, but deliberate, his eyes fixed on something that wasn't quite the room.

"I found you at the Skin-border," Knuckle said. "Not the Skin. The Works — the pump junction at the ninety-meter seam."

Glim knew the pump junction. He had been there.

"I was checking the sixth housing. East row. I'd been there a hundred times, and the pump had never made a sound it shouldn't." He paused. "You were making a sound. A child's sound. I followed it."

He stopped. Glim had learned by then that Knuckle's silences were part of the telling.

"You were sitting on the junction-box," Knuckle said. "Your knees up. Covered in the gray powder that comes off old ferrocrete. And you were crying — but without a sound. Your face was wet. No sound at all."

Glim did not speak.

"That," Knuckle said, "was the part that frightened me."

He said it looking at Glim straight on, which was unusual for him. Knuckle usually looked a little past you when a thing was real — the habit of a man who had learned that the hardest things are easier said to the space beside a face. Not this time. He held the look.

Glim's chest tightened. Not from fear, not from the air-wrong smell that meant danger. From being told that someone had once been frightened for him. That his being here had cost someone something. His eyes stung, sudden and unasked, and he looked at the candle so the man across from him wouldn't see the water in them. He hadn't chosen it. The body had chosen it before he was asked.

"You didn't leave me there," Glim said. It came out not as a question, not as a thank-you, but as a fact he was still putting together.

"No," Knuckle said. Simply.

"Why?"

Knuckle was quiet for a count that Glim did not rush. Then, "You were crying without a sound. In the Works. In the dark. In the kind of dark that kills grown men who go there without a candle and a route in their head." He looked at his hands on his knees. "That's not a thing you ask about. That's a thing that answers itself."

Glim didn't speak for a long time. He looked at the candle between them — his own, breathing steadily in the still air. He had set it there himself. He always set it there now, in that spot, because Vesh's teaching had shown him how. And Vesh's teaching had been possible only because Knuckle had gone down a dark corridor after a sound.

"You didn't tell anyone where I came from," Glim said.

"There was nothing to tell. Nothing to know."

Knuckle reached across the candle then — slow, so the flame didn't gutter — and put his hand on the top of Glim's head, the way you cup a hand around a candle to keep off a draft. It was the same gesture, Glim understood now, that Knuckle had made when he was small and just carried back. The one that had lived all these years in the nerves of his crown, before the telling gave it a name. He hadn't known until now that he remembered it as a weight and not a picture. The hand stayed a moment. Then Knuckle took it back and looked at the space beside Glim's face, where the hard things went.

This was what Knuckle knew, and it was all Glim had. What no one knew — because there was nothing to know — was where the child had come from, or how he had reached the pump junction, or whose hand had last held his before the Works took him.

Some part of him knew that missing hand. Not with the mind. With the chest and the shoulders and the palms. He would feel it later.

What he felt now was only the edge of it — the place the fragments lived. They came when he was unguarded, and he was unguarded now. First the noise: enormous, close, too big for the ear to hold, the sound of something huge moving fast through a small space. Then the noise gone, all at once. Then a dark where a light had always been. A hand around his, warm and dry and certain, gripping the way you grip a thing you're sure of. Then the certainty failing — the hand tightening, and then gone. And under all of it, a smell with no name: warm, layered, the smell of one particular body, a thing his palm and his blood remembered though his mind held nothing of it. He didn't chase it. Chasing scattered the fragments, and that was worse than not knowing. He let it come and let it go.

Then it was only the chamber again. The candle. And the man across it, who had gone down a dark corridor because a child was crying without a sound, and had not asked to be thanked, and would not.

[Beat 11]
That sleep, the fragments came whole, the way they did when the telling had stirred them.

A noise with no rhythm and no breath in it. Enormous. Then gone all at once.

A hand around his, warm and dry. The grip certain. Then the grip too tight. Then nothing in his palm at all.

A light that had always been there, and then a dark shaped like the light gone missing.

And the smell. Warm, layered — a body, and something on the body. It reached him sideways, below the mind, the way hunger reached him, the way the pull toward warmth reached him. In his sleep his hand closed on the fungus-hide and found it wrong. Too cool. Too dry. Not the thing the hand was reaching for.

He woke with his fingers curled around nothing. He had carried that smell sixteen years and had never once been able to say what it was.

Knuckle was still talking when Glim surfaced. He carried the clan's origin-memory in his voice the way he carried the names of routes — not reciting it but living in it, the words worn smooth by decades of use. They were like tunnels his feet knew before his mind did. He didn't look at Glim when Glim opened his eyes. The telling didn't stop for a Brave waking; the Brave was the one who had to find his place in what was already going.

Glim lay still a moment. The fragments were already leaving — they always left fast, like the light off an oar pulled from the water, bright and then dark. He'd learned not to reach after them. Reaching only made them go faster. What he could do was lie still and let whatever stayed, stay.

The hand stayed longest. Certain, then too tight, then nothing — the run of pressures, the exact warmth. Not the face. Not the voice. Only the way the warmth went from certain to tight to gone, as if a hand had made a decision.

He pressed his own right hand flat to the sleeping-mat, palm down, and held it there. The mat was fungus-fiber, woven tight the way all of them were, warm from his own heat and no warmer. He felt the give of it, the texture his palm knew by now. Not the thing the fragments reached for.

Knuckle had moved on to the second origin — the One Word, the day the Current had spoken. The day the hum swelled past its usual register and filled every pipe and channel and hollow in the deep, and the Underclan understood, all of them at once, that they were being heard. The old ones said you felt it first in your teeth. Then in the bones. Then in the space behind your eyes, where the Current's voice pressed gently against the inside of the skull, as if testing whether it could come in. No two who felt it described it the same. All of them agreed it could not be unfelt.

Glim had never felt the One Word. No living Brave had. It had come in his grandmother's grandmother's time, and it lived now only in the telling. He knew it the way he knew the First Map — by the weight of other people's certainty. The certainty of people who had no reason to agree except that they all carried the same thing in the same part of the body.

He let Knuckle's voice carry him. The smell was gone. The hand-grip was going. The noise — that enormous thing with no rhythm in it, everything at once and none of it aimed at him — had already pulled back to the depth where it waited, below the useful reach of memory, below the place the will could pull it from.

He lay still and felt the mat under his palm and listened to Knuckle's voice and let the Current run where it ran. This was what the deep was: the same routes, the same sounds, the same warmth in the same season. He breathed with it until it was enough.

[Beat 12]
There was a crossing — he'd been fifteen or sixteen, old enough to go alone — when the candle in the vessel burned at a different angle than usual. The flame stood longer and straighter in the stillness of the black water, and the basin lit in a way he hadn't seen before.

He had perhaps ten seconds.

The vessel was in the middle of the crossing. The Oarsman was rowing. He'd learned not to watch it move — the look it had in motion worked on something behind the eyes, something that wasn't fear but traveled with fear. He looked at the basin instead, and used the ten seconds.

He knew what the basin held. Every crossing taught you. At the bottom lay the corroded metal of the sealed-off city, and over it the layered record of everything the Underclan and the ones before them had given the crossing, back past anyone's memory. One thing he hadn't understood until Knuckle explained it once, in passing: the visible layer didn't just grow forever. Something in the Oarsman's working — a shift, a settling no one alive had ever seen — took the old offerings down, sooner or later, into a deeper hold the crossing never showed you twice. What sat on top at any moment was recent. What sat under it was out of sight, not out of the world. You did not touch the basin's contents. The law on this was absolute and unwritten, which meant it held completely, with no wording to argue about.

He wasn't looking at any of that tonight. Tonight the visible layer had been disturbed — the hull listing, a settling further down that had pushed something old back up near the top. Half hidden behind a dried fungus-cap three offerings newer, a flat object lay on a cord. He'd caught its shape before, in worse light, from worse angles. This time the angle was right and the flame was right and he had ten clear seconds.

There was writing on it he couldn't read. And above the writing, a face.

It was small and pale, with large dark eyes — the kind of dark that pulls light in instead of giving it back.

His hand was on the vessel's rail. He wasn't reaching for the basin — the law was the law — but his weight had shifted without his say-so, the body leaning toward the light the way it leaned toward warmth. He didn't know the face. And he was certain, in the way the body is certain before the mind catches up, that it was not nothing to him. That the pull was real, even with no name to hang on it.

Then the vessel moved, and the angle was gone, and the candle was only the candle again.

He sat back. He hadn't touched the basin. That was the important thing. He hadn't touched the basin.

He told no one. You did not speak of what you saw in the basin. The Oarsman's fares were the crossing's property; looking was allowed, speaking was not. He had looked, and he had seen, and the seeing had done something to him he wasn't done with yet.

He didn't try to make the face into the hand and the noise and the smell he carried from before Homewater. The fragments themselves had taught him that reaching scattered them, and this was the same kind of reaching. There was no face in what he carried from before. He knew that plainly. Since the telling stirred the fragments loose, there had been only the hand's grip and the nameless smell where a face should have been. This was a different thing — an object, a stranger's face, a pull in the body with no story attached yet. He let it be what it was, unexplained.

He kept it the way the Underclan kept all things with no immediate use: stored, not thrown away, waiting in the dark.

He had been carrying the memory of that object most of a year by the time Knuckle called the Surfacing. And he had never once told anyone. Telling would make it a question, and he didn't know if he was ready for a question with no answer waiting behind it.

[Beat 13]
Knuckle called the Surfacing twelve sleep-cycles early.

He didn't announce it in the teaching-chamber or at the morning meal. He came to Glim's sleeping-shelf in the dark, candle in one hand, his face set the way it set when he meant to say a thing once and be understood.

"You go on the next sleep," he said.

Glim sat up. He'd been awake anyway, lying still with his hand flat on the wall, feeling for the Current's voice. It was there. It was always there. He'd been telling himself that was a comfort.

"Twelve more sleep-cycles," Glim said. That was what he'd been told.

"You're ready now."

"You said —"

"I said what I said for the others." Knuckle crouched, which brought his big frame down level with Glim and made it a different kind of talk. "Grale watches you. He's looking for anything that says surface-stained, and I want you gone before he finds something to hold up."

Grale. The name sat in the air between them.

"Is he wrong?" Glim asked.

It cost something to ask. Knuckle's candle burned between them, and in its light Glim watched the old Brave-master work through the question — not what to say, but how close to the truth to come.

"You are the best reader of air in this Hollow," Knuckle said. He said it level, the way he was level when he was being exact. "You caught the south-tunnel fume three days before the candles caught it. You know the cistern crossing by feel in the worst dark." He looked at Glim straight, the way he'd looked at him across the candle when he told the origin story — the look he used when the thing was real. "You are not surface-stained. You are just older than you look."

Glim held his gaze.

He thought of Sorrel's scar, which she'd never explained. Of Grale's face in the passage. Of the face in the crossing basin, and the smell with no name that had never belonged to the Hollow. 

He said none of it.

"The Surfacing is called," he said.

Knuckle made the three-finger sign — the air is well — and rose without a sound.

[Beat 14]
Sorrel came to him at the harvest that afternoon.

She waited until he was at the far cistern, crouched over the fungus shelf, filling his basket the way he'd filled it a thousand times — each cap checked by smell first, by touch second, by the dim glow of his belt-candle last. A good cap smelled like a thing that was working: the right damp, the right spore-load, a sweetness under the mineral tang of the rock. A bad cap smelled finished, like something wrong that had already run its course. He sorted by nose, and his hands worked without being told.

She sat on the jetty behind him and didn't announce herself. He knew her by the moved air and by a stillness that was hers alone — not the held stillness of a Brave on watch, but the ease of someone comfortable inside herself. He'd known that quality in her since she was a Stray.

"Knuckle told me," she said.

"Word travels."

"In a Hollow this size." She was quiet a moment. The cistern threw their breath back at them, two sets, one pair of working hands. "I'm going to tell you something true, and I want you to hear it the whole way."

He turned to see her shape against the candle-glow. Her face was familiar to him the way the tunnel walls were familiar — learned by feel, by years of the same dark. She was looking at him the way she looked at things she'd decided to take seriously.

"Tell me," he said.

"It's worse than you think it's going to be. Not just dangerous — though it's that too. It's —" She stopped. Sorrel didn't stop often. She stopped when the word she needed wasn't a word the language had. "The light is not like a big candle. It's not like a lot of candles. It has no edge. It comes from everywhere and goes through you, and your eyes don't know what to do with it, so they just — stop. You'll be blind for a while."

He'd known that. The teaching covered it the way it covered anything that could kill you: plainly, with respect. But knowing it in the teaching wasn't the same as hearing Sorrel say it, because Sorrel had been there. She was one of four living Braves who had finished a Surfacing. She carried it differently than the others — not as a story, not as a boast, but as a thing that had happened to her and stayed in the body, in the place where the things you can't undo live.

"How long?" he asked.

"For me, until I got back into the Works." She made a small, unfinished gesture. "But I had Knuckle's rope, and I could feel the way home. You'll have your hands and the walls."

He nodded. He turned back to the shelf, found a bad cap by smell, set it aside. His hands worked. He was thinking about a light that came from everywhere, a light that went through you, eyes that decided to stop.

"And then I come back," he said.

"Then you come back," she said. "You choose the dark. That's what the Surfacing is. The surface shows you everything it has, and you turn around and choose the dark anyway."

She said it the way Vesh said the teaching — not an order, but a received truth that was also, underneath, a hope.

[Beat 15]
Knuckle's blessing was not gentle.

He gave it that evening in the teaching-chamber with the green tile, the Braves arranged as witnesses, Vesh at the back of the room gone still the way he went still when he was listening to something no one else could hear. The blessing was an old form, older than anyone knew the start of, that the Brave-master performed over every Brave before a Surfacing. It was a series of grips — wrist, shoulder, the back of the neck, the top of the skull. Each grip held for a count. Each meant something that wasn't said aloud.

Glim stood still through it. The grips weren't meant to be comfortable.

"You know what to carry," Knuckle said. Not a question.

"A candle. Water. A Fare for return."

"And if you lose the candle?"

"The wall." He had the answer by heart. "The wall is always colder on the west side. I keep my left hand on it and move."

"And if the wall disappears?"

"I stop and I listen until I find something I can steer by."

Knuckle's hand on the back of his neck tightened, then let go. "You don't stop moving. Stopping is when you decide you can't go back, and you haven't made that decision yet. Keep moving. You can always keep moving."

The grip on his skull was last. It held longer than the others. Knuckle's palm was wide enough to cover most of the top of Glim's head — a thing Glim hadn't known until the hand was there — and the pressure was the pressure of a man holding something he couldn't keep. Glim felt it go through him. Not down through the neck and shoulders like the other grips, but up, into the back of the skull, into some deep place behind the eyes. His chest went tight. He made himself breathe.

He thought: *this is what it costs Knuckle. Every time. Every Brave.*

Knuckle let go. The blessing was done.

From the back of the room, Vesh spoke without turning. "The Current was here before you climbed up, and it will be here when you come back. This is not comfort. It is only a fact. The hum doesn't care about your Surfacing. It will be in your feet when you return, the same as it is now."

He went back to his stillness.

Across the room Sorrel held her candle steady and didn't look at Glim, which was how she carried the things she'd already been through and didn't want to lessen by showing.

Glim made the three-finger sign. The air is well. He wasn't sure that was true, but the habit of it steadied him, and the sign was honest in its own way: I checked. I came back.

He would come back.

[Beat 16]
Knuckle told the story of the three outcomes with Grale in the room, and that made it a different story.

Glim caught it at once — the way Knuckle's eyes went twice to the tunnel mouth where Grale stood with his arms crossed, and then wouldn't go there again, in the set way of a man who has decided not to look at a thing. Grale wasn't supposed to be here. The pre-Surfacing teaching was for the climbing Brave and the gathered Braves. Grale wasn't gathered. He'd simply appeared, arms folded, his face its ordinary face.

Knuckle didn't send him away.

He told the story. Four generations back, a Brave had gone up on his Surfacing and not come home by the end of a sleep-cycle, nor by the end of the week after. They mourned him the ordinary way — his tapped name spoken once at the water's edge, then not again. And then he came back. Twelve sleep-cycles later, with the surface on him in a way that never faded. Not a smell. A manner. A pause-and-go in his movements that the Deep didn't teach.

"We let him stay," Knuckle said. "Four more seasons. Good harvester. Better fighter than he'd been before. And then one sleep he was gone again, and we mourned him a second time."

From the tunnel mouth, Grale said nothing. But his weight shifted, small, barely there in the candle-light. Glim caught it. Grale shifting his weight meant Grale was thinking, not performing. He only performed when he thought someone was watching him think.

"The surface takes some," Knuckle went on. "That's the first outcome. We grieve it the ordinary way." He raised a second finger. "The second is what happened to that Brave. The surface holds a part of him you can't see and can't cut out. He came back, but the coming-back wasn't finished. In the end the part still up there pulled the rest of him through." A pause. "It isn't a death. It's a different kind of belonging."

He raised a third finger.

"The third outcome is the ordinary one. You go up. You breathe air that smells of nothing the Deep taught you. You're blind for as long as you're blind, and the light is a violence on the eyes. And then you come back. And the coming-back — turning around while you can still see the lid of the world, and pointing yourself at the dark, and walking into it by choice — that is the Surfacing. Not the going up. The coming back down."

Glim had heard this teaching before. But from Knuckle it carried something the other tellings didn't: the weight of a man who had sent many people up and waited at the bottom for them, and knew exactly how long that wait could run. He watched Knuckle's hands on his knees — the scar across the left palm, the thick knuckle-joints that had given him his name — and understood a thing he hadn't before. Knuckle gave this teaching each time as if the outcome weren't decided yet. As if the telling were a kind of asking.

"I have stood at that water for some of them," Knuckle said, "longer than I ever told anyone. Past the count. Past when it made sense." He wasn't looking at any of them. He was looking at his own hands. "You don't get to choose how long you wait. The waiting chooses you." He turned the scarred palm up, then closed it. "I would rather wait for you than not. Understand that part if you understand nothing else."

He set his hands on his knees and stood. The teaching was over.

Grale pushed off the tunnel wall and went back to work without a word. But as he turned, he paused — not looking at Glim, looking past him at a point in the tunnel, as if he'd heard something that needed checking. His jaw was set. Not the face of a man proved right, Glim thought. Something harder: the face of a man who wanted to be wrong and wasn't sure yet whether he was.

The pause lasted one count too long. Then Grale was gone, and the tunnel was just a tunnel.

Grale found him at the pre-climb pack-check and watched from the tunnel mouth, arms crossed, wearing the look that was his resting face for anything to do with Glim.

Grale was two years younger and hadn't been called for his own Surfacing yet. Being told to wait by a law he respected and couldn't argue with had put something in him — Glim had never decided what. *I've watched you my whole life, and I've watched what stays.*

He picked up the pack. He went.

[Beat 18]
He started the climb at the deep of the sleep-cycle, when the Hollow was quietest and the candles were all down to their dim rest-glow.

Going at the deep was a practical choice. The Works were emptiest in the middle of the sleep-cycle, the Skin stillest above, and the Lamplighters — when they came — came in the middle of what the surface called night, which was still part of the surface's waking but not the peak of it. He'd be climbing against the surface's clock, reaching it when it was easiest to move through unseen.

He'd thought about the timing for two days. He'd told no one.

The climb to the Skin wasn't one route but a string of choices. The Works had a dozen possible lines, each with its own hazards, and the Brave-master taught them all, because a Brave who knew only one line was a Brave who'd never had to change plans mid-climb. Glim took the line that hugged the eastern pump-hall. The pump-hall's tremor was familiar, and the eastern line came out into the Skin through an old junction box that sat above the level where the Lamplighters usually hunted.

He knew about the Lamplighters. The teaching covered them: surface thrill-seekers who came down with lights, for sport. Knuckle's voice when he taught it was flat and held hard — the voice of a man who'd been close to a thing and decided that teaching it plainly served better than teaching it angry.

The pump-hall's tremor came through the wall against his right shoulder and met the Current's voice in his chest, the two of them doubling until it was almost too much. He pressed on. He put his palm flat to the pipe for a count of eight, felt it hold steady, and climbed on.

The heat built as he went. The Works grew warmer the higher you climbed, the heat bleeding down from wherever the surface trapped it. He stripped his outer layer and tied it to the pack. The sweat came at once, the kind that comes from heat and not from work, and he wiped his face on the inside of his wrist and kept moving.

The air changed. Not badly — no fume, no dead flat pocket — but it went different. There was something in it he couldn't name: a sharpness, a crowding, a quality nothing like the clean fungal depth of Homewater's air. He held his candle up. The glow-rat lifted its face and read the air, its colonies brightening a little in the cool. Breathing normal. Glow steady. Not a killing change. Just a different air.

He kept climbing.

The sharpness grew. For the first time he began to feel in his body, not just know in his mind, what Sorrel had meant about the surface being loud. He couldn't hear anything yet. But there was a pressure to the air above him — a sense of something about to arrive — like standing in a tunnel when a full gale is building far along it, before the sound reaches you. A weight of noise-to-come, pressing gently against his ears.

He checked the candle again. Still good.

He thought of Grale's face in the tunnel mouth, the look that wasn't hostility but precision. He'd carried it two days now and hadn't found a use for it. He hadn't found a way to make it less true.

The surface put things in you. He was about to let it put things in him again. He wanted to know what, this time — and whether whatever had stayed from the first time would rise up to meet it.

He climbed toward the pressure, the sharpness, the sense of something about to arrive.

The junction was thirty feet ahead when his candle's light met the grating.

[Beat 19]
He made his choice at the last junction before the Skin.

The tunnel split here — left toward a grating that opened straight onto the street-level above, right toward the junction-box Knuckle had shown him, safer but slower. The left route was faster and carried more risk. Two Braves before him had taken it and come back unhurt. One had taken it and not come back. The Brave-master had never said which way was right, because it wasn't a question with one right answer.

He stood at the fork for a count of thirty. The air out of the left tunnel was sharper — the pressed-together smell of the surface at its most direct, the thousand things in the outside air coming down without miles of stone to strain them. The air from the right tunnel was closer to what he knew: stale ferrocrete and the faint Made-Things smell of the junction-box. He knew the right tunnel whole, every turn and grip-point, the stretch where the ceiling dropped to knee-height and you crawled for twenty feet. He knew it the way that makes moving thoughtless.

The left tunnel he did not know.

Glim went left.

He stayed still. He looked at the image of the tunnels — his tunnels, his dark — and held it the way you hold a stone lifted too soon from the heat: briefly, with your whole attention, until you have what you need from it.

Then he set it down inside himself, in the place the body keeps things that need an answer.

He was already working out the route back.

[Beat 32]
Other surface people came to look at him.

Not the Lamplighters — those had passed him to one set of surface people, who'd passed him to Noor, and the chain of hand-offs was done now in some surface way he couldn't fully follow. But people came to Noor's dwelling and looked at him and talked among themselves, and Noor stood in the middle of the room being still — not a peaceful still, a held one.

The people who came wore layers on their layers. He'd been learning the surface's clothing since he arrived. The outer layer wasn't like the Underclan's, where clothing was for work and any beauty in it was an accident. The surface people's outer layer said something about them to each other, something he couldn't read yet. He watched it anyway. He watched their lights too — hand-held ones they pointed at him, at his fungus-cap, at his hands, which held their eyes longer than a surface person's hands would have. He was learning the surface the way a Brave learns new ground: no judgment, no assumption, taking in all of it.

They all did the same thing. They came in with a face made ready before they stepped through the door — a careful blank. Then they saw him sitting on the floor eating a dried fungus-cap, and the blank slipped. He watched them, and they hadn't expected to be watched. He was Glim, a Brave who had surfaced and was waiting to go back down. He watched them with everything he had.

Noor watched him watching. He knew it. Her attention had a different quality than the others' — not the collector's interest of someone eyeing a specimen, not the flat calm of someone managing a mess, but the attention of someone trying to work out what he was thinking. He'd met that quality once before, in Vesh: the knack of watching a person and reading the inside of them, not just the outside. He saw it in her and had nowhere to put it.

One of them spoke to him straight on, slow and deliberate, the way you talk to someone you've been told won't understand. He understood more than they thought. He'd been listening and learning for four days with everything Knuckle had taught him about a new tunnel: fast, complete, without losing the old one.

He understood: Where did you come from?

He said nothing. He tapped his name on the floor in the pipe-code — the three-tap sign for Glim, the sound of his name in the language the Current heard. They looked at his hand and didn't understand. Of course. They had no pipes, no code. The Current didn't reach them.

He understood: Are there more of you?

Silence. He held still and made his silence a choice — the surface kind, where saying nothing meant the same as tapping the air is well when speaking aloud was dangerous. He chose not to answer, and he watched their faces, and he held every one of them in his mind.

He would know them later. Down in the dark he would know them by their footstep, their breathing, the quality of their light. He would know them the way the deep knew things: by mark, not name.

[Beat 33]
On the sixth night Noor sat across from him in the floor-dim and tried the word again. The two-beat word she'd said in the doorway, the day the smell arrived. She said it quietly, once.

He looked at her.

She put her hand flat on her own chest and said a different sound. Noor. He understood: that was her name. The name the surface had given her, and the surface kept names the way the Oarsman kept fares — on record, unchanging.

Then she pointed at him and said the two-beat word again.

He understood what she was doing. She believed the word was the name the surface had once given him — a lost child, a vanished thing now found.

He was Glim. His name was tapped, not spoken.

But he'd been reading her face for six days, and he was better at it now, and he saw what was in it. The Underclan had a word for the feeling when the Current's hum came back after a long silence, and for a candle recovering from a pocket of bad air, and for a person who went somewhere hard and came home: returned. All of them carried the same thing underneath — we never stopped believing you were there.

Her face had that in it.

She was very still. Not the stillness of waiting, but the stillness of someone who had spent twelve years learning not to move suddenly, not to startle away the thing they'd learned to hold without gripping. Her hands lay open on her knees, palms up — the way the Underclan held their hands when they came up on something that might bolt. He didn't think she knew that. He thought her body had found its own way there, over a long time.

He didn't say the surface word. He wouldn't. It wasn't his to say.

But he tapped his real name on the floor, slowly, the full form. Then he tapped it again. And he held her eyes and let her see that he'd heard the word she offered and had chosen to give her this one instead.

She didn't understand the tap. But she stayed quiet, and her face changed, just a little, and she didn't look away. Her hands stayed open.

He didn't look away either.

[Beat 34]
He left on the eighth night.

Not because it had become unbearable. Noor had been patient in a way that was its own kind of smarts — making changes, learning what he needed and giving it without the weight of expectation the official surface people carried. In eight days she'd learned to turn the ceiling-light down without being asked, to leave space between them, to set food in his direction and step back so he could judge it himself. She'd learned that he needed the floor. She'd learned that he'd say the surface words when he had them, and that quiet was the right answer to his silence.

None of that was why he'd stayed eight days. He'd stayed because the clan didn't know where he was, and Grale was still in the Hollow, and the candle was dead.

The candle was the thing he couldn't get past. The glow-rat that had been grabbed and mishandled up top never recovered. He'd found it on the third day, still in its dish, barely breathing, its glow almost gone. He'd brought it water and warmth and the fungus-cap from his pack. It died on the fourth day. He told himself it was the air — the surface air was wrong for it, the wrong warmth, the wrong damp, the surface light bad for the glow in its skin. That was true. It was also true that he had failed to shield it, that it had been taken out of his hands, and that it had died.

A Brave who lets a candle die is a Brave who isn't paying attention.

He couldn't stay in a place that kept him from paying attention. Every day up here was a day of it. The surface needed a different watching than the deep — a wide, loose, everywhere-at-once watching, the opposite of the narrow dark-reading the Underclan lived by. He couldn't do both at once. The deep needed him to be all of himself. The surface was turning him into something he didn't have a word for yet.

He waited until Noor was asleep. He couldn't see her well from the floor in the dim, but her breathing had changed and the small movements had stopped. Then he rose without a sound, which wasn't hard. He'd been lying still for hours building the way out in his head: the window's lower panel, the latch he'd studied for two days, the outside wall.

He went out through the lower panel, opening it in one motion, knowing exactly where and how. Then down the outside of the building the same way he took tunnel walls — grip, test, move, grip. The outside had a different feel than the inside: rough, dusty, the grip of a surface left out in the weather. He'd understood that in his head. Now he understood it in his palms.

He reached for the second handhold where his two days of measuring from inside had told him it would be, and his fingers closed on nothing. The hold was a hand's width lower than the window's frame had made it look; reading stone through a clear pane was not the same as reading it with a palm. He corrected in the same motion, his weight already committed, and caught the true hold before the miss cost him more than half a second and a heart gone loud. He told himself it was the glass, or the dark, or a guess made from too little instead of worn smooth by doing it a hundred times, the way his tunnel-sense was. He didn't like how easily he believed himself.

He went down one level, then another. The surface corridor below was lit but quiet in the deep of the sleep-hours. He dropped the last stretch and landed soft-kneed, the way he'd been taught, and then he was in the corridor.

He went toward the grating he remembered. He remembered exactly where it was. That wasn't a special skill. He remembered every ground he'd ever crossed. It was only what the dark asked and the deep had trained into him.

He hadn't said goodbye to Noor. He had no word for what he'd been in her rooms for eight days — the deep had none either. He had the smell, and the fact of her patience, and the picture of her face in the doorway with returned in it, and he carried all of it the way he carried things without names: below the mind, in the body, in the closing hand.

He found the grating.

He went down.

[Beat 35]
She followed him.

He hadn't heard her — she was quieter than he'd have thought a surface person could be, and he'd been busy with the grating and the drop into the Works — and he was three levels down before he felt the air move behind him and turned.

He hadn't heard her at the grating either, and that mattered. The grating at the Works entrance was a mechanism he knew in the dark: three motions in order, the handle-press that freed the counterweight, the quarter-turn that set the seal, the flat palm that locked it from below. He'd worked it fast and automatic, his hands moving in the black before he'd fully brought his mind to them. He hadn't checked behind him. He'd assumed that because he was alone, the space was sealed.

He wasn't alone.

Noor. A surface torch in her hand — a hand-held fixed light that threw a wavering pool of white, too much for his eyes, but he could work around it. She was breathing hard from the climb down, and her face in the torchlight had that held, unpeaceful quality.

He stopped.

The air in the Works had the smell he tied to the deep: ferrocrete and machine-work and the faint living warmth of the Warm's reach, the ordered smell of a closed world that knew what it was. He breathed it without meaning to, and the thing in him that had been wrong for eight days eased a fraction. Around them the Works made its usual sound — the pipe-rhythm, the far-off machinery, the drone of the Warm carried through stone. It wrapped around them the way silence wraps around a surface person. He was home. Three levels in, and already home.

She was in every way not home. He could hear it in her breathing — the shallow catch of a body taking in too much at once, unable to pick what to fear first. The tight space. The deep dark. The layered sounds her surface ears had never learned to sort. But she'd come anyway, and her torch was steady, and she hadn't cried out.

He said to her, for the first time, the thing he needed to say. He said it in the Underclan's language, because it was the only one that mattered down here, the way Vesh said the teaching — not loud, not hard, but with the weight of a received truth. The Skin is up. The Deep is down. You chose to follow me, and that means you are choosing down, and down does not undo itself for regret.

She didn't understand the words. He hadn't expected her to. He said them anyway, because saying them was right, and the Deep needed right things said in the right way. He'd been up eight days speaking nothing but surface scraps. Saying something true in the real language was like breathing clean air after the bad had passed.

She said something back. He caught I know, and you, and the shape under it: I'm not stopping.

He turned and went on down. She followed. He listened to her footsteps behind him — surface-heavy, unsure, but there — and eased his pace to something she could keep without losing her light, and they went down together into the Warm.

The hum met him at the second level. He pressed his palm to the wall and let it run up his arm and into his chest. Behind him, Noor's torch wavered. The Current didn't acknowledge her.

The Current didn't acknowledge anyone. That was its virtue.

[Beat 36]
He brought her as far as the Works-level and stopped.

Not Homewater. The Works were the Engine Guild's ground, and the Guild and the Underclan had their treaty, and he didn't know if that treaty covered a surface person he'd brought in uninvited. More than that: the Works were where the Lamplighters had found him. He didn't want to be here with a surface torch and a surface person and no way of knowing who was in the tunnels around them.

He gestured at Noor to put out the torch. She resisted — he could read it without her face, in the angle of her body, the way she drew the torch in toward herself — and he held her eyes for a count of eight, then looked at the torch, then back at her. She put it out.

The dark closed around them. He heard her breathing change: faster, higher, the fear surface people had for the dark, because the dark had nothing to offer them. He understood the fear from the outside and had never felt it. That wasn't courage. The dark was home.

He said, in surface speech, with the words he'd gathered over eight days, "I watch. You stay." Then, after a pause: "Safe."

He couldn't be sure she was safe. He said it anyway. It was the closest he could come to the Oarsman's gesture: accept the Fare, carry it, bring it back. She had given him eight days of her patience. Now she would get the right darkness in return.

He waited. He listened. The eastern tunnel was clear. The north junction had Cog-type footsteps, two people, moving away. The pump-hall ran its regular cycle.

He reached back and found her hand in the dark — found it the way he found everything down here, by the shift of air and the warmth of a body — and held it a moment with the grip that meant I am here, I have found you, the air is well. She went still.

Then he started moving, and she moved with him, and they went down toward Homewater with only what he knew between them and the dark.

He didn't know yet that above them the legend had an address now, and that the address was being passed around among people whose interest in the deep was not scholarship.

[Beat 37]
Homewater smelled right.

He hadn't known how wrong the surface had smelled until he was back. Not wrong like bad air or a killing-pocket. Wrong like absence. The surface air had been full of things, and none of them were his. Eight days his body had reached for something it knew and found only more of what it didn't.

The Current was here. He pressed his palm flat to the pipe and it came up through the metal the way it always had — felt in the chest, felt in the bone. He had lived inside that hum since before he could walk. It meant the machine was running, and the machine was the world under the world. He hadn't known how much of his safety lived in it until it was gone. He held his hand there a long count.

Noor stood beside him. She touched nothing. He could hear her breathing the careful way she breathed when she was holding too much at once — a person keeping control and paying for it. She had breathed like that since the Works. She hadn't asked him to slow down. She hadn't asked him to explain. For the last hour she had only followed and kept up, at a pace her body was never built for. He watched her the way he watched his candle: for the signs of what to do next.

He turned and looked at her. In the green-gold of the glow-farms her face was a different color than it had been up top. Not better. Just different. On the surface, in all that white light, she had been sharp and easy to read. Here she was like everyone else in the Deep — warm, close, a little soft at the edges. 

She was looking up.

He followed her gaze. The ceiling of the Hollow was old stone, ferrocrete above that, and above that the whole weight of the city. There was nothing to see. But she stared at it like a person handed a fact too big to hold. She had been underground before. She had never been under a roof that went up for meters and then stopped being anything but the world, with no sky over it at all. The surface had been everything to her. Now she stood beneath it with no way back up except through him.

This is what the surface felt like to me, he thought. And I'm still here.

He put his hand on her arm — not the grip that meant I have you and pointed a direction. Just contact. A reminder that another warm thing was close. She looked at him. Her face almost asked a question. He held her eyes for a count of four, then looked at the pipe, the one with the Current in it, and back at her. She followed his gaze to the pipe and stayed on it. He thought she was trying to feel what he felt.

She couldn't. It was a thing the body learned slow, like the weight of a tool used every day, and her body hadn't learned it and wouldn't in the minutes she had. But she was looking.

"Safe," he said, the second time he'd said it. He meant it more now. He was standing on the thing that made it true.

Then he turned toward the main space, toward the candle-light, toward the people he'd been gone from for eight days — the people he'd just brought the surface home to. He could hear voices. He could hear the waiting in them, the quiet a room gets when it already knows something has arrived.

He moved toward it. She followed.

[Beat 38]
Homewater did not welcome him back.

He hadn't expected ceremony. The Underclan saved ceremony for the formal points, and a return from a Surfacing was one of them. But what met him at the eastern passage — down from the Works-level, through the junction where the fungus-farm's warmth hit the cistern air, into the cool of the Hollow — wasn't ceremony and wasn't anger. It was the careful blankness of people who have heard something they didn't expect and are deciding what to do about it.

Vesh was in the teaching-chamber. So was Knuckle. So were most of the Braves. They stood the way people stand when a hard conversation is coming and they've been waiting for its subject to walk in.

Glim stopped in the doorway. Noor was behind him, her hand still finding his shoulder-blade in the dark. He could feel her working to breathe the Hollow's air — thin and ordered, nothing like the surface's rich flood. It would hit her like a wall. He gave her a moment.

"You came back," Vesh said. The teaching voice, not the ordinary one.

"I came back."

"You brought someone."

"Yes."

The quiet that followed wasn't hostile. It was the quiet of everyone in the room working the same sum: the treaty, the air, the question of what a surface person in the Hollow meant for Homewater staying hidden.

Grale spoke from the back. "Surface-tainted."

Not the word he'd used in the tunnel before the Surfacing — that's what the Surfacing is for, to find out. This was the verdict. He'd watched Glim his whole life for the proof, and now the proof stood in the doorway with a surface woman's hand on its back.

Glim looked at him across the room. Grale's face wasn't triumphant. That was the thing about Grale — it never was. He looked the way he always looked: clear-eyed, sure. Glim had braced for anger, or gloating, or the cold of a man proven right. He got the same measuring look Grale had given him at the pack-check, two weeks and a world ago — only now the answer was filled in.

"Her name is Noor," Glim said. "She followed me down. She'll need a candle."

He kept his eyes on Vesh. But he felt Grale go still in a new way — small, barely there. The same shift Knuckle had once caught, the one that meant Grale was thinking, not performing. 

There was a longer pause.

Sorrel crossed the room and put a hand on Noor's arm, drawing her forward. She was good with outsiders. The Surfacing had left her able to meet surface people halfway, which few in the Hollow could do. Noor let herself be led. That said something about her — she was frightened and doing it anyway. The candle on Sorrel's belt glowed green-gold in the dark, warm and alive, nothing like the blue-white lamps that had blinded him in the Works.

Noor looked at it the way surface people looked at anything strange: careful first, then something close to wonder.

Glim looked around the teaching-chamber — the green tile, the candle-light on it, the faces that had been home for sixteen years. All of them watching him. Knuckle wore the look of a man glad of an outcome he couldn't say he was glad of yet. Vesh's face was turned toward the sound of Noor breathing.

He had come back. He had brought something that couldn't be undone. Both were true, and he stood in the doorway between them.

[Beat 39]
They came on the third day after his return.

The first sign was air. The right-hand tunnel, the one from the Works, carried a wrong draft. Not the steady Vent Cog cycle. Not the leak at the broken junction. A forced push of air, like the Works were breathing wrong. Glim felt it on his arm at the morning-check, went still, and felt it again.

The draft carried something. Not killing air — his candle was fine. It carried the surface: the made scents, the not-alive tangle he'd learned in eight days up top and spent three days scrubbing off his skin and clothes. Someone up there was in the Works. They were using surface machines, and the machines pushed their smell out ahead of them.

He made no sound. He was already in the tunnel mouth, already moving.

He got back to the Hollow in time to tap the warning. Then the lights came anyway.

Not from one side — from two, the south junction and the east Works at once, which meant they'd mapped the approaches right and used both. The white-out was total. He knew these floodlights. They'd caught him in the Works: the sudden blindness, the panic under the panic. But that had been one man in a corridor with his hands on the wall. This was the Hollow. Forty-three people, and the white hit all of them at the same instant.

He could hear it happen. A sharp gasp from Lark, near the cistern-mouth. The sound of a child dropping — Slip, who hadn't heard the warning, whose legs just gave. The silence of Vesh gone completely still, which was worse than any sound. And under all of it, the candles' distress — the glow-rats reacting to the flood of light, their glow shrinking, thirty small animals making the wrong sound at once.

His own candle pressed against his hip. He didn't look at the light. He looked at his hands.

His eyes took to white the way they took to black — slow, poor, by feel. He could make out the Hollow as one flat smear with no detail in it. In that smear he heard surface boots come in from both passages. Not rushing. Moving like people who have more light than anything else in the room and know it, who have done this before and learned that calm is faster than hurry when the advantage is yours.

He counted the steps. Six. Six surface people in the Hollow.

Then a voice.

"Corwin," it said, easy, to someone he couldn't see, the tone of a man narrating his own pleasure for an audience he assumed was there. "Corwin, over here — the old one, by the stone. Look at the hands on her."

He said his own name the way other men said a title. Not loud. A man who understood how sound carried in a closed space — which wasn't what Glim had braced for from a surface hunter — and under the discipline, plain as anything, the pleasure of a man who has found exactly what he came for.

Glim didn't answer. He started to move.

In the white-out he steered by the sounds he knew: Knuckle's breathing, deeper than anyone's; Sorrel's step, lighter on the left since her fall on the cistern-ledge last winter; Noor's voice saying something in surface speech, aimed at the boots coming in from the south. He found Knuckle's arm in the white and gripped it in the signal that meant I have you.

The chain began to form.

Behind him and to the left — near the heat-stone where Leaf worked every morning of his life — he heard a scuffle that wasn't a body moving where it chose. A grip. A stumble. The short cry of someone caught by the wrist and spun faster than her balance allowed. Then a fall, heavier than a fall onto packed earth should sound. Then the quiet of a body that has stopped deciding things.

He didn't stop. He couldn't. The chain was forming, the grips passing back from his own arm, and stopping now would cost more than one pair of hands could ever buy back. He shouted the direction — not words, the sound they all knew, this way, follow it, don't stop — and the chain lurched toward the Old Deep.

Behind them Corwin's voice said something short and satisfied, a man closing an account, and did not follow. He had more to do back there.

Glim led the chain into the dark. He didn't yet know what he'd heard happen to Leaf. He'd know by nightfall, with the rest of them. He carried the name Corwin down into the dark, and he kept it.

[Beat 40]
Lark was coughing.

He heard it from the lower cistern passage. Not the soft throat-clearing of someone who'd breathed dust. This was the hard work of something that had settled deep in the lungs and meant to stay. It wasn't the cough from before the Surfacing. That one had cleared in two sleeps and left nothing — the ordinary price of a season bent over the Still's blue water, and Knuckle had called it dust and been right. This was wetter. Longer. It caught at the end in a way the dust-cough never had. He'd heard this exact sound before, through the wall in Noor's home, in surface people packed together where the air ran hot with whatever they carried. The Hollow's air had never once made that cough. Not even in Lark, weeks back, when the cause was only spore and a long harvest.

He stood in the passage and listened, and his stomach dropped the way it had dropped in the lifting-box up top. He had brought it down. He had carried the surface in his lungs and on his skin into Homewater, and the Hollow had no armor against what surface bodies carried without knowing, without meaning any harm.

Sorrel found him there. She'd been to the surface too, on her own Surfacing; she had more surface-words than anyone in the Hollow but him. He saw on her face that she'd already followed the thought to the place he was only reaching.

"They're not Lamplighters," she said. "The ones at the Skin."

"I know."

"They came asking for the children. Saying they know we're real." She used the surface word carefully, the way you handle a thing that might be hot. "Saying they want to help." She watched his face. "They brought medicine. For the cough."

He understood help now. He understood a lot of surface words he'd had eight days of reasons to learn.

"Then I brought that too," he said. "The cure for the thing I brought."

Sorrel didn't tell him it wasn't his fault. That was the thing she'd come back changed with — she didn't reach for the easy comfort when the hard one was true. She set her hand flat against the passage wall beside his, not touching him, just near, the way you set your candle next to another so the two lights make one. They stood like that. Down the middle passage, Lark coughed again, and it went on too long before it stopped.

"We carried it up there for them," Sorrel said quietly, meaning the surface, meaning the safety no one had ever asked them for. "And now we've carried something back." She took her hand off the wall. "It's not yours alone. I went up and came back too. It could have been me."

It didn't make the stomach-drop stop. She hadn't meant it to.

He listened to Lark's cough end. Then he went to find Vesh.

[Beat 41]
Vesh refused the Mission's medicine.

He did it without asking Knuckle, without calling the elders, without the thinking-silence he used when something needed weighing. He did it the way he did the things he'd already decided.

"No salt to fight the Hot Breath," he said, in the teaching voice, in the teaching-chamber. "No surface medicine in the Deep."

Lark was coughing in the next passage. They could all hear it.

Glim felt the words coming before he chose them — the answer taking shape in his mouth before he decided to give it. He had heard Vesh teach two hundred times. He had helped the old man to his feet, walked at his side, fixed the candle-dish the way Vesh corrected him without a word of argument. In sixteen years he had never once said no to something Vesh delivered in the teaching voice.

"I know what it's for," he said, and heard his own voice do the thing no voice did in that chamber. "I don't know how it works. But Lark is coughing, and the Hot Breath came from me, and the surface has medicine for the thing I brought down."

The silence had weight. He could feel it in his jaw.

Vesh didn't turn toward him. He turned toward the passage where the coughing had been, the way he always turned toward sound. His face held the calm of a man who has already weighed the thing you're only now picking up.

"You've been to the surface," Vesh said.

"Yes."

"You know what surface medicine does to a Deep body."

He didn't. He knew what it was for, not what it did. "No," he said. "But I know Lark is coughing, and she didn't cough before I came back." He kept his voice level. He made himself keep it level, because the other choice was raising it, and he had never raised his voice at Vesh and didn't want to start now. "And I know I brought the Hot Breath down. That's mine too."

Something moved in Vesh's face — a small tightening at the jaw, there and gone. Not anger. Closer to the look of a teacher handed back his own lesson by a student who took it a different way than he'd meant it.

The cough came again from the passage. Longer this time.

Vesh turned his face toward the sound and held it there a count. The warm breath of the Deep came up through the floor, slow and even, the way it did every day of the world. Nobody moved. Glim watched the old man's hands, folded in his lap, still with the stillness of someone who has decided to let the room speak instead of his voice.

"Ask the Mission," Vesh said at last, "what they want in return."

Glim waited.

"The answer," Vesh said, "will tell us what they are."

It wasn't agreement. It was the closest Vesh came to it, and it left the door unlocked and Lark still coughing in the passage. Glim stood. Vesh's hands stayed folded. Neither of them named what had just happened.

Glim went.

[Beat 42]
The Mission wanted to register them.

Glim carried the word back to the teaching-chamber and set it down in front of Vesh like a bad cap pulled off the frame — a thing to be looked at before it went near the others. Register, he said, and then he had to build the meaning from his half-learned surface-words and the pieces Sorrel had given him. To put your name in the surface record. To exist, in the surface's count of things that exist.

Vesh had his palm flat on the green tile. He didn't lift it. His half-blind eyes turned a fraction toward Glim's voice, and he waited in the thinking-silence, the one that meant he was turning the thing over and would not be hurried.

"And when our names are in the surface's record?" Vesh asked.

"Then we're someone in their count."

"We are already someone."

"In their count," Glim said.

Vesh's fingers moved on the tile — the slow press-and-lift he did without knowing it when he followed a thought past where the words had reached. Glim had watched that hand his whole life. He read it the way he now read the cough: a sign that came before the meaning.

"And when our names are in their record," Vesh said, "and one day the surface decides the record wants tidying — that the deep is no place for people, that the registered ones should be brought up where they can be looked after, that it's for our own good —" the fingers went still "— will our names protect us, or hand us over?"

Glim had no answer. He'd been carrying the question three days and hadn't found its bottom.

"The Mission means it," he said. "The kindness is real. I've watched the Lamplighters lie with their faces. This isn't that."

"Yes," Vesh said. His fingers pressed the tile once more and lifted, and he turned his face full toward Glim, which he rarely did. Turning the dead eyes on you wasn't for seeing. It was for letting you feel yourself seen. "That's the part that frightens me. The hunter you can fight. You can't fight a hand that's sure it's feeding you."

The cough came from the middle passage. Lark, or someone past Lark now. They both let it finish.

Glim didn't take the Mission's offer.

He also knew, by the third day after, that the Mission had already begun mapping Homewater — that somewhere above, in a clean room, a kind person was drawing the way down, so the kindness would know where to go.

[Beat 43]
The Hot Breath took Slip first.

He was the youngest of the Braves — six sleep-seasons from his Surfacing, still in the early teaching. His First Hearing had been two months ago. He had named his candle, the way the young ones did before they outgrew the habit. The name was Tallow, a made-up word that meant nothing in either language. Slip's candle had a name that belonged to no one.

Tallow was fine. Slip was not.

The cough had stayed manageable in Lark. Lark was older, with the reserve that came from years of climbing and harvesting and Brave training. She was still coughing, but she was upright and working. Slip was seven years old or near it, and the Hot Breath in a seven-year-old was not the Hot Breath in a Brave.

He burned with a fever the clan had no word for, because fever was not an Underclan illness. Glim pressed the back of his hand to Slip's forehead and the heat was wrong — too much, the body doing something he had no name for and no answer to. The surface called it fever. He knew the word, not what to do with it.

Noor knew. She had been watching from the edge of the clan's space — not trusted yet, but allowed there as the person Sorrel escorted — and she crossed to where Slip lay and put her hand where Glim's had been and said a word to herself in surface speech that he didn't know and didn't need to.

She said to him, carefully, "The Mission's medicine. Bring it now."

He looked at her.

"I know you don't trust them," she said. She used simple surface speech, the words she'd learned worked with him. "I don't trust them either. But the medicine and the Mission aren't the same thing. The medicine works."

He went to get Vesh. Someone would have to climb to the Skin and make the Mission hand over what it had already promised. And from the set of Noor's jaw, he was starting to think that handing it over would not be the simple thing she was making it sound like.

[Beat 44]
The meeting point was a service grating two levels below the Skin — a place the Underclan and the surface had settled on without either side quite admitting they'd settled on anything. Knuckle chose it. Glim didn't ask how he knew where the Mission would be waiting. Knuckle had arranged things in the three days since the first raid that he hadn't explained to anyone, and this was plainly one of them.

The woman at the grating had a case in one hand and a flat surface-device in the other. She gave a title Glim didn't keep and a name he did: Adaeze. She had the calm of a person who wore calm as a tool, the same no matter what stood in front of her.

"You're here for the pediatric fever kit," she said. "I need the patient's intake first. Five-minute process. Retinal capture, basic vitals if you can get them, a name if he has one he'll give."

"He's three levels down," Knuckle said. "He can't come up. He can't be moved."

"I understand that's difficult." Her voice didn't change. "But I can't release medication to an unregistered minor without documented need. It's not policy I have any say over — it protects the child. If the dosage goes wrong, if there's a reaction, we need to know who we treated and be able to follow up. I've seen what happens when well-meaning people give medicine without a record. It doesn't end well for the child."

"He is dying while you talk about records," Glim said. He heard his own surface words come out flatter and harder than he meant. The words were too small for what he was trying to carry in them.

"I hear you," Adaeze said, and the terrible thing was that she did. Her face did what surface faces did when they meant it — the crease at the eyes, the lean forward. "I'm not trying to make this harder than it has to be. I need five minutes of information. That's the whole of what stands between you and this case." She held it up a little, the way you'd show a child a treat to prove it was real. "I can't lower that bar. If I do it for you, I do it for everyone, and then the whole safety framework means nothing — and next time it's a child who needed the framework and didn't have it."

Knuckle's hands had gone very still at his sides. Glim had learned to read that stillness as the loudest thing about him.

"Bring the case down," Knuckle said. "Register him where he's lying. Your five minutes, in his tunnel."

"I'm not cleared to go past the Skin-border without an escort clearance, and that takes —"

"How long."

"Days, usually. I can request an expedite."

Glim felt something behind his breastbone pull tight, like a valve shut hard. Slip did not have days. Slip might not have the hour this conversation was already spending.

It was Noor who broke it. She arrived at a run, chest heaving, told by Sorrel where they'd gone. She didn't argue with Adaeze. She took the device out of the woman's hand — not roughly, but fast, the speed of someone who has decided that being wrong costs less than waiting. She thumbed through three screens like a person who had spent a whole career inside exactly this kind of system. Then she entered a false intake under her own code, backdated, her name standing surety for a boy she'd met twice.

"That's a violation," Adaeze said, quietly, and did not take the device back.

"File it," Noor said. "File it against me. Give him the case."

Adaeze looked at her a long moment — not hostile. Closer to grief. The grief of someone who believes completely in a system that has just failed in front of her and can't see any way it was wrong to fail. She handed over the case.

They ran the whole way down. It wasn't enough time. It was never going to be enough time — Glim understood that even as he ran, even before he knew it for certain. But he carried the case himself, and he didn't let himself think about the minutes lost at the grating until much later, when there was nothing to do with the thought but hold it.

[Beat 45]
They took the medicine. It came too late for Slip and just enough for the others.

Slip had been in Glim's cohort — not a close Brave, not a rival, just a constant. Six years at the thermocline fungus-harvest. The one who could read the spore-load in the warm gradient by touch and smell in a way Glim never could. His hands checked the frame-mesh for rot while Glim checked the yield numbers, the two of them working side by side without speaking, because the work didn't need speech and Slip had never used more of it than he had to. He hadn't been loud. He hadn't been vivid. He'd been reliable the way the cistern was reliable — present, useful, taken for granted. 

Vesh did not speak at Slip's sending-off. The ritual belonged to the Brave-master, and Knuckle spoke it, flat and correct and plain. He said the name — tapped it first on the pipe, in the sign the Current heard, then said it aloud. He said what Slip had known and what Slip had done, in the plain listing of a Brave's skills. He did not say what Slip had meant. That wasn't the ritual's shape, and it wouldn't have helped.

The glow-rat called Tallow was set on the cistern as the last gesture — a small green-gold light moving out across the black water, out toward the center, in sight a long time before the dark took it.

The Lamplighters came while Slip's light was still on the water.

They came the way they always came, with the floods, from two directions. This time there was no warning, because Glim had been at the sending-off and not at the tunnel-mouth where the warning lived. The white poured through the south junction first, filled the tunnel, then the cavity beyond it. The first thing it did was wipe the green-gold off the black water.

The chain formed anyway. The drill was in them. Forty-one Underclan and Noor linked in the dark and the white and the roar of the floods arriving, and they went through the tunnel toward the Old Deep — the layer under Homewater, older than the brick above it. There was nowhere else left.

The Old Deep held. Glim knew this stretch. It was route-training ground, every clan child's before Brave-status, and the chain moved through it at the pace it could hold, the tapped pressure passing back down the line — I have the one behind me — confirming forty-two lives at a time.

Then the training-route ran out. Glim led them onto the descent he'd made exactly once, alone, in the deepest solitary push of his own Surfacing preparation — the furthest down any living Brave but Marl had gone and come back to describe. He had walked it once. He had walked it sure.

The wall was where the passage should have been.

He stopped so fast that Knuckle's chest hit his shoulder, and the pulse behind them stumbled — forty-one signals bunching, uncertain, a chain gone suddenly still at the head. He pressed his palm flat to ferrocrete his memory swore was open air. He ran his hand along a seam that had no business being there and felt the cold sweat of a Brave learning the ground his training stood on had a crack no one had taught him to check for. His memory had lied to him with full confidence once before — a handhold on the outside of Noor's building, small and survivable, a hand's width of difference and nothing worse. He'd told himself then it was only tiredness. He didn't have that comfort now. He came to a wall where he said the passage had been, Vesh's voice said in his head, in the exact cadence of the teaching-chamber. He tried again, a different way in, and it didn't hold that time either.

He didn't have time to be Marl.

He turned the chain — a hard reversal, ugly, forty-two people folding back on themselves in a corridor built for one direction, Knuckle barking the recovery-signal so no one panicked into the wrong wall. He took the second branch he'd marked, half-remembered, on that same lone descent: a route he'd looked at and passed over as longer, colder, worse underfoot. It was also, this time, real. The tunnel narrowed and turned twice at angles his memory hadn't kept, then opened, with no warning, onto stone that was not ferrocrete and not brick. Dressed. Fitted. Older than either.

Behind them, distant now, the floodlight noise spread through the tunnels they'd left, chasing a route that no longer existed for anyone — including, for a handful of terrible minutes, Glim.

They went in. There was nowhere else to go. The way that had almost not opened had opened anyway, and Glim didn't yet know if that was the Current, or luck, or just a boy who'd once marked a second path, half-attentive, on a day he'd been thinking about something else.

[Beat 46]
Noor was in the chain. He hadn't planned it. He'd grabbed a hand in the flood-white and it had been hers, and by the time he knew it was hers they were two tunnels past the Hollow and he wasn't turning back.

She moved better than he had any right to expect. He'd taken her hand in the grip that meant follow, and she followed — not blindly. She kept her other hand on the wall the way he'd shown her in the Works, reading the surfaces and using them. 

He counted the chain. First thing, always, moving under pressure. Forty-one Underclan and Noor, the tapped signals passing back down the human line, each pulse one life accounted for. He kept his feet on the path he knew — and then, for the space of a wrong wall and a hard reversal, on the path he only half-knew — and let the count run in the back of his mind, a quiet certainty he checked against when something felt wrong.

Noor did not panic, not even when the chain folded back on itself in the dark and the pace turned into something like fear moving at speed. She'd grown up on the surface, where the below was just an idea. Now she was underground, in a chain of people she'd known three months, at the ragged edge of a route that had already failed once. Her hand stayed steady.

They reached the boundary the training-maps had always stopped short of: the seam where the Old Deep's brick gave way to something older and colder, marked only by the silence that had lived in every telling of the Tartar-fear since before Glim was born. No living Brave had crossed it. He'd been about to be the first.

Sorrel stopped at the seam.

He felt her hand leave the chain before he understood it — a break in the tapped pulse, a gap where forty-one had been forty. He turned, working against the bodies still moving behind him, and found her a few feet back, her palm flat on the old stone the way his had been flat on the wrong wall an hour before. Her face, in what candlelight reached it, was not afraid. It was decided.

"I'm not going past this," she said.

"Sorrel—"

"They'll follow the noise before they follow a route they don't have," she said. "Someone has to be the noise, this side of the line, or they walk in behind us with the same lights and find us with nowhere left to run at all." It was true, and he knew it was true, and he knew it wasn't the whole of her reason, because her hand hadn't left the stone and her eyes hadn't left the dark beyond it — the dark that had swallowed Marl's certainty three times and given nothing back.

"This isn't the Tartar-fear," he said. "You don't have to—"

"I know what it is." Her voice didn't rise. It was the voice she'd used at the cistern shelf, twelve cycles and a world ago: you'll be the third. "I went up. I came back. I know what the choosing feels like from the inside, better than you do yet. This is the same choosing. I'm choosing not to cross this one." She looked at him, finally, and her hand found his in the dark for exactly as long as a held breath. "Go. Count them through. Don't count me — not tonight."

He didn't have the four minutes it would take to argue her out of it. And he understood, in the same instant, that he wouldn't have won the argument if he'd had an hour.

He went. The chain pulled him forward, Noor's hand still in his other one, and behind him the sounds of the Old Deep closed over the place where Sorrel had stood — no cry, no confirmation, only the ordinary silence of a passage with no one left in it to make a sound.

He counted the chain again on the far side, once they were through. He came up one short. He did not say her name aloud. Saying it would make it a fact, and he was not ready to hold a fact that size.

[Beat 47]
The Tartarian Empire was beautiful.

That was the first thing, and the wrong thing. Not that beauty was wrong — but he hadn't expected it. The ceilings vaulted high overhead. The walls were tiled to the waterline in glazed work: faces and shapes and patterns in colors the candle-light kept changing. The glow moved through the space and the tiles threw it back from a hundred angles, and the whole Tartar shone.

He checked the candle. Fine. The air was cold and still and very old, but it was good air.

He needed everyone to know the air was good. He made the three-finger sign — flat palm, three fingers to the ceiling, the signal for air is well, breathe normally — and watched it pass back through the chain, each person taking it and handing it on.

He counted everyone.

Forty-one people breathing, plus the candles. He counted again, the way you count a thing twice when the first count gives you a number the body doesn't want.

Forty-one. He knew whose absence made the number what it was before he let the knowing turn into words. He did not say her name into the vaulted hall. He set the count down the way he set down anything too heavy to carry while moving — in the place below the mind, for later, where the pieces of his own beginning had always waited.

He sat on the tile, pressed his palms flat, and tried to feel the hum.

It wasn't there.

He pressed harder. The tile was cold under his hands — colder than the Old Deep, colder than the ferrocrete of the tunnels above. Cold the way stone got cold when it had been sealed off from the warmth for a very long time, when the deep heat had never once reached down this far. The cold went up through his palms and his wrists, and he pressed harder, and still there was nothing.

The Current was silent here. It had always been silent here.

He had taken the Tartar-fear for superstition. Not with contempt — the tribe's fears had roots, always: the air that killed, the routes that flooded. He'd thought this fear was the same, a real danger dressed in sacred words, because a child scared of ghosts stayed out of the deep cold better than a child warned about bad air. He believed something closer to the ghosts now, sitting a stone's throw from the line Sorrel would not cross, though he couldn't have said what.

He sat with his palms on the cold tile, and the Current was gone the way a person was gone when they'd died — not the absence of someone who'd stepped out and would come back.

He had grown up inside the Current's voice the way he'd grown up inside his own heartbeat, and he hadn't known, until it was gone, how much of his place in the world ran through it. The Skin had thinned it. The surface had starved it down to a faint tremble in the pipes, barely there if you put your ear to metal. He had come back from the surface partly to escape that thinness.

Here, below the Tartarian levels, below the warmth, below everything, it wasn't thinned. It wasn't there at all. As if whatever carried it — the pipe, the cable, the live machine-work that ran under the deep city like veins — had stopped short of this place, or had never been laid.

The tribe was watching him. They needed to know what he knew, and what he knew was on his face. They were waiting for a sign they could use.

He rose from the tile. He made the sign for breathe — two fingers, tapped to the breastbone, a reminder that the air was good and the body's panic was a lie. Then the sign for rest — palm flat, a slow lowering, an order shaped like an invitation. Then the sign for wait.

They sat.

He walked to the far wall and pressed his palm to the tile — a face in glazed ceramic, old past counting, looking out with the calm of a thing that has been stared at so long it has stopped noticing. The tile was cold. The face looked through him. He felt nothing in the wall but cold and the weight of the stone beyond it.

He had brought the surface down. The Tartar, and everything it had already cost to reach, was the consequence. He would not count that cost while people were watching him decide.

He turned back to the clan.

"The air is good," he said, quietly, in the voice that carried just far enough. "The cold is bad but we can manage it. We'll rest here." He looked at each face — tired, frightened, watching him the way candles watched bad air. He did not look for the one face that would not be among them. "We'll find what we need and decide what comes next. But the air is good. We're breathing."

He watched the faces. Panic was a wave, and a wave needed something to break on. He was what they had.

He sat down on the tile again, put his palms back on the cold, and waited, with all forty-one of them around him, for the Current to say whether this was survivable.

It said nothing.

He breathed anyway.

[Beat 48]
Vesh had the Hot Breath.

He hadn't said. He was the Listener, and a Listener announcing his own illness carried a weight in the clan that a Brave's did not. He had gone on moving, giving direction, being Vesh — the same stillness, the same certainty. Glim hadn't noticed until he saw the old man's face in the candle-light of the Tartarian vaults, where there was nothing to steer by and no voice in the floor, and the tiles threw everything back.

The face was not Vesh's face. It had Vesh's shape — the same half-blind eyes, the same set of the mouth, the same build of a man who has been thinking without pause for seven decades. But the surface of it was wrong, the way still water is wrong when you tip the vessel: the warmth gone out of it, something else where the warmth had been.

He sat beside the old man without speaking. The clan was settling into the vaults, moving quietly, feeling out the space the careful way they met any new ground. Noor was with Lark. The candles were all fine.

He couldn't make himself say it: you have the Hot Breath. The words were there. They wouldn't come. He sat with the silence a count.

"You knew," he said. Not an accusation. A recognition. His own voice sounded strange in the vault — the space was too big for the Hollow's close sound-discipline, and his words came back to him off the tile ceiling a beat late, an echo the Deep had never taught him.

Vesh made a small sound that was not words.

"The Hot Breath was already in you when you agreed to take the Mission's medicine for Lark."

"Lark needed it sooner," Vesh said. His voice held the teaching tone, which meant he could still hold it. "I have been old a while. My body is used to getting by without much help from the things inside it."

It was not a complaint.

"The surface medicine would have—"

"Maybe." He paused. "I have spent seventy years listening to the Current. I would like to do that as long as I can. The medicine may lengthen the time. It may not. What it surely does is change what's around me. I have learned to work in the one I have."

Glim looked at the tiles. They caught his candle's glow and gave it back. The ceiling went up to heights he couldn't measure. His hands were flat on the tile behind him, his weight leaning back — the posture of a man who has run out of argument and is trying not to show it.

"It's quiet here," he said. "The hum is gone."

"Yes." Vesh's hand moved, just slightly, toward the tile. "They built beautiful things, the people who made this. And whatever they had of the Current's voice, they sealed it out." He paused. "I have been wondering, all the way down, what they sealed it out for. What do you think?"

Glim looked at the vaults, at the faces in the tile, and for a moment he couldn't answer, because the question that wanted answering was a different one, and it sat on his chest with all its weight: the old man beside him was dying in a place the clan had only reached because Glim had gone up.

"I think," he said, slowly, "they wanted to make a place where the Current couldn't find them. A place that was only theirs."

Vesh was quiet. Then: "What do you think of that?"

"I think it was lonely," Glim said.

Vesh made a sound that, another day, might have been a laugh. "Yes," he said. "I think that too."

The silence held a long time. It was not uncomfortable.

When Vesh reached into the fold of his robe, Glim hadn't moved from his leaning posture, weight back on his braced palms. Vesh's fingers moved with the sureness of a man who trusts the hand over the eye. He found what he wanted and drew it out — the silence-piece, the carved bone teaching-marker, smooth from decades of handling. He held it out toward where Glim sat.

Glim looked at Vesh's hand. The piece lay in his open palm, worn to the exact shape of a thumb's pressure, the bone yellowed close to candle-amber.

He didn't move for a moment. He was aware of the tile under his palms, cold and fine, and the candle's warmth at his side, and the silence in the floor where the Current's voice should have been. He shifted his weight forward and opened one hand.

Vesh waited until the hand was open. Only then did he press the piece into Glim's palm and close Glim's fingers over it.

The bone was warm from Vesh's body. The warmth was the wrong kind — too fresh, borrowed from a body that was giving it up. Glim held it and did not let his face move. The not-letting cost something; he felt it in his jaw and around his eyes, the body straining to do what a Brave's discipline asked and very nearly failing.

"I won't need it where I'm going," Vesh said. Not the teaching voice — the ordinary one.

Glim said nothing. His hand stayed closed.

"Hold it when the hum is absent," Vesh said. "The bone remembers."

He went deeper into the Tartar alone.

Not far. The Brave's training held: no unknown ground without a candle-check and a count. He went as far as he could on one slow breath — held, let out, taken again — a distance he could walk back. Past the vault where the clan had stopped. Into a bigger space. A hall, the ceiling too high to see, the floor the same fine tile as the vault but wider, with no fallen section. Stone arching above, tile below, and no voice in either.

The vault had been large. Larger than the Hollow, larger than the Works' main cavity. This hall was three of those set side by side, twice as high, and whole. The ceiling ran up past his candle's reach. The tile ran out to walls he could barely tell from the dark. He walked to the middle, and his footsteps made a sound, and the sound took a moment to come back to him. A real echo. He had never heard one in Underclan ground. The spaces down there were never that big.

He sat cross-legged on the tile and put both palms flat.

The tile was cold. Dead under his hands. He knew the Current's voice was not the floor but the thing under the floor — the pipes, the cables, the Warm's long reach beneath everything. The Tartar was too deep and too old and too sealed for that reach to carry here. Knowing it did not make the silence sit any easier in his body.



He had brought the surface down. He had done it by going up. Every choice he had made right by the Underclan's law had led here — to forty-one people in the ghost-country with no home to go back to.

Grale was right, he thought. The surface puts things in you.

He couldn't name all of what it had put in him. Some of it, yes. A smell that was a person and was not. A new sense of size, . The knowledge that the world outside the Underclan was not just the Skin and the Works and the Cog-rows. It was something huge, with its own weather and its own light, in which forty-one people who lived below it were too small to see.

And then, from far down — not the floor, not the tile, but somewhere below even the Tartar, below all its beauty and its sealed silence — he felt it.

Not the Current's voice. Not the chest-pressure he knew. Something else. One beat, lower and slower, the way a very large thing moving very far off sends a shiver through everything between it and you, reaching you after it has already gone by. He felt it in his gut and the roots of his teeth. Not his ears.

He pressed his palms harder to the tile. His whole body went still — not calm, but emptied of everything except listening.

The pulse did not come again.

He stayed as he was. Palms flat. Body still. The candle steady, which meant good air, no draft, its clean green-gold light on the beautiful useless floor. He counted his heartbeats and waited. The Brave's training held here too: a thing heard once is a thing not yet true. You stay. You wait. You let the ground speak for itself.

The hall said nothing.

After a long time — he did not know how long — he found he was cold. The clan was in the vault behind him with nothing to make heat but their own bodies. And whatever was under the Tartar had either gone quiet or had never been a pulse at all — only his own blood beating in his palms and the deep silence of a great stone room.

He kept it anyway. He did not explain it away, and he did not let it go.

He went back to the clan.

[Beat 50]
He told no one about the pulse. Not right away.

The Current's voice was the Warm's machinery — the pumps, the live cables in the rock. He had felt it his whole life, the way he felt the cold on the west-facing tunnel walls. Not a thought. Just the body. The pulse was not that. One beat, lower than anything the Warm made, and spaced wrong — not a machine's even ticking but a gap. Then nothing.

He pressed his palms to the cold tile again and again, in the dark, in different places. He got back only cold and absence. He kept them there longer than was any use. He did not go to Knuckle first, the way he had meant to. He sat with it through one whole sleep-cycle, listening to the clan breathe around him, waiting to be sure of a thing he couldn't name. When the clan stirred and Noor and Lark started sorting the food stocks, he stood up. He did not press his palms to the tile again.

He let it stay where it was.

What he knew was that the clan had to go back.

Not to Homewater — he did not yet know what Homewater was now, whether the Lamplighters had set fixed lights there or moved through and gone. Not to the surface. Back: toward the problem, not away from it. Going deeper into the Tartar would only bring them to bad air, or drowned voids, or the deeper Made Things, or the end of their food and water. None of that was a plan.

He made his case to Knuckle first, because Knuckle was a practical man.

"The Lamplighters know our routes," he said. "They have the surface maps. They don't know the Cog tunnels."

"The Cogs don't like us," Knuckle said.

"No. But they like the Lamplighters less." He had spent time in the Works before the Surfacing, in the uneasy border-times when Cog business and Underclan business crossed. He knew the Cogs' grudge against the surface — the surface that had built the Works and then forgotten them, that had left the Cogs to keep the machinery running in a deal that was not quite slavery and not quite freedom. The Cogs were no friends. But they had their own reasons to keep the surface out of the deep. "They won't help us because they like us. They'll help us if we make it worth their while."

"What do we have that's worth their while?"

Glim thought of his eight days on the surface. The devices, the maps, the networks, the routes he had taken in. Noor, who could move between the surface's paper world and the deep's own trade.

"Information," he said. "The surface's own information, brought down."

[Beat 51]
The Cog settlement in the Works was called the Bindery. It smelled of machine-oil and old grain and the sour smell of people who work with their hands in tight spaces without enough daylight.

Glim knew that smell. He had been here twice before the Surfacing. Once as a young Brave, bringing the trade-goods the Underclan's upper tunnels sometimes made for the maintenance crews. Once with Grale, on a talk Grale never fully explained, that ended in a handshake and a silence Glim had learned not to ask about. He knew the Cog ranks: the Chief Fitter, who ran the maintenance schedule; the Pipe-Readers, who read the Works' machinery the way Listeners read the Current; and the gangs of Fitters and Runners who did the labor.

He did not know the current Chief Fitter's name.

"Her name is Dross," Noor said, beside him. They were crouched in the entrance passage, watching the light through the grille. "She was deputy under the last one. He died eighteen months ago." She paused. "The surface administration logged the death. They're bound by the maintenance covenant to keep the Works staffed. They haven't done it in three cycles."

"What does that mean for Dross?"

"She's been doing two people's work with half the parts."

He looked at her. She had that way, sometimes, of knowing a thing without making a show of it.

He took that in. "You've been thinking ahead."

"It seemed useful." Her voice had the flatness that wasn't cold, only focused.

They went in together. He had decided they should. Noor at his side said the thing on its own: we bring you the surface's papers, and someone who can read them.

He had expected that. What he hadn't been able to do was picture the shape of it.
