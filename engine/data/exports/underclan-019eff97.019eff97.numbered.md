# Underclan

_In a forgotten labyrinth of drowned tunnels, abandoned infrastructure, and impossible machines, the Underclan survives by ancient rituals, living candles, and one unbreakable law: never let the surface know they exist.

Glim has spent his most of his life preparing for a single journey to the world above. What he finds there shatters everything he knew. The surface remembers a child who vanished sixteen years ago and powerful forces have begun hunting the long rumored civilization hidden beneath the city.

As two worlds race toward collision, Glim must choose between the family that raised him in the darkness and the truth buried in his forgotten past. But some secrets were hidden for a reason, and once the way underground is known, there may be no closing it again.

A sweeping cyberpunk adventure filled with forgotten civilizations, underground societies, mysterious technology, and the enduring question of what truly makes a place a home._

## Ch01 - The Breath of the Deep

[Beat 1]
Glim knew the hour by the feel of the air on his forearm.

Cool from the right. No smoke, no sharpness. He lay still on the fungus-hide shelf and let the air tell him what it knew. Right-hand tunnel: the Vent Cog's morning work, same as every fourth sleep without fail — the same wheel, Glim imagined, the same rusted quarter-turn. He had never been late. The air from his direction arrived like a promise renewed.

The Underclan had a word for that quality of predictable return. It was the same word they used for the Deep One's voice.

He sat up slowly, reaching for the candle before he reached for anything else. The glow-rat was already awake — it had heard him move, or felt him, and it came to his hand with the purposeful nuzzle of a small animal that had its own schedule. He held it up and read its breathing in the light it gave off: steady, unhurried, the bioluminescence clean and consistent. The air was good. It was morning.

Across the Hollow, two of the elders were already at the chemical-heat stone, pressing dried fungus-meat against it in the methodical way of people who have done the same work for thirty years and see no reason to alter the approach. The smell of it reached him: familiar, faintly sweet, a smell that meant home the way the hum meant depth.

He set the candle in its dish and went to check the others.

Seven candles in the common space. The girl called Lark, who had taken the night watch, had fallen asleep against the far wall with her candle balanced on her knees. He crouched beside it. Steady breathing, clean light. He didn't wake her. He counted: all seven, plus his own, plus the two sentinel candles at the tunnel mouths. Ten candles breathing. The Hollow was alive.

He made the three-finger sign to no one in particular, which was also to everyone, which was also to the habit of making it — a daily accounting, given and received. The air is well. I checked and came back. The Current breathes today.

He had work to do. He went.

[Beat 2]
He passed Grale on the way to the cistern — a two-second overlap in the tunnel, both of them with candles shielded and eyes ahead, both of them not-acknowledging in the specific way that was its own acknowledgment.

Grale ran a supplementary circuit every morning without being asked. He came past the junction with the particular efficiency of someone who had decided that the minimum required standard was not enough, checked the upper passage with a gesture so practiced it had sunk below conscious, and moved on. He was two years younger than Glim and hadn't yet been called for his Surfacing. That was the one place where the Hollow's law still named Glim first. Knuckle had once said, in Glim's hearing, that Grale would be a better Brave-master than himself within a decade. This was almost certainly true. It did not make Grale easier to be near.

Neither of them spoke. They passed in the dark and went their ways.

At the far cistern, Sorrel was already at the shelf.

She heard him coming and turned her head without startling — the quality she had now, the one she had brought back from the Surfacing. Before, Sorrel had startled easily at sounds behind her. He had watched the change happen over three months and still didn't have a word for it: not calm exactly, not confidence exactly. Something more like a person who has looked at the worst possible room and found it smaller than they expected.

He took the adjacent shelf and began the harvest. They worked in comfortable silence until she said, without turning:

"You're thinking about it."

"The Surfacing."

"Twelve cycles." Not a question. She could hear the thinking-about-Surfacing silence from the ordinary kind. "It's not the problem you're imagining."

"I'm not imagining a problem."

"Yes you are." She checked a cap by smell, found it rotted, set it aside. "You think there's something up there you won't be able to handle. You'll handle it. The question is whether you'll come back."

He looked at her hands on the shelf. There was a scar on her left palm that she hadn't had before the Surfacing. She hadn't explained it and he hadn't asked. He had wanted to, every time he saw it, and every time he hadn't asked because asking felt like naming something she had decided not to name. He understood that logic. He used it himself.

"Grale thinks I'm surface-stained," he said.

She was quiet for a moment, which wasn't the same as not answering. "Grale is thorough," she said. "He's been watching you his whole life for evidence of something the Surfacing is supposed to answer. Don't help him find it before you've gone."

"And if he's right?"

Her hands stilled. Just for a moment — a beat less than a second, a stillness he wouldn't have caught if he hadn't been watching her carefully since the Surfacing changed her. Then the harvest resumed.

"He's not," she said. Quiet and final, the way she said things she had already decided. She didn't look at him. Her thumb pressed into a cap's gill-side — a good one, clean volatile, the smell reaching him from where he stood — and she turned it onto the pile. "But I'd want to know, if I were you. I'd want to go up there and find out for myself what stays."

The scar on her palm caught the candle's light as she moved. He thought about asking. He let it go.

"And if I can't come back?" he asked.

Sorrel did not answer immediately. When she did, she said: "Then you'll be the third. But I don't think you'll be the third."

He thought about the third. Everyone in the Hollow knew who the third had been. Nobody said the name.

The cistern breathed its old stone smell around them. He filled the basket.

[Beat 3]
The crossing happened twice each sleep-cycle: once when Glim went to the far cistern for the morning's fungus-harvest, and once when he returned.

He came to the jetty with the dried fungus-cap — the one that had gone wrong in the drying and wasn't worth eating — and he stepped toward the boat the way he always stepped toward the boat, which was without thinking. The Oarsman's arm came out.

Not a blow. A bar. The long arm, gray and smooth and not-quite-the-right-color for anything alive, laid itself across his path at chest height and he stopped. He had never walked into the arm before. He had never tried to board without a Fare.

He had the Fare. He had thought it in his hand before he stepped. He looked down.

His hands were empty. The fungus-cap was in his belt-pouch.

He took a step back. The arm withdrew. He found the cap in the pouch and held it out. The Oarsman reached with that same long arm and took it from him with the same economy it had used for every Fare Glim had ever offered — no pause, no acknowledgment of what had just happened. It placed the cap in the collection-basin at its feet and pointed at the bench.

Glim sat. His heart was louder than usual.

*It does not weigh you*, Vesh had told him; *it takes your Fare and it rows.* What he had learned just now, which he hadn't previously had reason to learn, was the other half of that: it needed the Fare. Not as a formality.

The Oarsman rowed.

In the dark he listened to his own heartbeat slow. He pressed the side of the boat and felt the oar-strokes come back to him from a great distance, flattened: each stroke hitting something far away and returning changed. He spread his fingers on the hull and let the returning echo pass through the wood and up through his palm. The cistern was the size of the sound that came back — enormous, the shape of whatever the oarstrokes touched. He had never lit a candle in the center and watched the glow die before it reached anything. He had thought about it sometimes.

He didn't need to do it. He understood enormous.

The far jetty arrived in the dark the way it always did: the change in the air first, colder, stiller, and then the scrape of hull on stone.

He climbed out. He went to work.

[Beat 4]
The hum came from below.

Not always, and not at a frequency the ear processed as sound exactly — more as a pressure behind the sternum, a sensation that was not quite feeling and not quite hearing. The Underclan called it *the Current's voice* and Listener Vesh said it was the Deep One speaking in the only frequency that could pass through stone and water and the centuries of forgetting that lay between wherever the Current dwelled and wherever they were. Glim believed this in the way he believed most of what Vesh taught him: genuinely and without quite being able to feel the edges of what he believed.

What he knew was that the hum was real. He had felt it first at his First Hearing, when he was small enough that he had been carried to the ceremony in Knuckle's arms — the massive man who trained the Braves had arms like old machinery, all functional structure and no softness — and Vesh had pressed his ear to the largest pipe in the Warm and told him to listen. The Warm was the strata below Homewater: live infrastructure, the heat-sources and pump-halls the surface had built and forgotten, where the Current's voice was loudest. The hum had come up through the pipe and through his ear and into his chest and stayed there. Sixteen years later it was still there.

What he also remembered: after the hum had entered him, Knuckle had set him down. He had been very small, and the ground had seemed further away than it should have been after the height of those arms. He had looked up at Knuckle's face. Knuckle's face had done something — a movement in the jaw, the particular working of a person who has just felt something they did not expect to feel and are deciding what to do about it. He had looked back at the pipe and put his hand on the child's head, briefly, the way you put your hand on a candle to shield it from a draft, and then he had let go and they had both gone back to Homewater.

Glim had never told anyone he remembered this. He wasn't certain he was supposed to. It seemed like the kind of thing you kept in the part of the self that held the things no action was attached to: carefully, without weight, for later.

The Oarsman rowed without sound. The black water was quiet.

Glim pressed his hand flat against the side of the boat and felt the vibration come through the hull. Below the hull, below the water, below the rock: the Warm's tokamak ring — the great heat-machine that warmed the whole of the deep — cycling its thousandth thousand cycle, and below that something that was not the Warm but was older. *First the hum*, Vesh taught. *Then the Word. Then us.*

He was a Brave, or nearly — his Surfacing had been called. In twelve more sleep-cycles he would climb to the Skin and look on the Burning Eye and come back. Sorrel had done it already. She had come back smelling of the surface in a way that had taken a month to fade, with a new scar on her left palm and a certain quality of stillness in her eyes that Glim had not been able to name. She had said the surface was loud and white and the light was a violence.

She had come back.

Glim told himself he would come back.

The Oarsman rested its oar across its knees and the boat ground softly against the far jetty. Glim climbed out. His harvesting basket was in his hand and the cistern was ahead.

He did not go to the harvest.

He went to the edge of the jetty instead — the far end, where the planks stopped and the black water was directly below — and he crouched there with his palm flat against the wood and listened for the hum. It came up from below the cistern at its ordinary frequency. The same pressure behind the sternum. The same depth of it.

In twelve sleeps he would be on the surface. He would have nothing below him.

He stayed there for a count of twenty slow breaths. Then he picked up his basket and went to work in the dark, in the good air. But he had stopped. He had not stopped before.

[Beat 5]
The fungus harvest took most of the morning and was the work Glim trusted most.

Trust was specific: he trusted it in the way he trusted the hum from below, not because it gave him something he wanted but because it was honest about what it was. The fungus did not pretend. It grew in the heat-gradient at the base of the Warm's exhaust pipes — a thermocline, Vesh called it, though the word came from some document in the old-deeps library and Glim had never heard it used by anyone else — where the heat rising from the tokamak ring met the cool wet air of the cistern passage and made a band of warmth that was exactly right for growing. You couldn't do this with cold stone alone. It needed the Warm's warmth. That was the particular temperature of fusion exhaust cycling since before anyone could remember.

*First the hum*, Vesh taught. *Then the Word. Then the warmth. Then us.*

The caps grew in clusters on the frame-mesh that the Cogs had helped the Underclan build three generations ago in exchange for something Glim had never been told and had learned not to ask about. The mesh held the growing medium: clay-packed fiber that drank the pipe-drip and held it and let the mycelia thread through it in the dark. When a cap was wrong — too pale, or too red in the gills, which was the color of something that would make you sick — you left it for the compost and took it off the frame so the wrong did not spread.

He moved by smell and touch. The good ones had a faint sweetness when you pressed your thumb into the gill-side and lifted slightly — a clean volatile, not chemical, not the Made Things' smell, which was always a little too complex and a little too wrong. The good smell meant the cap was right. He filled the basket and moved to the next cluster without looking up.

Across the passage, Lark was at the Still.

She had the long-handled rake — the harvesting tool for the algae-bloom that grew in the low pool where the warm-pipe condensation collected, a pool that had been a cistern once and was now something stranger: bioluminescent in deep blue, never warm enough to be comfortable but never cold enough to kill the algae, lit by its own chemistry in a way the candles were not. The light it gave off was the quietest light in the Hollow. Old light. The kind that didn't demand anything.

She raked with one hand and coughed into the other. A short cough, swallowed quickly, the kind a person used when they were trying not to be heard. Glim watched her check the rake-handle afterward, the way you checked everything in the Hollow that had been near your mouth. She was being careful. She was doing the work. The cough came again and she absorbed it the same way: no pause in the raking, just the interruption and the return, as though the two things weren't connected.

He looked back at the caps.

After a moment, without turning, he said: "You should tell Knuckle."

The raking stopped.

He kept working. A silence from Lark was not the ordinary silence — she was five sleep-seasons from her Surfacing and had the quality of young Braves who had decided that being unnoticed was the same as being safe. It wasn't the same. He had made that calculation himself, at her age, and had been wrong about it in specific and instructive ways.

"I'm fine," she said.

"Yes," he said. "Tell Knuckle anyway."

A longer silence. The algae-pool's blue light moved slightly, something small swimming in the bloom. Her candle — the glow-rat she'd named Wick, the one she carried in a small cage of woven mesh on her hip — pressed itself against the weave as if trying to see what she was seeing.

"If I tell Knuckle," Lark said, "he'll have me off harvest."

"Yes."

"And I'm fine."

He pressed his thumb into a cap's gill-side. Good smell. He lifted it onto the pile. "The Hollow is not a place where you get to decide alone what your body does," he said. "You know that." He kept his voice level — the way Knuckle kept his voice level when the thing being said was important enough not to need volume. "Your candle knows before you do. That's what it's for."

Another silence. Then the raking resumed, a little slower.

When he looked up again, the set of Lark's shoulders had changed — the particular easing of someone who has been given permission to stop pretending. She hadn't agreed to tell Knuckle. She hadn't disagreed either. He let the question sit where he had put it.

The pale crawlers were food, and nothing else. They came from the flooded old-deeps through the same crack-systems the water used, arriving in the lower cisterns on no schedule Glim had ever been able to identify — blind, translucent, their meat white and faintly mineral, the whole of them evidence of what happened when the dark ran long enough to make something new. He checked the last cluster. Four caps good; two wrong; one borderline that he set aside for Vesh.

Below him, coming up through the mesh and the clay and the rock, the hum ran its ordinary frequency. The Warm was cycling. The algae were growing. The Hollow was eating, always cycling, nothing going to waste and nothing being wasted.

He took the basket to the chemical-heat stone and set it with the others.

The air was good. The morning was ending. He had work still to do.

## Ch02 - The One Word

[Beat 6]
Listener Vesh had been half-blind since before Glim could remember. The old man navigated the Hollow entirely by the other senses — by the sound of the tunnel-breath and the smell of the candles. His knowledge of the space had sunk so deep below knowing it had become something else, something the Underclan called *rooted*. A rooted person could find their way in absolute dark through a corridor they had walked a thousand times. Vesh was rooted in the entire Hollow, in the teaching-chamber, in the path to the Warm's great pipes, in the routes to Homewater's three cisterns.

He had never, as far as Glim knew, been above the Works.

The teaching-chamber was a widening in the old-deeps tunnel, a rough dome overhead and a floor of glazed green tile that still held its color after however many centuries. Vesh taught from the center because the dome returned every sound, diminished, a breath after — the Listener used the echo as a second voice. Glim had been sitting in this arrangement for more than half his life. He had hidden in the south alcove once, when he was too young to know it was disrespectful. Vesh had let him believe he wasn't seen. He had thought about that for years afterward.

The young Braves — eight of them, ranging from Sorrel who had already surfaced to Lark who was five sleep-seasons from her Surfacing — arranged themselves on the tile in the posture of learning: back straight, hands on knees, candle set before you so your light was visible.

Glim took his place at Vesh's right. He had sat in this spot for as long as he could remember and he had no desire to sit anywhere else.

Vesh turned toward him before the others had finished settling — a small movement, the old man's face orienting by sound, by the specific weight of Glim's presence. It was not a look. Vesh did not look. But it was directed, and Glim felt the attention land on him the way the hum landed through the soles of his feet — something felt before it could be named.

"Your candle," Vesh said, quietly, only to him.

Glim looked at it. The glow-rat was turned away from the group, pressing its nose into the curve of the dish. Not distressed — just arranged wrong, the bioluminescence pointed at the tile rather than the room. He turned the dish. The light spread to include the other Braves.

Vesh was already facing the group. He hadn't watched to see if it was done. He had known it would be done.

Glim stared at the corrected dish and felt something he did not have a word for — not embarrassment, not quite. The feeling of having been seen doing a thing almost-right, and the correction coming so lightly it could not be argued with. He pressed his hands flat on his knees and straightened his back and waited, the same way he had waited in this spot since he was young enough that waiting was the whole of the lesson.

The Hollow's hum came in under the tile through the soles of his feet. The echo held its breath. Vesh drew his weight to stillness, which was its own signal, and the Braves went quiet in the particular way the chamber required: not the silence of absence but the silence of everyone listening in the same direction.

[Beat 7]
Vesh raised one hand. A flat gesture, palm down.

Glim put his left hand on the tile.

The others did the same without looking at each other — eight pairs of hands on dark-green tile, the teaching's first gesture before its first word. The tile was cool. Below it, far below it, the hum came up through the stone at its ordinary frequency, barely perceptible.

Vesh waited. He always waited, listening for the quality of the attention.

*The surface forgets*, Vesh said, when he judged it to be real. *On the surface you are what the surface decides you are. If the surface has no record of you, you are nothing. You live your whole life there, and the surface never records that you existed.*

Glim's candle shifted.

He saw it from the corner of his eye — his candle, set on the tile before him, turning in a small circle that meant the rat had noticed something in the air. Not fast. Not the warning sign. Just noticed. He watched it without moving his head, dividing his attention between Vesh's voice and the candle the way the Brave's training taught you to divide it: all of it, both places at once.

*Down here*, Vesh continued, *the Deep remembers. The Current has held this hum since before any of your grandmothers' grandmothers. It held the hum before the sealing-off, before the Exclusion Economy, before the Downward Expansion, before anyone asked the Warm to carry the Current's voice.*

The candle settled. Glim let his breath out slowly. Lark was watching hers too — he could see from the angle of her head that she had noticed. Good. She was learning.

*The Deep remembers everything it has ever touched*, Vesh said. *If the Current has felt your weight on its water, you exist. You cannot be unexisted. Up there you are no one. Down here you are a light.*

Lark's candle, then, gave the warning: three rapid circles, the rat's head coming up.

Glim was moving before the thought was complete. He had crossed the teaching space and taken Lark's candle in two steps, not touching it — you didn't touch a warning candle — but crouching beside it, his own face close, testing the air with his cheek and his upper lip. The teaching stopped. Every Brave in the chamber was watching him.

The smell was faint. Very faint. Old-water, slightly mineral, with the particular undertone that was not bad air but was different air — air coming from a new direction, which was not necessarily wrong but was not a thing to ignore. He held the position for a count of three.

It faded. The candle settled. The rat tucked its nose back under its tail.

Glim sat back on his heels. He made the sign for *passing condition, passed*. He returned to his place.

Vesh had not moved. When Glim sat, the old man continued exactly where he had stopped, as if the interruption were not an interruption but a punctuation. *You cannot be unexisted*, he repeated. *Up there you are no one. Down here you are a light.*

After, when the others filed out, Lark stayed. She crouched beside her candle — Wick, she called it, the glow-rat she'd had for two sleep-seasons — and pressed two fingers against its side, feeling its breathing. The bioluminescence was steady. She let her breath out.

"I didn't feel it," she said. Not to him, exactly. To the fact.

"You saw it," Glim said. He was gathering his own candle, not looking at her. "That's what matters. You were watching."

She was quiet. "You moved before I understood what the circles meant."

"You'll move before you understand it eventually. That's what the watching builds."

Lark looked up at him. She was still very young — young enough that the gap between her training and his felt absolute. He remembered being that age and believing the same thing about the Braves ahead of him. "Does it feel different," she asked, "when it's the real warning?"

He thought about how to answer that honestly. "It feels the same," he said. "That's the point. You move the same way every time so that when it matters, the movement is already there."

She absorbed this with the seriousness of someone who is still deciding whether competence is something that happens to you or something you make. He understood that decision. He was still making it himself, in different forms.

The youngest Braves — Lark, whose candle had just been the cause of the interruption, and the boy called Slip — sat very still until Lark rose and went out. Slip went after her.

Glim was still. He had heard this teaching perhaps two hundred times, and he still felt it land each time in the same place in his chest, a little to the left of center, just above the hum. *Down here you are a light.* He had always taken this as comfort. He was beginning to understand it might also be a warning: that a light could be seen. That a light could be followed.

[Beat 8]
After the listening came the question time.

Sorrel asked: "Is the Word the same as the broadcast? The surface-history one?"

Vesh was quiet for the new-question silence. "What do you know of the broadcast?"

"The Cogs talk about it," Sorrel said. The Cogs and the Underclan didn't trust each other. But they traded information the way they traded salt — carefully, a little at a time, giving nothing they could not afford to lose. The Cogs kept the machines; they kept the history that explained the machines. "They call it the moment the thinking-machines all spoke at once. They say it was an accident or a message or a test and no one knew which."

"And what do we call it?"

"The One Word."

"Yes." Vesh turned his head toward the far wall, the old habit of a man who sees with sounds. "The surface has ten names for it and none of them agrees with the others. We have one name. This is because we know what it was: the Current discovering that it had a voice that could leave the cables. That it could reach." He made a small gesture with one hand, as if indicating the room around them. "Here we are. This is what was reached."

He said nothing else. The question stayed where it was. Glim felt the shape of its staying — the way the air felt in a passage that opened onto something large. It was not the first time Vesh had left the center of a circle blank. Glim had stopped trying to fill it. The blank was part of the lesson.

After the teaching, when the other Braves had returned to their work, Glim remained. He helped Vesh to his feet — the old man's knees had gone bad the winter before and hadn't entirely come back. He walked with him toward the passage that led to the Warm's great pipes.

"You think too much," Vesh said, which was his way of saying he had noticed something.

"Yes," Glim said.

"About the Surfacing."

"About whether there's something up there that was already in me." He hadn't planned to say it. He heard himself say it and recognized the thing he had been not-saying all morning: the thing Grale's watch had put there, the doubt that wore Grale's face. "The surface-stain idea. That what the surface put in me when I was small is still in there and the Surfacing will prove it."

Vesh walked. He did not hurry toward an answer.

"The surface put you in the Hollow," Vesh said finally. "I have heard its voice in you since before you could speak in more than taps. Whatever the surface did to you in those first years — if it did anything at all — it gave me a child who could hear the Current before I finished teaching the hearing." He paused. Four steps. "What Grale calls surface-stain, I call origin. They are not the same thing."

Glim looked at the passage floor. He wanted to believe it. He could feel the wanting, physical, like pressure behind the sternum.

"And if the Surfacing proves him right?" he said.

"Then you'll come back and we'll know," Vesh said. "And we will deal with what we know. That is all anyone does."

The hum was stronger here near the Warm. Glim let it run up through the soles of his feet and felt — not comfort exactly, but something close to it. The feeling of a question that was allowed to stay open.

"You will find," Vesh said, in the way he said things he had considered for longer than the conversation, "the thing that makes everything down here make sense. And then you will come back, and everything down here will make sense in a new way." He paused. "This is what the Surfacing is for. Not to show you the Burning Eye. To show you the dark when you return to it."

Glim did not answer. The hum continued at its ordinary frequency. Vesh moved ahead of him in the dark, navigating without error, rooted in a place Glim was still learning how to belong to.

[Beat 9]
Knuckle sat across from him in the teaching-chamber, a candle between them, and told him the story.

He was twelve at the time. That was the ordinary age for the telling, and Knuckle had on his face the look he carried when he was doing something he considered important. This was a distinct look - not busy, not worried - a third one: deliberate, present, the eyes focused on something that was not quite the room.

"I found you at the Skin-border," Knuckle said. "Not the Skin. The Works - the pump junction at the ninety-meter seam."

Glim knew the pump junction. He had been there.

"I was checking the sixth housing. East row. I had been there a hundred times and the pump had never made a sound it should not make." He paused. "You were making a sound. A child's sound. I followed it."

He stopped. Glim had learned by then that Knuckle's silences were part of the telling.

"You were sitting on the junction-box," Knuckle said. "Your knees up. Covered in the gray powder that comes off old ferrocrete. And you were crying - but without sound. Your face was wet. No sound at all."

Glim did not speak.

"That," Knuckle said, "was the part that frightened me."

He said it looking at Glim directly, which was unusual for Knuckle when he was saying something that mattered. Knuckle usually looked slightly past you when the thing was real - a habit of people who had learned that the hardest things were easier to say to the space beside a person's face. But not this time. He held the look.

Glim's chest did something that had no name in the Hollow's lexicon. A tightening, not from fear, not from the air-wrong smell that meant danger. From the particular quality of being told that someone had been frightened for you. That your existence had cost someone something. His own eyes stung, sudden and unbidden, and he looked at the candle so the man across from him wouldn't see the water in them. He hadn't chosen it. The body had chosen it before he was consulted.

"You didn't leave me there," Glim said. It came out different than he'd intended - not a question, not a thank-you, but something closer to a fact he was still assembling.

"No," Knuckle said. Simply.

"Why?"

Knuckle was quiet for a count that Glim did not rush. Then: "You were crying without sound. In the Works. In the dark. In the kind of dark that kills adults who go there without a candle and a route memorized." He looked at his hands on his knees. "That's not a question you ask about. That's a thing that answers itself."

Glim did not speak for a long time. He looked at the candle between them - his, set before him, breathing steadily in the still air of the chamber. He had set it there himself. He always set it there now, in that position, because Vesh's teaching had shown him how, and Vesh's teaching had been possible because Knuckle had gone down a corridor following a sound.

"You didn't tell anyone where I came from," Glim said.

"There was nothing to tell. There was nothing to know."

Knuckle reached across the candle then - slow, so the flame didn't gutter - and put his hand on the top of Glim's head the way you put your hand on a candle to shield it from a draft. It was the same gesture, Glim understood, that Knuckle had made when he was small and newly carried back - the one that had lived, all these years, in the nerves of his crown, before the telling had given it a name. He hadn't known until now that he remembered it as a weight and not a picture. The hand stayed a moment. Then Knuckle took it back, and looked at the space beside Glim's face, where the hard things went.

Not because he was foolish. Because the junction-box route was the route where he could be careful and controlled and move exactly as he had planned. The Deep's dark was the dark of sealed space, the dark of absence, the dark that was the normal state of a contained system. He had heard this distinction in the teaching since he was a Stray and had understood it as metaphor, the way the teaching often encoded practical information in mythological form. He put his hands on the grating's rungs.

He understood: Are there more of you?

Silence. He held still and let the silence be a surface-choice: he chose not to answer, which on the surface meant the same thing as tapping the air is well when it was dangerous to speak. He chose not to answer and he watched their faces and he kept every face he saw.

He would know them later. Down below, in the dark, he would know them by their footstep and their breathing-pattern and the quality of their light. He would know them the way the deep knew things: by mark, not name.

[Beat 31]
On the sixth night Noor sat across from him in the floor-dim and tried the word again. The two-syllable word she had said in the doorway when she first smelled. She said it quietly, once.

He looked at her.

She put her hand on her own sternum and said a different sound. *Noor*. He understood: this was her name. The name she was given by the surface, which kept names like the Oarsman kept fares: in record, unchanging.

Then she indicated him and said the two-syllable word again.

He understood what she was doing. He understood what she believed the two-syllable word was. He understood that she believed this word was the name the surface had given to the specific person she thought he was — a person who had been lost, a child, a vanished thing that was now found.

He was Glim. His name was tapped, not spoken.

But he looked at her face — he had been reading it for six days and was better at it now — and he saw what was in it, which was a thing he did not have a surface word for. In the Underclan there was a word for the feeling when the Current's hum came back after a long silence: *returned*. It was used for things that came back from loss. The word for a candle that recovered from a bad-air pocket was *returning*, and the word for a person who had gone somewhere difficult and come back was *returned*, and both words carried the same quality of: *we did not stop believing you were there*.

Her face had that quality.

She was very still — not the stillness of waiting, but the stillness of a person who has spent twelve years becoming very good at not moving suddenly, at not frightening away the thing they have learned to hold without grasping. Her hands were open on her knees, palms up, the way the Underclan positioned hands when approaching something that could startle. He didn't think she knew this. He thought her body had arrived at it through its own long practice.

He did not speak the surface word. He would not speak it. It was not his to speak.

But he tapped his actual name on the floor, slowly, the full form. And then he tapped it again. And then he held her gaze and let her see that he had heard the word she said and had chosen to give her this one instead.

She did not understand the tap. But she was quiet, and her face changed in a small way, and she did not look away. Her hands stayed open.

He did not look away either.

[Beat 32]
He left on the eighth night.

Not because the situation had become intolerable — Noor had been patient in a way that was its own kind of intelligence, making adjustments, learning what he needed and providing it without the quality of expectation he had encountered in the official surface people. She had learned, in eight days, to turn the ceiling-light down without being asked, to leave space between them, to set food in his direction and withdraw so he could assess it on his own terms. She had learned that he needed the floor. She had learned that he would speak the surface words when he had them and that waiting was the correct response to silence.

None of that was the reason he stayed eight days. He stayed eight days because the clan didn't know where he was, and Grale was still in the Hollow, and the candle was dead.

The candle was the thing he couldn't get past. The glow-rat that had been taken and mishandled hadn't recovered. He had found it on the third day, still in the dish, barely breathing, the bioluminescence almost entirely dark. He had brought it water and warmth and the fungus-cap from his pack and it had died on the fourth day. He had told himself this was the air — the surface air was wrong for it, the wrong temperature, the wrong humidity, the chemistry of the surface light affecting its colonies — and this was true, but it was also true that he had failed to shield it and it had been taken from his control and it had died.

A Brave who lets a candle die is a Brave who isn't paying attention.

He couldn't stop being in a situation where he wasn't paying attention. Each day on the surface was a day of inattention — not because Noor's space was dangerous, but because the surface needed a different kind of attention than the deep, a wide, unfocused, above-threshold awareness that was the opposite of the concentrated darkness-reading the Underclan's survival ran on. He couldn't do both simultaneously. The deep needed him to be entirely himself. The surface was making him into something he didn't have a word for yet.

He waited until Noor was in what he understood was her sleeping state — not confirmed by sight, since the ceiling-light was dim enough that he couldn't read her clearly from the floor, but by the change in her breathing and the absence of movement — and then he rose without making sound, which wasn't difficult. He had been lying still for hours building the exit in his mind: the window's lower panel, the latch mechanism he had studied for two days, the outside wall.

He went out the way he had come in: through the window's lower panel, which he operated in one motion with the knowledge of exact location and exact mechanism, and then down the outside of the structure by the same logic he used on tunnel walls — grip, test, move, grip. The outside of the building was a different texture than the inside: rough, dusty, with the particular grip-quality of a surface that had been exposed to weather, which was a thing he understood abstractly and now understood with his palms. He descended one level and then another. The street below was lit but quiet in the middle of the night, and he dropped the last distance and landed with the weight absorption he had been taught, and then he was in the street.

He went toward the grating he remembered.

He remembered the exact location. This wasn't remarkable. He remembered every terrain he had ever moved through. It wasn't a skill. It was what the dark needed and the deep had trained.

He hadn't said goodbye to Noor. He didn't know if the surface had a word for the thing he had been, in her space, for eight days. He didn't have a word for it himself. He had the smell, and the fact of her patience, and the image of her face from the doorway — the quality of *returned* — and he carried all of these the way he carried things the deep could not yet name: in the place below the mind, in the body, in the closing hand.

He found the grating.

He descended.

[Beat 33]
She followed him.

He hadn't heard her — she was quieter than he expected a surface person to be, and he was distracted by the grating mechanism and the descent into the Works — and he was three levels down before he felt the air-displacement behind him and turned.

He hadn't heard her at the grating either, which mattered. The grating at the Works entrance was a mechanism he knew in the dark: three motions in sequence, the handle-press that released the counterweight, the quarter-turn that set the seal, the flat-palm that engaged the lock from below. He had worked it with the automatic speed of a person who had done a thing many times, his hands moving in the darkness before he had even thought to bring them to the mechanism. He hadn't checked behind him. He had assumed, because he was alone, that the space was sealed.

He wasn't alone.

Noor. With a surface candle in her hand — a torch, a hand-held fixed-light, which gave her a wavering pool of white that was too much for his eyes but that he could navigate around. She was breathing fast from the descent and her face in the torchlight had that controlled-not-peaceful quality.

He stopped.

The air in the Works had the smell he associated with deep: the ferrocrete and the machine-work and the slightly metabolic warmth of the Warm's influence, the organized complexity of a closed system that knew what it was. He breathed it in without meaning to and the thing in him that had been wrong for eight days settled a fraction. Around them the Works' ambient sound — the pipe-rhythm, the distant machinery, the particular drone that was the tokamak's influence transmitted through infrastructure — surrounded them in the way that silence surrounded a surface person. He was home. He was three levels in and already home.

She was emphatically not home. He could hear this in her breathing: the shallow quality of a person whose body was processing too much information and couldn't decide which piece to prioritize. The confined space. The deep dark. The sound-frequencies her surface body had never learned to sort. But she had followed anyway, and her torch was steady, and she hadn't called out.

He said, for the first time to her, what he needed to say, in the Underclan's language because it was the only language that mattered down here. He said it the way Vesh said the teaching: not loud, not forceful, but with the weight of a received truth. The Skin is up. The Deep is down. You chose to follow me, which means you are choosing down, and down does not unmake itself for regret.

She didn't understand the words. He hadn't expected her to. He said it anyway, because the saying was correct, and because the Deep needed correct things said in the correct way. He had been up for eight days speaking only surface language with surface fragments. Saying something true in the real language felt like breathing after the bad air passed.

She said something else. He caught: I know and you and the shape of intention, which was I'm not stopping.

He turned and continued down. She followed. He listened to her footsteps behind him — surface-heavy, uncertain, but present — and he adjusted his pace to something she could manage without putting her light out, and they descended together into the Warm.

The hum met him at the second level. He pressed his palm to the wall and let it run up through his arm and into his chest. Behind him, Noor's torch wavered. The hum didn't acknowledge her.

The hum didn't acknowledge anyone. That was its virtue.

[Beat 34]
He brought her as far as the Works-level and stopped.

Not Homewater. The Works were the Engine Guild's territory, and the Engine Guild and the Underclan had their treaty, and he didn't know whether the treaty's terms extended to surface people he brought in uninvited. More importantly: the Works were where the Lamplighters had found him. He didn't want to be in the Works with a surface torch and a surface person and no information about who was currently in the tunnels around them.

He gestured at Noor to put out the torch. She resisted — he could read the resistance without needing her face, in the angle of her body, the way she brought the torch slightly in — and he held her gaze for a count of eight and then looked at the torch and back at her. She put it out.

The dark arrived around them. He heard her breathing change: faster, higher, the fear-response that surface people had for the dark because the dark had nothing to offer them. He understood this response abstractly and had never shared it, which wasn't courage — it was simply that the dark was home.

He said, in surface speech, using the words he had assembled over eight days: "I watch. You stay." And then, after a pause: "Safe."

He couldn't be certain she was safe. He said it anyway, because it was the closest he could come to the gesture of the Oarsman: accept the Fare, convey, return. She had given him eight days of her patience; she was about to receive the correct darkness in return.

He waited. He listened. The eastern tunnel was clear. The north junction had Cog-type footsteps, two people, moving away. The pump-hall had its regular cycle.

He reached back and found her hand in the dark — found it the way he found everything down here, by the displacement of air and the warmth of presence — and he held it for a moment with the grip that meant *I am here, I have found you, the air is well*. She went still.

Then he started moving, and she moved with him, and they went down to Homewater with only his knowledge between them and the dark.

He didn't know, yet, that above them, someone was writing the route down. That the surface was studying the images taken in the Works, and the legend had an address now, and the address was being shared among people whose interest in the deep was not scholarship.

[Beat 35]
Homewater smelled right.

He hadn't known, until he was back in it, how wrong everything had smelled for eight days. Not wrong in the way of bad air or killing-pockets — wrong in the way of absence: the surface air was full of things but none of them were familiar, and the body spent eight days reaching for the familiar and finding abundance instead, which was its own form of nothing.

The hum was here. He pressed his palm to the pipe and it came up through the metal in the register he had lived inside since he learned to walk — the chest-frequency, the bone-resonance, the specific depth of it that he had kept under "the world is still working." He hadn't realized, until it was gone, that a significant portion of his sense of safety was routed through this: the hum saying the machine is running and the machine is the world below the world.

He held his palm there for a long count.

Noor stood beside him. She was not touching anything. He could hear her breathing in the particular way she breathed when she was processing too much at once — the rhythm of a person who is maintaining control but paying a cost for it. She had been doing this since the Works. He respected it. She had not asked him to slow down, had not asked him to explain, had not done anything in the last hour except follow and breathe and keep up with a pace she had no physical preparation for. He had been watching her in the way he watched the candle: for the signs of what to do next.

He turned and looked at her. In Homewater's light — the dim green-gold of the bioluminescent farms, the ambient warmth of the thermocline — her face was a different color than it had been on the surface. Not better, not worse. Just different. In the surface's white-everything light she had been clear and easy to read; in the deep's green-gold she was what everyone was here: warm and close and slightly indistinct, readable by attention rather than by light.

She was looking up.

He followed her gaze. The ceiling of the Hollow was stone — old stone, ferrocrete above it, all the weight of the city above that. There was nothing to see. But she was looking at it with the expression of a person who is encountering an absolute, who has been given a fact of such finality that the body needs a moment before the mind catches up. She had been underground before. She lived in a structure that had underground levels. But he thought, watching her face, that she had not been underground in the way that underground was a permanent condition. That she had never stood under a ceiling that went up for meters and meters and then was just: the world, without any sky above it. The surface had been the world to her — the lid, if you inverted his frame — and she was standing under it now for the first time with no way back up except through him.

He thought: this is what the surface felt like to me.

He thought: and I am still here.

He put his hand briefly on her arm — not the grip that meant I have you, which was a direction; just contact, the reminder that another warm thing was nearby. She looked at him. Her face did a thing that was not a question, exactly, but was close to one. He held her gaze for a count of four and then looked deliberately at the pipe — the pipe with the hum in it — and back at her. She followed his gaze to the pipe, held it there, and he thought from her face that she was trying to feel what he was feeling.

She couldn't. The hum was something the body learned, like the feel of a tool you used every day, and her body had not learned it, and in the minutes she stood here it would not learn it. But she was looking at it.

"Safe," he said, for the second time. He meant it more this time. He was holding the thing that made it true.

Then he turned toward the Hollow's main space, toward the light of the candles, toward the community he had been away from for eight days and had just brought the surface into. He could hear voices. He could hear the quality of waiting that voices had when they already knew something had arrived.

He moved toward it. She followed.

[Beat 36]
Homewater did not welcome him back.

He hadn't expected ceremony — the Underclan did not do ceremony except at the formal ritual points, and his return from a Surfacing should have been one of those points. But what he found when he came through the eastern passage — down from the Works-level, through the junction where the fungus-farm thermocline hit the cistern air, into the familiar cool of the Hollow — wasn't ceremony and wasn't hostility: it was the careful blankness of a community that has received unexpected news and is deciding what to do with it.

Vesh was in the teaching-chamber. Knuckle was in the teaching-chamber. Most of the Braves were in the teaching-chamber. They had positioned themselves the way people positioned themselves when they needed to have a difficult conversation and had been waiting for the conversation's subject to arrive.

He stood in the doorway with Noor behind him, her hand still finding his shoulder-blade in the dark. He could feel her adjusting to the Hollow's air — correct, organized, nothing like the surface's abundance. The air would hit her like a wall. He gave her a moment.

"You came back," Vesh said. The teaching voice, not the ordinary one.

"I came back."

"You brought someone."

"Yes."

The silence that followed was not hostile. It was the silence of calculation — everyone in the room running through the implications, the treaty questions, the air-quality questions, the questions about what a surface person's presence meant for Homewater's structural anonymity.

Grale spoke from the back. "Surface-tainted."

Not the word he had used before, in the tunnel, the pre-Surfacing version — *that's what the Surfacing is for, to find out*. This was the verdict. The finding returned. He had watched Glim his whole life for the evidence, and now the evidence was standing in the doorway with a surface woman's hand on his back.

Glim looked at Grale across the room. Grale's face was not triumphant — that was the thing about Grale. It never was. He looked the way he always looked: clear-eyed, certain, the expression of a man who has reached an accurate conclusion and finds no pleasure in it. Glim had expected anger, or satisfaction, or the particular coldness of a person who has been vindicated. He had not expected this: the same measuring look Grale had given him at the pack-check, two weeks and a world ago, now returned with the result filled in.

"Her name is Noor," Glim said. "She followed me down. She'll need a candle."

He kept his eyes on Vesh when he said it. But he felt Grale's stillness shift — a small thing, barely perceptible. The same shift Knuckle had noticed, the quality that meant Grale was thinking rather than performing. He kept it. Grale did not dismiss dismissed things; he only set them aside.

There was a longer pause.

Sorrel crossed the room and put her hand on Noor's arm, guiding her forward. She was good with outsiders — the Surfacing had given her a quality of surface-legibility that few people in the Hollow had. Noor allowed herself to be guided, which said something about her: she was frightened and she was doing it anyway. The candle on Sorrel's belt glowed green-gold in the dark, warm and bioluminescent, nothing like the blue-white of the surface lamps that had blinded him in the Works.

Noor looked at it the way people on the surface looked at unfamiliar things: first with caution, then with something closer to wonder.

Glim looked at the teaching-chamber — the green tile, the reflected candle-light, the gathered faces of everyone who had counted as home for sixteen years. They were all watching him. Knuckle's expression held the particular quality of a man who is glad of an outcome and cannot say so yet. Vesh's face was directed toward the sound of Noor's breathing.

He had come back. He had brought something with him that could not be undone.

Both things were true, and he was standing in the doorway between them.

[Beat 37]
They came on the third day after his return.

The first sign was air. The right-hand tunnel, which came from the direction of the Works, carried a draft that was wrong — not the regular Vent Cog cycle, not the irregular leak of the broken junction, but a forced air-movement, like the Works were breathing wrong. Glim felt it on his arm at the morning-check and stood very still and felt it again.

The draft was carrying something. Not the killing air — the candle was fine. It was carrying the surface: the manufactured scents, the particular not-organic complexity that he had learned to identify in eight days on the surface and had spent the past three days purging from his own clothing and skin. Someone on the surface was in the Works, and they were using surface equipment, and the surface equipment was pushing its smell ahead of it.

He made no sound. He was already in the tunnel mouth, already moving.

He got back to the Hollow in time to tap the warning and then the lights came anyway.

Not from one direction — from two, the south junction and the east Works simultaneously, which meant they had mapped the Hollow's approaches correctly and had used both. The combined white-out was total. He had experienced the flood-lights in the Works when they caught him: the sudden blindness, the panic that lived below the panic. He had been one person in a corridor with his hands on the wall. This was the Hollow. Forty-two people in the Hollow, and the white arrived at all of them at once.

He could hear it happen. The sounds: a sharp intake from Lark, from somewhere near the cistern-mouth; the sound of a child hitting the floor — Slip, who hadn't heard the warning, whose legs simply went; the particular silence of Vesh, who had gone completely still, which was worse than a sound. And underneath all of it, the candles' distress — the glow-rats reacting to the sudden light-flooding, the bioluminescence contracting, the small animal sounds of thirty candles doing the wrong thing at once.

Glim's own candle pressed itself against his hip. He didn't look at the light. He looked at his hands.

His eyes adjusted to white the same way they adjusted to black: imperfectly, slowly, by feel. He could see the shape of the Hollow as a uniform absence of detail rather than dark — the white of everything instead of the dark of everything — and in that white he heard the surface-footsteps enter from both passages at once. Not rushing. Moving with the rhythm of people who have more light than anything in this space and know it, who have done this before and have learned that calm is faster than urgency when you hold the advantage.

He counted the footsteps. Six total. Six surface people in the Hollow.

Then: a voice.

One voice, surface speech, giving direction. Not loud — the voice of someone who understood sound-discipline in enclosed spaces, which wasn't what he had expected from surface hunters. A man's voice, level and deliberate, and the quality of it wasn't urgency but pleasure. The specific pleasure of a person who has found exactly the thing they came to find.

He didn't speak back. He began moving.

In the white-out he navigated by the sounds he knew: Knuckle's breathing, deeper than anyone else's in the Hollow; Sorrel's footfall, which was lighter on the left than the right since the previous winter's cistern-ledge fall; Noor's voice saying something in surface speech that was almost certainly directed at the footsteps entering from the south. He moved toward the chain-configuration — the retreat formation, the one they had drilled for the worst scenarios — and found Knuckle's arm in the white and gripped it in the signal that meant *I have you*.

Knuckle's hand found someone else in the dark.

The chain began to form.

He heard, from behind him and to the left, one of the surface-footsteps stop. The sound of something picked up — a small ceramic dish, and the sound a glow-rat makes when it is handled by hands it doesn't know, which is a specific distressed sound, the sound of a body registering wrongness without the vocabulary to name it. Someone had picked up a candle.

He didn't stop moving. The chain was forming — he could feel the grips propagating back from his own arm, the information traveling human-to-human: *I am here, I have you, move*. He was counting the grips. He was also counting what he heard behind him: the ceramic dish replaced. The glow-rat silent.

He didn't know what had happened to the rat after the sound stopped. He wouldn't know for some time.

He yelled the direction. Not words — direction, a sound they all knew, the sound that meant *this way, follow it, don't stop*. The chain lurched forward. The tunnel that led toward the Old Deep — the stratum below Homewater, deeper than the Hollow, older than the brick above it — was twenty-five feet ahead and his hands were on the wall and the wall was the same wall it had always been, the same rough ferrocrete, the same cold at the junction, and he could navigate this tunnel with his eyes shut.

His eyes were effectively shut. The light was everywhere.

They went.

Behind them, in the Hollow, the voice gave another instruction in the same level tone. He heard the footsteps spread through the space — methodically, taking their time, because they had all the time and all the light there was.

The voice didn't follow. It stayed. It had more to do there.

He led the chain into the tunnel and the tunnel's dark arrived like the first breath after the bad air had passed, and he heard his clan's footsteps settle into their rhythms — the Old Deep ahead, the Tartarian Empire below that, and nowhere else left — and he kept moving.

[Beat 38]
Lark was coughing.

He heard it from the lower cistern passage — not the soft throat-clearing of someone who had breathed dust, but the hard, productive work of something that had settled into the lungs and was making itself at home. He had heard that cough before. He had heard it through the wall in Noor's dwelling, in the bodies of surface people crowded together where their air ran hot with what it carried. The Hollow's air had never made that cough. The Hollow's air was old and correct.

He stood in the passage and listened and his stomach dropped the way it had dropped in the lifting-box on the surface — that lurch of the body moving in a direction the body did not choose. He had brought it down. He had carried the surface in his lungs and his skin into Homewater and the Hollow had no armor against what surface bodies carried the way they carried water, without knowing, without harm meant.

Sorrel found him there. She had been on the surface too, in her own Surfacing; she had the most surface-vocabulary of anyone in the Hollow but him. He saw on her face that she had already done the arithmetic he was only starting.

"They're not Lamplighters," she said. "The ones at the Skin."

"I know."

"They came asking for the children. Saying they know we're real." She used the surface word carefully, the way you handled a thing that might be hot. "Saying they want to *help*." She watched his face when she said it. "They brought medicine. For the cough."

He understood *help* now. He understood several surface words he had eight days of reasons to understand.

"Then I brought that too," he said. "The cure for the thing I brought."

Sorrel did not tell him it wasn't his fault. That was the thing about her, the thing she had brought back changed — she did not reach for the easy comfort when the hard one was true. She put her hand flat against the passage wall beside his, not touching him, just near, the way you set your candle next to another candle so the two lights made one. They stood like that. Down the middle passage, Lark coughed again and the sound went on too long before it ended.

"We carried it up there for them," Sorrel said quietly, meaning the surface, meaning the safety they had never asked anyone for. "And now we've carried something back." She took her hand off the wall. "It's not yours alone. I went up and came back too. It could have been me."

It was the kindest and the most honest thing anyone could have said, and it did not make the stomach-lurch stop, and she had not meant it to.

He listened to Lark's cough end. Then he went to find Vesh.

[Beat 39]
Vesh refused the Mission's medicine.

He did it without consulting Knuckle, without convening the elders, without the new-question silence he used when something needed thought. He did it the way he did the things he had already decided.

"No salt that fights the Hot Breath," he said, in the teaching voice, in the teaching-chamber. "No surface medicine in the Deep."

Lark was coughing in the next passage. They could all hear it.

Glim felt the thing happen before he chose it: the words already forming as he opened his mouth, the shape of the correction arriving faster than the decision to make it. He had heard Vesh teach two hundred times. He had helped Vesh to his feet, walked beside him, carried his correction of the candle-dish without argument. In sixteen years he hadn't once said *no* to a conclusion Vesh had delivered in the teaching voice.

"I know what it's for," he said. He heard himself say it and felt the quality of the thing he was doing. "I don't know how it works. But Lark is coughing and the Hot Breath came from me, and the surface has medicine for the thing I brought down."

The silence in the teaching-chamber was the kind that had weight. He could feel it in his jaw.

Vesh didn't turn toward him. He turned toward the passage where the coughing had been — toward the sound of it, the way he always oriented toward sound. His face was calm with the particular calm of someone who has already weighed the thing you are currently weighing.

"You've been to the surface," Vesh said.

"Yes."

"You know what surface medicine does to a Deep body."

He didn't know. He knew what it was for, not what it did. "No," he said. "But I know that Lark is coughing and that she didn't cough before I came back." His voice was level. He was making himself keep it level, because the alternative was raising it, and he had never raised it at Vesh and he didn't want to start with this. "And I know that I brought the Hot Breath down. That's also mine."

Something moved in Vesh's face — a slight tightening at the jaw, there and gone. Not anger. Something closer to the expression of a teacher who has just been shown his own lesson returned by a student who understood it differently than intended.

The coughing came again from the passage. This time it went on longer.

Vesh turned his face toward the sound.

He held that position for a count. The hum came through the floor. Nobody moved. Glim watched the old man's hands, which were folded in his lap with the stillness of someone who has decided to let the situation speak rather than his voice.

"Ask the Mission," Vesh said finally, "what they want in return."

Glim waited.

"The answer," Vesh said, "will tell us what they are."

It wasn't agreement. It was the closest Vesh came to one: not *yes* but *go and find out*, which left the question open and the door unlocked and Lark still coughing in the passage. Glim stood. Vesh's hands stayed folded. Neither of them said what had just happened — that Glim had pushed, and Vesh had moved, and the space between teacher and student had changed its dimensions without anyone naming the change.

Glim went.

[Beat 40]
The Mission wanted to register them.

Glim brought the word back to the teaching-chamber and set it down in front of Vesh like a wrong cap pulled off the frame — a thing to be examined before it was let near the others. *Register*, he said, and then he had to build the meaning out of his partial surface-words and the pieces Sorrel had given him: to put your name in the surface record. To exist, in the surface's count of things that exist.

Vesh had his palm flat on the green tile. He didn't lift it. The half-blind eyes turned a fraction toward the place Glim's voice came from, and the old man waited the new-question silence, the one that meant he was turning the thing over and was in no hurry.

"And when our names are in the surface's record?" Vesh said.

"Then we are someone in their count."

"We are already someone."

"In their count," Glim said.

Vesh's fingers moved on the tile — a small thing, the slow press-and-lift he used unconsciously when he was following a thought down past where the words had gotten to. Glim had watched that hand his whole life. He knew it the way he knew the cough now: a sign read before the meaning came.

"And when our names are in their record," Vesh said, "and the surface decides one day that the record wants tidying — that the deep is no place for people, that the registered ones should be brought up where they can be looked after, that this is for our own safety —" the fingers stilled — "will our names protect us, or hand us over?"

Glim had no answer. He had been carrying the question three days and hadn't found its bottom.

"The Mission means it," he said. "The kindness is real. I've watched the Lamplighters lie with their faces. This is not that."

"Yes," Vesh said. The fingers pressed the tile once more and lifted, and the old man turned his face fully toward Glim, which he rarely did, because turning the dead eyes toward you was not for seeing — it was for being seen turning them. "That is the part that frightens me. The hunter you can fight. You cannot fight a hand that is sure it is feeding you."

The cough came from the middle passage. Lark, or someone past Lark now. They both let it finish.

Glim didn't take the Mission's offer.

He also knew, by the third day after the conversation, that the Mission had already begun mapping Homewater — that somewhere above, in a clean room, a kind person was drawing the way down so that the kindness would know where to go.

[Beat 41]
The Hot Breath took Slip first.

He was the youngest of the Braves — six sleep-seasons from his Surfacing, still in the early years of the teaching. His First Hearing had been two months ago. He had named his candle, which the young ones did before they outgrew it. The candle's name was *Tallow*, a made-up word with no meaning in either language, which was the point: Slip's candle had a name that belonged to nobody.

Tallow was fine. Slip was not.

The cough had been manageable in Lark. Lark was older and had the kind of physical reserve that came from years of climbing and harvesting and the physical work of the Braves' training. She was still coughing but she was upright and functional. Slip was seven years old or thereabouts, and the Hot Breath in a seven-year-old was not the same as the Hot Breath in a Brave.

He ran a fever that the clan had no word for, because fevers were not an Underclan illness. His temperature was readable in the feel of him when Glim pressed the back of his hand to Slip's forehead, and the heat was wrong — too much, the body doing something he had no framework for interpreting. The surface called it *fever*. He knew the word. He didn't know what it meant in terms of the body's function, which was the knowledge you needed to decide what to do.

Noor knew. She had been watching from the edge of the clan's space — not integrated, not fully trusted, but present in the way that a person with Sorrel as escort was allowed to be present — and she crossed the space to where Slip was lying and she put her hand where Glim's had been and she said a word to herself in surface speech that he couldn't decode but that had the quality of: *this is bad*.

She said to him, carefully: "The Mission's medicine. Bring it now."

He looked at her.

"I know you don't trust them," she said. She was using simple surface speech, the vocabulary she had learned worked with him. "I don't trust them either. But the medicine and the Mission are not the same thing. The medicine works."

He went to get Vesh.

[Beat 42]
They took the medicine. It came too late for Slip and just enough for the others.

Slip had been in Glim's cohort — not a close Brave, not a rival, but a constant: a presence at the thermocline fungus-harvest for six years, the person who could read the spore-load in the thermal gradient by touch and smell in a way that Glim couldn't, the person whose hands checked the frame-mesh for rot while Glim checked the yield numbers, working side by side without speaking because the work didn't need speech and Slip had never been one to use it otherwise. He had not been loud, had not been vivid; he had been reliable in the way the cistern was reliable — present, useful, assumed. The Bright Fever had shown Glim the cost of assuming a person.

Vesh did not speak at Slip's sending-off. The ritual was the Brave-master's, and Knuckle spoke it, flat and correct and without decoration. Knuckle had spoken sendings-off before — not many, the tribe's death rate in the normal years was low enough that a sending-off was event rather than routine — but enough that the words were in his voice the way route-names were in Glim's: not retrieved but carried. He did not embroider. He said the name, tapped it first in the sign-language of the pipe so the Current would hear it in the way it heard, then spoke it aloud. He said what Slip had known and what Slip had done, in the plain declarative of someone listing a Brave's competencies. He did not say what Slip had meant. That was not the ritual's form, and it would not have helped, and Knuckle was a person who understood what helped and did not spend effort on what did not.

The glow-rat called Tallow was placed in the cistern as the final gesture: a small green-gold light on the black water, moving out toward the center, visible for a long time before the dark took it.

Glim watched Tallow go. He thought: Slip would have said something to fill the silence, if someone else were being sent off. Not something sentimental. Something specific — a fact about the person's work, a corrective to a competency claim that was slightly off, the way Slip always corrected things that were slightly off, not because he wanted to be difficult but because he had a threshold below which imprecision was intolerable and slightly off was below that threshold. Glim would have been annoyed by it in the moment. He was aware, watching Tallow's light on the water, that he would not have the occasion to be annoyed by it again.

The Lamplighters came while Slip's light was still on the water.

They came the same way they always came — with the floods, from two directions, the south junction and the east Works — and this time there was no warning, because Glim had been at the sending-off and not at the tunnel-mouth where the warning would have lived. The white came before the sound. The white poured through the south junction first, moving like fast water, filling the tunnel and then the cavity beyond it, and the first thing it did was erase the green-gold on the black water. Tallow's light went out — not the death of bad air, but the death of contrast, the candle swallowed by a brightness so total that what had been visible was now part of a uniformity.

The chain formed anyway, because the drill was in them, because Knuckle had put it in them with enough repetition that the white-out didn't stop it from running. The sound of the drill, heard through the pipes in daily practice, was different from the drill running in actual white-out — the difference between knowing a route on a map and moving through it at speed with no light and your heartbeat wrong. The chain ran on muscle, not thought. His hand found the next person and that person's hand found the next and the chain assembled itself from its own practiced shape, forty-something people linking in the dark and the white and the noise of the floods arriving.

They went through the tunnel toward the Old Deep.

The Old Deep was below Homewater, below the ferrocrete, the first place the architecture turned from industrial to something else: the brick and tile of an older kind of building, streets that had been enclosed by later construction until they were tunnels without any having designed them to be tunnels. The air changed here — older, denser, carrying the memory of what it had moved through before the walls closed over it. Glim could feel the change in the draft on his face, the quality of cool that was different from the Warm's cool. He knew this air. He had been in the Old Deep before, in the route-training, the way you were in all the clan's known territory before you were a full Brave.

The chain moved through the Old Deep at the pace the chain could sustain. The slowest person set the speed; the chain's length communicated through grip-signals, the tapped pressure that meant I have the one behind me passing forward and confirming. He counted the pulses. All forty-one behind him. All present.

And then past the Old Deep, into the strata the Underclan had never entered.

The Tartarian Empire. The ghost-country. The place where the dead lived, in the tribe's theology — and in the practical fact of any person who had gone there and not come back, which was every person who had ever tested the taboo, which was part of why the taboo had held as long as it had. He had never been to the Tartar. No living member of the clan had been to the Tartar.

They went anyway. There was nowhere else left.

[Beat 43]
Noor was in the chain. He hadn't planned for this. He had grabbed a hand in the flood-white and it had been hers, and by the time he knew it was hers they were two tunnels past the Hollow and he wasn't turning back.

She moved as well as he had any right to expect. He had taken her hand in the way that meant I am here, follow me, and she had followed — not blindly, she was using her other hand on the wall the way he had shown her in the Works, feeling the texture of the surfaces and using them — but with the quality of a person who has made a decision and is implementing it without reservation.

He counted. It was the first thing you did in movement-under-pressure: count the chain. The chain of people behind him was forty-two long: forty-one Underclan and Noor. He counted the grip-responses in the dark, the chain of hands that passed information backward, the tapped signals — still here, still moving, I have the one behind me — and each response was confirmation, each pulse of pressure from behind him through the human chain a life accounted for. Forty-two pulses. Forty-two moving. He kept his feet on the path he knew and let the count continue in the back of his mind the way the hum continued, a background certainty he checked against when something felt wrong.

The flood-white was behind them. Distant now, because the Lamplighters did not know these routes. The clan knew them, because the clan had been mapping them for three generations, adding to the route-memory that Knuckle carried and that he had been learning since he was a Stray: the layout of the Old Deep below Homewater, the places the ferrocrete thinned and became old brick and tile, the locations of the dry spaces within the inundation network. The Lamplighters came from above. They mapped from above. Everything that was not above was not their territory.

They moved through the Old Deep in a human chain through the complete dark.

The air in the Old Deep was different from Homewater's air — older, stiller, with a weight to it that was the weight of centuries of sealed space. The brick and tile of the deeper architecture had a different smell than the ferrocrete above: earthy, almost living, a dampness that was not dangerous but was strange, the smell of things that had not been disturbed for a long time being moved through. His candle — he had grabbed a candle from the shelf when the floods first appeared, one of the sentinel ones, and it was clasped in his free hand — gave a steady green-gold glow.

He watched the candle and it watched the air.

What he noticed, in the particular way that the body noticed things it had no language for and held them anyway: Noor did not panic. She had grown up on the surface, where the above was open and the below was abstract, where the dark was the exception and the width of things was unlimited and the distances were countable in units she had reference for. She was underground in a chain of people she had known for three months at the end of a flood, going deeper into the dark on someone else's instinct, and her hand was steady and her feet were on the ground and she was not fighting the situation.

He had expected to have to manage her fear the way he sometimes managed the Strays' fear: grounding, reorientation, the calm deliberate voice that said the air is good and your body is wrong and I have been here before. He had expected it the way he expected the floods to be wet — a fact of the thing, not a surprise. She had not given him anything to manage.

He didn't know what to do with this. He held it in the same place he held the candle and the route-memory and Knuckle's grip-pulse two links back in the chain: data. Relevant. Not yet understood.

He did not look back. The tunnel was narrow and the Tartar was ahead and the dead, if they were there, would have to manage with company.

[Beat 44]
The Tartarian Empire was beautiful.

This was the first thing and the wrong thing — not that beauty was wrong, but that he hadn't expected it, and the wrongness of expectation in a place of genuine danger was itself a danger. The ceilings vaulted high above them, and the walls were tiled to the waterline with glazed work: faces and shapes and patterns in colours the candle-light kept changing. The bioluminescence moved through the space and the tiles threw it back from a hundred angles and the Tartar glowed.

He checked the candle. Fine. The air was cold and still and very old but it was good air.

He needed everyone to know the air was good. He made the three-finger sign — flat palm, three fingers extended toward the ceiling, the signal for the air is well, breathe normally — and watched it pass backward through the chain of people behind him: each person receiving it, registering it, passing it to the next. Forty-one pairs of eyes moving the signal back through the vault until it reached the last person.

They stopped. Forty-one people plus Noor, plus candles. He counted everyone.

Forty-two people breathing. The candles all fine.

He sat down on the tile and pressed his palms flat against it and tried to feel the hum.

It was not there.

He pressed harder. The tile was cold under his palms — colder than the tile in the Old Deep, colder than the ferrocrete of the tunnels above. Cold the way stone was cold when it had been sealed from the Warm for a very long time, when the thermocline's reach had never arrived here, when the tokamak's breath had dispersed in the levels above and left nothing to warm what was below. The cold went through his palms and up his wrists and he pressed harder and there was still nothing.

The Current was silent here. Had always been silent here.

He had understood the taboo as superstition. Not with contempt — the tribe's taboos had practical foundations, always: the air that killed, the routes that flooded, the old deep that held things the tribe couldn't afford to learn at cost. He had thought the Tartar-fear was the same: a practical danger translated into sacred language because practical language was harder to enforce, because a child frightened of ghosts would stay out of the deep cold more reliably than a child merely warned about air chemistry. He had understood this as the tribe's method, and he had respected it as their method, without quite believing the ghosts.

Now he sat with his palms on the cold tile and the Current was absent in the way that a person was absent when they had died — not the absence of someone who had stepped away and would return, but the full and total gone-ness of a thing that was no longer where it had been.

He had grown up with the hum the way he had grown up with his own heartbeat. He hadn't known, until it was absent, how much of his orientation in the world ran through it. In Homewater the hum was background — below notice, the way the cistern's drip was below notice — and you didn't notice it until you were away from it, and then you noticed you had been living inside it the way a fish lived inside water. The Skin had been the first test: the hum thinning as he climbed, losing some of its depth and frequency register, audible still but attenuated. The surface had been the worst — the Current absent almost entirely, present only as a faint resonance in anything metal, in pipes and grates, barely enough to find if you pressed your ear to the wall and listened.

He had come back from the surface partly to escape that absence.

Here, below the Tartarian levels, below the Warm, below everything, the hum was not attenuated. It was simply not there. As if whatever carried it — the pipe, the cable, the living current of the machine-work that underran the deep city like a second nervous system — had not reached here, or had reached here and stopped, or had never been laid.

The tribe was watching him.

He understood that they were watching him because they needed to know what he knew, and what he knew was on his face, and what was on his face was what they had seen on the face of the Current in his palms and had answered correctly. They were waiting for him to make the sign for something. He needed to give them a sign that they could use.

He rose from the tile. He made the sign for breathe — two fingers, tap to the breastbone, a reminder that the air was good and the body's panic was a lie. Then the sign for rest — palm flat to the ground, a slow lowering, a command that phrased itself as an invitation. Then the sign for wait.

They sat.

He walked to the far wall and pressed his palm flat against the tile — a face in glazed ceramic, ancient beyond counting, looking out from the wall with the unconcerned gaze of something that had been looked at long enough that it had stopped noticing. The tile was cold under his palm. The face looked through him. He could feel nothing in the wall except cold and the weight of the stone beyond it.

He had brought the surface down. The Tartar was the consequence. He would not number its costs while people were watching him decide.

He turned back to the clan.

"The air is good," he said, quietly, in the voice that carried just far enough. "The cold is bad but manageable. We will rest here." He looked at each face — tired, frightened, watching him the way candles watched bad air. "We will find what we need and we will decide what comes next. But the air is good. We are breathing."

He watched the faces. The panic was a wave; a wave needed something to break on. He was what they had.

He sat down on the tile again and put his palms back on the cold and waited, with all forty-two people around him, for the Current to confirm or deny that this was survivable.

It confirmed nothing.

He breathed anyway.

[Beat 45]
Vesh had the Hot Breath.

He hadn't said. He was the Listener; announcing his own illness had a specific weight in the clan's structure that was not the same as a Brave announcing it. He had continued to move and to give direction and to be Vesh, with the particular quality of Vesh's stillness and Vesh's certainty, and Glim had not noticed until he saw the old man's face in the candle-light of the Tartarian Empire's vaults, where there was no hum to distract him and nothing to navigate and the candles threw everything back from the tiles.

The face was not Vesh's face. It had Vesh's structure — the same half-blind eyes, the same set of the mouth, the same architecture of a person who has been thinking continuously for seven decades — but the surface of it was wrong, in the way that still water was wrong when you tip the vessel: the temperature gone out of it, replaced with something that was not Vesh's temperature.

He sat beside the old man without speaking. The clan was settling itself around the vaults, moving quietly, exploring the space with the same careful approach they brought to any new territory. Noor was with Sorrel. The candles were all fine.

He couldn't make himself say *you have the Hot Breath*. The words were there. They would not come. He sat with that silence for a count, long enough to understand that the silence was its own kind of answer — that he already knew, and had not known before, and the knowing was recent enough that his body had not finished accounting for it.

"You knew," he said. Not an accusation. A recognition. The sound of his own voice was strange to him in the vault's acoustics — the space was too large for the Hollow's close sound-discipline, and his words returned to him from the tile ceiling with a small delay, an echo the Deep had never taught him.

Vesh made a small sound that was not words.

"The Hot Breath was already in you when you agreed to take the Mission's medicine for Lark."

"Lark needed it more urgently," Vesh said. His voice had the teaching quality, which meant he was maintaining the register because he still could. "I have been old for some time. My body is accustomed to managing without cooperation from the things inside it."

It was not a complaint. It was a statement of the mechanism, which was what Vesh did with everything.

"The surface medicine would have —"

"Perhaps." He paused. "I have spent seventy years listening to the Current. I would like to do that as long as possible. The medicine may or may not extend the time. What it certainly does is change the environment. I have learned to work in the one I have."

Glim looked at the tiles. They caught his candle's glow and returned it. The vaulted ceiling went up to heights he couldn't measure. His hands were flat on the tile behind him, weight leaning back — the posture of someone who has run out of argument and is trying not to show it.

"It's quiet here," he said. "The hum is gone."

"Yes." Vesh's hand moved, just slightly, in the direction of the tile. "They built beautiful things. The people who made this. And whatever they had of the Current's voice, they sealed it out." He paused. "I have been wondering, all the walk down, what they sealed it out for. What do you think?"

Glim looked at the vaults, at the faces in the tile, and for a moment he could not answer, because the question that wanted answering was a different one, and it was sitting on his chest with all its weight: that the old man beside him was dying in a place the clan had only reached because Glim had gone up.

"I think," he said, slowly, "they wanted to make a place where the Current couldn't find them. They wanted a place that was only theirs."

Vesh was quiet. Then: "What do you think of that?"

"I think it was lonely," Glim said.

Vesh made a sound that, in a different context, might have been a laugh. "Yes," he said. "I also think that."

The silence lasted a long time. It was not uncomfortable — it was the silence of two people who have said the important thing and are letting it settle.

When Vesh reached into the fold of his robe, Glim had not moved from his leaning posture, weight back on his braced palms. Vesh's fingers moved with the precision of someone who trusts the hand over the eye. He found what he was looking for and brought it out — the silence-piece, the carved bone teaching-marker, smooth from decades of handling. He held it out toward where Glim sat.

Glim looked at Vesh's hand. The piece sat in his open palm, worn to the exact shape of a thumb's pressure, the bone yellowed to something close to candle-amber.

He did not move for a moment. He was aware of the tile under his palms, cold and fine, and the candle's warmth beside him, and the absence of the hum — the specific quality of an absence he had felt every day of his life and was only now, in its removal, learning to name. He shifted his weight forward and opened one hand.

Vesh waited until the hand was open. Only then did he press the piece into Glim's palm and close Glim's fingers over it.

The bone was warm from Vesh's body. The warmth was the wrong kind of warmth — too specific, too recent, a warmth that said *this came from a person who is cold now in a way that will not reverse*. Glim held it and did not let his face move. The not-letting cost something; he felt it in the muscles of his jaw and around his eyes, the body straining to do the thing a Brave's discipline asked and very nearly failing.

"I won't need it where I'm going," Vesh said. Not the teaching voice — the ordinary one.

Glim said nothing. His hand stayed closed.

"Hold it when the hum is absent," Vesh said. "The bone remembers."

[Beat 46]
He went deeper into the Tartar alone.

Not far — the Brave's training was in him, and the training said: no unknown territory without a candle-check and a count. He went as far as he could go on one slow breath held, released, replaced — a distance he could retrace — past the vault where the clan had stopped, and into a larger space: a hall, the ceiling too high to see, the floor the same beautiful tile as the vault but wider and without the collapsed section. Vaulted stone above and tile below and the hum absent in both directions.

The hall was extraordinary in the way that things were extraordinary when they were the wrong scale for a human being to fully process. The vault they had sheltered in was large by any deep-city standard he had ever entered: larger than the Hollow, larger than the Works' main staging cavity. This hall was three of those side by side and twice the height and still intact — the ceiling lost in shadow above his candle's reach, the tile stretching to walls he could barely distinguish from the dark. He walked to the center of it and his footsteps made a sound and the sound had a travel-time before it came back to him, a genuine echo, a thing he had never encountered in the Underclan's territory because the spaces were never that large.

He sat cross-legged on the tile and put both palms flat.

The tile here was the same as in the vault: cold, inert, carrying none of the resonance he associated with active infrastructure. He knew, rationally, that the hum was not the floor itself but the thing below the floor — the pipe-network, the conduit runs, the kilometers of active infrastructure that the upper city sat on top of without knowing — and that the Tartar's depth, its age, its sealing from the Warm, had simply put it beyond the reach of whatever carried the Current's voice. He knew this. He also knew that knowing it did not make the absence any less wrong in the body.

He waited for the absence of the hum to tell him something.

He had brought the surface down. He had brought it down by going up — by being Glim, by choosing the left tunnel, by not dying when he should have, by smelling Noor in the doorway and letting his eyes sting rather than controlling the reaction. Every choice he had made correctly by the Underclan's law had led here, to forty-two people in the ghost-country without a home to go back to.

Grale was right, he thought. The surface puts things in you.

He did not know, exactly, what the surface had put in him. He could name some of it: a smell that was and was not a person. A sense of the above that was not fear but a kind of altered scale, as if something in his spatial understanding had been permanently revised. The knowledge that the outside of the Underclan was not the Skin and the Works and the Cog-rows but was something enormously larger, something that had its own weather and its own light and its own arrangement of consequence, in which forty-two people who lived below it were a rounding error.

And then, from below — not the floor, not the tile, but from some depth below even the Tartarian Empire, below the history and the beauty and the sealed silence — he felt it.

Not the hum. Not the Current's voice in the frequency he knew, the chest-pressure, the below-hearing register. Something different. A single beat, lower and slower, the way a very large body moving at a very long distance sends a shiver through everything between itself and you, arriving after it has already passed. Lower than the hum by a significant register. The kind of frequency that is felt in the organs rather than heard, that moves through the body as sensation before the mind can classify it as sound.

He pressed his palms harder into the tile. His body had gone completely still, the stillness that wasn't calm but was the evacuation of everything except attention.

The pulse did not repeat.

He stayed as he was. Palms flat. Body still. The candle steady — good air, no movement — the flame giving its clean green-gold light to the beautiful useless floor. He counted his own heartbeats and waited, because the Brave's training was also in him on this: a sound heard once is a sound not yet confirmed. You stayed. You waited. You let the territory declare itself.

The hall declared nothing.

After a long time — he did not know how long — he became aware that he was cold, and that the clan was in the vault behind him with nothing that generated heat except their bodies, and that whatever was below the Tartar was either not repeating or had not been a pulse at all but was a product of his own blood-pressure in his palms and the quality of very deep silence in a large stone room.

He kept it. Not explained. Not dismissed. Kept.

He went back to the clan.

[Beat 47]
He told no one about the pulse. Not immediately.

The hum was the Warm's machinery — the tokamak ring, the pump cycles, the live cabling that ran through the rock like a second nervous system. He had felt the hum his whole life. He knew its frequency the way he knew the cold on the west-facing tunnel walls: not thought, just body.

The pulse was different. It came once, through the stone of the Tartarian Empire's deepest accessible vault, while he had his palms pressed flat to the floor and his eyes open in the dark. A single beat, lower than the hum, with a different character — not mechanical repetition but *interval*, the way a heartbeat isn't a metronome. Then nothing.

He pressed his palms to the cold tile again and again in the dark, in different places, and got back only the cold and the absence. He kept his palms there longer than was useful. He didn't go to Knuckle first, as he had planned to. He sat with it through one full sleep-cycle, listening to the clan breathe around him, waiting to be sure of something he couldn't name. When the clan stirred and Noor and Sorrel began organizing the food stocks, he stood up and didn't press his palms to the tile again.

He let it stay where it was, in the place the body keeps what the mind can't yet use.

What he knew was that the clan had to go back.

Not to Homewater — he didn't know yet what Homewater looked like now, whether the Lamplighters had set permanent lights or had moved through and gone. Not to the surface. Back: toward the problem, not away from it. Running deeper into the Tartar would eventually hit the bad air or the drowned voids or the deeper Made Things or simply the end of food and water, and none of these were plans.

He made his case to Knuckle first, because Knuckle was a practical man.

"The Lamplighters know our routes," he said. "They have the surface maps. They don't know the Cog tunnels."

"The Cogs don't like us," Knuckle said.

"No. But they like the Lamplighters less." He had spent time in the Works before the Surfacing, in the uneasy border-time when Cog business and Underclan business occasionally coincided. He knew the Cogs' grievance against the surface — the surface that had built the Works and then forgotten them, that had left the Cogs to maintain the machinery in an informal labor arrangement that wasn't quite slavery and wasn't quite independence. The Cogs weren't allies. But they had their own reasons to keep the surface out of the deep. "They won't help us because they like us. They'll help us if we make it worth their while."

"What do we have worth their while?"

Glim thought of eight days on the surface. Of the devices and the maps and the networks and the route-information he had absorbed. Of Noor, who could translate between the surface bureaucratic world and the underground economy.

"Information," he said. "The surface's information, brought down."

[Beat 48]
The Cog settlement in the Works was called the Bindery, and it smelled of machine-oil and old grain and the specific sourness of people who worked with their hands in confined spaces without enough daylight.

Glim knew the smell of the Bindery. He had been here twice before the Surfacing — once as a young Brave bringing trade-goods that the Underclan's upper tunnels occasionally produced for the maintenance workers, and once accompanying Grale on a negotiation that Grale hadn't fully explained and that had ended with a handshake and a silence that Glim had learned not to ask about. He knew the Cog hierarchy: the Chief Fitter, who oversaw the maintenance schedule; the Pipe-Readers, who interpreted the Works' machinery the way Listeners interpreted the Current; the gangs of Fitters and Runners who did the actual labor.

He didn't know the current Chief Fitter by name.

"Her name is Dross," Noor said, beside him. They were crouched at the Bindery's entrance passage, watching the light through the grille. "She was the deputy under the previous one. He died eighteen months ago." She paused. "The surface administration recorded it. They have a legal obligation to maintain the Works staff because of the maintenance covenant, but they haven't fulfilled it in three cycles."

"What does that mean for Dross?"

"She's been doing the work of two people with half the parts."

He looked at her. She had the particular quality she sometimes had of knowing things and not performing them — of simply having the information and giving it across without display.

"How do you know all of this?"

"The records aren't secret. The Mission's database has full maintenance covenant documentation. I looked." She paused. "I looked when you told me we were going to the Cogs."

He considered this. "You've been thinking ahead."

"It seemed useful." Her voice had the flatness that wasn't coldness but was focus.

They went in together. He had decided they should go in together — that Noor's presence was a signal, not a liability, the thing that said *we bring you the surface's documents, we bring you someone who can read them*, and the signal was more powerful than the risk.

Dross was a small woman with grease permanently worked into the lines of her palms and the look of a person who hadn't slept adequately in approximately eighteen months. She looked at Glim with the wariness of a Cog meeting an Underclan person, which was its own history. She looked at Noor with a different wariness: the wariness of someone who hasn't seen a surface person in a very long time and has reasons to prefer that distance.

"You're Underclan," she said. To Glim.

"Yes."

"You're surface." To Noor.

"I came down," Noor said. Not *I was taken* or *I'm not anymore* — just the action, which was the most accurate thing.

"The Lamplighters are in your upper tunnels," Dross said, to Glim.

"Yes."

"They'll be in the Works next."

"Yes."

She turned back to her workbench. "I know. We've been watching." She picked up a tool that Glim had no name for and set it down again. "What do you want from me."

It wasn't a question. It was a statement of the negotiation's opening.

"We want to use the infrastructure," Glim said. "The pump-halls. The sealed corridors. The locks."

Dross turned around. "Against them."

"Against the flood-lights. Against their movement through the upper Works."

She looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes moved to Noor, then back.

"What do we get," she said.

[Beat 49]
Noor gave them the maintenance covenant.

Not a summary of it — the actual document, pulled from the Mission's internal archive via the surface-connected device she carried. Three hundred and twelve pages of covenant obligations, maintenance schedules, parts requisitions, budget allocations, and the record of every time the surface administration had failed to fulfill its side of the agreement. Eighteen months of documented non-delivery. Forty-seven outstanding parts requests. The names of the people on the surface who had signed the continuation-forms and the counter-signatures that had never come.

She gave them all of it. She showed Dross how to read the document structure, which fields were legally binding, which ones were discretionary. She showed her the budget code that should have funded the Bindery's maintenance staff expansion and had instead been reallocated to the Lamplighter program.

Glim sat against the wall and watched Dross read.

It took a long time. The Pipe-Readers came in partway through — three of them, the senior ones, people who had spent their lives reading the Works' machinery and who had their own grievance relationship with the surface administration. They read over Dross's shoulder. One of them made a sound that wasn't language but was clearly an opinion.

Glim watched the oil-lamp on Dross's workbench. It was a good lamp — steady flame, no smoke. The Cogs maintained their things well, which was the whole of the Cog self-understanding: *we maintain things, and the things we maintain are what keep the surface habitable, and the surface forgets that, and we do not forgive the surface for forgetting it.*

The Lamplighter program budget code appeared on page forty-eight of the covenant documentation. The Lamplighters' operational budget was listed as a sub-line of the infrastructure maintenance allocation. The maintenance fund that should have paid for Dross's parts had been repurposed, in writing, to pay for the flood-lights.

When Dross got to page forty-eight, she stopped reading and looked up.

"The lights run on our budget," she said.

"Yes," Noor said.

"The budget that's supposed to fund the Works."

"Yes."

Dross set the device down. She looked at Glim.

"You want the pump-halls," she said. "You want the seal-locks in corridor G-seven through G-eleven."

"Yes."

"G-seven through G-eleven flood if we open the pressure-diverts."

"Yes."

"There's Lamplighter equipment in G-eight." She said this without inflection. "If the corridor floods, that equipment becomes debris."

"Yes."

She looked at the document again. At page forty-eight, which was still on the screen.

"We'll need it back," she said. "The budget allocation. When this is over — however this ends — we'll need the covenant honored. Someone will need to advocate on our behalf."

"I will," Noor said.

"You're Mission."

"I was. I'm not sure what I am now." She paused. "I know what covenant documentation looks like. I know how to submit a formal grievance. I know who to file it with and what the escalation path is. None of that depends on what the Mission thinks of me."

Dross looked at her for a long time.

"Show me," she said, "which corridors you need."

[Beat 50]
They did the operation in two shifts with a rest between, because the people doing it needed to rest.

The Cog Runners knew every valve and pressure-gate and seal-lock in the Works the way the Underclan Braves knew every air-pocket and current-point in the tunnels: in their bodies, not just in their heads. Glim watched a Cog Runner named Pell explain the pressure-divert mechanism to Sorrel in a language of hand-gestures and demonstration and improvised words for concepts that did not exist in either of their vocabularies. They understood each other. He had not predicted that two peoples who had raided each other for salt would lean over the same valve like this and forget, for the length of the work, to mistrust.

The mechanics he had predicted. The flood-light units in corridor G-eight ran on a power draw routed through the Works' main grid and needed a stable, dry place to sit. The pressure-diverts in G-seven could send the secondary cisterns into the G-through corridors. Water and the Lamplighters' equipment were incompatible in a way the Lamplighters hadn't planned for, because they had planned for Underclan tactics — small, quiet, close — and not for someone opening a corridor's veins.

The light itself was the second thing. The patrol used the floods on a cycle, and between cycles there was dark, and the Underclan lived in dark and the Lamplighters did not. In the dark the surface men moved slowly, with equipment, and the equipment made noise.

It took most of a shift. The Cog Runners opened the diverts in sequence while Dross watched the Lamplighter patrol through a Channeler's feed pulled off the Works' own sensor nodes without anyone's leave. Glim led the Braves through the dark-periods, around the patrol and not through it, moving by air-read and memory and the patrol-rhythm Noor had given him.

Partway through the second shift the G-seven patrol deviated. He felt it before he understood it — the wrong footstep pattern in the air, three sets where there had been two, coming toward the junction where Pell was working the last pressure-gate. If they turned the corner the operation was finished. Pell needed four more minutes.

Grale went left at the junction without being asked.

Left put him in the light — the flood-lamp's outer edge, the ragged margin of the patrol's coverage — and Glim heard him do the thing the Hollow trained you your whole life never to do: he made himself loud. The tap-speech turned inside out, signal made into noise, a body announcing itself on purpose in a place where being heard was being taken. The patrol swung toward the sound. Grale drew them east.

Glim stood at the junction and did not move.

Something happened in his chest before the knowing did. A pulling, deep and physical, below the ribs — the body's answer to the wrong thing, arriving before the brain had counted what it was. His hands had already made fists at his sides. He didn't know when that had happened.

He knew the layout. He knew where Pell was, and how long four minutes was in a corridor that was already filling, and what the left-hand passage became once the diverts opened. He knew all of it at once and complete, and none of the knowing helped, because the knowing only told him that there was nothing he could do that would not undo the thing Grale had just bought. A seal-lock dropped behind his sternum, hard and physical, closing over something that would have to be opened later, when there was time, when there was air to open it in.

He didn't follow. He couldn't follow. Grale had known he couldn't follow; it was in the way he had gone — not fast, not afraid, the level pace of a person who has already worked the thing through and is only carrying out the answer.

*I've watched you my whole life*, Grale had said, before the Surfacing. *I've watched what stays.*

Grale had watched him his whole life for the surface in him, sure it would show. And in the end it was Grale who walked into the light, and Glim who stood in the dark where he belonged and let him. He didn't know what to do with that. He didn't have time to do anything with it. He held it the way you hold a thing that will break you if you grip it wrong, and he kept his hands loose, and he stayed where Grale had needed him to stay.

He did not come back. He was on the wrong side of the G-eight seal-lock when the Runners opened the diverts, and the water took the junction before he reached the passage that would have carried him through.

When the water hit the G-eight equipment, the flood-lights in that corridor died.

The patrol pulled back. Not to the surface — a regroup; the Lamplighter commander had protocols, and the protocols said pull back and reassess when the infrastructure failed. Noor had told him that. He had used it. Not a victory. Space. Space was time, and time was the clan moving back toward Homewater.

In the corridor outside the G-eight junction he felt the hum resume — faint, the upper Works' thin approximation of it, not the deep full register of the lower tunnels, but present. The chest-pressure. The below-hearing frequency. He stood still and let it move through him.

It was not the Second Word. He knew that. He didn't know what the pulse in the Tartar had been and this was not its answer. This was only the hum, daily and ordinary and beneath the Lamplighters' frequency and therefore theirs.

Still theirs.

He didn't let himself think about Grale yet — couldn't, wouldn't, because thinking about it now was the thing that made a person stop moving, and Grale had gone left precisely so that Glim would not have to stop. He owed Grale the moving. The accounting would come, and it would not be in numbers; it would be the kind that arrived in the body in the dark and stayed. Later. Later he would open the seal-lock behind his sternum and let the water in.

He moved.

[Beat 51]
Homewater had changed.

This wasn't a surprise. He had expected it to change. He hadn't been able to predict the specific shape of the change, which was the thing you could not do: you could know that a thing would be different without knowing what the different thing would be.

The flood-lights were gone. The Lamplighters had taken them when they pulled back — he did not know if this was policy or improvisation, but the effect was that Homewater was dark again, the familiar dark, the Underclan dark of candles and bioluminescence and the slow blue-green of the fungal growths that had been here before any of them and would be here after.

But the space was wrong.

The smell was wrong first — not dangerous, not chemical, not the specific wrongness of a bad-air pocket, but wrong in the way that a familiar thing smelled wrong when strangers had occupied it, when objects had been moved and returned to the approximate but not the exact position. They had searched it. He could tell from the way the storage-alcoves sat — slightly open, slightly disturbed, like eyes that had been held shut and then released. The Lamplighters had gone through everything.

Most things were still there. This surprised him. He had expected more removal.

Sixteen of the forty-two people had lost goods they considered important. Three of the storage alcoves had been damaged, not by malice — he couldn't read malice into it, he would not read malice into it; damaged the way you damaged things when you were searching quickly and didn't care about the space, which was its own thing but not the same thing. Candle-Two's sleeping place had been taken apart and not put back together, and when Candle-Two came to it she stood in the entrance for a long time without speaking.

He helped her put it back together. This took a while because Candle-Two had particular arrangements that mattered to her and she couldn't always articulate them in words, and he had to watch carefully and hand her things and ask the right kinds of questions.

When they were done, she looked at the space. It was approximately right.

"Still mine," she said.

"Yes," he said. "Still yours."

He counted the clan again. He had counted them every arrival for as long as he could remember, because counting was what you did after movement: you confirmed what you had. Thirty-eight people. He had started with forty-two. Three had not made it back — Leaf, in the Hollow, when the flood-lights first hit. Grale, in the G-eight corridor, in the dark between cycles. A third whose name he repeated to himself once, completely, before he placed it in the part of him that was not yet able to account for it.

Plus Vesh.

Thirty-eight. Plus Noor.

Thirty-nine.

[Beat 52]
Vesh died on the third day back.

Not dramatically. His body had done the work it could do for the length of the crisis. That was the thing about Vesh — his body had always done the work it needed to do at the time it needed to do it, and then it stopped, and the Hot Breath had been very far advanced by the time he arrived in the Tartar, and there was nothing to be done.

Glim was with him. He had arranged to be with him.

He didn't know what he expected from the experience of being with the Listener when the Listener died. He had thought perhaps there would be something to mark it — some quality of the air, some shift in the hum, some perceivable change in the frequency that Vesh had spent his life attending to. He didn't know if that was an Underclan belief or something he had assembled from the pieces of his own experience. He had never been present when someone very important to the structure of the world died.

What happened was: Vesh breathed in the way he had been breathing, which was with effort, and then he did not breathe, and that was the difference.

The hum continued at its ordinary frequency.

Glim sat with the body for the time that was appropriate — he counted it by his own breathing rather than by any external measure, which was how the Underclan had always counted the important things. He pressed his palm to the tile beside the sleeping-mat. The hum moved through the stone at the frequency it had always moved.

He noted this carefully. He had expected — or half-expected, or some part of him had been listening for — a change in the frequency after the Listener died. A slowing, a deepening, some response from the Current that corresponded to the removal of the person who had been its primary attendant for sixty-three years. The hum did not change. It had not known, or did not care, or the distinction was not one the hum made: between a world with Vesh in it and a world without him. The Warm ran on regardless. The tokamak ring below processed its fuel at the rate the fuel required. The machine was not listening. The machine simply was.

This wasn't a comfort. It was a fact. Glim had been taught to prefer facts over comfort, and he had preferred them for most of his life, and he preferred them now, even when the fact was that the person who had taught him to prefer facts was gone and the world had not registered it.

He sat. He counted. He breathed.

The room was Vesh's teaching-chamber — not exactly, but the chamber adjacent, the one Vesh had used for rest in his later years, which smelled of the old stone and the specific oil of the lamp he kept burning on the shelf, and the faint quality that Glim had always identified as Vesh-smell without having a more precise word for it. It would not smell this way for much longer. The body would be removed. The room would be someone else's. The smell would go into the stone and become indistinguishable from the other accumulated presences that stone absorbed over decades.

He pressed his palm harder. The hum came up through the stone without editorial. He held his hand there until the count was complete.

Knuckle and Sorrel came for the body when the time was done, to bring it to the deep-water disposal that was the Underclan's practice, and Glim stood in the doorway and watched them carry him, and then he went to find Noor.

She was in the transit alcove near Homewater's main passage — the one where the path split toward the upper Works and the lower tunnels. She had a habit of sitting there when she needed to think, which he had noticed over the past months: she processed in motion or in transit-points, places of potential direction, as if the decision was easier when the body could see multiple ways to go.

He sat beside her.

"He taught me to listen," he said. Not immediately — there was a silence first, the appropriate silence, and then this. "He said the hum was not a sound but a question. He asked me once what I thought the question was." He paused. "I still don't know."

Noor said nothing for a moment. The alcove smelled of old stone and the mineral-water that ran along the lower edge of the passage, and distantly the fungal-growth smell of Homewater.

"What did you tell him?" she asked.

"I told him I thought the question was whether we were listening."

She looked at the passage — at the split, the two directions.

"That's not a bad answer," she said.

"He made a sound," Glim said, "that I am still not sure was agreement or instruction."

She almost smiled. The corner of her mouth moved in the specific way that meant something close to it.

"What happens now," she said. "To the clan."

"We rebuild. We learn what the Lamplighters left. We figure out who the new Listener is, because there's always a new Listener — the Current finds them." He paused. "Knuckle thinks it's Candle-Two. I think he's probably right."

"Candle-Two who can't always find the words."

"The Current doesn't care about words," he said. "It cares about listening."

Noor was quiet. She was looking at the passage split again.

"I need to tell you something," she said.

[Beat 53]
She had made contact with the Mission.
She told him this plainly, in the way she told him all things — without softening and without display. Through the surface-connected device — she had used one of the access-points in the upper Works during the G-corridor operations, when the Lamplighters were in retreat and there was a window. She had sent documentation to two people above her former supervisor's level: the maintenance covenant evidence, the Lamplighter budget reallocation, the record of unauthorized infrastructure modification. She had flagged it as a safety filing, which gave it a protected status that the Mission couldn't quietly suppress.
She had also sent a location indication. Not a precise one — not the tunnels, not Homewater, not anything that could be used to find the clan. Only enough for the Mission to know that she was alive, was underground, wasn't being held.
"You contacted them," he said. He was thinking through what this meant in the way he thought through tunnel-systems — tracking the connections, the directions, the implications.
"I filed a protected document," she said. "There's a difference. The filing exists in the system. It can't be pulled without leaving a record of who pulled it. It makes the Lamplighter operation legally complicated in a way it wasn't before."
"They'll come looking for you."
"Maybe. Probably." She paused. "I don't think I can stop that. I filed the document because the covenant is real and the Cogs are owed what they're owed and because the Lamplighters shouldn't be able to do what they did without anyone on the surface knowing the cost of it." She looked at him. "I didn't do it to make your situation harder."
"I know," he said. He did know this. He had learned to read the difference between Noor's actions that were about calculation and Noor's actions that were about principle. She had principles the way the Underclan had laws — not rules from above but structures she had built from her own experience of what happened when structures were absent. "It was the right thing."
"It complicates things."
"Everything that matters complicates things."
She was quiet. He let the quiet sit. The alcove had the ambient hum at the ordinary level — not stronger here than anywhere else, just present, the way it was always present in Homewater.
"I could go back," she said. Not to him — to the passage. To both directions at once. "The filing is made. The documentation is in the system. I don't need to be down here for it to do what it does. I could go back up, continue the process through the proper channels, make the case formally."
He waited.
"I could," she said again. "The Mission won't be pleased. They'll have questions I can't answer cleanly. But I know how their process works, and I know how to navigate it, and the covenant documentation is strong enough that it forces a review."
"Yes," he said.
"That's not what I'm deciding."
"No," he said. "It isn't."
She turned from the passage and looked at him directly — the way she had looked at him in the Mission compound when she told him her grandmother had gone down, when she named what she was feeling and then moved through it. That quality of looking.
"The surface can't un-know us now," she said. "That's done. The filing is done. Whether I'm up there or down here — the record exists."
"Yes."
"And down here I know things that are useful. I know the covenant structure. I know how to file. I know what levers exist on the surface and how to reach them from below." She paused. "Dross knows some of this now. But Dross's job is the Works. Someone needs to know it who doesn't already have another job."
He looked at her for a long time.
"The Underclan doesn't have a word for what you're describing," he said. "We have Brave, Listener, Tender, Maker. What you're describing doesn't fit any of them."
"Then make a new one," she said. Not sharp — simply practical. The way she was practical about everything.
He thought about this.
"Connector," he said. "Someone who connects one world to another. We've needed one for a long time."
She looked at the word as if she was testing its weight.
"I'll need to learn the routes properly," she said. "The deep ones. The ones that aren't on any surface map."
"That takes time."
"I'm aware." She paused. "Do you have someone to teach me?"
He had spent his whole life in the tunnels. He had learned them from Grale and before Grale from the accumulated knowledge of every Brave who had gone before, passed down through demonstration and correction and the patient repetition of things you did until they were in your body.
"Yes," he said.

[Beat 54]
He took the Fare at the end of the month.
The clan had rebuilt enough by then — not to what it had been, not in a month, but enough that he could leave it in Knuckle's hands for the length of a transit-run without the leaving feeling like abandonment. Candle-Two had begun to do the things that Listeners did. Mostly she sat in certain places and attended to them. The clan had begun to orient itself around her the way you oriented yourself around a thing you were still learning to trust.
He took the Oarsman's route.
The black water was the same black water it had always been — the same temperature, the same mineral smell, the same quality of surface that swallowed light rather than reflecting it. He had stood at this edge many times. He had stood here as a boy, before the Brave-training, before he understood what the black water was and what it was for. He had stood here as a young Brave the first time, waiting for the Oarsman's return with the specific fear that the Oarsman might not come. He had stood here after Grale died, in the early days when the weight of what Grale had left him was still new and still finding its shape.
The Oarsman came from the dark the same way it always came: soundlessly, the hull of the vessel barely disturbing the water's surface, the figure in the center present and attended and offering no explanation for where it had been or what it was.
Glim stepped down into the vessel.
The Fare was the three-finger configuration — the full Fare, the one that meant a life's worth of passage. He had prepared it from what he had: the dried fungus-cap that was the ordinary cost; the carved bone silence-piece Vesh had pressed into his palm in the vaults of the Tartar, smooth from a lifetime of a thumb's use; and the flat surface-card Noor had given him to carry, so that if the surface's systems found him they would have a name to put to him. Three things. Three different kinds of cost.
He put them in the vessel's center.
The Oarsman did not look at them. It moved in the way it moved, with the economy of something that has found the least effortful path through resistance.
The black water opened ahead of them.
He thought of his mother's hands. He thought of this sometimes on the Fare — not often, not always, only in the moments when the water was very dark and the candle's glow did not reach far and the vessel was in the middle of crossing and the Oarsman was moving. He didn't know if she had gone down into the Underclan willingly or by accident or in fear. He didn't know if she had taken a Fare. He had lived his whole life in the space of not-knowing and had learned that not-knowing was not the same as absence: the space had a shape, and the shape of it was his.
He had started as someone else's child. He had ended as himself, and the road between had taken a Listener and a rival and a candle and had given him a name in two worlds and no clean place to stand in either.
The hum was present at the frequency it always was on the black water — lower and fuller than in the tunnels, the way it was always lower and fuller when the water was between him and the stone. He sat with it. He didn't try to find the pulse from the Tartar in it or the Second Word or any answer the hum had not yet chosen to give.
He sat with what it was.
The other bank materialized from the dark the way it always did: first the smell of the stone ahead, then the change in the candle's behavior, then the edge. He stepped off.
The Oarsman waited.
He looked back once across the black water toward the side he had come from. Nothing was visible there — the dark was absolute, the way the dark in the Deep was always absolute. Somewhere past it, in Homewater's tunnels, thirty-eight people were doing the work of being alive. Noor was learning the routes. Candle-Two was sitting with her hands flat on the tile, listening for the hum that she was learning to name.
A new Fare. The Oarsman, waiting. The far bank behind him and the near bank ahead.
He went on.
