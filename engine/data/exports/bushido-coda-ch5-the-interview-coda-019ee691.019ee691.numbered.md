# Chapter 9: The Interview

_Sable brings the Lotus audition offer — she can't vet this client; Kyle's call. The corridor fight (three operatives, the hardest early cost). Mira (cell captain) names what Kyle chose in the corridor. The togishi of the Narrows examines Silence: "the loneliest piece of steel I have held." The arrangement signed. Mira plants the spine clue: plural subscribers on Kyle's file now — ask the Antiquarian._

[Beat 1]
Sable arrived on a Thursday, which was how he knew the job was real — she never brought him hypotheticals.

She sat in his second chair with the contract summary on the table between them and explained it the way she explained everything she couldn't fully vet: once, clearly, without the decorative language she used when she had looked at every angle and liked what she saw.

"Lotus Syndicate, Bucktown cell," she said. "Hua's arm. They want a quarterly arrangement — discretionary security consults, off-book, compensated at market plus twenty percent. The twenty is for the discretion." She moved the sheet a half-inch toward him. "The catch: they want an audition first. Their word. Three operatives, controlled corridor, no kill brief. They want to watch you fight before they decide to buy it."

Kyle looked at the sheet. The fee line was exactly what she'd said — market plus twenty, quarterly, no retainer. A clean offer.

"What's the problem?" he asked.

"The problem," Sable said, "is that the Lotus is the one client in this city I cannot vet. Not because I haven't tried. Because their security architecture is better than my tools, and they know it, and they know that I know it, and the offer still puts no vetting on the page as a condition." She did not reach for the sheet. "My rule is my rule for a reason."

He knew the reason. The Gennaro job had been a corpo client Sable couldn't fully vet — the wrong file delivered, Vo Dinh's table the one who paid the debt, three weeks of cleanup before the chain was clean again. The rule had been the lesson.

"Your call," she said. She meant it. The sheet sat equidistant between them, waiting for a hand to pick it up or a decision to leave it.

Kyle thought about the twenty percent premium on a quarterly contract. He thought about who Hua was, and what the Lotus Syndicate built its parallel city out of, and which parts of it he could work beside and which he couldn't. He thought about the code — cut everything; cross nothing — and noticed that he had never confused the code with a map of who deserved to be cut.

He reached for the sheet. Sable's finger came to the far edge — not pulling it back, just the weight of a fingertip on the corner, on the last line she'd been reading. Three seconds. Her finger lifted. He picked up the sheet.

"Then it's on me," he said. "Not on you. I take this, the Gennaro rule stays clean — you didn't vet a client I chose without you."

Sable looked at him for a beat. "That's a careful distinction."

"It's the one that keeps your rule working."

She was quiet for one second. Then she stood, put on her coat, and said, not looking back, "The audition is Friday. The address is on the back."

[Beat 2]
He told Pixel where he was going the way he always told her — the logistics, the timeline, the relevant gear state — and she received it the way she received all of his logistics: by listening through the information for the actual sentence.

"Lotus," she said when he was done. Somewhere under the bench a small speaker was playing a baseball game from a season that had ended before either of them was born, the announcer's voice low and unhurried, the crowd a steady tide she clearly wasn't listening to so much as keeping company with. She was reseating a lens element on the bench, both eyes on the optic. It was a prosthetic eye, the cheap mass-market kind, in for a rebuild she wasn't charging for — a courier two blocks over had taken a curb at speed and cracked the iris diaphragm, and the manufacturer wanted more for the part than the woman made in a month. "Bucktown arm."

"Quarterly arrangement."

"Mm." She turned the element in the light, checking the seat. "I can't monitor your array at the other end. Whatever they run in those corridors, I'm blind on your diagnostics until you come home."

"I know."

She set the element down and looked at her hands, which she did when she was deciding something. "Your forearm held the last one. It'll hold this one." She went back to the optic. "Come back in a number of pieces I can count."

He packed the gear. He left the door the way she always left it — open the width she always left it, which was exactly enough.

[Beat 3]
The Bucktown basement was a cleared corridor, fifty meters, load-bearing columns running the length — the kind of space that had spent years as a live-fire range and kept the habit in its concrete. Hua stood at the far end under a lamp, hands folded, gray qipao, and did not greet him, because the contract was not started yet.

Mira was at the near end.

She was not compact and she was not warm. She was the kind of tall you only notice when she turns to show the profile — the length of a person accustomed to being the last thing in a room that moves. She read Kyle the way a captain reads a unit assessment: all the way through, back to front, spending a beat on the forearm seam that had been sutured and the stance that told on the hip tape even though the tape wasn't visible.

"The brief," she said. Her voice had already decided the friendliness question and moved on to the one she cared about. "Three operatives. Live hardware, safety profile on. The test isn't whether you can clear the corridor."

"What's the test?" he asked.

"I'll tell you at the end." She stepped aside.

The first came from behind the left column at the eight-meter mark — better positioning than Kyle usually saw at this scale. She had ceramic wrist plates, a shock-baton, and the economic stride of a woman who fought the way she did everything else: as little as necessary to reach the outcome. He took her baton arm on the third swing with the column at his back and put her down with her own momentum, the baton ringing off concrete, and she stayed down and said count, which was the range word for stopped.

The second came from both sides at once — a pair, not one, which the brief hadn't mentioned. One was feinting. Kyle spent the array's budget pricing it in half a second, took the real one first on the inside line, shoulder to the column, and the feinter ran into the fact that his partner was already sitting down. Two counts.

The third was at the twenty-meter mark, already in motion before the second one was finished, and she was the corridor kind of good — the kind you only develop when you have actually been ambushed in one and it didn't kill you but it taught you. She came with a blade on the left and a thumb ring on the right, and the thumb ring was the one to solve.

Kyle let the blade shoulder come in.

He didn't have to. The array had the geometry — the forward pivot, the outside line, the disarm — all of it priced at 6.2% risk on his current hardware state. He could have taken her clean and paid nothing.

He let it come in because the taser was the point. The taser was going to fire regardless of what happened to the blade arm, and the array said: take the shoulder to get inside the taser's radius before it cycled, and the shoulder costs, and the corridor floor is concrete.

The taser fired into his upper back and the charge rode his mesh. His left hand closed on nothing and his jaw locked and every nerve on the left side from shoulder to hip registered the kind of thing that usually means the end of the sentence, and Kyle put both boots into her center while the charge was still running. She sat down hard against the column and said count.

He stood in the corridor a moment while the left side of his body filed its report.

Three counts. Five seconds.

From the far end, Hua's hands remained folded.

[Beat 4]
Mira walked the corridor toward him. Not toward — the distinction was in the way she moved, like a captain inspecting an experiment she'd already formed a hypothesis about.

"You had the outside line on the third one," she said.

"I had it."

"Forward pivot, full disarm, taser out of reach. Six-point-two percent on your hardware state."

Kyle looked at her. "You ran the array geometry?"

"We ran your file." She looked at where the taser had caught him. At the sutured forearm. Back up. "You chose the expensive version."

"Inside radius before it cycled. Cheaper than six percent on a pivot I might not complete."

"No," she said. "You could have completed it. I watched you price the clean version for a third of a second before you moved. You chose the one where you absorb it instead." She studied him. "She's got a three-day-old mesh repair on her left knee. Stairs, not a fight. Your array had the read."

"I had the read," he said.

"The code," Mira said. "Cut everything; cross nothing. The cross nothing part." She wasn't asking.

"That's the code."

She looked at his back where the taser had fired, the way Pixel looked at a diagnostic she already knew the answer to. "Cost you a rib."

"Probably two."

"You want Lotus medical."

"I want Pixel."

She made no comment on the preference. It was a preference she understood. "The arrangement," she said. "Hua will want it quarterly. I want first call on the discretionary consults. Standard rate plus the twenty for both." She looked at him with the flat attention of someone who has stopped measuring and started accounting. "I don't need your fixer's vetting channel. I'll handle my own."

"She'll notice that."

"She will." No apology in it, just the fact. "What I need from a Lotus-adjacent freelancer is someone the Lotus doesn't own. Your fixer keeps you that way. I'm not asking you to cut the channel — I'm asking you to keep it separate from mine." A pause. "Those are different asks."

Kyle turned them over.

"Friday," he said. "Standard rate. We start there."

Mira nodded once, the way she probably nodded at everything that went where she expected it to. "There's a man in the Narrows I want you to see before you leave. He'll want to look at the blade." She looked at Silence at his hip — not with the combat assessment she'd given it in the corridor, but with something quieter. "Let him."

[Beat 5]
The Togishi of the Narrows worked out of a two-room shop three blocks from the cherry trees, down a flight of stairs polished smooth by forty years of foot traffic and the weight of blades going in and coming out. The sign on the door was a single character, brushed by hand. It said nothing useful to anyone who hadn't already been sent.

He received Silence across both palms, blunt spine toward Kyle, the way a bow receives a blade — not surrender, courtesy: an acknowledgment between people who understand that the steel is the point.

He did not speak for three minutes. Kyle stood in the outer room and waited, the ribs keeping up their running commentary.

The togishi returned Silence in the same manner he had received it and stood at the worktable with his hands at his sides.

"How long have you carried it."

"Eleven years."

"Before that."

"I don't remember."

The togishi looked at him. Not at the blade. "You don't remember."

"I was given it. I don't remember the giving."

The old man turned back to the worktable. "The work is skilled. Not excellent — skilled." He laid two fingers flat on the flat of the blade, near the hamon, the way a doctor reads a pulse. "I cannot tell you what it's made of. This is none of the alloys I know, and not a blend of any of them. Whatever is in this edge, I do not know the door you would walk through to buy it." He ran his thumb along the saya. "There is no school in it either. The finishing cuts are individual — the hand made a decision at the end that isn't in any lineage I know."

"Is that a problem."

"It is an observation." He looked at Kyle. "I have held perhaps eight thousand blades in my career. Every one of them has a history I can read some part of. This one remembers nothing I can read. The steel is good. The work is honest. The edge is precise." He set his palm flat on the saya. "It is the loneliest piece of steel I have held."

Kyle said nothing.

"You came because someone sent you," the togishi said. "Not because you wanted the answer."

"That's right."

"Come back when you want the answer." He bowed. "The blade is well. I have no repair for you."

Kyle bowed. He left the answer in the room, the way he left most of what he hadn't decided about yet.

[Beat 6]
Mira was waiting on the block above the stairs, leaning against the cherry-blossom gate the way she waited for everything — not impatient, not comfortable, just accounting for time.

"Well?" she asked.

"Skilled, not excellent. No lineage."

She made no comment on what else the togishi had said. She had sent him; she already knew what he would say.

"A thing came to my desk last week," she said, in the tone she used for things she'd decided were his to know. "The desk that bought your file from Vey — the one through Halvorsen Civic. The Continuity Office requisition."

"I know about that one."

"Then you know there's more than one buyer now. Plural subscribers in the last four weeks, neither through Halvorsen. One is architecture I don't recognize — patient, months old, routes clean. One is four weeks old and loud enough that I noticed it from here." She looked at the cherry trees, past their bloom now and just trees again, with their reasons behind them. "Somebody with more patience than budget found you at the same time as somebody with more budget than patience. They're not working together, which means two different people decided you were worth watching in the same month." A pause. "Ask the Antiquarian who else subscribes."

"He won't sell me my own file."

"Ask him anyway. There may be a thing he tells you that he hasn't decided to sell." She buttoned the third button on her coat, which was her tell for a clean exit, and stepped off the gate. "Friday. Standard rate. We'll start there."

"Friday," Kyle said.

She left the way she'd found him — without ceremony, and without looking back, which was how she treated everything she'd already decided.

[Beat 7]
He rode home through the Narrows. The taser burn, the togishi's words, the two new names on his file — he needed the city to be quieter than Bucktown before any of it would sit still long enough to sort.

The Narrows was the strip of low blocks where the freight viaduct cut the district in half and rents went to whatever the noise didn't drown. At this hour a woman was hosing fish blood off a loading dock into a drain that had stopped draining years ago; the blood spread across the concrete and pooled at the curb, and nobody looked twice at it because nobody looked twice at anything here after midnight. That was the whole arrangement. He rode through it with his lights low, and the Narrows let him pass the way it let everyone pass who didn't need it to be anything else.

The taser burn in his ribs filed its ongoing report. He breathed through it with the flat attention he gave any cost that had already been paid — it was there, it was real, it would resolve on its own schedule and not a day before. The mesh had held. The code had held. The arrangement was signed.

What wouldn't lie flat was the togishi.

He pulled out of the Narrows at Madison and let the city come back around him — the overnight freight, the gray-zone markets cracking their early windows, the particular density of a block that had been cooking its own air for thirty years. Mrs. Chen's counter would be dark by now. Across the hall, a light would be on.

He thought about asking Pixel to chase the harmonic again. Not the answer — he wasn't built for the answer yet. Just whether she could tell him more about what it was, without telling him where it came from.

He rode the last blocks home.
