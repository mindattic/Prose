# The Dumpling War

[Beat 1]
The Loop's third underlevel had its own ecology: paper shop, shuttered watch-repair, Vong's Dumpling Franchise #7 on the right under a red awning, and Mei's Dumplings on the left — no franchise, no awning, forty stolen centimeters of counter she'd annexed off the watch-repair's wall by aggressive proximity. The stationer's honest door sat dead center.

The two men stood in the middle with their backs to that door, which meant their backs to Vey, which was the kind of error that compounds.

Vong was a compact sixty-year-old with the posture of a man who'd been absorbing bad news upright for decades. The clipboard man was presenting him a form. "—monthly insurance assessment. Standard for all food vendors on this underblock. The Tsui Group's protective services, standard rate—"

"Thirty-five Φ a week," Kyle said from behind them, reading the figure upside-down. "Four hundred percent over the Meridian Merchant index. And your policy number's nine digits. GLMZ policy numbers run eleven. That's somebody's phone number on a form."

The clipboard man turned. Long enough in the work to have stopped enjoying it. "Not your stall."

"Go, Mr. Kyle," Vong said quietly. "This is mine to carry."

"I eat here," Kyle said, and that was the whole of his position.

[Beat 2]
He'd half-expected the paperwork to end it. He pulled up the Tsui Group's public filing anyway — all collection franchises filed public or didn't operate — and the search took under three minutes to surface the part that mattered.

"Your protective license lapsed," Kyle said, turning the screen. "The fourth. Not expired — lapsed. You've been writing paper on a dead license eleven days. There's no reinstatement pending; the portal would show it."

The clipboard man looked at the screen a long moment. Then he handed the clipboard to his partner — the big one, the expensive shoes — and rolled his neck.

Kyle had read it as a departure. He was wrong. A man whose paper is exposed has two options, and only one of them is going.

"License is for the ones who pay clean," the clipboard man said. "Vong's not paying clean anymore. So this is the other visit." He looked at his partner and the big one didn't go for Kyle, because the big one was a professional and professionals understand leverage. He went for Vong's broth pot.

Forty liters of scalding pork-bone stock. A sixty-year-old man standing next to it.

[Beat 3]
Kyle was moving before the big one's hand finished closing on the rim.

He did not draw Silence. A drawn blade in an underlevel full of lunch civilians was a conversation he'd have to finish, and this didn't need finishing, it needed interrupting. He took the man's wrist off the rim — not a block, a redirect, the shove steered ninety degrees into the franchise's steel prep counter — and forty liters of scalding stock rocked on its ring and held, one tongue of it slopping over the lip to hiss across the floor where Vong had been standing a half-second before, because Kyle's other hand had already moved him.

The big one came off the counter fast and threw the elbow Kyle had left him room to throw. It caught Kyle across the cheekbone — a real hit, a bright white line of it, a mark by evening — and the cost bought the opening: weight forward, lead knee proud. Kyle took the knee from the side with the flat of his boot. Not the joint. A man who limps for a week tells the story right; a man who never walks again tells a worse one and brings friends. The big one folded into the spilled stock, swearing, done.

The clipboard man's hand went inside his jacket. Kyle looked at him.

"Cacophony holds five," Kyle said. He hadn't drawn it. The sentence drew it. "You came to scald a sixty-year-old over thirty-five Φ a week. Take your partner, take your dead license, and tell Marcus Tsui that Vong's block is documented now — times, faces, the shoes." A beat. "There's a kid on that roof who drew the shoes."

The hand came out empty. They hauled the big one up between them and went, leaving wet bootprints and the smell of spilled stock, and the underlevel breathed out.

Vong stood by his rescued pot with both hands flat on the counter. Steadying it or himself.

"The tea stall on sub-two," he said. "Lin's. Forty years. She told them no, same as me, and they didn't burn it — they just visited, and visited, and one week the customers decided sub-two wasn't worth the walk, and then Lin decided too. Now it's a phone-charging kiosk and nobody remembers there was tea." His hands were not quite steady. "That's how it's done now. Not a fire. An erasure. You wait for the block to forget."

"It's documented now," Kyle said. "The next visit isn't a visit. It's a filing." He looked at the blood on his thumb without surprise. "Lin's mistake was being the only no on her block. You've got a whole block, Vong. Bill me for the dumplings I'm about to eat."

From his doorway, Vey made a small sound that was not a cough.

[Beat 4]
They came back at 14:00.

No clipboard this time. Three of them — the clipboard man, the big one moving carefully on his bad knee, and a third Kyle hadn't seen before, with the chrome thumb-rings and the flat affect of a man brought in specifically. The underlevel had thinned out after lunch. The third man's eyes went to Vong's counter first, then to the stairwell access on the far wall, mapping the routes out, which told Kyle more than the chrome.

"Marcus Tsui filed a complaint," the clipboard man said. He was talking to the room, not to any one person. "Assault of a licensed representative. Interference with contracted services. You've got a warrant flag running on your neuretics, samurai — check it if you want. We'll wait."

Kyle didn't check it. Warrant flags cost sixty Φ to file and twenty to pull. It was a stall, not a threat. He also did not move from where he was standing, which was between the third man and Vong's pot.

"The complaint was filed to a licensing board that hasn't had a quorum since the fourth," Kyle said. "Same day your license lapsed. The filing is stuck behind the lapse review, which means it's administrative vapor. It dissolves when your license reinstatement is denied, which it will be, because I've already submitted the eleven-day gap documentation to the Office of Merchant Protections." He hadn't done that yet. He was going to do it in the next forty minutes, which was close enough. "You want to check THAT warrant, I'll wait."

The third man looked at the clipboard man. The clipboard man looked at his shoes.

The chrome rings were the tell. This third man was not here for paper. He was here to stand in Vong's stall and make the customers who came for afternoon dumplings see what they were walking into. He would not hurt Vong. He would just be present, every afternoon, until it cost more to keep the franchise than to surrender it.

The erasure technique. Patient, geometric, and technically legal.

"Vong," Kyle said, without turning, "is there a back entrance to this underlevel?"

"Loading dock. Sub-two corridor, behind the watch-repair."

"The watch-repair is shuttered?"

"Three years."

"Whose lease?"

A pause. "Mine. I took it when they left. Cheaper than advertising."

Kyle looked at the third man. "You're standing in an extended franchise footprint. The loading dock access, the sub-two corridor, and the two meters of watch-repair annexation all come off the same lease. You can stand here, but you're standing in his house. You want to explain to Marcus Tsui that you took a loitering position inside a vendor's licensed square footage, in front of witnesses, on the day I submitted that lapse documentation?"

The third man had the chrome rings and the flat affect and nothing else. He was hired for presence, not decisions. He looked at the clipboard man, who had been wrong about this block twice now.

They left.

Vong sat down on his prep stool and said nothing for a while. From the sub-two corridor below, faint and steady, the Pulse ran its underfloor hum through the tiles — thirty meters down, Mach 6, the whole moving world.

"Every time I tell them no," Vong said, "I think about Lin."

"You're not Lin," Kyle said. "Lin didn't have the sub-two corridor and a kid on the roof and a samurai who eats here on Tuesdays."

He went upstairs and filed the documentation.

[Beat 5]
The franchise survived until noon, when Mei opened her awning two centimeters further left than yesterday and started the other war.

Mei's Dumplings had seventeen years on Vong's franchise, a recipe that allegedly predated the zone restructuring, and a standing position that Franchise #7 was an encroachment on her base, her air rights, and her sense of how the underlevel should smell. Vong answered the awning by nudging his broth pot one centimeter toward the center line. By 14:00 there was a chalk line on the floor. By 15:30 there were two, with a disputed zone between them and three customers eating in the gap with the posture of people braced to be asked to choose sides.

Kyle was at Mei's — alternating weeks, six years, both owners aware of it — with a cut cheek and a late lunch when Mrs. Chen arrived, clearly sent for, with the expression of a woman asked to do a thing beneath her competence and above her patience.

"Both of you," she said, from the neutral chalk, "have sold dumplings down here longer than half these buildings have stood, and you've both overcharged me, and I've paid, because the product was worth it." She looked at the lines. "I'm not an arbitrator. I'm a customer owed better than this."

"You're the only one on this block both stalls will hear," Vong said.

"You started it," Mei said.

"The awning—"

"The broth pot—"

Mrs. Chen looked at Kyle the particular way a woman looks who has spent seventy years not asking for things. He ate the rest of his dumpling and accepted the expression as the invoice it was. His cheek had started to stiffen, which felt, all things considered, like the right price for an afternoon that had nearly cost Vong his hands twice.

[Beat 6]
The taste-off was Boost's idea, and the speed the logbook adopted it as official suggested it had been everyone's at once.

DUMPLING COMMISSION: OFFICIAL RECORD. CHAIR: MR. KYLE (VISIBLE INJURY, SEE 11:40 ENTRY). RECORDER: RAFTERS. PANEL: DENTS, BOOST. OBJECTIVE: BETTER DUMPLING, FOR BLOCK PEACE. NOTE: ALSO THE FUNNIEST THING WE'VE BEEN ASKED TO DO. SECOND NOTE: BOSS GOT HIT TODAY. TWICE. WE LOGGED IT. THIS LOG IS HONEST.

Both stalls produced their house dumplings. Vong ran a standard pork-and-chive, thicker wrap, machine-even pleating, nineteen quality ratings it deserved. Mei produced the original — thinner-wrapped, hand-crimped in a pattern Rafters sketched in the margin for documentation, ginger-forward and something older underneath. Kyle ate four of each. The crew filed scorecards ranging from TWO POINTS AND A STAR to a drawing of a thumbs-up with a 1Φ price tag.

An audience had accumulated the way audiences do around chalk lines and commission proceedings, and Vey had migrated from his doorway to the building's first internal window for the better angle.

[Beat 7]
"For the record," Kyle said, to the chalk and the crowd and both owners, "Vong's franchise makes a technically excellent dumpling. Consistent. Well-priced. You know exactly what you get any day of any week." Vong nodded with professional dignity. "Mei's original is better."

Mei made a sound that was not quite a sound. Vong's jaw went through an expression and out the far side.

"By a margin that doesn't justify a chalk line," Kyle went on, over the crowd's murmur. "Vong, your guarantee covers the gap — inspection rating, delivery network, the app. Mei's hand-folding seventeen years of recipe. Two stalls, two reasons to buy a dumpling. You're both leaving money on the floor fighting over two centimeters." He let that sit, and then he said the part that was actually the point. "This afternoon, Marcus Tsui sent three men to stand in Vong's stall and wait for the foot traffic to decide it wasn't worth the walk. They left because the documentation went in and the stall had defensible footage. That changes nothing about next week, or the week after, or Lin's tea stall on sub-two that is now a phone-charging kiosk and will never be tea again." He looked at both of them. "A block with one stall is a block that can be made to forget it. A block with two stalls arguing about awnings at lunch is a block that is LOUD. It's a block people come to on purpose. You two are each other's insurance, and you've been drawing a chalk line down the middle of it."

He looked at Rafters. "That how the minutes read?"

Rafters consulted the logbook with the gravity of a senior analyst. "Exactly how they read. I can charge for certified copies."

The chalk was gone by 16:30. Vong sent one complimentary order of pork-and-chive to Mei's counter without comment; Mei received it without comment. Closest the underlevel had come to a diplomatic event in twenty years. Vong put the franchise testimonial card in his window next to a second card, hand-lettered, that hadn't been there this morning: BEST ORIGINAL ON THE BLOCK — SEE MEI, NEXT COUNTER. Insurance, posted where the block could read it.

[Beat 8]
Kyle was leaving at 17:20 when Vey opened his door the particular width of a man who'd been saving something all day.

"Come in. The occasion warrants the good shelf." Inside, the drawers watched in their brown-inked calm. Vey poured without asking, pushed the cup handle-first. "The Tsui Group is not my clientele — impersonation filings are public, and I don't sell what I can read for free." He settled onto his stool. "I watched the whole proceeding. Morning and afternoon. The license. The pot. The chrome thumb-rings." His eyes went, briefly, to Kyle's cheek. "The interruptions, plural."

"You watched a man go for a sixty-year-old and made tea."

"I sell maps, Mr. Kyle. I do not walk the territory; that has never been my trade, and a stationer who picks up a weapon is a stationer no one trusts with a secret again." He said it without apology, which was its own kind of honesty. "But I notice you did not draw the sword. A man went for Vong's pot — twice, in two different configurations — and you put them down with documentation and a boot and a sentence. The leverage was real, the filing was real, the sub-two corridor was real. Three kinds of paper and a measured violence, in sequence." He polished his glasses. "The Tsui Group needed a reason to stop. The stalls needed a verdict. Your standing client needed" — the smallest brightness — "a dumpling methodology, apparently. You arbitrate better than you bill. A man could make a file of that."

"Don't."

"The file is already open," Vey said gently. "It has been since your first visit; I open one on everyone. I am telling you its rating, which is a courtesy I extend to perhaps four customers a year." He put the glasses back on. "Repeat customer. Tone: admiring. The cheek will scar small if you leave it; I'd leave it. Scars are also a kind of record, and this one's honest."

Kyle drank the tea, which was from the good shelf, and set the cup down exactly.

The terminal chirped on his way to the stairs.

STANDING CONTRACT — AMENDMENT. CLIENT HAS REVIEWED TODAY. CLIENT NOTES: THE INJURY WAS AVOIDABLE IF THE ENGINEER'S DRONE PROPOSAL (FILED, DECLINED) HAD BEEN ADOPTED.
CLIENT HAS RE-FILED THE PROPOSAL.
GRATUITY: 0.5Φ — ITEMIZED: THE CHEEK. CLIENT DISLIKES THE CHEEK.

Kyle read it twice on the stairs, with the cut stiffening and the underlevel settling into its evening behind him — Vong's closing sequence, Mei's last fold, the Pulse running its thirty-meter underfloor hum through the tiles — and decided the client could dislike the cheek all it wanted. Some prices you pay with your face, so a noodle man keeps his hands.
