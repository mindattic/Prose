# The Crossing

[Beat 1]
The contract posted at 04:40, while the GLMZ was still deciding whether the dark was done.

TASK: FREIGHT ESCORT — SEED STOCK CONSIGNMENT (AGRICULTURAL, PERISHABLE).
ROUTE: GLMZ FRINGE → CEDAR RAPIDS DEPOT, CEDAR RAPIDS TERRITORY.
TRANSIT: IOWA CORRIDOR — BEHEMOTH OPERATIONAL ZONE, ACTIVE SCHEDULE.
CREW CONFIRMED: RIA (DRIVE), GANTRY (RIGGING), KYLE (SECURITY).
BEHEMOTH NAVIGATION ADVISORY: CLIENT WILL ISSUE IN-TRANSIT.
STANDARD RATE. FUEL AND TOLLS BILLED AT COST.
ADDENDUM: BRING SNACKS. THE HAUL IS EIGHT HOURS.

Ria was already at the hauler when Kyle arrived — a blunt-nosed cargo rig with sponsorship logos running down its flanks, the chromatophore panels on her legs cycling Ferrogate Transit's sunrise campaign at one-quarter brightness, which was her entire opinion of Ferrogate Transit. She had the cargo manifest in one hand and a thermos in the other and the look of a woman who had done this run twice and was getting paid to do it again.

"Fourteen crates," she said, by way of greeting. "Thirty-two varieties of heritage soy, two of nitrogen-fixing legume, one of something the manifest describes as experimental and the shipper describes as proprietary and I describe as not-my-problem-until-it-becomes-my-problem."

"Does the experimental crate make noise?"

"Not yet."

"Then it's not yet your problem."

She handed him the thermos, which meant the thermos had been brought for sharing. Kyle took one mouthful, found it was pu-erh that had been steeping since before either of them were up, and handed it back without comment. The thermos was not payment and could therefore be accepted.

Gantry was already on the rig's flatbed frame, checking strap tension on the cargo pallet with the particular focus of a man who knew things could move. He was about two meters of quiet in a rigger's coat, a person who talked the way some people painted: deliberately, economically, when the subject warranted it. He had, in Kyle's experience, roughly forty words a day he was willing to spend, and he rationed them. Currently he was not spending any. He was talking to the load straps instead, low, second person, the way Kyle had heard some haulers talk to a questionable weld — not believing it could hear, exactly, but not willing to bet the load on it.

"Anything shifting?" Kyle asked.

"Not yet," Gantry said. That was two words. He had thirty-eight left.

They pulled out of the Ashgrave fringe at 05:15, the hauler's engine doing its honest work, the city folding away behind them in vertical layers until the last tier dropped below sight and there was just the elevated causeway, the pre-dawn brown of open sky, and the flat country beginning to assert itself past the ferrocement boundary wall.

[Beat 2]
The Iowa corridor started at the wall's west service gate, which cost Ria a 45Φ transit stamp and a seven-minute argument with a gate officer about whether "perishable agricultural" covered "experimental proprietary" on the same manifest.

It did not. It cost 8Φ extra.

"Logged," Ria said, making a note on her tablet, "as a cost of goods." She drove through the gate without looking back.

Past the wall the land opened in a way that had no equivalent in the GLMZ. The hauler's elevation sensors counted down from the city's 16th-floor surface level to something like ground, actual ground, the old kind that didn't have a building underneath it, and the sky became an implausible quantity of sky. Kyle sat in the cab's jump seat and watched it arrive.

The terminal in his coat chirped.

CLIENT ADVISES: THIS SEGMENT IS OWNED SCHEDULE, NOT OPEN ROAD.
CLIENT ADVISES: DO NOT DEVIATE FROM POSTED WAYPOINTS.
CLIENT ADVISES: IF CLIENT POSTS STOP — STOP. DO NOT ASK.
ADDENDUM: THE HAULER'S CARGO IS WORTH MORE THAN THE HAULER. DRIVE ACCORDINGLY.

"Your client is posting," Ria said.

"It's navigating."

"It's navigating us through IOWA." She glanced at him over the hauler's center console. "How does it have Behemoth schedules?"

"Ask it," Kyle said. "It charges for answers, though."

Ria looked at the cab ceiling the way people looked at the sky when they wanted a specific kind of information about it.

"Never mind," she said. "I'll drive."

[Beat 3]
The first Behemoth appeared at kilometer 44, and appeared is the right word — it didn't come from somewhere, it simply resolved out of the middle distance the way a weather system resolves, until the mind accepted that the scale was real and the flat land was not so flat.

It was a harvester-process unit, one of the Iowa Cooperative's agricultural fleet: eighty meters at the shoulder, track width wider than the causeway, a processing tower along its dorsal frame venting steam in columns the height of office buildings. Parallel course, two kilometers east, moving at the pace of something that had no concept of urgency and had never needed one.

Gantry, visible through the cab's rear window, watched it with the stillness of a man recalibrating the scale of what the world contained.

"That's a Behemoth," Ria said.

"Yes," Kyle said.

"I've seen photos."

"Photos are wrong," Kyle said.

The terminal chirped.

CLIENT ADVISES: STOP.
WAIT 214 SECONDS.
DO NOT ASK.

Ria brought the hauler to a full halt, engine idling. The next waypoint was thirty meters ahead. Whatever the window was, the entity had measured it to the second, and a second early or a second late would be the entity's problem only if it chose to share why.

At second 214 a second Behemoth crossed their line of travel two hundred meters ahead — perpendicular, heading south, a combine-and-sort unit on its own errand. The ground arrived through Kyle's boots before the sound did: a mechanical thrum his neuretics logged as seismic, equipment grade, no threat response triggered. The vents fired and the plume went straight up and the hauler's windows flexed in the pressure wave.

They sat quiet until it passed.

"Okay," Ria said, at some point after. She put the hauler back in drive.

[Beat 4]
They stopped for fuel at a waypoint depot run by a woman named Paz who kept three lumen mice in a cage behind the counter and charged eleven Φ a liter for diesel, which was price-gouging that the middle of Iowa earned. Kyle paid it. Paz threw in a bag of dried apples without saying so, which was the prairie version of Mrs. Chen's fish ball, and could be accepted on the same terms.

Gantry rechecked straps while the fuel ran, spending something close to twelve of his daily words on the pallet. Ria watched from the driver's door.

"He does that every run," she said.

"The cargo holds," Kyle said.

"Yes," she said. "He seems comfortable with the question."

The terminal chirped.

CLIENT ADVISES: RIVER CROSSING — KILOMETER 187. MUNICIPAL TOLL: 4Φ. ADVISORY ONLY.
CLIENT ADVISES: THREE-HOUR WINDOW OPENS IN FORTY MINUTES. WAYPOINT 7 APPROACH TIMED FOR WINDOW.
ADDENDUM: THE EXPERIMENTAL CRATE IS FINE. NITROGEN-FIXERS USUALLY ARE.

"Tell your client I didn't ask about the crate," Ria said.

"It knew you were thinking about it."

"That's not better," she said.

[Beat 5]
The awe set piece was kilometer 119, which was not in the schedule.

The terminal had posted a revised waypoint — maintenance detour — and Ria had followed it west onto a secondary haul road, and the Behemoth had simply been there. Not transiting. Stationary in its own repair cycle, one track disassembled on the ground in segments, a maintenance crane working its mid-section, the thing filling the near distance the way a mountain fills near distance: completely, without apology.

Close enough to count rivets. Close enough for the cab's exterior sensors to log a two-degree rise from residual heat. Close enough that the ground-shake came through the frame and the jump seat and up into the spine before any of them had heard anything.

Ria brought the hauler to a crawl. Not a stop — the waypoint said keep moving, and the waypoint was the only map they had.

Kyle did not speak. There was no one to ask.

Gantry, who had thirty-eight words left, spent none of them. He was at the cab's rear window, grip on the frame, watching.

The maintenance crane swung through an arc, a component the size of a shipping container at the end of it, moving with the precision of a thing that weighed what it weighed and was governed by nothing else. The vents fired. The steam column went up. The crane swung back. The Behemoth's sensor array — a housing the size of the hauler — swiveled a few degrees and pointed somewhere else. They were below the threshold of the thing's attention, like the dried-apple bag had been.

Somewhere in the back, a hand found a sleeve.

Kyle did not look to see whose.

They drove past at walking speed, the hauler's engine the smallest sound in the world, and when the secondary road curved away and the Behemoth receded to the size of a building and then the size of a thought, Ria exhaled a breath she had been carrying for approximately four hundred meters.

"Right," she said.

That was the whole review.

[Beat 6]
Kilometer 187 was the river crossing, and the toll was not the problem.

The hauler was ten meters onto the bridge when the winch-rig emerged from the drainage cut on the east bank — a repurposed salvage crane on a flatbed, cable-spool running, bolt-thrower on each side, a crew of four who had been working this choke point long enough to stop being nervous about it. Good position: the bridge funneled everything into a six-meter lane and the crane's reach covered the full span. Kyle catalogued it at a glance: maybe 800Φ of field-modified hardware, a tablet with a forged permit that had cost less than the tablet.

The crew's spotter was on the upstream support structure, which was what they thought, and what made it a good choke point, and what Boost had been watching since kilometer 184 from the hauler's rear-mounted camera — having climbed out of the experimental crate that was exactly the right dimensions for a fourteen-year-old who had decided, with the logic of a fourteen-year-old who was good at surveillance, that this run was worth seeing firsthand.

The bolt-thrower crew had not been watching the cargo, because the cargo did not watch back.

Boost's voice came over the cab channel, flat, professional, reporting:

"Spotter on the upstream strut, port side, nine meters up. Walking-stick grip, inverted. He's been tracking since the turn-in. The rest are waiting on his signal."

There was a silence in the cab of approximately two full seconds.

"I am going to discuss this with you," Kyle said, "later."

"Noted," said Boost, from the crate.

Ria had already stopped the hauler. She did it the way she did everything — smoothly, without theater — and put both hands on the wheel where the toll crew could see them, because she had been running freight long enough to know which moves were readable and which weren't.

The crane operator came to the driver's window. He was working a bolt-thrower slung over one shoulder with the ease of a prop he'd carried a long time, and he had a tablet with a right-of-way notice on it that Kyle catalogued as stolen on first look — the letterhead was correct, the stamp was not.

"River bridge toll," the man said. "Non-standard approach, right of way purchased from the county. Forty-five Φ per axle." He did the math with the confidence of someone who did it daily. "Your rig's got four drive axles — that's three hundred and sixty Φ, plus the equipment surcharge on the cargo."

"That permit's forged," Ria said.

"That's a big claim," the man said, not particularly upset.

The terminal chirped.

CLIENT ADVISES: THE RIGHT-OF-WAY REGISTRATION NUMBER ON THAT DOCUMENT DOES NOT EXIST.
CLIENT ADVISES: REAL COUNTY CROSSING FEE IS 4Φ FLAT.
CLIENT ADVISES: THE WINCH DRUM IS THE PROBLEM.
ADDENDUM: KYLE.

Four in the cylinder. Kyle pulled Cacophony.

He shot the winch drum housing at the base of the crane — once, the round opening the cable-spool cleanly, cable running free in a long unspooling clatter across the bridge deck — and then he was out of the cab, because the throwers were swinging and a stationary cab was a bad answer to two bolt-throwers.

One spent. Three left.

The nearer thrower's operator had a power-cell housing at the grip — not load-bearing, which made it a seam — and Silence found it. The launcher went from operational to an expensive paperweight, approximately 180Φ of salvage crane hardware declaring itself a total loss. The operator backed off with the expression of someone reassessing their cost-benefit structure.

Gantry, in the meantime, had been watching the counterweight situation on the crane rig from the moment the flatbed appeared. He had done the math the way riggers do: once, quietly, and then he waited for it to become someone else's discovery.

He spent his last five words of the day on the crane operator: "That's going to fall."

The crane operator looked at the counterweight. He looked at where the counterweight was going to fall, which was directly onto the winch assembly of his own flatbed. He had four seconds to form an opinion about this, and to his credit, he used them productively.

He was three meters clear when it landed.

The winch assembly became the most honest piece of salvage in Iowa — crushed flat, cable hopelessly tangled, the rig converting from a viable toll operation into an infrastructure problem in one second of kinetic accounting. The second thrower operator stood in the road and looked at 400Φ of ruined equipment with the expression of a man doing the math on that number at territory wages. The flatbed's driver had both hands on his head in the posture of someone composing a reimbursement filing in real time.

The spotter on the upstream strut climbed down. He had seen the math complete from the best seat available and had no notes to add.

"We're done," he said. Professional resignation: the tone of a man whose shift had conclusively become a recovery operation. "Bridge is yours."

Kyle watched the casing arc off the bridge rail into the river. Four in, one spent. Three left.

He put Cacophony away.

[Beat 7]
The toll crew was sorting out their counterweight situation behind them when Boost climbed through from the cargo bed. She had the build of someone who climbed things for a living and the expression of a person who had weighed the cost of this particular decision and decided to hold her chin at full height regardless.

Kyle let the bridge get behind them. He waited until the hauler had its speed back.

Then he asked the question the discipline asked for him.

"The spotter. You called him at one-eighty-four. What was the tell?"

Boost blinked. Not what she had prepared for.

"Walking-stick grip, inverted. Nobody uses that grip unless the stick is actually a ranging tool and they don't want you to know the tool."

"Filed," Kyle said. "Log it yourself in the report you're writing when we're back. That call is worth having on record." He paused one beat. "Now. You're on this rig, unbriefed, in Behemoth country, without telling anyone, because?"

"You hired me for eyes," Boost said. "These are eyes."

"These are eyes," Kyle agreed, "in a seed-stock crate, in Iowa, two hundred meters from a machine the size of a city block, for eight hours, without telling me."

"You would have said no."

"I would have said no," Kyle said.

The hauler ran. Iowa ran. The sky was still that preposterous quantity of sky.

"You're good at this," Kyle said, which cost him something. "You know you're good at this."

Boost's chin stayed up, the younger face trying to match the older voice and almost managing it.

"I'm telling you from now on," Kyle said. "That's not a request. It's the price."

"Standard terms," Boost said, with exactly his inflection.

"Standard terms," he said.

She had a bolt-thrower quarrel through the right shoulder of her jacket — shallow, already clotted, the kind that marks coat more than skin — and she had not mentioned it, which he had noticed from the moment she climbed through the bulkhead. He catalogued it: shallow, closed, watch for heat. His own coat had new ventilation. Both items went in the ledger.

"Gantry has a first-aid kit," Ria said, not looking up from the road. "He packs for the brief."

Gantry produced it from under the bench and handed it back. He had no words left.

[Beat 8]
The Cedar Rapids depot took all fourteen crates on a signed manifest and issued a receipt in local currency that converted at 1.4 to the Φ, which Ria checked twice before signing with the satisfaction of someone who knew what heritage soy was worth to a territory that had been replanting since the blight.

Full delivery. No crates short.

They sat in the depot's small office while the receipt processed. It smelled like grain and hydraulic fluid and old radiator. A sundial dog was asleep in the corner under a bench, head on paws, its fur cycling hour-colors in the wrong season — amber at what should have been noon, trending rust.

The terminal chirped.

DELIVERY CONFIRMED.
FEE RELEASED: STANDARD RATE (RIA) + STANDARD RATE (GANTRY) + STANDARD RATE (KYLE).
GRATUITY: 0.5Φ EACH — ITEMIZED: EXPEDIENT RESOLUTION, KILOMETER 187.
ADDENDUM: B. (JUVENILE, CONTRACTOR) — SPOTTER CREDIT, KILOMETER 184. FEE: 2Φ. REASON: EYES.

Boost read the receipt over Kyle's shoulder and said nothing for a moment.

"It knew I was here," she said.

"It knew you were here," Kyle said.

She absorbed this with the stillness of a person who has learned they were being watched by something that paid fairly regardless.

Ria looked at her own receipt. "The vending machine has a personnel file on a fourteen-year-old."

"She's on payroll," Kyle said. "It keeps track."

[Beat 9]
The drive back ran the same waypoints in reverse, the Behemoths on their own schedules in the late afternoon light, the shadows of the processing towers very long across the flat country.

Boost worked her own patch in the jump seat, Gantry's kit on her lap, jacket off the wrong shoulder. Ria drove. Gantry had a window cracked. Iowa smelled like turned earth and something green and recent.

The terminal chirped.

RIA ASKS: HOW DOES CLIENT HAVE THE BEHEMOTH SCHEDULES?
CLIENT RESPONSE:

They waited while the characters arrived, each one slow, the way they arrived when the client was choosing its words.

CLIENT HAS CORRESPONDED WITH THE IOWA COOPERATIVE OPERATORS' MAINTENANCE SYSTEM FOR YEARS.
IT DOES NOT WRITE BACK.
CLIENT CONTINUES TO WRITE.

The cab was quiet.

The hauler ran west. The sun was behind them, the sky ahead a deepening blue, and somewhere out in the operational sectors a Behemoth vented steam into it — a white column, brief, then gone — on a schedule that had nothing to do with them.

Ria's hands were steady on the wheel. Gantry watched the column until it dispersed. Boost had stopped working the patch kit.

Nobody said anything for a while.

That was the right call.
