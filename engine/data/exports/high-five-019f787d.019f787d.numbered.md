# The Way Up

## Kicked Out

[Beat 1]
The door caught Tavi's garbage bag on the way out, not his back. It was the only mercy Ozzie had left in him.

"She's coming at eight," Ozzie said from the doorway, backing into the squat's orange glow. The place had one working bulb, wired straight into whatever the building still had left to give. "Eight, Tavi. You know what eight means."

"It means seven fifty-five," Tavi said.

The door sealed with a magnetic click, then the heavier thunk of the dead-bolt Ozzie had welded on himself two years back. Reza dropped onto the stoop beside him a half-second later, his own bag over one shoulder, a foam pillow tucked under his arm that he had definitely stolen off Ozzie's couch.

The Gray Zone opened around them the way it did every morning at this hour. Gray light, no color anywhere — nothing out here had ever been painted on purpose. The street ran east into a row of dead storefronts, plywood over the display glass. West, it ran into an alley so tight you could touch both walls with your arms out. The city's nano-coverage did its bare minimum here, maybe fifteen percent on a good day, thinner the farther you got from anything wearing a badge. No patrol came through before nine. This early, they were nobody's problem.

Reza looked up and down the empty street, wearing the face he wore when he was trying not to look delighted. His diploma was in his bag. Tavi knew, because his own was in his bag too — the same laminated nothing, from the same basic-education cohort, signed by the same administrator. The man had spelled Tavi's name wrong and never fixed it. Neither of them had chrome. No etch at the temple, no augment, nothing you could point to. Just the civic neuretics everybody got seeded with before they could walk — the baseline lattice, a civic marker, a dead ad-channel, and nothing on top.

South, past the rooftops, the Thread went up. The orbital tether, anchored somewhere near Crestwood, climbing until it stopped being visible and then climbing further into a sky that didn't care. Most people here stopped seeing it after a year. Tavi had been on this block three months and hadn't stopped once.

Reza raised his hand. Tavi looked at it for a full second, then slapped it anyway. The crack of the high-five bounced off the boarded window behind them and died down the empty street.

"Okay," Reza said, grinning now that Ozzie couldn't see it. "So. Up's a direction."

"Up's one direction," Tavi said.

[Beat 2]
The block south of Ozzie's squat opened onto Skillet Row through an alley that smelled like hot oil and wet concrete, and under that, something sweet from the stalls ahead. Skillet Row ran the length of a dead loading dock nobody had bothered to seal off — a hundred booths crammed into a space built for delivery trucks that hadn't run in a decade. Each one was a fold-out counter, a burner, and whoever was willing to stand behind it. The smell hit them at the mouth of the alley. Tavi's stomach answered before he did.

They ate standing at a counter whose owner never once looked at them. The bowls were the kind of cheap that meant the broth was real, because nothing else was. Reza ate like a man who'd skipped breakfast. Between them they had Φ38, all of it on Reza's credstick. Tavi's had read Φ0.00 since Tuesday.

"We could do courier work," Reza said.

"Courier work pays eleven quanta a day."

"We could do a lot of courier work."

Tavi looked at him. Reza kept eating.

The booth two down had no sign. That was the first thing. The second was the woman behind it — broad, unhurried, in an apron that had been white once and had reached its current color through honest work. She watched them the way you watch the weather. Not interested. Just tracking. When Tavi met her eyes she didn't look away. She ladled broth into a bowl for someone else without breaking the look.

"That one," Tavi said.

Reza glanced up. "The lady with the ladle?"

"The lady with the ladle."

They left their bowls half-finished — the only thing either of them had left to spend on dignity — and moved down the Row. Up close the booth smelled like pork fat and ginger and something chemical underneath that Tavi couldn't name. The woman set the ladle down. Her name was Scraps, though nobody said so; a name like that just piles up, the fifth or sixth time you hear someone use it. She looked at their temples — no etch. Their hands — no chrome. Then their faces, the way you read a page you've read many times and find it hasn't changed.

"You're not here for noodles," she said.

"We're looking for work," Tavi said.

"What kind."

"The kind that exists."

She picked up a cloth and wiped the counter. It didn't need it. "You ever hurt anyone?"

Reza said, "Not professionally."

Something moved in her face, a thin flicker that wasn't quite a smile. She reached under the counter and set a folded slip of paper on top. An address, a name, a number. The number was Φ200, split two ways — Φ100 each. Tavi hadn't seen that much in one place in six weeks. His face didn't move.

"Remind him," she said. "Don't break anything you can't explain. Come back with confirmation and I'll have your hundred each. No advance. No exceptions. No sympathy if you don't come back, because I'm not your mother and this isn't that kind of booth."

Tavi picked up the slip. Reza was already grinning. Tavi didn't ask what confirmation meant, which was the thing he should have been thinking about, and wasn't.

They left thrilled, walking fast, the slip folded flat in Tavi's shirt pocket. Behind them, Scraps picked up her ladle. She didn't watch them go. She was on to the next bowl.

[Beat 3]
The address was a walk-up three blocks over and around the corner. They found it on the second try. The first door turned out to be a laundry, and its owner threw it open mid-rant about something that had nothing to do with them. Wrong door — except not really. Tavi had read the number right. The building had numbered its units wrong. Two different problems.

The debtor's name was Pellucid, which was not a name, and the door with it stenciled in flaking yellow was answered by a man who was not Pellucid. He was very large, and he stood close enough that Tavi could smell the protein supplement on his breath. Behind him a Volume feed played something with a lot of red in it. The unit was the size of Ozzie's squat, but fuller and hotter, with three more people in it who all stopped to look.

"We're here about the money," Tavi said, and knew it was wrong the second it left his mouth. The room's temperature changed before he finished the sentence.

The big man's hand came off the door frame — not a punch, a grab, closing on Reza's collar, because Reza had stepped forward to look friendly. That had seemed like a good idea eight seconds ago. Reza went sideways into the frame and the man hauled him in. The only thought Tavi's body had room for was that the slip had said remind, not ask. Then he was inside too, and the door sealed behind them with a magnetic click.

What happened next took eleven seconds. Tavi counted, because counting was something he could do while his brain caught up. Reza hit the floor once, found his feet, and came up at the wrong angle — too close, no room, the big man already moving again. Reza threw his whole weight forward, all mass and no technique, and his elbow caught the nose at an angle no one had planned. The sound it made was specific. The man's head snapped back. He sat down on a folding chair that buckled and dumped him on the floor, and he stayed there. The room went quiet, except for the Volume feed, which kept going.

Reza wasn't moving like a man who'd made a mistake. He stood over the body, breathing. His hands hung at his sides, and they were completely still.

The three others were watching Reza. Reza was watching the man on the floor. His face wasn't the grinning face. It was something else, something neither of them had a name for yet, and it lasted three seconds. Then he turned to Tavi, almost calm, waiting to be told what came next.

Tavi found the panel. Every squat like this had one — maintenance access, easy to miss until the day you needed it. He pried it open with the edge of his credstick and looked at the board inside and understood about forty percent of it, which turned out to be the right forty percent. He pulled the correct wire on the second try. The lights dropped to emergency orange. In the confusion — and there was confusion, the three others now moving for the door — Reza said, very clearly, like he already knew someone would ask him to say it again, "He says next Tuesday." Then they were out the door and moving, running south, because south was away.

They were four blocks and two turns gone before they stopped. Reza bent forward with his hands on his knees. Tavi's lungs were doing something he wouldn't have called breathing. Around them the Gray Zone churned on, the haze thick and brown and full of things that weren't their problem. Reza made a sound that was either a laugh or the thing right before one. Tavi looked at his own hands, which were shaking, and then at Reza's, which were not.

"Confirmation," Reza said, still bent over. "What's confirmation?"

"We go back and tell her what he said," Tavi said. "That's confirmation. Proof we were in the room."

Reza straightened up. The grin was back, but it was sitting on top of something that hadn't been there this morning, and Tavi saw it and didn't say so. He put his hands in his pockets to quiet them, and they started walking back toward Skillet Row.

[Beat 4]
Scraps took the credstick without looking up from the ladle. She tapped it against a reader mounted under the counter lip, handed it back to Tavi, and that was it. No ceremony. No sign anything had happened. No version of good work he would ever get from her. He looked at the display.

Φ200.00.

He stared, waiting for the zeroes to correct themselves. He looked at Reza. Reza looked at the display. The number sat there, patient and real. Then Reza made a sound that wasn't a word and grabbed Tavi's arm with both hands, and the credstick nearly slipped into the gutter running along the Row, and Tavi snatched it back against his chest like a child.

"Don't," Tavi said, but Reza was laughing, turning a full circle in the middle of the Row, and a woman with a bag of vegetables stepped around him without breaking stride, because the Gray Zone had seen worse.

They walked fast, talking over each other, planning the night with the reckless certainty of two nineteen-year-olds holding the biggest number either of them had ever owned. Drinks first — something real, not the floor-sweepings Ozzie bought in bulk. Then something to smoke or swallow or breathe in, whatever Reza's guy in Bridgeport was moving this week. Then girls — or whoever, Tavi said, and Reza said, "obviously, whoever" — and the rest could sort itself out by morning. Every quanta of it was getting spent, and spent tonight. That part was never in question.

Reza put his hand up and Tavi hit it, hard, palm to palm, the crack swallowed by the Row's noise. They were both grinning now, the same grin, the one that didn't know yet what it was standing on top of. The credstick was warm in Tavi's pocket. His hands had stopped shaking somewhere between the squat and here, and he hadn't noticed when.

## Or We Do It Again

[Beat 5]
The credstick sat on the bar between them, balance strip lit, and Reza was already working it out loud — the way he did everything, at volume, with his hands. He split it in two and named what his half could buy. A room with a window. A meal that came on a plate instead of in a bag. Maybe the Cermak Tier-2 consult he'd been putting off — get the basic mesh started, stop being the guy in every room who was slowest to catch up.

Tavi looked at the number on the strip. It was enough to stop.

"Or we do it again," he said.

Reza stopped mid-sentence. Turned. He had the look of a man who'd just heard a language he almost spoke.

"Do it —" he said. "The same job?"

"Different job. Same decision."

Reza picked up the credstick, turned it over in his fingers, and let it drop back to the bar. It was loud around them — someone's Volume feed bleeding sports commentary over the corner booth, the bartender running a rag across chrome that didn't need it — and for a moment neither of them said anything, which for Reza almost never happened.

"Brother," he said finally, "I have been sleeping in a stairwell for six weeks."

"I know."

"I have been eating —" he paused, hunting for the word. "I don't know what I've been eating. Gray things."

"I know."

"And you want to put the number back in the machine."

"I want to put half the number back in the machine," Tavi said. "Take the room. Eat something with a color. Just —" He stopped. He was not good at finishing this kind of sentence, and didn't pretend to be. He looked at the credstick instead of at Reza. "Keep the other half working."

Reza stared at him a long moment, real work going on behind it, and then he laughed. Not the loud one he used when something was actually funny — the shorter one, the sound that came out when something landed somewhere true.

"You've been wanting something," Reza said. Not an accusation. Quieter than that.

Tavi didn't answer, which was also an answer.

Reza picked up his glass, drank, set it down, and looked at the strip one more time. The number glowed. He pushed the credstick across the bar toward Tavi — not all the way, just far enough that it was Tavi's problem now — and said, "Split it clean. You run the reinvest. I don't want to know the details until there are details worth knowing."

"That's not how it works."

"That's exactly how it works," Reza said. "I got the door. You got the plan. That's the whole us."

[Beat 6]
The job was a runner intercept — lift a data package off a mid-level Axiom logistics courier before he made his drop at the Cermak border market. Ozzie pulled the brief from a contact he wouldn't name and took his fifteen percent off the top before handing it over. That was fair. Ozzie had a room and a contact list. They had neither.

Tavi spent two days on the approach. The courier's route had three soft points. He mapped all three and weighed each one for time and exposure, and sat with it until only one was left. A four-minute handoff window. A loud market. The courier wore the package on a lanyard under his jacket, which meant somebody had to get close — and close meant the courier had to be looking at something other than his own chest.

"That's me," Reza said, when Tavi walked him through it.

"That's you."

"What do I do?"

"You knock something over."

Reza looked at him.

"You knock something over," Tavi said again, "and when he turns to look, I'm already past him."

The thing Reza knocked over was a three-tier display of vacuum-sealed protein blocks at the market's west entrance. It went down with the specific catastrophic sound of something stacked by a person who cared about it. The courier turned. So did every vendor within ten meters. Tavi went through the crowd at an angle that wasn't quite a run — too deliberate for panic, too fast for browsing — and when he came out the other side, the lanyard was in his jacket pocket. The courier was still apologizing to the vendor, because Reza was helping restack the blocks, one at a time, with enormous solemnity, and it was very hard to be angry at a man that large being that careful.

In the freight corridor behind the market, Tavi handed the package to Ozzie's contact and took back a credstick that read higher than the last one. Reza came around the corner still wearing the solemn face, lost it the second their eyes met, and something went through Tavi's chest, quick and warm, before he had a word for it. Being good at a thing. On purpose. Nobody had ever told either of them that was on the menu.

He checked his hands anyway, because he wanted to know. Steady.

[Beat 7]
The clinic was called Biter, which Ozzie said was either a recommendation or a warning. The sign above the door was a tooth in chrome-flake paint that had started to peel at the canine. Bloom Quarter fringe — not inside the Substrate seam, but close enough that the nano coverage ran thin and the air tasted faintly of something the body knew was wrong. The woman at the front desk had eyes that tracked half a second behind her head. Tier-1 mesh at least, probably installed here, probably by whoever was in the back room with the bone saw.

Reza went first, because he was always going to go first.

The subdermal work took three hours. They laid him on a table that smelled of isopropyl and something organic underneath it. The technician had small hands and no wasted motion. She worked the composite sheeting in through four incisions along the ribs and two more at the shoulder caps, and used a thermal press that made a sound like a staple gun turned up loud, a sound Reza felt in his molars. The reflex splice was faster — a cluster of six thread-anchors seated at the C6 vertebra — but it needed him awake for the second half, lying facedown, not moving. The technician told him not to move. He didn't. He kept his hands flat against the table and focused on them.

Tavi watched from the waiting chair until they told him to stop watching, and then he looked at the wall.

The jack behind Tavi's ear took forty minutes and a local anesthetic. He felt a pressure through his skull that he couldn't call pain — more like the world insisting on itself in a place it had never been. Afterward the technician handed him a small mirror. The port was flush-set and matte gray, barely bigger than the nail on his smallest finger. He touched it once. The edge was smooth. His face in the mirror looked the same as before, which felt like a small, specific lie.

They bought the pistols from a counter at the back of the same building — Ozzie had arranged that too, and taken fifteen percent, because Ozzie arranged everything and took fifteen percent of everything. Two identical matte-black holdouts, small enough to sit in a waistband without printing, simple enough to strip down without a manual. No branding. The man behind the counter called them Ankle-Biters, either a model name or a joke about their range. Tavi took his and turned it in his hands twice, checking the action, and then looked at Reza, who had already taken up the same grip without thinking about it. Same angle. Same hold. Something moved across Reza's face that wasn't quite a smile.

"Same," Reza said.

"Same," Tavi said.

Reza holstered his and winced when the motion pulled at the incisions. For the next three days the hardware argued with him — low fever, the sheeting hot to the touch through the skin, a deep ache in the shoulder caps that woke him twice a night. He lay in the room he'd rented — a window, as promised, looking out on a ventilation stack — and let the wrongness pass through him like weather. Present. Specific. Temporary. On the fourth day it stopped arguing and started being part of him, and he pressed two fingers against the shoulder cap and felt the resistance there, something that hadn't been there before, something that would stop what it was built to stop.

Tavi's jack itched for a week. Not on the surface — underneath, where the anchor threads had seated into the tissue. He didn't touch it. He checked the mirror each morning and it looked the same as the day before: flush, matte, unremarkable. The first time he plugged a test line into it, the data arrived as something other than sound or sight — a temperature, a quality of the air. He sat very still for a long moment in Ozzie's Squat, with Ozzie's soldering gear on the table in front of him.

He unplugged the line. Then, after a moment, he plugged it back in.

## The Angels

[Beat 8]
Three flights down from the street, the Substrate coverage thinned to something you could feel — a faint absence in the air, like the pressure dropping before weather. Reza paid at the front desk, the credstick chirping twice for both their tabs. A woman with neuretic etching running silver from her jaw to her ear showed them down a hall that smelled of cedar oil and recycled breath and, under both, something sweet and slightly wrong, like fruit going over. The rooms were divided by curtains of weighted beads. Tavi pulled his aside and looked at the bed and the two women already on it — one sitting cross-legged, one lying with her arm over her eyes — and looked back at Reza with an expression that had nothing in it but the fact of the moment, which was enough.

Sunday was the one sitting. She smiled when Tavi came in — not the professional smile, or not only that, something warmer under it, the way a person smiles when they've decided to be present. She held up the jack lead between two fingers, nails short, wrists small. Tavi sat on the edge of the bed. She leaned forward and seated the lead behind his ear at the port, and the connection opened, and the Honeycomb's Glass flooded in, and the ceiling went away.

The Glass didn't invent anything. It took what was already there — weight, warmth, the grain of another person's skin under his hand — and opened every channel past the point the body knew how to hold it. Sunday's collarbone under his mouth was real, and then more than real, a texture that landed in his chest before it reached his lips. Heat moved through him in a slow wave with no ceiling. He heard her breath change. Her hand pressed flat against his back and pulled, and the pull was an answer to something he hadn't known he'd asked.

Across the bead curtain — close enough that the sound carried, far enough that it was its own world — he heard Ivet say something low to Reza, one sentence, then Reza's laugh, then a silence that wasn't empty. Tavi's hand found Sunday's hip and she shifted her weight into it, a small deliberate motion, her eyes open and on him. Inside the Glass she felt enormous, the only fixed point in a room that kept going soft at the edges. He raised his palm off the mattress and held it out sideways, through the gap in the curtain, fingers spread. A beat. Then Reza's palm met his from the other side, the slap of it loud and stupid and perfect, and Tavi laughed into Sunday's shoulder, and she laughed too — the real kind, her ribs moving under his arm — and he pressed his face into the warmth of her and was certain, down every open channel, that this was love.

[Beat 9]
The Glass never eased them down. It just let go — no ceremony, no gradient, a threshold crossed and the world ordinary again, worse for the comparison. Tavi lay on his back and the ceiling was a ceiling, water-stained foam tile, one corner peeling away from its glue. Sunday was on her side facing him, awake, the jack lead coiled on the pillow between them. Her eye makeup had tracked a little. She looked at him like someone who had been watching him come back to himself and hadn't looked away, and there was nothing in her face trying to be anything.

"You've got money left," she said. "For the morning. If you want."

Tavi said nothing.

The bead curtain shifted. Ivet came through dressed and ready to go, credstick in her jacket pocket, one earbead in. She looked at Reza — sitting up with the sheet around his waist and his hair wrong, blinking at the room like it owed him an explanation — and said nothing. Her bag was still in the corner where she'd left it the night before. Set down, not dropped, the strap looped neatly over the chair. She picked it up and was gone, the curtain settling behind her, her footsteps fading on the stairs before it stopped moving.

Sunday watched the curtain. Then she looked back at Tavi, and something moved across her face, fast and practical, and she reached up and unclipped a second earbead from the shelf above the bed and seated it. In that one motion she was somewhere else entirely, three transactions further down her day. Tavi saw it happen. Something cold settled in his chest — not about her, about the ceiling, the foam tile, the exact smell of this room in the morning. He sat up. Reza, across the curtain, was very quiet.

"We're not doing this again," Tavi said. Not to Sunday. To Reza, through the gap.

A pause. Then: "No," Reza said. No joke in it, no warmth, no exclamation. Just the word. Sunday pulled her jacket off the foot of the bed and didn't look at either of them. She had heard boys say it before. Tavi didn't look away.

[Beat 10]
The next eight months had a shape you could only see afterward: a narrowing. Reza found a trainer named Breckenridge, ex-Arcturus, who ran a gym out of a gutted auto shop two blocks from Ozzie's Squat and held opinions about footwork the way other men held religion. He charged Φ40 a session and Reza paid it every time. What he got back was a body that already knew how to be large, learning how to be precise about it — where the line sat between a presence and a problem, how to fill a doorway so that filling it said something. Tavi watched him run the drill once, near the end of those months. Three of Breckenridge's other students took turns rushing the frame, and Reza gave nothing back, weight low, breath even, until the count ran out and he was still standing in the doorway like he'd grown there.

Tavi, across those same months, found a different kind of narrow. He had always been fast at reading a room. He got faster. He started taking apart security systems for the pleasure of it — pulling a thread because pulling felt good and he wanted to know how far it went.

Ozzie's board ran two kinds of listing, mostly: solo work and paired work. Paired paid less per head. A fixer selling a client two bodies was selling insurance, not skill, and insurance came at a discount. A ghost booked alone got a solo rate. A wall booked alone got a different one. A package — ghost and wall, guaranteed together — bought the client peace of mind and cost Reza and Tavi money apiece. Neither of them minded yet. Neither had needed to be apart.

The job was a courier intercept in the upper Yards, a Lotus-adjacent contract off Ozzie's board — a data package moving through a mid-tier logistics node with better security than it deserved. The client wanted the package and wanted no noise. They went in through the service corridor on Tavi's count. He was at the terminal in forty seconds, fingers already moving, and the node opened for him the way a lock opens when someone who's been listening for years finally hears the right click. Alarms, cameras, the desk ident panel, the access log, the flag protocol — he took them in order, not fast, exact, each one clean. When he was done the room was dark in a way the lights being off couldn't explain. Erased-from-the-record dark. Somewhere above, in the node's incident queue, there was nothing. He pulled the package and stood up. A runner named Vasquez, waiting at the corridor's end, said "what the fuck" under her breath, and then, quieter, "Cutout." Tavi heard it, didn't answer, and carried it out with the package.

Reza's part came twenty minutes later, on the way out. Three of the node's private security rounded the stairwell corner and found Reza in the doorway of the only exit that mattered, and Reza did what Breckenridge had taught him, which was nothing complicated. He held the door. The first came in low with a shock baton. Reza caught the wrist and drove it into the second man's jaw, and the jaw made a sound, and the second man went down and stayed down. Then it was two, and then it was one, and the last one looked at Reza in the doorway and understood the door was not going to open for him — not through anything he had — and he was right. Vasquez went past with the package. Tavi went past. Reza stepped back and let the door close, and the last man didn't follow. Out on the street, in the dark, Vasquez looked at Reza a moment and said "Rampart," like she was testing whether the word fit. It did. She didn't say anything else.

Tavi felt it on the walk back to Ozzie's Squat — not as a problem, not yet, just a fact he turned over like a stone. Reza had stood in a doorway and made his body the only door that mattered. Tavi had stood at a terminal and made himself into something the cameras couldn't find a reason to remember. Nobody had trained them to be opposites. It had just come out that way. By the end of the week they were on Ozzie's board as a package, Rampart/Cutout, and it was a good board, and their rate began, slowly, to climb.

## The First Job Without Him

[Beat 11]
The job was a pull-and-carry: one data slug from a dead-drop inside the Ashgrave perimeter, no contact with anyone who worked there, no trace on the way out. Auda Vane had said precision the way other people say please — like it was a courtesy and a threat at once.

Tavi had asked, at her counter, whether it was a two-hand job. He hadn't said Reza's name. He hadn't had to. Vane didn't look up from her annex. "The client bought a ghost," she said. "Bring a second and the contract voids and you eat the kill fee." She slid the brief across the counter with two fingers. "You want to carry a wall around, carry it on your own time." She looked at Tavi when she said it. She did not look at Reza, who wasn't in the room, because there'd been no reason to bring him. That was the new thing, and neither of them would have said it out loud.

Tavi moved through the Corridor's service margin at the hour when the haze thickened enough to cut sight to fifteen meters and the perimeter lights went amber and useless. The smell was chemical and wet — sulphur, and something sweeter under it, the Substrate's cleanup nano losing its argument with whatever Ashgrave was cooking in the secondary refinery. He kept to the drainage channel, ankle-deep in runoff that left an oily film on his boots, and did not think about Reza sitting in the rented room above the noodle counter in the Yards, waiting for a ping that wouldn't come until it was over.

Twice on the approach his hand drifted toward the channel to loop Reza in — reflex, two years deep, the wall goes where the ghost goes — and twice Vane's terms stopped it before the thought finished. No second body. No noise. No door to hold. A wall in a place like this was just mass that got seen. He took his hand off the channel. What he couldn't stop looking at, later, was how little it had cost him to do it.

The dead-drop took four minutes to find — a maintenance panel with a loose lower-left fastener, a tell Vane had described in twelve words. The slug was there. He pocketed it without breaking stride, came back out through the channel, and was two blocks into the buffer zone before Ashgrave's next perimeter sweep finished. Clean and quiet, an exit that left nothing behind to explain. For a moment he stood in the gray space between the Corridor's glow and the dark of the Gray Zone proper, feeling — briefly, and without permission — the particular lightness of a job with only one weight to carry.

He pinged Vane. One character. She sent the transfer confirmation before he'd closed the channel. Somewhere behind him, in the rented room, Reza's screen was still dark.

[Beat 12]
The job needed both of them. Auda Vane had booked it as a package, Rampart and Cutout — a ghost to open a mid-tier depot in the Bloom Quarter, a wall to hold the loading dock while the ghost worked. One rate, split two ways. The kind of job they'd run a dozen times.

Reza pinged him an hour out.

Tavi took it on the walk over, the words opening behind his eyes the way neuretics traffic did. *Can't make it, brother. Coeli got us into the Volume — real seats, the arena, not the feed. I'll square the split with you, I swear.* Then, a moment later, softer, like he'd thought about it: *She says bring your friend next time. She means you.*

Tavi stood on the corner and read it twice.

He'd met Coeli once, for about a minute, outside a lift terminal. Finnish-Somali, easy, warm, a level up from anything he and Reza had ever touched. She'd been kind to him. That was the part he kept catching on. She'd done nothing wrong. She just lived somewhere Tavi wasn't headed, and Reza was already halfway up the stairs.

He could have voided the job. He worked it instead.

The depot opened for him the way they all did now. He took the idents, the cameras, the dock log, in order, clean, and pulled the package. He had never needed Reza for that part.

The guard came around the container stack from the right. Reza's side.

The reflex was two years deep. Reza takes the right, so you take the left, and you stop even looking that way. Tavi went left. There was no one there.

Auda Vane sent two messages. That was how it happened.
