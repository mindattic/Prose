# Mnemosync

_Amara Osei and Seto Banda are both in Batch 44-C — Orison Neuretics' 847-client calibration pilot. Their carrier-grade neuretics create a two-way bleed: they share each other's memories. The memories that arrive are the ones the suppression protocol stripped of meaning. For the wrong person, those arrive whole._

[Beat 1]
The anomaly log had thirty-one entries.

She'd started it in March, kept it in a personal archive that Cellvault's system couldn't see. The format was clinical: client code, calibration date, observed drift pattern, delta from expected baseline. The delta column was the problem. Normal neuretic drift dispersed across the cognitive architecture like dye in water — diffuse, even, predictable by the standard model. These drifts clustered. Specific associative nodes, the same cognitive region across four different clients, showing a pattern that no maintenance error would produce.

She'd flagged it for herself. Not for Cellvault. Filing it would mean a compliance review, and a compliance review would mean a hold on her maintenance access while they routed everything to Orison for evaluation. Orison held the master maintenance contracts. Orison evaluated everything.

She went home through Zone 6, the freight grid moving slow in the afternoon heat. Her nav overlay — arbitrated by one of the transit intelligences that actually ran the grid, not the corp whose logo it wore — routed her wide around three Orison-controlled corridors, the way it had started doing weeks ago, for reasons it did not surface and she had stopped asking it to. The roadbed healed itself as the freight ground over it, hairline cracks closing in pale nanite seams, the city repairing its own surface a micron at a time the way it always had, under everything, whether or not anyone watched. She made dinner and thought about the delta column. At eleven, with the apartment quiet and the lumen mouse doing whatever it did inside the wall, something bled into her signal that wasn't hers.

Someone else's evening. A freight route. The texture of a city she recognized from a different angle — not her zones, not her corners. She sat with it for four seconds. Then she opened her log and added a new column.

*Unidentified signal — first receipt, 23:07. Duration: ~12 seconds. Origin: unknown.*

She filed it next to the drift patterns. One more thing that didn't match the model.

[Beat 2]
The silence had a shape.

Eleven years of courier work through the Gray Zone was long enough to know the difference between a market that had dried up and a market where specific knowledge had been removed. These were different textures. A dry market went still evenly — no particular direction, the whole system quiet. What he was tracking moved like a shadow with a specific source.

He kept the map in a carrier-grade encrypted archive. Routes marked in black for active, gray for reduced, white for gone. The white routes were the problem. They corresponded too precisely to a cluster of industrial corridors, processing facilities, a medical maintenance sector he'd worked under Orison-adjacent contracts eighteen months ago.

He'd been running queries against GLMZ health data. Cross-referencing public records against what he knew about his contact network. The pattern wasn't random. It had a radius and a direction.

He finished the evening's run, dropped a data parcel at a Zone 7 relay, took the long route home. In the freight grid his neuretics ticked through the ambient — standard city traffic, nav overlays, the background noise of ten million people who didn't know each other. And beneath it, something that didn't belong. A fragment. The specific texture of someone else's work: numbers, a delta column, a clinical attention he didn't have a name for.

He filed it under diagnostic anomaly and ran a full security sweep when he got home.

Clean.

He didn't sleep well.

[Beat 3]
Five weeks in, she had a profile.

He worked variable hours — the rhythm of someone whose schedule moved with demand rather than routine. He had two consistent points in his day: a Zone 7 relay he stopped at most evenings, and somewhere he went early on Wednesdays that felt different in the ambient than the rest of his week. She'd received forty-seven fragments. She knew he maintained something mechanical by hand. She knew he had a sister somewhere he thought about the way you thought about gravity — always present, never examined.

The hardest thing she'd received was his attention. Whatever he was doing with his work, he was precise about it in a way she recognized professionally. He paid the same kind of attention to things that she paid to calibration anomalies.

She composed the message carefully. Technical, diagnostic — the kind of communication you sent to a quality team when you'd found an unusual result. She explained the mechanism: harmonic resonance in neuretic systems was documented but rare. Two systems running at close enough frequency in a high-density signal environment could begin reflecting each other. Not a hack. Not a malfunction. The way two tuning forks in the same room both hum if you strike one.

She sent it into the frequency. Into whatever this was.

Forty-one minutes later: *Who are you.*

Not a question. She noticed this.

*My name is Amara,* she wrote back. *I work in neuretic maintenance. I've been receiving your signal for five weeks and I think you've been receiving mine. I have a theory about why — and I think you should hear it before you file this as a security incident.*

She sent it. Then she looked at her anomaly log and thought about what she hadn't included in the message yet.

[Beat 4]
He ran six diagnostics.

Clean, clean, clean, clean, clean, clean.

The message had arrived on a frequency that his carrier-grade security architecture should have flagged as unauthorized access. It hadn't. The sender wasn't routing through any path he could identify. She'd found him through something his system didn't model, which meant either the security architecture had a gap he'd been paying eight hundred Φ a month to not have, or she was right about the mechanism.

He checked the citation she'd included. The literature was real.

He spent two days thinking. What he was actually doing: deciding whether to respond. Eleven years of sealed-system reputation. Clients who paid specifically for the absence of entanglements. A contact network that depended on his signal being clean.

On the third day he wrote back: *What have you been receiving.*

She sent him the list. Twelve minutes. She knew his schedule, she knew he maintained something mechanical, she knew he had a sister. None of this existed in any registry. It had been in his head.

He sat with that.

He wrote: *My name is Seto. Tell me something — what are you noticing in the Orison maintenance contracts you work under.*

A long pause. He felt the pause differently than he would have felt a pause in a text message — it had a texture. Something weighing a decision.

She wrote: *That's a very specific question.*

*I know,* he sent. *Tell me anyway.*

[Beat 5]
Ciro Fonseca introduced himself on a Wednesday.

He came to Cellvault as Orison's account liaison for the quarterly contract review — routine, except that Orison's reviews were handled remotely, always, and in four years at Cellvault Amara had never seen an Orison representative come in person. Physical presence was for acquisitions and audits.

She noticed him before she knew who he was. The Zone 6 main floor didn't produce men like this. He wore a suit that understood the difference between precision and restraint. His hair was within a millimeter of Orison's maximum length standard — she only knew this because she'd processed the Orison grooming policy once in a compliance briefing and it had been specific enough to stick. He moved through the room with the quality of someone who'd already read it before walking in.

He came to her station. He'd asked who handled Orison sub-contract anomaly flagging and been directed to her specifically.

"You keep a personal quality log," he said. Not a question. He'd already reviewed Cellvault's system.

"Unofficial," she said. "I flag things I want to understand before I escalate."

He was quiet for a moment that felt deliberate. "That's a good instinct," he said. "Most maintenance techs flag or don't flag. The space between takes professional judgment."

His questions were technical. They were well-spaced, which meant he was listening to the answers rather than queuing the next one. At the end he gave her a card — official Orison channels, standard format.

"If you find something in that data that concerns you," he said, "I'd rather hear it from you than find it in a compliance report."

He smiled. He left.

Through the ambient, hours later, Seto felt something she wasn't naming yet — the temperature of a room just after something unexpected had entered it.

[Beat 6]
The white routes kept spreading.

He spent a Tuesday night overlaying his silence map against the quarterly GLMZ health data the Infrastructure Authority posted publicly. He was looking for coincidence and found correlation — the knowledge gaps in his contact network matched a distribution of neuretic maintenance visits to Orison-affiliated facilities. Same corridors. Same eighteen-month window.

He'd worked Orison-adjacent contracts three times in eleven years. Carried data for two Orison facility operators. One of them — a logistics coordinator named Bayo who'd been reliable for six years — had stopped taking his calls in October and started again in December with a specific gap. Everything between 2222 and October last year, just gone. Bayo had blamed it on stress. Had believed it himself.

The other was a med-tech named Shen whose knowledge of anything before her Orison contract now had a selective quality — like a document with specific paragraphs missing.

He sent Amara the map.

Her response took twenty minutes. What came through the ambient wasn't words — it was the quality of someone looking at something and understanding it was larger than the room they were standing in.

She sent: *I need to show you something. Where do you get your neuretics calibrated.*

He told her: a freelance specialist in Zone 7, no Orison affiliation. He'd chosen her specifically for that reason.

The pause before Amara's response was longer than anything she'd sent him since the beginning.

She sent him the batch documentation — what she'd pulled so far. He read it. He sat with it. In the ambient he felt her still working — the door-closed quality of someone running a search they needed to finish before they could stop.

[Beat 7]
She took the lunch.

She told herself it was fieldwork, which was the kind of thing you told yourself when you weren't ready to examine the alternatives. She dressed correctly — professional, not trying, the calibration of someone who knew the difference.

Ciro had chosen the restaurant. Good without being conspicuous, which was its own kind of statement. He was seated when she arrived, which she registered as a choice. He looked up from something he was reading and put it away without making a thing of it.

"Amara," he said. As if he already knew her.

He was better at this than she'd expected, which was saying something. He didn't ask about Orison. He didn't ask about the anomaly log. He asked about how she'd come to neuretic maintenance — what drew someone to a field where you worked inside people's cognitive architecture every day. His opinions about the gap between neuretics as a product and neuretics as an experience of living were precise and unsentimental. They matched hers more closely than she was comfortable with.

She ordered the vegetable congee. He ordered the vegetable congee. She noticed this and didn't say anything.

Over coffee, he reached into his jacket pocket and took out a small straight razor — bone handle, folded shut. He ran his thumb once along his jaw, checking, and put it away. The whole gesture lasted four seconds. He talked through it without pausing, about the difference between calibration as a technical procedure and calibration as a relationship between the tech and the patient.

She agreed with him. She was interested in him and she recognized that as a fact and didn't know what to do with it yet.

Through the ambient, Seto said that evening: *What did you find out.*

She sent: *Nothing I didn't already know.*

She meant something else by that. She thought he understood.

[Beat 8]
Ekow Ato stepped out from behind the HVAC housing at 6:14 PM.

Seto knew the name from the Gray Zone circuit — NGRA veteran, freelance contractor, the kind of work history you didn't have to advertise because the weight of it did the work. Ekow had a machete at his hip, handle worn, nothing decorative about it. His hands were open. He wasn't starting.

He'd read the rooftop before stepping out onto it — Seto could tell. The specific quality of someone who'd already placed everything in the space and confirmed the exits before announcing themselves.

"You've been pulling correlations from GLMZ health data against private courier records," Ekow said. He said it the way you stated a logistical fact. "Walk away from the Orison work."

Seto stayed still. "What is the Orison work."

Ekow looked at him.

"I was told," he said slowly, "you were moving stolen proprietary neuretic data."

"I'm not moving anything. I've been mapping silence — information that used to flow that isn't flowing anymore. Specific corridors. Specific knowledge." Seto paused. "What exactly did they tell you was stolen."

The machete stayed at Ekow's hip. His hands were still open. He was running the math — Seto could see it. The specific quality of a professional reassessing the stated terms of a contract.

"Gray Zone intermediary," Ekow said finally. "I didn't ask the chain."

"You should."

Ekow looked at him for a long moment. He reached into his jacket and took out a folded handkerchief — white, folded in quarters — and turned it once in his fingers without looking at it.

"I have seven days left on this contract," he said. "Make it easy for me and don't be where I can find you for seven days."

He left without touching anything.

Seto stood on the rooftop and thought about seven days. Then he thought about Amara's next scheduled Cellvault maintenance visit, which she'd mentioned once. Eight days.

He called her.

[Beat 9]
She found her batch number on a Thursday evening.

She'd been pulling at the Orison sub-contract archive for two weeks. The thread she was following was a calibration protocol that applied to specific tissue batch numbers and wasn't documented in any standard procedure manual she'd read. She followed it because it was anomalous and anomalous things were her professional instinct. She followed it further because once she saw the shape of it she couldn't stop.

Batch 44-C. Certified 2222. Her maintenance record from the Orison-affiliated upgrade three years ago — standard procedure at the time, nothing unusual. She'd had her neuretics serviced at the authorized facility, received the documentation, gone home.

She stood up from the terminal. She walked the length of the server row and came back.

The contamination flag on batch 44-C was in an internal Orison system that Cellvault's sub-contract access tier shouldn't have reached. She'd found the permissions gap two weeks ago and hadn't reported it because she was the kind of person who logged anomalies and waited to understand them.

She stopped scrolling. Her hands went flat on the desk.

Batch 44-C. 847 recipients. Documented accelerated degradation rate beginning 18–24 months post-certification. Internal classification: *managed liability.*

She looked at the calibration protocol attached to the batch. It targeted specific associative nodes — exactly the nodes she'd been flagging in her anomaly log. The same drift pattern. Not random. Designed.

She pulled everything she could reach and sent it to the secondary storage path Seto had set up two weeks ago for exactly this kind of material. She didn't let herself think about what she was reading until she'd pulled everything available.

Then she sat in the server room and let herself understand: her neuretics were failing because of a decision someone made on her behalf without her knowledge, and someone had been coming to her calibration visits to manage the evidence.

Her next visit was in six days.

She called Seto.

[Beat 10]
They moved everything in four hours.

It wasn't clean. Clean wasn't the standard when the timeline was six days and the opposition was a CorpoNation with a public contract pending. The standard was: what actually moved. What actually stuck.

He called three couriers he trusted — none Orison-affiliated, none on contracts traceable to the same intermediary chain. He set up three separate routing paths to three destinations: a GLMZ Infrastructure Authority auditor with a known record for not being buyable, a journalist at the Meridian 88 network who'd broken two corponation liability stories in four years, and a legal advocacy firm in Zone 3 that took neuretic rights cases on contingency.

Amara pulled from inside Cellvault while she still had access, working from a terminal that wasn't her station and a connection path he'd told her would leave no trace in the access logs. He told her what to prioritize: the batch certification, the contamination flag, the calibration protocol, the maintenance visit records tying specific client identities to specific suppression events.

She knew which data was worth having. He knew how to move it so it didn't disappear between here and wherever it needed to arrive.

What worked was the overlap. Her inside access and professional knowledge. His routes and channels and eleven years of knowing which paths held under pressure.

The third package reached the journalist at 11:47 PM. He confirmed receipt. He said he'd need a week to verify.

Seto said: *You have six days.*

The journalist said: *Then I have six days.*

Through the ambient, something in his sternum dropped a half-register — her breath, he thought, steadier than it had been in weeks.

[Beat 11]
She was three commands into a query on the Orison calibration records when Ciro came to her station on a Friday morning with coffee. She closed the window before he reached her desk.

He brought the specific blend she'd ordered at lunch, which she hadn't mentioned ordering regularly. He set it on the edge of her desk and asked if she'd found anything in the sub-contract data she wanted to bring to him.

His hair was exactly as it had always been. His jaw was the same. The quality of attention he paid her was, as it had always been, the kind that made you feel specifically considered.

What was different: his timing. Everything Ciro Fonseca had done until now had been deliberate, calibrated, arriving when she would expect it. He'd shown up this morning without a meeting request, without a reason she could locate in the Orison contract calendar. She'd been a neuretic tech long enough to know when a calibration was running half a beat wrong.

"The usual noise," she said.

He sat on the edge of her desk in a way that was technically appropriate and communicated that he was comfortable making himself at home in her space. He talked about the public infrastructure contract — the scope of the work Orison was moving into — in the tone of someone describing something already decided.

He reached into his jacket pocket and took out the straight razor. He ran his thumb along his jaw, slow, checking. Put it away.

"You're careful," he said. Not about the anomaly log.

She looked at him. There was something behind the assessment that was almost genuine — a person who might, under other circumstances, have meant the things he'd seemed to mean. She understood now that this was the most dangerous thing about him. Not the alternative.

"If anything comes up," he said, "you know how to reach me." He picked up his coffee and left.

She sat very still.

Through the ambient, Seto felt the temperature drop three degrees without being told what had caused it.

She sent: *We need to move faster.*

[Beat 12]
The story ran at 11:22 AM on a Wednesday.

Five days and twelve hours after his call to the journalist. Four days after Amara's maintenance visit had been originally scheduled. Approximately nine hours before the city contract committee was due to convene for the final vote.

He read it on a Zone 6 tram, standing, holding the overhead grip. The story was thorough — it had the batch numbers, the contamination timeline, the calibration protocol in detail, three named former Orison clients who'd consented to having their maintenance records disclosed. It named the public infrastructure contract. It asked specific questions about the relationship between the contract scope and the suppression timeline.

By noon the contract committee had announced a delay pending independent audit. Orison's legal team had begun issuing statements. The Infrastructure Authority had opened a parallel inquiry.

He put his phone away. He thought about Ekow Ato's contract and what happened to a Gray Zone contract the moment its stated premise collapsed. Ekow was a professional. He'd have known it the second the story ran.

He sat in his apartment and felt the ambient. Amara was somewhere in the city. He'd felt her wake up that morning — the specific texture of a day she was ready for. He felt when she read the story: a brief stillness, then something that didn't simplify into one word.

She sent: *Thank you.*

He sent: *You did the work.*

She sent: *We did.*

He looked at the Zone 7 map on his table — the one she'd given him, her notation, her hand. He sat with it for a while.

She sent: *I have the procedure scheduled. Thursday.*

He already knew. He'd felt her make the call.

*I know,* he sent.

[Beat 13]
The procedure was at ten.

She'd had it scheduled before the story ran, before Ciro had stood at her station with coffee and a razor and the specific patience of someone running a closing strategy. The scheduling hadn't changed. Everything around it had.

She made coffee. She fed the lumen mouse — it had graduated from nameless anomaly to ambient household fact over the last months; it took what she left and retreated to wherever it went during the day. She looked at her flat case of maps. Zones 1 through 10, her hand throughout, minus the ones she'd given Seto.

She sent him: *I'm going in.*

His response arrived before she'd put her phone down. She'd noticed this about him since the beginning: when she reached for him, he was already there.

*How are you,* he sent.

*Tell me something first,* she wrote. *What did you actually think when you got my first message.*

A pause. Then: *That you were a security threat. I spent two days prepared for you to be a security threat.*

*And then.*

*And then I got fragments of your anomaly log through the ambient and thought: this person is running better diagnostics than I am. That was the more interesting problem.*

She smiled. The lumen mouse was doing something in the walls.

*I didn't decide to find you,* she sent. *I want you to know I know that. It wasn't a choice I made.*

*Neither did I,* he sent. *It became one, though.*

She sat with that for a while.

*Tuesday morning,* she sent. *Zone 7. The fountain.*

The response came back before she'd put the phone in her bag.

*I'll be there.*

She picked up her bag and told herself she was ready. The difference between now and the first morning she'd received his signal was that now it was true.

[Beat 14]
The signal stopped at 10:47 in the morning.

He was at his kitchen table. He'd cleared his schedule — no work, no queries, nothing that required him to be a sealed system. He'd made coffee. He'd been sitting with the Zone 7 map she'd given him, her notation, her careful hand marking where people actually went versus where the official charts said they went.

At 10:47 the ambient went ordinary. The frequency he'd been living alongside for four months — that he'd come to locate the way you located your own heartbeat without trying — simply wasn't there. Replaced by the ordinary static of the city. Ten million people who didn't know each other, going about their morning.

He sat in it.

He thought about what she'd said: *It wasn't a choice I made. It became one, though.*

He pulled out a clean sheet of paper. He had her notation system memorized — the three parallel lines that marked significant intersections, the different route weights, the specific mark she used for places that mattered for reasons that weren't on any official map. He started copying Zone 7. His handwriting was worse than hers, courier-functional, but the route was the route.

At the bottom of the page he wrote in her notation system:

*Zone 7. The fountain. Tuesday, 0900. I'll be there.*

He folded it and put it in his jacket pocket.

Then he looked at the rest of her maps — Zones 1 through 6, her hand throughout — and thought about how long you could know a person's city before you knew their face, and how the second thing was probably easier than the first, and what that said about which one mattered more.

Tuesday was five days away.

He put on his jacket and went to learn the rest of her city.

[Beat 15]
# The Log She Didn't File

The arcology had no clocks, which Amara had learned to read as a kind of clock.

Daud Mtembe's building sat in Zone 5, one of the Orison-affiliated residential towers where the air was managed to a flat, conditioned neutrality — no diesel, no lake, no season. The lobby news feed ran a curated loop of good infrastructure announcements and weather that was always about to improve. She had been in a dozen towers like it. They all smelled faintly of nothing, which was a smell, and they all withheld the time, because a person who could not check the hour was a person who stayed.

Daud met her at the door with both hands out. Forty-three, broad through the shoulders, second-generation neuretics — you could tell from the older mount scar behind the ear, the seam slightly raised where the first-gen housing had been swapped. A freight scheduler. He remembered her. He remembered the segment.

"The coffee correspondent," he said, warm, and she let him, because warmth was data too.

She set her recorder on the table between them and ran the standard follow-up — the polite architecture of beat journalism, checking in on a series subject two years out. How's the routing work. How's the implant holding. He answered easily. The implant was fine. The work was fine.

Then she asked about the workers' association.

In 2223, over coffee after the cameras were off, Daud had talked for forty minutes. The Z5 freight corridor was being re-routed without community input. He'd been organizing — quietly, carefully — with the transit workers' association. He had used the word *theft* about the corridor. He had used the word *survival*. She had not been able to record it and had written it down afterward in the back of the cab on the way home, the way you preserve a thing you might need.

"That all worked out fine," Daud said now.

She kept her face in its broadcast neutral. "Worked out how?"

"The routing went through. It's fine. The association looked at it and we were all" — he paused, found the word, and she watched him find it — "fine with how it landed."

"You had concerns. About the corridor."

"I remember that." He said it without defensiveness, which was the thing. A man hiding a reversal got defensive. Daud was reporting weather from a country he no longer lived in. "I had a lot of concerns. I don't really feel that way about it now. It's fine."

Four times in seven minutes. She counted, because counting was what she had instead of a name for it. *Fine.* Not resigned. Resignation had friction in it — the sandpaper of a man who'd lost and knew it. This was smoother than that. He remembered the opinion the way you remember a city you visited once. He could describe the building. He could not tell you it had ever been cold.

She thanked him, professionally. She took the elevator down through the managed air and out the lobby doors, and the Seam hit her in the face — the zone boundary where the conditioning ended and the real city began, concrete and diesel and, under it, the cold mineral edge of the lake. She stood in it a moment and breathed like a person surfacing.

---

On the platform she made her notes.

The professional ones first, into the recorder transcript that synced to the Tribune content management system: *Mtembe, follow-up, 2225. Implant performing to spec. Subject reports satisfaction with Z5 corridor outcome.* Clean. Filable. True in the narrow way that things were true when you only said the part that could be verified.

Then the other notes. Not the Tribune stack — those left a record, and the record went places. She kept the real log on her own credentials, a personal partition the CMS didn't index, a private taxonomy eight months in the building.

Thirty-one entries. People she'd interviewed after their calibration visits who had drifted in a specific direction. They used to have objections. Now they were fine. She had sorted them by severity. Type A was tonal — a softening. Type B was deflection. Type C was the one that kept her up: a complete reversal of a previously held strong position, with no life event to explain it, and the source remembering the position while feeling nothing toward it.

Daud was Type C. He made four.

Four in eight months. She wrote his entry beside the other three and sat with the number on the cold platform, and the train came, and she did not pitch it.

Because she knew what filing was. Filing meant carrying it to her editor or pushing a formal pattern query through the Tribune analytics stack, and both of those things named the sources, and a named source was no longer a question she got to ask. Four data points. She didn't understand the mechanism. She didn't understand who would care that she'd found it. *What she can verify is what's true* — that was the spine she'd built a career on, and right now she could verify a count and nothing else, so the count stayed private until she could verify a cause.

The train took her north along the Zone 6 industrial corridor, the windows giving her the Seam in long horizontal strokes — stacked freight, the lake going pewter, the towers of the managed zones lit so evenly they looked drawn. She'd covered all of it. She had three years of being the face that said the new thing was safe. She'd worn the upgrade on air. She'd reported her own ninety days as evidence.

She got off at her stop and walked home into a neighborhood with weather in it.

---

The apartment smelled of egusi — she'd made a pot the night before, grandmother's recipe transcribed in the back of a notebook in a hand that wasn't quite her grandmother's because her grandmother couldn't write and a cousin had taken it down. The smell was the best thing about coming home. At the baseboard, the lumen mouse was leaking again: a small phosphorescent seam where the wall met the floor, blue-green, some old bioluminescent sealant breaking down into a slow glow. She'd stopped meaning to fix it. It was company.

The maps were on the wall, official zone surveys overlaid with her own annotations — source geographies, the places people lived behind the places the city said they lived. She put the kettle on and opened the content management log out of habit, the end-of-day ledger of what her account had touched.

There was an entry she hadn't generated.

*Reader analytics: Source pattern J-44C. Tribune Wellness desk — active query: source wellness status check run against Batch 44-C cohort. Result: reviewed by Orison Communications content partnership desk, 21:44.*

She read it three times. She had not run that query. She had been careful all day not to run that query. But the credentials were hers, and the timestamp was hers, and somewhere between Daud's door and her own a process had reached into her source-check activity, pulled the Batch 44-C thread, and walked it to Orison's partnership desk before she'd finished her commute. They had the cohort. They had the shape of what she'd gone looking for. They had Daud's name, because Daud's name was the thread she'd pulled today.

She had not filed anything. The thing she'd most carefully not done had been done with her hands.

The tea went cold while she sat with it.

---

At 23:07, her hands closed around air.

She was at the table when it happened — not building from outside but arriving all at once, interior and total. Her hands were flat on the table. Then they are not doing that anymore. They are gripping a handlebar.

The specific pressure of standing weight over a bicycle's front fork: palms wrapped around a flat bar at shoulder width, the vibration of road surface traveling up through aluminum into the heel of each hand. Cold air on bare forearms at velocity — not the ambient cold of a room but the cold of movement, air made wind by passage through it. A city from a meter and a half above the pavement, no glass between her and it, just the road and the lean into a corner and the bicycle telegraphing every seam and patch in the asphalt up through the forks in a language she did not speak but, for eleven seconds, was fluent in. A district she had never entered — Zone 5 industrial, south of the Seam grid — rendered in smell and road texture and the sound of mag-lev passing overhead, the 139.2 beacon frequencies strobing through the frame metal, the way a courier would feel them and not notice.

She was at her kitchen table. She was also, entirely and at the same time, somewhere else, inside a body that knew things her body did not.

Then she was only at the table.

She looked at her hands. Still curved — fingers slightly bent, palms angled around a grip that wasn't there. She watched them return to flat over the span of three breaths.

The memory was in her tendons: the micro-tension of a grip held for distance, the compensatory pressure in her left wrist that corrected for a slight pull to the right in the front wheel. She had never held that tension. She had never noticed a pull in a front wheel. The knowledge sat in her hands with the specificity of repetition — a task done so many times it required no attention — and it had no origin she could account for.

She did not name it. Naming was filing of a different kind.

She opened the private log instead and added a column the taxonomy didn't have — not *Anomaly entry 32*, because this was not the same animal as the others, and she would not pretend a category before she had one. She dated it. 2225, 23:07. *Sensory, somatic, non-originating. Zone 5 industrial. Bicycle. Beacon freq 139.2.* She placed it on the page beside the four Type C entries and drew no line between them, asserted no connection she could not demonstrate, because the line was exactly the thing she didn't have yet.

She checked the content management log one more time.

She had not filed the anomaly. The query that had gone to Orison's desk at 21:44 was not hers. # The Dry Corridor

The handoff was at 14:00, a logistics firm in the Seam between Zone 3 and Zone 4 with no sign outside and no name he'd ever been given. Just an address and a frequency, the way most of his clients preferred it. Seto had run data for them on and off for three years, long enough that the contact protocols had simplified from six verification steps to two. Trust calculus. The most honest math he knew.

He came in off the underpass on the bicycle, coasting the last hundred meters to let his neuretics finish the ambient cycle. Zone 3's Arcturus Civil environmental processing ended at the street — a hard administrative edge, the air managed to the curb and not one meter further. Zone 4's Orison-adjacent overlay started twenty meters east. In the gap between them the air went unfiltered, both corps' beacon frequencies overlapping in a wash his tissue read as fullness, a specific resonance he'd learned the way a navigator learns a tide. Seam air, smelling of what the city was actually doing — exhaust off the freight access roads, someone cooking at a canteen window three floors up, the wet-metal smell of recycled condensate venting from the Arcturus boundary exchange. Carrier-grade tissue didn't chatter the way consumer implants did. It listened. The background peeled apart as he rolled: freight authentication beacons, the low hum of an Infrastructure Authority monitoring node, a commercial relay pushing discount pricing from something four blocks east. Nothing from the client building. Which was correct. Which was what you wanted.

The bicycle was a 2189 Ogaki track conversion, steel-lugged, the kind of frame that required actual knowledge to maintain. He preferred to know what was happening inside the things that were his.

He locked it to a conduit housing outside the building and took the freight elevator up. The contact was a woman in her fifties with a data coordinator's posture, slightly forward, slightly compressed, someone who spent her days making decisions about latency. She took the transfer chip without ceremony, ran the verification, handed back the receipt.

"Clean?"

"Sealed," he said.

That was the whole conversation. He was back on the bicycle in four minutes.

---

He ate at a counter in Zone 4 before pulling the map — a bowl of githeri — habit and hunger. The counter was loud enough. Nobody was paying attention to a man looking at a screen.

The map was encrypted at three layers, which was more than most people bothered with for a personal archive and less than he would have used if he'd known, eight months ago, what it was going to contain. He'd started it as professional notation. He'd kept route-quality data for years — more intuition than record at first, the kind of thing you develop after a decade of gray-zone courier work, when you begin reading traffic the way a navigator reads wind. A corridor going quiet meant competition, pricing shifts, occasionally enforcement pressure from one CorpoNation or another staking out territory. Dry routes had explanations. You found them, adjusted, moved on.

The map had three tiers: black for active, gray for degraded, white for gone.

What he'd been looking at for eight months was the white.

A market went dry the way ground went dry in a drought — evenly, gradually, moisture leaving the soil in rough proportion to distance from the source. You felt it everywhere at once. What he'd been tracking didn't behave that way. The white routes had a geometry. They clustered. They held a center of gravity.

He zoomed to the sector he'd mapped most recently, an industrial crescent between Zones 5 and 7: processing facilities, climate-management infrastructure, a medical-maintenance sector he'd run data through under an Orison-adjacent contract eighteen months back. Low-margin logistics, the kind of work you took when the premium jobs were quiet. He'd been in and out of those corridors for six weeks. He had an excellent memory for routes, and he remembered these clearly.

The white routes traced the crescent almost perfectly. Not all of it — there were active corridors running through, which was why the pattern wasn't immediately obvious. But when he overlaid his eight months of notation and filtered by sector type rather than by zone, the shape clarified. A radius. A direction.

He couldn't name yet what it was the shape of.

He finished the githeri, closed the map, paid in cash, and went back to the bicycle.

---

There were two people he'd been thinking about the way he thought about the white routes — not with alarm, but with a specific quality of attention.

Bayo had been a logistics coordinator in Zone 3 for as long as Seto had known him, which was six years. He was reliable in the particular way that people who'd grown up without reliable systems became reliable: deliberately, as a practice, something he renewed rather than assumed. They'd worked adjacent to each other a dozen times without ever actually working together, which was the cleanest kind of professional relationship. No shared clients, no debt in either direction.

Bayo had stopped taking his calls in October. Started again in December. He'd been paying attention to the texture of those conversations since — looking for the seam where something had been repaired. Bayo was fine, himself or close enough. But October through November was absent from his history in a way Bayo appeared to accept without examination. Not the gap. The acceptance.

He thought, briefly, about Nuru — Zone 6, a nutritionist at a community clinic, a life with hours and people who expected her at specific times. A life with edges he could trace from the outside and which appeared, improbably, to hold. He kept her outside his professional world deliberately, and she had the sense not to ask why.

Shen was different, and more troubling.

He'd carried data for her twice — medical research notation, the kind of thing a freelance med-tech produced working the gray zone between hospital contracts. Technically specialized, commercially modest. She'd been doing Orison-adjacent work since early the previous year, the kind of employment shift couriers noticed the way shift workers notice a changed schedule: not judgment, just updated routing.

He'd spoken to her four times since. She was the same in every important way — knowledgeable, careful, dry in her humor. But she no longer referenced the work she'd done before the Orison contract. Not deflecting. Not avoiding. Absent, the way you don't reference a room that is no longer there.

Three weeks back he'd tested it at a relay pickup in Zone 4 — the conversation easy enough that he let it develop naturally before mentioning the package. A methodology study she'd written on tissue degradation curves under extended carrier saturation. Important work. He remembered her exact words when she'd sent it: *This is the one that matters.*

"That period I was still finding my footing," she said. "Everything before the Orison work feels a bit underwater."

She had written that study herself. She had called it the one that matters. Her eyes stayed clear and present and there was nothing in them that recognized what they had just said.

She didn't know it. That was the part that stayed with him.

The geometry of the white routes made it less possible that this was anything ordinary.

He was the only person outside the system who held both halves of the shape. The geography of the silence lived in eleven years of personal route data that existed nowhere except his head — no log, no archive, no record that could be pulled by anyone with the resources to look. The contact network was the Seams, and the Seams didn't file reports. If he stopped mapping, the shape went dark. Bayo would not find his two missing months. Shen would not notice the room that was no longer there. There was no one he could hand the map to who would know how to read it.

---

He'd been running cross-references for two months, slowly, careful not to generate a pattern of inquiry that looked like inquiry. Public GLMZ health data was accessible through Infrastructure Authority portals — aggregate, anonymized, the kind of information that got published because the disclosure requirements were old enough that nobody had updated them to close the gaps. He pulled what he could. Zone infrastructure utilization. Maintenance contract records. The particular texture of high-traffic medical corridors showing reduced billing under Orison-adjacent facilities management.

He cross-referenced those against his map, against what he knew of the affected sector's commercial profile eighteen months back.

The radius tightened.

He didn't have a name for the shape yet. He had a shape.

---

The Zone 7 relay point was in a building that smelled of concrete and recycled air, the smell of functional Zone 7 infrastructure — not neglected, just unglamorous. Transit district. Relay-heavy. The kind of place where data moved through rather than stopped. He preferred it to the premium zones, where the architecture was always trying to tell you something.

The handoff was a standard drop, physical chip, no verbal exchange. The relay operator was a young man who'd been at the post for maybe eight months, judging by the way he still verified twice when once was sufficient. Seto said nothing about it. You didn't advise relay operators on their security protocols unless you wanted to be remembered, and he had no interest in being remembered.

Back on the bicycle.

He took the long way home along the freight grid — not the optimal route, which threaded through Zone 6 at lower elevation, but the longer cut that stayed higher, where the city's noise was different. More industrial. The sound of things moving through systems. He thought best in motion, which was maybe why he'd spent eleven years on a bicycle rather than taking the Pulse or flagging freight sleds. Proximity to the route mattered. You read the city with your body, not just your neuretics.

The zone transitions were legible at speed in a way they weren't on foot — the light quality changing, the beacon frequencies cycling through their registration bands, the background data environment shifting from one administrative fingerprint to another. The Seams between zones had their own texture: both administrative signals present, neither dominant, a frequency richness his carrier-grade tissue read differently from the clean corporate bandwidth on either side. Inside a CorpoNation footprint the signal was managed, curated, a single clean voice. In the Seam you got both voices at once and the interference between them, which was technically noise and practically information. That was where the commercial life happened that wasn't supposed to happen. That was where he worked.

He was two kilometers from his building when his neuretics cycled through the standard ambient check.

Navigation overlays. Traffic density estimates. Relay-band signal quality. The background noise of ten million people running consumer hardware that broadcast their presence in every direction, all the time, the kind of thing you stopped noticing the way you stopped noticing the pressure of your own clothing.

And then something else.

He almost missed it. It didn't announce itself — no warning handshake, no preliminary contact, just presence in the carrier band that was not supposed to admit contact from outside. The bicycle's front wheel drifted two centimeters before his hands corrected without his authorization.

Numbers. Organized by a mind that was not his — vertical, layered, the notation of someone who worked at depth in a single domain. Tissue calibration, delta offsets, the specific patience of someone who sat very still with fine instruments and moved slowly through small distances. His own data sense moved laterally: routes, adjacencies, the way corridors connected to each other. This was a different architecture entirely. It occupied the same register and did not ask permission and for twelve seconds it was simply there alongside him, clean and clinical and impossible.

He counted the seconds. He didn't decide to; his tissue did. His hands kept the bicycle level without his input.

Twelve. Then it was gone.

His heart rate had a quality that wasn't his. Not the wrong rate — the wrong texture. A steadiness that belonged to someone who spent their day very still. He breathed through it. Four hundred and twelve clients had sealed data under his guarantee.

He didn't alter course. He didn't run a diagnostic on the street, where any counter-sweep would be visible to ambient infrastructure. He kept the same pace and let the city pass the same way it always did — zone transitions, beacon cycles, the managed air hardening slightly as he re-entered Zone 5 administrative coverage. He catalogued all of it. He kept his breathing even. The wrong-texture steadiness sat in his chest like something borrowed, patient and clinical, belonging to someone who had never had reason to hurry. He let it sit there. He kept moving.

Four hundred and twelve clients. Sealed system. Guaranteed. That was what he'd told them, and the guarantee was based on eleven years of confirmation, and what had just happened was not consistent with eleven years of confirmation. He kept the same speed. He breathed at the same rate. He arrived at his building and locked the bicycle and went upstairs.

---

The sweep took forty minutes, which was the proper length for a sweep done correctly. He ran it in three passes, different methodologies, one of them a manual review of his neuretic logs by timestamp — his specialist in Zone 7 had given him read access to his own tissue data, which was not standard, which was why he'd chosen her. No Orison affiliation. No shared reporting structure with anything he'd been inside eighteen months ago.

The sweep came back clean.

Clean. Clean. Clean.

He sat with that for a moment. Then he ran the timestamp audit.

The log was immaculate. Every entry in the correct sequence, every interval within expected variance — except for a 0.003-second discontinuity at exactly the twelve-second window where the contact had occurred. Too precise for hardware failure. Too narrow for power instability. A seam. He'd have missed it if he hadn't been looking, and he'd only been looking because he'd felt something that shouldn't exist.

The edges of the discontinuity were clean in the particular way that only deliberate work produced. Someone had reached into his neuretic log from outside, removed whatever the twelve seconds had recorded, and left the rest intact. They had locked the door behind them. He was supposed to find a clean log and conclude nothing had happened.

He didn't conclude nothing had happened.

Four hundred and twelve people had handed him data under the guarantee that his sealed system produced no readable output. He had told four hundred and twelve people this could not happen. He sat with that for longer than he sat with the discontinuity.

Then he checked the relay bounce pattern.

The signal had entered through the tower in the industrial corridor between Zones 7 and 8.

He recognized the registration signature — it appeared in the Infrastructure Authority directory as a standard relay installation, which it was. But the secondary certificate had an issuer he'd encountered once before, in a contracts appendix for Orison-adjacent facilities work eighteen months back: an entity listed only by registration number, no public-facing name, the kind of organizational layer that existed specifically because it didn't need to be found. He had passed within two blocks of that tower eleven times in the past eight months, running route checks on the crescent. The corridor it anchored had been one of the first sections to go white on his map.

He sat with this longer than he'd sat with anything else tonight.

He had been mapping a silence. The silence had just reached back. And it had done so carefully enough to cover its tracks — which meant it knew there were tracks to cover. Which meant it knew he was watching.

He stayed at the table. Outside, the zone's relay beacons pulsed their thirty-second intervals, patient and indifferent. For eight months he had been the one with the map — the one who moved through the city, collected the data, noticed the shape no one else was positioned to see. An observer. That was a stable position. It had a specific weight and a specific set of obligations, and he had understood both. What had changed was the direction. Whatever he had been watching had been watching back long enough to find his log and clean it. He was no longer outside the shape. He was part of the data, and he had been for some time without knowing it.

He thought about Bayo, who had lost two months and didn't know to look for them.

He thought about Shen, who moved through her own knowledge like someone in a familiar room from which several objects had been quietly removed.

Somewhere out in the city, Nuru was living her life, which was a thing he never examined and always knew. Gravity.

The light changed. He got up.

He opened the map and flagged the Zone 7/8 relay tower. Routed tomorrow's crescent pickups to Meseret — she ran clean, she wouldn't ask why. He composed the Bayo message — a route inquiry, professional, nothing that read as urgency. Something he could have sent any week. He read it twice. Then he put it back in draft.

If whoever cleaned his log could see his outgoing relay contacts, a message to Bayo in the twelve hours after an intrusion was not a neutral act. It was flagging Bayo. He filed the message under the same category as the discontinuity: things that could not yet be moved. Bayo would get the message when the timing wouldn't cost Bayo anything.

At 02:40 he left the apartment. He took the bicycle east through the Zone 7 industrial corridor, the route he had run eleven times. He passed the tower on the Zone 7/8 margin without slowing. The mast was standard relay infrastructure, amber-lit for airspace clearance. He had passed it eleven times in the past eight months. Twelve now.

He made tea when he got back. He opened the client list, read the first three entries, and closed it again. He had been outside the shape. He was not outside it anymore. Whatever guarantee he had given four hundred and twelve people, he had given it under conditions that no longer obtained.

He did not sleep again.

# Interference Pattern

The first time it happened, Amara was standing in the Orison Communications lobby waiting for her access badge to clear.

She was early. The segment wasn't until two, and the security desk had her name wrong — Ossi, not Osei — which meant a call upstairs, which meant she was standing in the lobby for eleven minutes while the managed air moved around her at exactly the temperature of nothing. The lobby was designed to suggest transparency: glass floors over a two-story atrium, umber walls that held warmth without radiating it, pendant lights spaced so that the ceiling always looked lit from somewhere you couldn't identify. She had covered the Orison Communications Group acquisition of Tessera Tribune fourteen months ago. She knew this building.

She was reading the exit.

Not looking at it. Reading it. Her eye moved to the service corridor on the north side before she clocked the main doors, catalogued the glass panels on the east atrium wall — load-bearing, no purchase, skip — and settled for a half-second on the width of the security desk itself. Forty centimeters of clearance on the left side if you went low. The elevator bank behind her had three cars; the nearest was sitting at lobby level, doors open.

She became aware she was doing this the way you become aware you've been humming a song you don't know. The inventory was already complete.

Amara looked at her hands.

The badge reader beeped. The desk attendant said her name correctly this time.

She went up to the seventh floor and recorded the segment. She sat across from a VP of distribution named Halverson who used the word *aligned* fourteen times and meant something different by it each time, and she asked her prepared questions and did not think about the lobby. After, in the elevator down, she made a voice note on her tablet: *Anomaly. Lobby, 1:49 PM. Spatial inventory. Route-reading. Involuntary. Will document.*

She labeled it Fragment 1.

She had a column due Friday and a source who was pulling cold feet on a quote. She did not think about Fragment 1 for three days.

---

The second time, she was in the Seam.

The Tessera Tribune office kept a press room in Zone 4, which meant she spent forty minutes on transit three days a week moving through the Seam corridor between Z1 and Z4. She knew the Seam the way you know a route you've run for years: without looking at it. She could have described the damp smell, the poured-concrete construction joints every six meters, the overhead strip lighting that went blue-white somewhere around the Z2 junction. She could not have told you why she took the left branch at the first split rather than the right, except that the left was faster and she had learned that early and it had become body knowledge.

That morning she went right.

Her legs went right. The rest of her caught up at the junction and stood there for a moment registering that something had made a decision. The right branch added four minutes. It came out at a different exit, one level up, with a direct sight line to the street. She did not need a direct sight line to the street. She filed the story she'd been drafting in her head, rode the escalator up, and came out into a different block than usual.

Fragment 2: *Right branch, Z2 junction. No conscious decision. Emerged Level 3 exit — better sight lines, slower egress to transit. Someone prefers the visual cover.*

The someone was not yet a problem. She was a journalist. Sources did not always have names at first.

---

By the fourth week she had twenty-one fragments.

She kept them in a private document, raw logging format, timestamped. She had started treating this as a story — not a story she had pitched, not a story she expected to publish, but the only frame she had for the thing she was doing, which was paying close attention to something she did not understand. The fragments were consistent. They were spatial. They arrived in her body before they arrived in her head, which was what made them fragments rather than memories — she could not access them from the front. She could only notice the aftermath.

She had considered her neuretics three times in the document and crossed it out each time. The Batch 44-C enhancement was three years old. She had run ninety days of documented logging when she first received it, filed the experience report, and experienced nothing she couldn't attribute to standard enhanced processing. No anomalous cross-signal. No ghost spatial memories that belonged to someone in a completely different profession. She had checked the Tessera Tribune archive for other Batch 44-C reports. The closest parallel was a piece from 2221 about a warehouse manager who had spent six weeks experiencing involuntary ergonomic assessments of every room she entered, which she attributed to occupational overlap with her enhancement suite. The piece was reported without alarm.

Amara read the piece three times. She was not a warehouse manager. She had never learned to read a space as a courier. The knowledge in her body was not hers.

She went to Orison on a Tuesday for the third briefing in the series. They were doing a quarterly segment on corpo integration metrics, which was the kind of story that required her to sit in a warm room with someone who had adapted so completely to the internal atmosphere that they read, from the outside, as *fine*. Orison was good at producing these people. She had interviewed half a dozen of them across three years of coverage. They answered questions with the specific fluency of someone who had been briefed on what they believed.

This time it was a woman named Celestine Mora, mid-forties, communications director for the eastern distribution network. Celestine Mora had been with Orison or its predecessor entities for nineteen years. She spoke about integration metrics the way Amara spoke about transit routes — body knowledge, no looking. She was comfortable in the way that people are comfortable when they have stopped noticing the shape of the room they're in.

Amara set up her tablet, confirmed the recording level, and asked her first question.

The bleed arrived four minutes into the interview.

It came in through her shoulders first — a tension that was not her tension, a readiness she did not have a reason for — and then her eye swept the room. Conference room, Orison seventh floor. She had been here before. She knew the table was six meters by two, that the windows on the north wall were decorative (non-opening, laminated glass, would not yield), that the door behind Celestine Mora was the only egress, that the service corridor access was through the panel to the left of the whiteboard. She knew the weight of the whiteboard panel from the way it was mounted — push-pin anchors, not hinged — and she knew, with the specificity of someone who had tested it, that it would take three seconds to move and would not move quietly.

Celestine Mora said something about longitudinal metrics.

Amara said, "Can you walk me through the methodology?" Her voice was level. Her hands were flat on the table.

The readiness held for another forty seconds, scanning, and then it released and she was just sitting in a conference room. Celestine Mora was explaining methodology. The pendant lights overhead were warm umber, tuned to suggest late afternoon regardless of the actual hour.

She got through the interview. In the elevator, she was shaking.

Fragment 19: *Conference room, Orison 7F. Full spatial assessment — exits, surfaces, panel access. Duration approximately 40 seconds. High urgency. Scan pattern: systematic, not panicked. This is someone who is very good at this.*

She had not written *someone* in her fragments before. She had been writing *involuntary assessment* or *spatial processing anomaly.* She read the note back twice, left it, and took the elevator down.

---

Fragment 22 came on a Thursday, two days later.

She was in the Tribune press room, at her standing desk, reviewing footage from the Orison segment. She was not in a building that required spatial assessment. She was looking at a screen. Celestine Mora was explaining longitudinal metrics in the same measured cadence she had used in the room, and Amara was timing the cuts, and the grief arrived with no announcement. She did not look away from the footage, did not move, registered only the small green light on the camera dock and the cooling weight of a coffee she had set down and forgotten.

It was not a spatial inventory. It had no useful information in it. It arrived complete, the way a full breath arrives, and it was this: someone missing a person they had not stopped missing, had never managed to stop missing, the missing worn into something that was no longer sharp but was not gone. It was the kind of grief that had been compressed by years of not being expressed into a weight that lived at the back of the throat.

There was a name in it. Two syllables, held low, worn smooth the way a word gets worn when you've said it ten thousand times and never once aloud.

She did not catch the name. She caught the shape of it.

She stood at her desk for a moment after it passed. The footage was still running. Celestine Mora on the screen said, *the integration metrics reflect a genuine shift in how our people relate to their work*, and Amara stopped the recording.

She had been logging this as an anomaly. She had been building something that might, eventually, become a story about neuretics cross-signal, about what it meant that enhancement suites were sharing data through channels nobody had disclosed, about what Batch 44-C had actually delivered. She had twenty-two fragments in a private document. Twenty-one of them were someone reading rooms for a living, reading them with the careful automatic competence of a person who had been doing it long enough that their body knew things their mind did not need to articulate.

Fragment 22 was not that. Fragment 22 was a person.

She sat with this for longer than she meant to. She set both palms flat on the desk and let her shoulders come down out of her ears, breathed once on a four-count the way she did before a live read, and the professional posture reassembled itself around the breath. The press room was filling up around her — she could hear the 3 PM shift coming in, the ambient clatter of a working newsroom — and she stayed at her standing desk and did not look up.

The frame she had been using was: anomaly story, unknown source, document and investigate. The frame required that the source be a data type. Fragment 22 had changed the data type.

She opened her professional contacts database. She had covered neuretics for three years. She had sources across every tier of the ecosystem, from enhancement suite developers to registered neurotech researchers to the freelance community that had adopted early and documented the experience. She had not specifically cultivated courier sources. Couriers were operational. They did not, in her experience, publish their neuretics reports or sit for interviews or engage with the kind of coverage she produced.

She had a contact at the GLMZ Freelance Registry who handled courier certification renewal. She sent a brief message, professional, asking whether the Registry had records of Batch 44-C adoption among registered couriers in the Z1-Z4 corridor, circa 2222-2223. She phrased it as background for a piece on enhancement cohort tracking. That part was not untrue.

Then she opened a new message.

She had spent four weeks building a profile of someone from the inside of their spatial habits — the way they moved through a space, the routes they preferred, the weight they assigned to sight lines versus egress speed, the quality of attention they brought to a room. She had received, twenty minutes ago, the texture of their grief. She was writing to someone she had never met and had not agreed to know this much about.

She wrote:

*My name is Amara Osei. I'm a journalist at Tessera Tribune, neuretics coverage. I received Batch 44-C in 2222. I've been documenting what I now believe is a cross-signal intrusion — your spatial memory in my neuretics, involuntary, for approximately five weeks. I have 22 logged fragments. I am not publishing this without speaking with you first. If this is reaching the right person: I think we need to talk.*

She read it back. She considered the last line and left it.

There was a particular strangeness in the act, addressing a stranger by way of everything she already knew about them — the weight they assigned to exits, the quality of attention they brought to a room they had never expected to leave quickly — so that the reach toward them felt less like meeting someone than like writing to a room she had already learned to move through.

She marked the message priority and sent it before she could revise it into something more careful, and then she stood at her desk in the Tessera Tribune press room while the afternoon shift came in around her, and she waited.

# The Other Side of the Noise

At 0545 the rear hub bearing on Seto's bike had a tick in it he could feel through the frame before he could hear it. He'd noticed it the night before on the last run home, a half-beat hesitation in the spin-down, and he had not slept right knowing it was there. So he was out in the access alley behind his unit while the sky over Zone 9 was still the color of cold solder, the bike upside down on its saddle and bars, the rear wheel off, the bearing race in his palm.

He cleaned it with a rag and then with his thumb. Pitting on two of the balls, fine as salt. Not failure. Forty kilometers of warning, maybe sixty. He had a spare cartridge in the parts drawer he'd pulled from a dead courier's frame two winters back, and he pressed it in with the old socket he used as a drift, seated it, spun the wheel. The tick was gone. The spin-down ran clean and long and died without a stutter.

This was the part of the work nobody paid for and the only part he fully owned. The bike was his. The routes were his — five years of them, built relay by relay, a private map of the gray channels that didn't show on any corpo grid because he'd cut them himself, around the edges of campuses and checkpoints and dead zones, through the seams where one corpo's coverage ran out before the next one's began. He ran nothing that ran him. He answered to the parcel and the clock and otherwise to no system at all. He bolted the wheel back on, torqued the axle by feel, and rolled out into the gray with the count already starting in his head: first relay at the Mercer cut, four minutes, then the long straight down the old freight grade.

The signal arrived at 0612, between the second checkpoint and the third, on a stretch where there was nothing to do but pedal and watch the road.

It came in wrong. He knew the geography of his own stack the way he knew the routes — the intercept buffer where ambient traffic landed, the throwaway space he let fill and dump, the primary stack he kept clean for the work. Things came in through the buffer. That was the door. This came in through the floor. It seated itself in the primary stack like it had always lived there, no handshake, no source flag, a thermal shift at the base of his skull as if something had been switched on behind the bone.

It was not a message. There were no words in it. It was a weight, and it arrived in both his hands at once, and his hands were on the bars so he knew it was not his hands. It was the weight of holding something you had read so many times the reading had worn through — a page held open out of habit, the eyes moving, nothing arriving. The shape of feeling something, with the feeling itself scraped out. He coasted. He put a foot down at a corner he did not need to stop at.

His first read was hardware. A fault in the intercept stack, a buffer leaking into primary, some piece of his own gray-market kit finally throwing the error it had been promising for years. He ran the diagnostic he kept for exactly this and the diagnostic came back nominal. Everything nominal. Which was the second wrong thing, because the weight was still there, sitting in his hands while the readout told him his hands were empty.

He did not push it down. You did not push down a fault you hadn't sourced; you traced it to where it lived and you read what was actually broken. So he traced it. The signal had no origin flag but it had a shape, and a shape was a route, and a route could be walked backward. He pulled over into the lee of a stanchion under the freight grade and he walked it back.

It led to a credential. A live media registration on the open corpo directory, the kind anyone could pull: *Tessera Tribune, wellness-tech desk, accredited correspondent.* Carrier-grade neuretics, the good in-head hardware, the kind that didn't throw the errors his did. And a batch line, the manufacturing lot, the way a tire has a date stamped in the rubber. Batch 44-C.

He read the desk archive because it was attached and free. A series. Six pieces over a season on calibration culture — the wellness practice of going in for a tune, the way you'd take a car in, except the thing being tuned was the part of you that carried the weight of what you remembered. # Account Liaison

The thirty-second note began the way the others had, with a timestamp and a refusal to round.

Amara worked it standing, tablet flat against the press-room workstation, the local partition open and the cursor blinking inside a file that lived nowhere but here. Not the Tribune's CMS. Not the partnership portal. A notes-to-self file on a partition she'd carved out herself, six weeks deep into a thing she could describe but not yet name: behavioral drift in sources from the wellness series. People she'd interviewed two years ago, on camera, fine. People who were still fine. People whose fineness had developed a grain to it, a flatness in the places a person usually has texture.

Thirty-one notes. Not consistent enough to pitch. Specific enough to track.

Around her the newsroom ran its late shift the way it always did — somebody arguing a headline two desks over, the soft collision of a dozen audio feeds bleeding past their earpieces, the building's air handling pushing recycled cool across the open floor. She'd learned to write inside the noise. The noise was how she knew she was at work and not somewhere worse.

She'd typed four words of the thirty-second note when she stopped, because a man had come through the press-room doors and she could already tell he was the wrong shape.

She had time to read him. He crossed the floor at a pace that didn't apologize for being there and didn't announce itself either, and in the distance between the doors and her desk she catalogued what he wasn't. Not a source — sources hesitated in doorways, did the small chart-pull of someone deciding whether they were really going to do this. Not a colleague; she knew the colleagues. Not a delivery. He had the slight interior quiet of a man raised in spaces built to meet a body correctly at every surface — rooms that had never once been the wrong temperature for him, doors that had never stuck.

The suit was cut to be forgotten. That was the expensive part. Cheaper money bought a suit cut to impress; his had been cut to leave no afterimage, which cost more and said more.

His hair sat within a millimeter of Orison's grooming maximum. She knew the figure because she'd covered the compliance brief two years back — 1.8 centimeters above the collar, published, enforced — and the number had stuck the way numbers did with her. His was just under it. A man does not land that close to a published maximum by accident. He had measured himself against a standard and chosen to live one millimeter inside it, every day, on purpose.

He reached her desk and did not stand across from her, and did not come around beside her. He took the chair at an angle — the position that makes a conversation possible without anyone having to call it an interview.

"Ciro Fonseca, Orison Neuretics," he said. "Account liaison for the Tribune media partnership."

In four years on the neuretics beat, no one from Orison's internal side had ever come here in person. They moved everything through the content partnership portal — approvals, embargoes, the occasional firm note about framing. The portal was the relationship. The portal had a face precisely so no one would have to.

"I didn't know the partnership had a liaison," she said.

"It hasn't needed one." He let that sit without weight. "I wanted to understand the beat. Not the published work — I've read that. The work before the work."

"The work before the work."

"How you handle a lead that doesn't meet a publishing threshold. How you decide what to track before you decide whether to pitch it."

She answered him. It was an easy thing to talk about and a hard thing to talk about honestly, and she found herself doing the honest version, because he was listening with his whole attention and that was rare enough to be disarming. Partnership contacts heard you until they'd heard enough to check the box, then moved to the next item with the box still warm. He wasn't doing that. He was letting her finish sentences she'd have let herself trail off.

"How do you handle variance that falls below your publishing threshold?" he asked.

"I log it."

"Tribune's system?"

"And my own."

He nodded as if she'd confirmed something already settled. "I saw that in the account review. A personal notes file. Off-system." He said it the way a person says a thing he has read and found unremarkable — not the contents, she understood, but the access metadata, the shape of a file existing where the system could see that it existed and not what it held. He didn't lean into it the way oversight people did, hunting for the flinch. "That space between tracking and filing — most journalists file or they don't. Working the middle ground takes a kind of judgment you can't write into an editorial brief."

He had handed her own methodology back to her as a virtue, polished, and she felt the shape of it land: that was a sophisticated way to open a door. She kept her face the face she used on camera.

"You're tracking something now," he said. Not a question.

"I'm tracking a few things."

"Anything in the batch data?"

She decided how much to give him and gave it carefully. "Behavioral drift. Sources from the wellness series. Not consistent enough to pitch. Specific enough that I haven't stopped looking."

"Drift in the production, or drift in the subjects."

It was the right distinction, made too fast. Most people heard *drift* and reached for the cheap version — bad batch, manufacturing variance, a thing you recall and replace. He'd gone straight past that to the other thing, the subjects themselves, and he understood — she could see him understand it, in the half-second before he smoothed his expression — which of the two would be the more expensive thing for Orison to have to be true.

"That's the question," she said.

"It's a good one to be careful with."

Then he reached into his jacket.

What came out was a straight razor, bone handle, folded shut. He opened it with one hand and drew the underside of the blade along the line of his jaw — a checking motion, the way you'd test whether a surface had held since morning. His other hand stayed flat on his knee. Four seconds, maybe. Then he folded it closed and slid it back into the pocket, and the whole of it happened while he was still looking at her, which meant he had done it enough times that the gesture asked nothing of his eyes. He didn't break the sentence for it.

She did not read it as a threat. She'd interviewed men who performed their edges; this wasn't that. This was a man with a precise relationship to his own surfaces who did not particularly mind who watched him keep them right. She'd seen the same stillness in surgeons and in people who'd spent time learning to be very still for someone else's benefit.

She made a note in her head. The note was careful.

He stood. He produced a card — a physical one, pressed stock with his name on it, which in 2225 was not a convenience but a statement, because anyone could push a contact token to her tablet in the time it took to think of it and he had chosen instead to carry an object and hand it to her.

"If you find something in that data that concerns you," he said, "I'd rather hear it from you than find it in a published piece."

He said it the way a person says a thing he has thought through beforehand and still means.

She took the card. Holding it, she understood she'd just been handed a trap with the manners of a gift. The published piece was the floor — the worst outcome, from where he sat. He had named the floor out loud and then offered, generously, to stand between her and it, and the price of the offer was that everything she was tracking would now travel through him first. The kindness and the catch were the same motion. You couldn't take one without the other.

"Thank you for coming in," she said, because that was true and cost nothing.

"Thank you for the time." And he crossed the floor and was gone, leaving no afterimage, exactly as the suit had promised.

She finished the thirty-second note standing up.

She wrote him into it as a catalogue. The suit, cut to be forgotten. The millimeter under the maximum. The angle of the chair. The full attention, which she now distrusted precisely because it had worked. The razor got two lines — the bone handle, the four seconds, the eyes that never left her, the conclusion that it was maintenance and not menace. The card, physical, on the corner of the desk.

The last line she wrote and did not soften.

*He understands the work.*

The press room ran late. She'd sent Seto the batch documentation that afternoon — the whole private file, through the secure channel — and his answer came in a way that wasn't an answer.

The bleed arrived before his message did.

It came up through her the way it always came, body before mind, never a voice and never a picture. This time it was a stillness. The specific stillness of someone who has been reading for a long time and arrived at a number, and the number is sitting there on the page, and the mind has already accepted what it means while the body hasn't finished catching up. Not the decision. The moment before the decision, when there's nothing left to do but feel the figure be true. She knew the texture without being told the cause: this was what it felt like to understand something you had spent real effort hoping not to understand.

She set both palms flat on the desk until it passed. The newsroom noise came back in around the edges.

Then she sent: *Someone from Orison came to see me today.*

The ambient paused.

*Tell me what he wanted.*

She registered how fast that had come back — faster than typing, the speed of a hand that was already on the screen.

She looked at the card on the corner of the desk. Pressed stock. A name and nothing she'd asked for.

*He wanted to seem helpful,* she sent.

# Something Borrowed

# The Lunch

She told herself it was fieldwork.

# Black Spot

The model he had been running for three weeks had a hole in it the size of a door.

He knew where it was. It showed up in the aggregate as a smoothness that wasn't real — the way water lies flat over a submerged ledge, the surface telling you nothing about the depth or what the depth is covering. The aggregate told him how many neuretics clients had registered suppression events in the corridors he'd been mapping, and when. It didn't tell him why the timing clustered the way it did: not quite random, not quite patterned. He'd marked the model incomplete and sent Amara the underlying geometry anyway. The geometry was real even with the explanation missing.

He had the explanation now.

He just wasn't ready to say what it meant.

Eleven days of pulling IA scheduling data — the Integrated Assessment logs every licensed neuretics client generates, timestamped by the calibration authority each time they come in for maintenance. Public the way everything in the gray zones was technically public: if you knew the right relays, paid the access fees, waited out the response cycles. He'd done all three. He'd done all three because the hole bothered him, and when something bothered him he moved toward it.

Forty-seven facilities' worth of scheduling windows. The hole was gone.

What was there instead was a zone.

He had it on his route map now, drawn in red — not the red of danger, just the red of distinction, the color he used when something needed to stay separate from everything else while he thought about it. He used a physical map. He always used a physical map for the final overlay because screens could be adjusted and paper couldn't. The zone was roughly oval. The long axis ran approximately northwest to southeast, about two kilometers. The short axis was maybe eight hundred meters at its widest.

Pilsen. Or what was left of Pilsen after the wave.

He knew what it was because of what was not in it. He had pulled complaint filings from the block association system for the last eighteen months — noise, sanitation, encroachment, property boundary disputes. He'd pulled them for every zone he was mapping as a baseline. In every other corridor: complaints. Arguments about container placement. A building manager and a tenant who had been exchanging increasingly pointed filings about a drain for nine months. Normal friction. The kind of friction that told him a place was inhabited by people who were still people, with preferences and limits and the willingness to name a wrong.

In the Pilsen zone: nothing. Not a reduction. An absence. Fourteen months without a single noise complaint. The block association meeting minutes ran to a page and a half. Every vote unanimous.

He read the building maintenance records. The buildings were clean. Not maintained — maintained implied effort, implied a gap between current state and desired state that someone was continuously closing. These buildings had no gap. Every light fixture replaced within forty-eight hours of failure. Every common-area floor sealed on schedule. The schedule was not remarkable. The adherence was.

He put the maintenance records down.

He looked at the route map.

He looked at where he'd drawn the red oval.

He looked at the eastern margin of the red oval, where the oval thinned and became a boundary instead of a zone, and he saw — not for the first time, but for the first time as a fact rather than a geographical coincidence — where Nuru's clinic sat.

She was not inside the boundary. He checked this twice, with different reference points, because he was being careful. The clinic was outside the oval. It was two hundred meters from the margin. Maybe two-ten, depending on which corner of the building you measured from.

He set the pad of his index finger on the eastern margin and traced the boundary line where he'd drawn it, following the red until it stopped where the red stopped. He did not let the finger travel the two hundred meters past it to the clinic. He measured the gap instead — finger laid flat, knuckle to tip, against the scale bar in the corner — and made a small tick at the edge of the oval to hold the distance, and lifted his hand.

He wrote: *eastern margin — proximity unconfirmed* and underlined *unconfirmed* once.

He didn't write Nuru's name. He didn't write clinic. He wrote the coordinates and left them as coordinates.

---

The bleed came in the late afternoon, when the light off the window was doing something orange to the wall opposite his desk. He was deep in the scheduling windows, building the third layer of the model — the one that would show not just suppression clustering but suppression *absence* clustering, the shape of what wasn't happening — and then he was somewhere else.

He was in a corridor. Not the GLMZ, not any route he recognized. Institutional fluorescent light, the specific hum of a system that needed servicing. A waiting room. There was a woman across from him — not him, across from *her*, because this was Amara's — who was telling her something about her son's calibration results, and every sentence the woman spoke was slightly more than what she was saying, the words sitting over the real words the way the smooth zone's maintenance records sat over the real question of who was keeping things so clean.

Amara was measuring her response. He felt her measuring. Choosing each word with the awareness that the wrong word could close a door that couldn't be reopened. He didn't operate this way. He had never operated this way. His work was observation and transmission: he saw a thing, he sent the thing. He did not manage what the thing meant to whoever received it.

He was back at his desk.

He sat there for a moment with the orange light on the wall and the route map in front of him and the aftershock of someone else's professional patience moving through him like a tone. He didn't like it. Not because it was unpleasant — it wasn't unpleasant, exactly — but because it was demonstrably not his. He could not unsee the difference. He knew, in the way he knew distances, that there was a gap between how he moved and how she moved, and the gap was not neutral. It named something about him. It named the gap itself: that between seeing a thing and sending it he had never built any room, never held a word back to keep a door open, because in his mode of moving through the world there was nothing in that gap to hold.

He made a note in his working file: *Bleed — source: Amara. Content: clinical/language management. Duration: brief.* He dated it. He had started keeping this log because it was data, and data was the only thing that ever told him anything real.

He went back to the scheduling windows.

---

The third layer took another two hours.

When it was done he had a complete model. The hole was gone, replaced by an explanation. The explanation was a zone in Pilsen where neuretics clients did not generate suppression events because neuretics clients in that zone did not generate friction, did not generate the ordinary low-level social abrasion that produced suppression events, because they had been calibrated — or had calibrated themselves, or had been shifted by something he was still working out the mechanism of — into a register where friction did not arise. The buildings were clean. The meetings ran short. The neighbors waved.

He did not write: *this is bad.* He did not write: *something is wrong here.* He wrote: *suppression event absence correlates with geographic boundary consistent with Batch 44-C distribution models. High confidence. Mechanism underdetermined. Note: absence of signal is signal.*

He wrote: *eastern margin proximity — see coordinates.*

He did not write Nuru's name. He was still mapping.

He packaged the complete model — the geometry, the three layers, the overlay showing the zone boundaries, the scheduling window correlations, the complaint filing analysis — and he opened a channel to Amara. It was late. He didn't check what time it was. She worked late. He worked until the work was done, and the work was done.

He sent her the complete model.

Then he sat there with the orange light gone and the gray evening through the window and the route map on his desk with the red oval on it and the coordinates at the eastern margin that he had not labeled with a name.

The explanation was real. He had learned this a long time ago, not from anyone, just from moving through the city and seeing that the city did not adjust itself to what you were prepared to see. A route was a sentence and a sentence said what it said. The explanation was real even when it arrived before you were ready for it. Even when it arrived before you'd worked out what it meant for the other facts you were already carrying.

He was still working out what it meant.

He was being careful.

He folded the route map along the existing creases — he always folded it along the existing creases because refolding it wrong stressed the paper at new points and eventually the new points became tears — and he set it on the corner of his desk where he would see it first thing in the morning.

He had sent her the model.

He had not sent her the coordinates at the eastern margin. That was not yet data. That was still a position he was standing in, looking at a distance he had not finished measuring.

He would not transmit a position as a fact; that was the whole of the discipline, the one rule that had kept his maps worth sending, and a name on those coordinates would make it a fact before he had earned the right to call it one.

He would finish measuring it.

The lamp going out left the red oval where it was, on the desk, in the dark, still a position and not yet a fact.

He turned off the lamp.

# Rooftop, Zone 7

The relay drop was on the third floor of a building that had once held a shipping broker's annex, before the Zone 7 consolidation folded the brokers into a single tower and left their old offices to whoever wanted them. Seto found it eight months ago. The deadpoint was a function of position: at street level the signals from the Kessler mesh tower and the corrugated roof of the loading bay two addresses over ran into each other and degraded anything time-sensitive. Two floors up, the building stepped back from the loading bay's shadow and the signal resolved.

He came up the stairwell at 18:09 and crossed through the access door onto the utility walkway. He clipped his receiver to the parapet edge.

Zone 7 in the evening looked like someone had tried to pour the city into the wrong container and then left without cleaning up. The loading bays below ran in a crooked line west, their roofs at varying heights, and beyond them the ferrocement wave began in earnest — that gradual, decades-long extrusion of infrastructure past what infrastructure was supposed to do. Seto had spent enough time in Zone 7 to stop noticing it. He noticed it now. He looked at the sightlines, the access points, the two rooftops with line-of-sight to the walkway and what they would cost a patient man.

He had taken the long approach today, up the east stairwell instead of the scaffold he'd used the last four runs, and he had stood at the access door a beat longer than the door needed before he opened it. He'd checked the receiver's seating twice. None of it was the route. The route had been clean for two days, since the Anker relay — clean in a way that should have closed a loop in his planning and hadn't, a pressure left behind where a finished thing should be. Like a door he'd locked from the wrong side.

The receiver cycled through its handshake and held. He waited.

---

The man came up through the access door at 18:23.

Seto heard the door first, and the sound of it was wrong before he turned — too controlled, the hinges given exactly enough pressure to open and no more, no surplus swing. He turned and spent three seconds reading the approach. The man was sixty, maybe a few years past. Large through the shoulders but carrying it without display. He had walked out of militaries — not the walking of someone who wanted you to know, but the carriage of someone who had been told to stand a certain way for long enough that the instruction became structural. He wore a light jacket, field gray, no visible attachments. His left hand held a handkerchief, folded in quarters. He had not yet reached for anything else.

"The scaffold is watched. Not by me." He glanced once at the receiver clipped to the parapet, then back to Seto. "The scaffold approach would tell the people watching it something. This tells them nothing."

Seto noted the handkerchief. The way it was held — two fingers, no tension. Not a tell. A habit. Something physical to orient the hands in situations that required hands to hold nothing.

"Ekow Ato," Seto said.

The man's expression did not change. "You know the name."

"Before that other things," Ato said. "That's not why I'm here."

Ato looked out over the city for a moment — the same inventory scan Seto had made, Seto recognized it — and then back. "I have a contract. Courier moving stolen proprietary neuretic data. The data originated with a corporate architecture team, confidentiality tier two. It was taken in a handoff. I have been engaged to retrieve it before it can be further distributed."

The city below went on with its business. A cargo lift somewhere in the loading bays was cycling down, its motor a low grinding note that faded in and out on the wind.

"That's not information I'll share."

"Then tell me the framing. Confidentiality tier two — that's a medium lock. That's not the framing you use for something that was surgically extracted. That's the framing you use for a product specification that left on a drive."

"The framing is the client's assessment."

"And you take the client's assessment as given."

"I take the client's authorization as given. Assessment I do separately." Ato shifted his weight, a small adjustment, unhurried. "You're challenging the premise."

"Yes."

"That's an interesting thing to do." He looked at Seto with the patience of someone who had learned that patience was the only tool that didn't break. "Interesting because to challenge the premise, you have to know what the premise describes. And to know that, you would have had to be inside whatever the data came from."

The cargo lift stopped. The city noise redistributed itself.

"The data is not stolen," Seto said. "It is documentation. It was generated by a protocol that has been running on the subject without authorization, and it was removed by the subject as evidence of that protocol. That makes it the subject's property by three different frameworks, two of which your client does not operate under and one of which they nominally claim to."

He heard himself say it and was briefly aware of something — not his usual framing. He chose words carefully when he needed to, but this precision was different. The sentence had architecture he hadn't consciously built. He filed the observation.

Ato was quiet for long enough that the sky shifted another shade. "You're saying the retrieval framing is false."

"I'm saying the premise is contestable. Which is a different thing, and I think you understand the difference."

"I do." Ato folded the handkerchief once, a single crisp motion, and held it. "Here is what I will offer you. The retrieval authorization does not activate for seven days. If it does survive, someone comes. Not me. Someone else."

"I take minimized footprint contracts. That's what I can offer you — a minimal footprint. What comes after a failed verification window is not mine to shape." He said it without apology, without flourish. A fact, transferred cleanly.

The question of whether Amara understood what was happening — she didn't, not yet, not fully. The documentation itself, which proved the protocol existed, which proved who authorized it, which proved a chain of accountability that someone with a confidentiality tier two framing did not want documented.

Seven days was enough to move it. It was not enough to understand it.

"If I verify the false premise before the window closes —" he started.

"I would need to take that to the client. I can't promise an outcome." Ato paused. "What I can tell you is that I have paused retrievals before when the foundational framing did not hold. I take the work seriously. The work includes not doing work that shouldn't be done."

It was the longest statement Ato had made, and it cost him something small, Seto could see that — not much, but some. A man who protected his information as a habit, passing a fragment of his code across a rooftop in Zone 7 because the situation warranted it.

"Seven days from tonight." Ato turned. He moved to the access door with the same controlled economy he'd arrived with. At the door he stopped, not looking back. "Your receiver has a slightly warm RF signature. If there's a second transmitter on this building, someone else will find this location eventually. Not on my account. Consider rotating."

The door opened and closed. The hinges made the same sound — neither more nor less than necessary.

The fact that Ato had taken this contract meant the client was sophisticated enough to know what kind of operator this problem required. The fact that Ato had given him the window rather than holding it as leverage meant Ato had already started his own assessment the moment Seto said *documentation*.

He started working out what seven days could hold, and the countdown was already running, had been running since the moment Ato said *someone else*, and the city below went about its business completely indifferent to the fact that a window had just opened and was now, one breath at a time, closing.

# What She Knows

The archive bay opened at 06:40, which was the time she had requested, because 06:40 was the hour when no one in the building had an opinion yet. The retrospective was a legitimate ask. *Ten Years of Orison Communications: The Stories We Told.* She had pitched it herself, and the access had cleared in two days, which was fast, and she had registered the speed as goodwill rather than as a door someone had been happy to open.

She had been describing the suppression hiding itself and calling it quality, in her own voice, at her own pace, choosing the word that would survive a transcript read, and the word had survived, and it was *reassuring,* and it was sitting in twenty-seven press packages where she could not now reach it.

He looked at her directly. The attention was there — the attention she had come to understand as both real and calibrated at the same time. "No," he said. "Just checking in." A pause. "You've had a busy week."

"It's been eventful," she said.

He looked at her for a moment longer than he needed to, then he said, "Let me know if you need anything," and he left. He did not take the paper cup with him.

She watched him go. She did not open the query window until she could no longer see him at the corridor junction. Then she waited fifteen more seconds, because the junction had a camera she knew the coverage arc of.

She opened the query window. The progress bar was at 31%.

The query had not run. It had paused at the exact moment she minimized the window — not completed, not failed. Paused. Waiting. The proximity notification had done more than alert her. It had suspended her active query and logged the interruption. The infrastructure log now had a record of a query she had started and not completed.

She ran the rest manually, in sections, with the secondary monitor open to the calibration request queue, the paper cup sweating a ring onto the laminate beside her keyboard.

By the time she reached Zone 7, she had one hundred and twelve gaps.

She sat back from the desk. The newsroom was quiet around her, the Tribune's afternoon desk clearing out, the standby lighting warming up toward the day-end cycle, the row of dark monitors throwing back the corridor light. One hundred and twelve. She looked at the number on her screen and felt her hands settle, very deliberately, against the edge of the desk, the way you brace a thing that is heavier than it looked when you picked it up. Her breathing slowed after the number, not before — the body catching up to a count the mind had already finished. Not anxiety. Something more structural. The recognition of a thing that was larger than she had expected and was not going to stop being large.

One hundred and twelve people whose calibration events had been logged and then unlogged. One hundred and twelve gaps where a record should have been and was allowed to pass silently. The number did not feel like data anymore. It felt like a room she was standing in.

And she was in it. That recognition arrived last, the way the worst ones did — not as a thought but as a cold settling at the base of her skull, where her own neuretics did their quiet work. She was a ninety-day-cycle recipient. Her next calibration was already on her calendar, logged like a dentist's appointment, and somewhere in a file she had no clearance to open there was a row with her name on it and a window beside it. One of these gaps, someday, would be hers — and she would walk into a room with managed air and walk out fine, and she would not be able to find the place where the fear had been, because the fear was the thing they took. She had been reading the list the way a reporter reads a list. She made herself read one line of it as the subject she also was.

She had the drive in her jacket pocket. She had the paper with 112 on it, which she had written in the first moment she understood, the kind of thing you wrote because writing it made it real outside your own head. Her left shoulder registered a strap-weight that wasn't there, the diagonal load of a bag carried too long, something sealed that had nowhere to be put down.

She was reaching for her bag when the access panel in the corner of her screen changed. Not a banner, not an alert — a quiet system update in the border display: *Infrastructure tier reviewed. Level adjusted per compliance review: historical read-only. Batch query access suspended pending clearance.*

The window he had opened for her was closed.

She registered it the way you register a door locking behind you — not the sound of it, the fact of it, arriving a half-second after you've already walked through. Not alarm. Recognition. She had been let in for exactly as long as it took, and the timing of the closing told her more than the access ever had.

She folded the paper twice and put it back. She closed the query and logged out of the desk cleanly, the way she always did, the way a person logs out who had found nothing unusual and was going home at the end of a normal shift.

The newsroom did not know what she had found. The paper with 112 on it was in her jacket pocket, two folds thick against her chest, and she was going to carry it home on the transit with strangers who had their faces turned to the windows — strangers who might, any one of them, have their own number written somewhere they had not yet shown to anyone.

# Pressure and Counter-Pressure

The new checkpoint was at the receiving bay on his second Zone 7 stop.

Not a new guard. A new process: a secondary scan of the delivery packet before it could be signed off, running through a reader that was newer than anything else in the bay by a factor of at least three years. Everything in the bay had the patina of long use — the scuffed conveyor lip, the sign-off terminal with two dead pixels in the corner, the older Orison reader gone gray at the edges where hands had touched it ten thousand times. The new reader had none of that. It had an Orison NeoCortex branding plate on the casing, clean as a tooth. It had not been there on his last run through.

The guard — a woman whose name was printed on a badge he'd seen every delivery for two years — fed the packet through the reader. Her jaw tightened while it processed. He had passed through this checkpoint perhaps two hundred times. He had watched her be bored, be irritated, be friendly, be hungover. He had never watched her not say something. The reader was new and strange and sitting in her bay, and there was a thing on her face that wanted to be a remark and stopped at the hinge of her jaw, held there. Someone had told her not to discuss it. In two years it was the first time anyone had told her not to discuss anything in this bay, and her body did not yet know how to carry the instruction. He read all of this without lifting his eyes from the sign-off form. She handed the packet back, ran the confirmation, said nothing. He signed. He did not ask.

He rode the next leg with the pre-paid card in his jacket pocket. He reached for it once, at the end of the facility block, and stopped himself. The monitoring radius covered approximately three blocks in every direction from the receiving bay, and the relay timing would shift if he opened a gray-zone channel inside it. He rode out of the radius before he stopped.

The message was twelve words: *Found 112 gaps across all zones. Not just our batch. The paper's in my pocket.*

He read it twice. He put the phone away and sat still on the bicycle, balanced between his knees, which was something he never did mid-route.

He had parked beside a relay box on the Zone 5/7 boundary. The Seam worked past him: a vendor arguing about the price of something, a child in a yellow jacket walking a dog that wanted the opposite direction, the relay box ticking through its hourly test, one small green pulse per second. He let the number sit in front of him and refused to round it off.

One hundred and twelve.

He had read the manifest by zone and facility. He knew every name in Batch 44-C — 847 of them, carried whole, the way he carried route timings and relay positions and the weight of a packet by its feel through the bag. He had thought of that number as the size of the thing. It was not the size of the thing. If 112 gaps meant the protocol had run across other batches, then 847 was not the total. It was the fraction he happened to hold paper for. The rest were every neuretic client calibrated at these facilities in the relevant window — people with a managed absence sitting in their records where a week, a month, a face used to be, and no way ever to know it was gone, because the thing taken was the thing that would have noticed. He had been carrying a number he could hold in one head. The real number did not fit in a head. It fit in a city.

He breathed out slow. For a moment his hands, resting on the grips, were not his — they were narrower, surer, doing a small exact thing he had no name for, pressing a folded sheet of paper flat along a crease and smoothing it twice, the gesture of someone who handled paper for a living and trusted it more than screens. Then it was gone and his own hands were on the rubber grips and the child and the dog had reached a compromise and the relay box pulsed once, green, indifferent.

He rode the remaining two Zone 7 stops. Both receiving bays had Orison readers of the older model, gray at the edges, years in place. He checked each without appearing to check. No new equipment.

Just the one. Facility 4, eastern side of Zone 7, near the Zone 6 boundary — the shortest line from Orison's primary calibration center. If you were going to put a new mouth in the field, you would put it where it cost the least to feed.

He reached his apartment at 17:40. The three drives were in the lockbox under the floor panel by the radiator; he had not moved them in six days. He opened it, looked at them, closed it. His hands did not shake. He noted this because he had expected otherwise.

He wrote the number on the inside of his left wrist with a pen. He had been writing on his wrist for years — relay intervals, dock numbers, the timing of a gate that opened on a forty-second cycle, things he needed to hold for an hour and then let go. They were all the same kind of mark: a quantity that would be true until it wasn't, and then he'd wash it off. He had never once written a number that meant people. He set the pen to the skin and made the figures small and clean near the pulse point — *112* — and the act of it was wrong in his hand, too light a gesture for the freight, and he did it anyway because there was no heavier gesture available to him. It would wash off in the shower. The number would not.

He sat with it for an hour.

Then he opened the pre-paid card and sent to Amara's channel: *Same location. Tomorrow. Same time.* He considered adding *Are you still clear?* He did not. If she wasn't clear she wouldn't answer, and if she answered she was clear.

The relay beacons outside cycled through their hourly test. He had lived here four years and knew the schedule without checking: 18:00, 19:00, then a long pause to the 22:00 maintenance window. Tonight the 18:00 ran. Then the 19:00.

At 20:14 he heard a third cycle. Nothing in the schedule called for 20:14.

He went to the window. The corner box was running its indicator in the standard pattern. He counted the interval between pulses. Eleven seconds. The standard was eight.

He pulled on his jacket and went down. He rode to the box two blocks east and counted: eight. Standard. Back at the corner: eleven. The deviation was localized — someone had seated a monitoring node inside the relay infrastructure of his block, specific to this address.

He went back upstairs and moved the drives from the floor panel to the inside pocket of his courier bag, against his ribs, where they would stay until he had somewhere better. Then he ran the route, because that was the only way he knew to think. The drives were on his body now. The Zone 4 leg would have to change — he never took the same approach to a handoff twice, and his usual variation off the canal road fed straight back through a relay corridor he no longer trusted. The infrastructure he had used for five years, the eight-second pulses he steered by the way other men steered by stars, was reading him back. He worked the city in his head the way he worked it on the bicycle, weighing each segment for exposure, looking for the line that cost the least to ride — and for the first time in five years the route returned no clean answer, only a field of bad options he would have to choose the least bad of in the morning.

He lay listening to the relay beacons outside doing something they should not be doing, eleven seconds between each pulse, steady as a clock.

# The Turn

The message came at 08:22: *When you have a moment. No rush.*

She went immediately.

His office was the same. No window. The file on the desk. He was standing when she entered, which was different. He had the razor in his hand, but this time he had not drawn it along his nail. He had opened the blade. He was looking at it the way you looked at something you used for a specific purpose. He set it on the desk, open, and gestured to the chair.

"Sit," he said.

She sat.

He sat across from her. The razor was on the desk between them, blade facing up, which it had never been before. He didn't seem to notice it was there. Or he seemed to seem that way.

"You've been busy," he said.

"There's a lot in the queue," she said.

"I mean the portal queries." He said it the way you said something that wasn't a question because you already knew the answer. "The batch documentation. The administrative sub-tables. The cross-references." He looked at her. "I appreciated the thoroughness."

The razor was on the desk.

She had been going to say it was background research, a regulatory piece, the kind of thing a journalist on a beat followed until she understood it. She looked at the razor blade and did not say it.

"I work in media liability," he said. "Not communications partnership. That's what it says in the directory, but it's not what I do." He paused. "The journalists in the Orison media ecosystem are managed relationships. Their coverage is managed. My job is to ensure the management continues smoothly. When journalists become curious about how their coverage is managed, that's a liability situation."

"I was curious about the batch documentation," she said.

"I know what you were curious about," he said. He said it without anything in it — not anger, not warning. Just the factual version of knowing. "I provisioned your portal access, Amara. I can see exactly what you accessed."

He had provisioned her access. She had known this since she found her credentials already active at the infrastructure tier before she'd even asked. She had not understood until this moment what it meant: he had given her access not because she'd earned it but because he wanted to see exactly what she would look at with it.

She had looked at everything.

He knew what she had.

"I've arranged a professional leave," he said. "Two weeks. It starts Monday. The authorization is already in the Tribune's system." A pause. "You've had a demanding quarter. A rest is appropriate."

She had not requested leave.

"I'd rather not—" she started.

He picked up the razor. Not threateningly. The way you picked up a pen to sign something. He closed the blade and set it in the case beside his keyboard. The case closed with the usual soft click. His thumb pressed the latch once more, briefly, as if checking it had caught — a small redundant motion that his composure didn't intend.

He looked at her.

"Take care of yourself," he said.

He called her by her first name.

---

She had built it for three years. Not the byline — bylines were easy — but the platform, the access, the trust that made sources call her back rather than someone else. She had cultivated it deliberately and carefully, the way you cultivated a source: with patience, with consistency, with the understanding that trust was built in small increments and lost all at once. She had lost other things in her career. She had never lost this.

Two weeks' leave was not a firing. She knew how these things worked. Two weeks became four. The piece she'd been developing disappeared from the editorial calendar. The source calls stopped getting returned. Someone younger took the Tuesday wellness segment, which ran without her name, and the audience adjusted, and after a few weeks it was as if the Tuesday slot had always belonged to someone else. She knew this shape. She had watched it happen to other people.

She had been the voice they trusted. That was the specific thing she had. Not her reporting — other reporters were better on the policy, sharper on the science. What she had was the voice that made neuretics feel safe to ordinary people, the voice that made Orison's technology feel like something being done for them rather than to them. She had built that voice. Orison had built it with her, inside her, for three years, and now they were taking it off air.

And there was the other arithmetic, the one she did not let herself write down. Two weeks out of the building was two weeks no one who knew her would be watching — and her own calibration fell inside the window. She had known the date the way she knew all of them now, the way she had known Theodora's. They would have her in a room with managed air while her own people thought she was resting, and whatever was done there she would carry out as wellness and mean every word of it. The leave was not a punishment. It was an appointment. He had not taken her platform. He had cleared her calendar.

---

The elevator went down. The lobby had the news feed cycling — infrastructure investments in the outer ring, the managed warmth of the atrium through the glass partition, a desk attendant she didn't recognize eating something at her station. She walked through the lobby without stopping, out through the Seam entry, into air that moved.

The drive was in her jacket pocket — she had been carrying it for three days. She pressed her hand against it through the fabric. Small, flat, specific: the batch records, the Phase II brief, the citation list with her name in it twenty-seven times.

She was on two-week leave starting Monday. She had the material. She had the channel. She did not have the platform anymore.

She thought about what Seto had been doing without a platform for five years: moving things that mattered through channels no one was watching, in a city that needed him invisible. She thought about the weight she had received from him — the route-memory, the precise geography of a place you moved through rather than covered. Her feet found the canal-side angle before her eyes did, the low clearance of the Seam's service bridge arriving in her legs as a width she already knew how to carry. She had been receiving it for three months. She knew the shape of working without being seen.

She pulled out the pre-paid card and sent on the gray-zone channel: *Something's changed. Can we move up?*

She waited. A relay beacon overhead ran its test. Two women with food stall carts moved past arguing about a supply route.

The response came in forty seconds: *2PM. Same crossing.*

She put the card away. She had four hours before the leave authorization pushed through to her Tribune credentials. She was going to use them. She walked toward the Zone 6 transit line and did not stop until she was underground, where the air was recycled and the lighting was flat and nobody had designed it to make her feel like everything was fine.

She kept going.

# Story Runs

The pre-paid card was dead at 13:47.

He had been composing a message to the 2PM contact when the channel went offline — not an error, not a network gap, just nothing. The relay handoff that had worked for three weeks returned no acknowledgment. He tried twice. The card had carried six transmissions total and was now burned, or the relay infrastructure on the receiving end was gone. Either way the route was closed, and a route that closed without warning had been closed by someone.

He went to Shen crossing on foot.

She was already there, standing at the transit lane edge with her jacket collar up, the porridge stall doing its early-afternoon trade behind her. She looked like someone waiting for a connection. He had learned to read her body at distance the way he'd learned to read checkpoint behavior — not because he intended to study her but because the bleed had made it impossible not to. The way she held her left shoulder slightly forward: something was wrong.

He told her about the card. She told him about the leave — two weeks, starting Monday, arranged by Ciro without her input. She told him about the razor open on the desk, the phrase "loss mitigation," the way Ciro had provisioned her access before inviting her to use it and then watched exactly what she accessed. She told him about the wrong coffee.

"He was establishing presence," he said. "In case you noticed the proximity alert."

"I noticed," she said.

The Seam was doing its midday business around them — vendors arguing about stall placement, a relay tech running a junction box test, the Zone 6 transit pod cycling through on schedule. Two people having a careful conversation in a public space, which was what the Seam was designed for.

"What do we have?" she said.

He told her: the manifest, 847 names, three copies; the courier routing for the six Zone 7 facilities, timestamped against his own delivery logs so the receiving windows lined up; the monitoring node localized to his building, drawing power it had no license to draw. She told him about the infrastructure logs — 112 gaps, the query structure precise enough for someone else to reproduce. She had no saved data; the query had been intercepted mid-run. But the interruption was itself in the logs. The pause where her query had been stopped proved the suppression protocol was still live, and that the system was watching for exactly the person who would think to look.

"The query is the evidence," he said. "Someone who knows how to run it can verify it."

She had been reading the Seam traffic the way he read it. She looked at him directly.

"If it goes," she said, "it doesn't come back."

"No."

"The story runs without us."

"Yes."

She turned back to the transit lane. She had spent three years building the network that fed her the neuretics beat — the clinic techs who texted her at shift change, the records clerk who left envelopes, the two engineers who would only meet at the porridge stall. She had written the series that made the beat hers. She had been cited twenty-seven times in packages she had not been shown. To run a story, you held it until you had it whole; that was the whole discipline, the only thing that was hers. His jaw settled and held, a fraction more pressure than he had put there, the specific stillness of a breath withheld at exactly the right length. What she was weighing now was not the danger. It was that she would not be the one to hold it. He waited. He could feel the shape of the decision but not its inside, the way you can feel a load through a strap without knowing what's in the bag.

"Then the story runs," she said.

They went separately. He took the courier route through the Seam with the drive in the inner pocket of his courier bag.

The Zone 3/4 boundary checkpoint was staffed at elevated protocol — he registered this thirty meters out, in the spacing of the officers and the angle of the secondary scanner. He had not seen elevated protocol at this checkpoint in fourteen months. He rolled into the inspection lane and an officer held up a hand. Secondary bag inspection.

He unclipped the outer courier bag and set it on the table. The officer ran the outer compartments through the scan. Clean. She reached for the inner pocket authorization tag.

He pulled out the pre-paid card and held it toward her. Not asking a question. Answering one before she could ask it. "Gray-zone communications device," he said. "Requires separate logging under Code 7. I can hold the compartment open while you process it."

She looked at the card. She looked at him. Gray-zone devices went into a different log than physical goods — separate form, separate system entry. She took the card.

While she logged it, he kept his hand on the inner compartment tag and did not open it. She did not ask him to. She was reading the device ID off the card, entering it character by character into the inspection terminal.

"Clear," she said. She handed the card back.

He took it. The device was now in the checkpoint log, tied to his face and his certification. He would not be using it again — but the channel was already dead, and the only transmissions it had carried were already received. He rode two blocks past the checkpoint and dropped the card into a seam-side waste bin. There was nothing on it worth keeping, and now there was a record of him surrendering it. Both of those were true at once.

The relay shop in Zone 3 had a front window full of second-hand networking equipment and a back room licensed as a media archive. The owner was a man named Doru who knew Seto by the weight of his bags and asked no questions about introductions.

The journalist was not there. She had a drop protocol: the drive into a sealed case, the account written by hand on physical paper, transmitted by Doru on a channel she owned. Seto sat at the back table and wrote it all down — the batch number, the 847 names, the six facilities, the monitoring node at his building, the 112 gaps and exactly how the query had been structured.

Amara came in three minutes after him and stood at his shoulder while he wrote her part. He wrote it in her words because he had them, the bleed handing him her weighting before he understood he was using it: not *Orison ran an undisclosed protocol* but *the broadcaster whose series Orison cited twenty-seven times was the cover for the thing the series described*. He felt the sentence settle into place with a balance that was not his. He looked up. She was watching the paper. She did not correct a word of it. That was the cost — not the danger, not the leave. She stood and watched her own story go down in another man's handwriting, in her phrasing, and she let it.

Doru read the account. His thumbnail whitened where he pressed the paper flat without seeming to notice. He set it down.

"Give me three days," he said.

He took the drive. The drive was no longer in Seto's hands.

They stood outside the relay shop. The afternoon light caught the relay beacon casings at an angle that made them look older than they were. A woman two doors down was closing a shutter by hand, the ratchet catching and releasing in a regular click. Neither of them spoke. The thing they had carried for three weeks was now behind a door that had already locked, and there was nothing left in either of their hands to hold. The click of the shutter went on. He did not fill the silence and neither did she. It was the first time since the Shen crossing that there was nothing to decide.

"He called me by my first name," she said. "At the end. Like I was already gone."

He didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that changed what had been set in motion.

He rode home through Zone 4. The account was in Doru's hands. He thought about the 847 names he had read with full attention, zone by zone, facility by facility, each name a person with a clinic appointment and a gap in their record they would never be able to see. Nuru's name was the fourth in Zone 5. He had carried packets past her facility roughly a hundred times and had not once known she was inside the number he was carrying.

He had been building this case without knowing he was building it. Every timing anomaly, every checkpoint change — the case had assembled itself through the act of paying attention, and his sister had been inside it the whole time, and he had walked past her a hundred times.

His relay terminal flagged a compliance update at 18:03: *Zone 3/4 courier certification suspended. Orison Regulatory Compliance: licensing review pending, active routes suspended, estimated processing five to ten business days.* The routes were gone. The story was running. He did not know whether a story running would reach Nuru before it reached the people who had put her on the list, or whether it would reach her at all, or whether the protecting and the exposing were the same act wearing one name — and he rode the rest of the way home not knowing it, which was new, because this morning he had not known there was anything not to know.

# Thursday Morning

The hotel in Zone 5 charged by the day and asked no questions about guests who used the service entrance. Ekow had been here before. The room had the same desk lamp with the same slight lean, the same window that looked north toward the Zone 6 boundary. The boundary infrastructure was always worth looking at. It told you which way the data was moving, and the data only ever moved one way: toward the people who could afford to read it.

The dossier came through the consulting firm's secure drop at 06:14. He read it straight through without stopping, which was NGRA habit. Exhaust the material before you form an opinion, because the thing that doesn't fit appears last, after you've stopped looking for it.

The thing that didn't fit appeared fourth paragraph in. *Batch designation 44-C. Eight hundred and forty-seven patient entries.*

He stopped on it the way you stop on a wire that's the wrong color for the wall.

Calibration records were filed by client, by facility, by date — that was compliance taxonomy, the language of a CorpoNation accounting for its liabilities. Filing by batch was different. Batch was how you indexed a study. It was a designation that existed so that a result could be correlated against a population, so that eight hundred and forty-seven people could be held in one frame and read as a single curve. In the NGRA he had learned the difference between an intelligence index and an after-action report the way you learn the difference between a hand offered open and a hand offered to take. One described what was being done. The other described what had been done so it would not have to be answered for. This was the first kind. A courier wouldn't know to call it a batch. Whoever had written this brief had pulled from a research index and not bothered to translate it into the dialect of regret. He read the rest of the case and came back to it. It was still the wrong color.

One suspect: Seto Banda, Zone 3 residential. Carrier-grade sealed neuretics. Certified courier for six Zone 7 facilities. Checkpoint photograph, fourteen months old, slightly underexposed. Client: Orison Neuretics, Regulatory Compliance, through the firm's standard intermediary. Seven days before retrieval authorization activated.

He made a coffee from the room service unit and stood at the window with it. The Zone 6 boundary did what boundary infrastructure did: registered, logged, forwarded. Beyond it, community clinics, the kind of block where a nutritionist or a maintenance tech kept fixed hours because people were expecting them at those hours. He had been in places that kept fixed hours and didn't have the budget to ask what was being done to them inside those hours. Tuesday had been one. The Ocean had been another. He drank the coffee and did not think about them, which is its own kind of thinking.

Then he went to Zone 3.

---

The building was a standard Seam-adjacent block, three floors. He knocked on the door in the zone registry. A woman answered who was not Seto Banda — new furniture behind her, new face, five days of occupancy in the way she held the door. Banda had vacated six days prior. Standard move-out filing. No forwarding address.

He checked the date against the timeline in the corridor, where no one could read his face. Orison had filed the retrieval request seven days ago. Banda had moved out one day after that.

The building had no CCTV on this floor. He had noted that on the way up, the same way he noted exits and lines of sight before he knew whether he'd need them. Old wiring, the conduit gone soft at the joints. NGRA forward operating bases had looked exactly like this, before someone who knew better pulled the cable out of the walls and the silence afterward told you what the cable had been for.

Six days ago the man had been one step ahead. The question was whether that was luck or whether Banda had a hand inside the filing process. Ekow stood in the corridor a moment longer than the answer required, because the answer wasn't the part that was bothering him.

He went to Zone 7.

---

Facility 4 ran an Orison NeoCortex inspection reader at the secondary checkpoint. He showed his credentials and waited while the guard pulled Banda's visit record. When she looked up, her jaw had set.

"He's been through," she said. "Last visit six days ago. Passed secondary inspection."

"The inner compartment. What did it contain?"

She looked at him. Not hostile. Careful, the way you are careful around something you've been told to be careful around. "The scan was clean. I logged the inspection complete."

"I'd like to see the inner-compartment log."

She asked him to wait. She was gone four minutes — long enough to walk somewhere and bring back what she returned with, which was a man in Orison compliance grey, lanyard badge, a face that had decided something before the door opened.

"Inner-compartment records are Orison proprietary," the man said. "Not available under a consulting-firm retrieval order. Zone 7 Medical Board authorization required. Processing runs five business days." He said it without emphasis, the way you state a regulation you've already confirmed will hold.

Ekow had six days. "I'll submit the request," he said. He thanked them both and left.

Outside, in the Zone 7 corridor, he stood with it.

*The scan was clean* and *the inner compartment was clear* were two different sentences, and he had been allowed to hear only the first. That was the smaller thing. The larger thing was that the man in grey had arrived before the guard could finish a sentence, which meant he had been watching the checkpoint desk, or had been told to. You did not pre-position a compliance officer at a secondary checkpoint to stop a routine retrieval inquiry. You pre-positioned him because someone had decided, in advance, that a question about the inner compartment was a question that would be asked, and had to be the kind of question that found a wall instead of a record. The facility had not been guarding a courier. It had been waiting for the man who came after the courier. He had walked into a door that had been built around the shape of his arrival.

The relay shop in Zone 3 held the outgoing transmission behind a forty-eight-hour archive authorization. He couldn't reach it before that cleared.

He had a face, a route, an apartment vacated on impossible timing, an inner-compartment record sealed behind five days he didn't have, and a checkpoint that had been covered before he reached it. He had spent one day.

# Contract Delayed

The notification came in at 4:11 in the afternoon, while Ekow was reconciling the last of his Facility 4 mileage against the per-diem schedule, because even a suspended contract paid out its incurred expenses and he intended to be paid what he was owed.

He had read ten thousand of these. He read it twice. Not because it was unclear. Because the second reading was for the part of him that had learned, in two countries, to confirm a thing before he let his body believe it.

He was the compliance officer. That was the whole of the position. He went into a facility, he checked the maintenance records against the work performed, he wrote down where the two did not agree, and he certified the rest. The review now opening against him would, by definition, be a review of compliance. They had taken the function he performed and pointed it at the man who performed it. The mechanism did not even have to be clever. It only had to be the same shape, turned around.

The apartment was small and orderly the way the apartments of men who had lived in barracks stayed orderly: the bed made, the single chair square to the table, the window giving onto the gray light of eastern Zone 7 going to evening. He had seen this before. He let himself see it again, fully, because pretending he hadn't was a kind of lie that cost more than it saved.

In the Ocean this would have been a termination notice, and the difference between a termination notice and the man being terminated was sometimes a matter of hours and sometimes a matter of which vehicle you were standing near. The terminology had been harder there. Here it was a suspension form and the terminology was softer by the culture, and the function was identical. You did not need a weapon to remove a man from a system. You needed only to revoke the part of the system that recognized him. His credentials were contract-based. He was freelance. He did not write much. He had never written much.

He saved it anyway. # The Last Route
