# The One Who Doesn't Stop

_A burned-out freelancer with a dying mind carries a stranger's six-hour-old transplant, and the terrified child it belongs to, in a straight line north across a hostile vertical Chicago: corporate checkpoints, a refinery lockdown, a thin spot where the world stops keeping its promises, and a tunnel war, at a cost greater than the run was ever worth._

## Hegewisch

[Beat 1]
The contractor brings his Gauss rifle up, and Kyle already knows how the next second goes. Thirty seconds ago this was a conversation — an Arcturus contractor deciding whether to shoot Kyle tonight. He decided yes, and he brought a rifle to put an exclamation point on it: one hypersonic slug, dead straight, nothing in the world to argue with it. He fires it at Kyle's chest. Kyle drops. The slug finds nothing but the air he just left — and it does not stop. It crosses the intersection in a flat line, punches through the windshield of an ambulance running its lights north, and kills the driver where he sits. Steered by no one now, the ambulance slews into a stalled hauler and T-bones it, and the impact throws the back doors wide. And there in the wreck-light, strapped to a gurney inside, is a small girl — delivered to Kyle's feet by a shot that was meant for him, like the universe went to great trouble to make the introduction. Kyle comes up with Chorus already speaking; one round takes the contractor's trigger hand apart at the wrist, and the rifle clatters off to begin a quieter life on the pavement. Then Kyle looks at the girl, and the girl waves. He waves back.

[Beat 2]
"Are you the new one," the small face asks, and Kyle understands the question is not the one she's asking. She's eight, maybe nine, her chest is taped under a flexible biopolymer brace, and she is more composed than the second-tier corponation security officer currently bleeding out a yard to Kyle's left. Kyle steps up into the bay. Driver is dead. Medic in the passenger seat is also not coming back. The patient tag on the gurney reads IMANI OKONKWO-LINDQVIST. RECIPIENT. POST-OP, FIRST 6 HOURS. NORTHWESTERN MEMORIAL. Kyle reads it twice. The math does itself. He says, "Hi, Imani. I'm Kyle." She says, "I know. My uncle says you're the one who doesn't stop."

[Beat 3]
He thumbs the tag's emergency contact. One ring. "Vance," a voice says, already running. Kyle says, "Your driver's dead. The kid is on the floor of a south-side ditch with a bioprinted heart wired to a battery. I have a motorcycle." Silence. Then Vance says, "What's the brace reading." Kyle looks. "Sinus, ninety-two, paced." Vance says, "You have sixty minutes before the vascular scaffold throws a clot. Maybe seventy. Keep her calm — a new graft that races can tear off the suture line. Those wires aren't for decoration." Kyle says, "Northwestern." Vance says, "Streeterville. Trauma Bay Nine. I'll have a slot." Kyle says, "I'm in Hegewisch." Vance does the same math, almost as fast as Kyle's Neuretics had. "Straight line. North. Don't stop for checkpoints. And mind the weather — there's a thin spot parked over 35th, has been since Tuesday, and your straight line runs right under it. If the read in your skull drops out, you're in it: steady speed, eyes on a far light, don't look at the edges." Kyle says, "See you in a few." Vance hangs up.

[Beat 4]
Here is how Kyle knows it's sixty minutes and not sixty-one — the same way he knew the contractor's trigger a beat early. Everyone in the GLMZ runs Neuretics: compute grown into the skull, worked by thought, no jack, no blink-clicking, no poking at empty air. Kyle's is a military NeoCortex, an Atlas build, tuned so far past the safe envelope that it is, slowly and without drama, killing him. He is at peace with that. Somebody built him into a weapon and called it a gift, and the only thing he ever got to decide is who the weapon is for. It is not one mind but sixty-four — spliced sub-minds wired to vote — and right now sixty-three of them are voting, fast and cold, and they agree: the kid is a six-hour loss already logged, the contract was never his, and a dying man has no business spending what's left of himself on a stranger. It is good math. It is correct. The sixty-fourth splice — the one no designer drew and no surgeon can find to cut out — overrules all sixty-three the way it always does, on no evidence and against the count, because it insists one man can still make a difference. It is almost certainly a defect. Kyle has spent his whole life voting with the defect, and the katana on his back is that vote made of steel-CNT meta alloy. The right people, tonight, weigh less than his jacket and want to know how fast the bike goes.

[Beat 5]
He lifts Imani onto the pillion, sidesaddle, threads the medical harness through his belt-loop so the battery rides over his kidney instead of swinging, ties her wrists loosely through the back of his coat so she will not fall off if she sleeps. Her cheek finds the back of his shoulder like it has been there before. "Hold on," he says, which is a stupid thing to say to a kid whose hands are too small to grip his ribs, but it is what you say. She says, "How fast." He says, "Fast." She says, "Good." Silence rides across his back where it always rides — a matte-black carbon-nanotube katana, no edge that catches the light and no trick to it but the hand that holds it, the sixty-fourth splice's argument sharpened to a foot of metal. Chorus sits low on his left hip where it always sits. The kid is the only new weight, and the only one he is doing math about. The bike starts. Eight cylinders' worth of audacity.

[Beat 6]
The first mile is gray zone — Hegewisch sprawl, no sovereign in residence. Kyle takes Avenue O at eighty, threading two rows of collapsed light poles his Neuretics flags and lines up before his eyes have finished arriving. Imani's breathing is a small warm patch against his back, even, slow, deliberately controlled. He says, "You're not scared." She says, "I'm being efficient with my fear." Kyle almost laughs — then doesn't. "Where'd you learn that." "The lady who got me ready. She said to say it until it's true." She says it the way you say a line you have been handed, because it is one. "Is it true yet," Kyle says. Imani considers it, honest. "No." So it is not bravery. It is a borrowed sentence a scared kid is holding onto because someone put it in her hands and it was the only thing in reach. "Keep saying it," Kyle says, and does not tell her it never quite comes true. Some lines you say anyway.

## Crucible Genomics

[Beat 7]
The checkpoint is staffed by two guards in soft-helmet armor and a third behind a blast-shield. They have the bored faces of people who expect a manifest. Kyle does not have a manifest. He has a child and a battery and a smile. He brakes hard, throws his hands off the bars in the universal not-a-threat posture, and shouts, "POST-OP TRANSIT, LACUNA COOLER, NORTHWESTERN," before the guards can finish their reach. Left guard looks at the brace. Right guard looks at the cooler logo. The guard behind the glass — Crucible internal, white coat under the armor — runs Imani's face against a phenotype registry, because in a genomics zone the gate reads the person, not the plate; the bike is nobody here, it's the blood they care about. Whatever the registry tells him drains the color out of his evening. "Let him through," he says. "Phenotype clearance." The barrier sinks. Kyle says, "Have a great evening," and means it.

[Beat 8]
A mile and a half of Crucible private artery, manicured, palm-lit, river on the right. Kyle pegs the throttle. The bike makes the unsubtle music of a thing built before noise ordinances. Workers in white smocks look up from the greenhouses and decide not to look long. The corporation has classified Imani as outgoing inventory, which means Kyle is allowed to drive through Eden as long as he keeps moving. He keeps moving. Imani says, "Why are the trees in rows." Kyle says, "Because the company likes to know where things are." She says, "That seems anxious." Kyle has nothing to add. The kid is correct.

[Beat 9]
The north exit is a sally-gate — a slab of layered ballistic shutter that only lifts for a scheduled manifest, and Kyle's manifest is a lie he told a mile ago. He does not slow. His Neuretics reads the gate the way it reads everything — for the part that's load-bearing and the part that's only confident — and paints the hydraulic spine where the shutter meets its track. He draws Chorus left-handed, thumbs the cylinder to the breaching load, and puts one round exactly there. The actuator gives up its entire career at once; the slab jams a third of the way up, shrieking on a dead track. Kyle flattens to the tank and takes them under it with inches to spare. They drop off Crucible's glass-smooth pavement onto the broken asphalt of a gray zone and the suspension finds out about Chicago all at once. Imani laughs — small, clean, a tuning fork struck somewhere behind his ribs. "Loud part," she says. "Told you," he says.

## The Pullman Seam

[Beat 10]
Pullman Works glows on his right the way a museum glows — restored row houses, heritage cameras in period housings, an Axiom company town keeping perfect time for people who don't own a minute of it. Kyle threads the gray-zone seam along its edge, the unincorporated strip Axiom never bothered to fence. Heads come up as the engine passes. One kid shouts something the engine eats, but the shape of the word is unmistakably *legend*, and Kyle lifts a hand like he's in a parade. Other heads come up too, and these don't care about the bike. They're fledglings — pledges to the Vultures, the crews that scavenge the dead and the dying, the ones who'll cut the transplant back out of your chest if you miss a payment and sell whatever's left for parts. A fledgling runs salvage on spec — no patch, no blessing — hoping to drag home something worth a place in the flock. A post-op transplant riding pillion through open country isn't a target. It's a winning lottery ticket — the exact score that turns a fledgling into a Vulture. Two of them are already on their bikes and on their phones.

[Beat 11]
Imani's hand finds the side of his coat and clutches. Not afraid — the grip is steady, intentional — she is *noticing*. She has not been outside Crucible's pavement before. Kyle understands this without being told. He says, "First time off the block?" She says, "First time on the ground." He says, "Well. Buckle up." She says, "Buckle up to what." He says, "Existentially." She thinks about it. She says, "Okay."

## Ashgrave Corridor

[Beat 12]
The Ashgrave Synthesis Corridor announces itself with a wall of haze you can taste before you see it. The perimeter lights glow the wrong color — too yellow, too low — the way a refinery glows when it is having a feeling. Kyle has a respirator clipped to his jacket; Imani does not. His Neuretics throws the air up as a number he doesn't like and a clock he likes less. He reaches back without slowing, finds her face with his palm, presses his bandana over her mouth. "Breathe shallow. Through this. Not your nose." She nods against his hand. He bites his own respirator's mouthpiece, snaps the seal, and rides into a sky the color of old bruises like he was invited.

[Beat 13]
Two refinery towers exhale orange fire on either side of him, close enough to read rivet patterns. The air pressure shifts as a vent stack sixty feet up blows downward; the bike yaws; he corrects before the yaw finishes, because the correction arrived in his hands a half-second before the problem did. Imani's grip becomes a small steel claw at his belt. Out loud he says, "We're fine." She says, through the bandana, "I know. I can tell from your shoulders." Kyle has met adults who could not read his shoulders. He is starting to take this kid personally.

[Beat 14]
Halfway through, the corridor's throat is shut. A leak somewhere upwind has tripped a containment lockdown, and Ashgrave's answer to a containment lockdown is to drop a blast-rated bulkhead across the only lane and let whoever's caught inside hold their breath about it. The bulkhead is built to stop a runaway tanker. It was not built with Kyle's night in mind. He brakes to a crawl in the burning dark, draws Chorus, and lets the Neuretics find the manual-override housing bolted to the right-hand pillar — the one part of a door like this that trusts a human hand. Two breaching rounds. The first peels the cover. The second finds the release. The bulkhead groans and begins to lift, far too slowly, and Kyle is already moving, laying the bike flat and dragging them under the rising lip while Imani buries her face in his back and the toxic dark closes over them like water and is, a heartbeat later, behind them.

[Beat 15]
He clears the Corridor's north ramp at a hundred and ten, rolls off the throttle on the way down, and spits the respirator's mouthpiece out so it swings free on its lanyard. He reaches back one-handed and tugs Imani's bandana down off her face. "You with me?" She coughs once, twice, then says, "I'm with you. Your shoulders said." He says, "My shoulders are working overtime." She says, "Give them a raise." Kyle laughs so hard he almost loses the line.

## Englewood

[Beat 16]
Englewood at night looks like a city that's been told a joke nobody is laughing at *yet*. Half the streetlights work; the other half are someone's collateral. Kyle means to take 63rd to Halsted, but 63rd dead-ends in a viaduct that finished collapsing sometime this spring — a slope of broken deck and rebar where an underpass used to be. No way through. There is a way *over*: a tilted slab the locals have braced into a ramp, because in a gray zone every disaster eventually becomes infrastructure. He takes it at speed, the bike going light at the crest, Imani's battery thudding once against his kidney, and lands hard on the far side with thirty-four minutes left and a new ache behind his teeth. Two headlights take the ramp a beat behind him. Then a third. His Neuretics tags them, ranges them, and tells him the only useful thing there is to know: they are matching his speed on purpose. He does the only useful thing back. He goes faster.

[Beat 17]
Faster does not lose them — fledgling bikes are stripped down to engine and nerve — and a quarter mile on, with the clock and the kid both riding him, Kyle quits trying. He picks the narrow place where 63rd necks down between two dead cars and turns the chase into a different kind of problem: he lays the bike sideways across the lane as a wall, sets Imani down behind the engine block, and meets the first fledgling as the kid overcommits — lets the swung chain bite his forearm instead of his skull and puts the rider on the asphalt to study it up close. The second comes off his seat with a length of pipe; Kyle takes the knee, then the jaw, alive and unconscious, a distinction he still bothers to make. The third gets a hand on a pistol and gets an electric-bola round from Chorus instead, and a long bad minute alone with his own nervous system. The last one came for a flock and found this; Kyle gives him the only thing honesty requires — "Walk away and keep your hands" — and the kid walks. Kyle's own bike does not get up. Somewhere in the scrum a fledgling's wheel found the engine, and the eight cylinders that never did deserve this city tick, and cool, and quit. He looks at the bikes still standing, picks the one that sounds least like dying, and rights it. Imani comes out from behind the dead engine and considers it. "Is it ours now," she asks. "Yes," Kyle says, threading her wrists back through the back of his coat. "Until some bigger kid wants to try and take it." She thinks about that, and finds it fair — which is, Kyle suspects, the truest thing anyone has told her all year.

## The Cinderfall Anomaly

[Beat 18]
Halsted north of the tracks runs over Cinderfall Energy's bones — eleven subterranean thermal installations whose surface is a grid of steam-vent grates and convection waves and asphalt re-laid three times this year because it keeps warping. Imani says, "Why is the air doing that." Kyle says, "Because the corporation under us is cooking." She says, "Oh" — the *oh* of a child learning the world has a basement. He does not tell her the air is about to do something Cinderfall has nothing to do with. His Neuretics flags two headlights still hanging back where they have hung since Pullman: patient, matched to his pace, in no hurry, which is its own kind of bad news. Then it flags what is ahead instead. Two blocks on, the streetlights stop agreeing on where they are, and the lattice, which has had an answer for everything tonight, goes quiet in a way it has only gone quiet once before. Weather, Vance had called it.

[Beat 19]
He should turn two blocks west. West is Bridgeport, which is normal. He goes straight, because straight is what the call is for, and at 35th and Halsted the world stops being a place. The headlight hits something and the something does not hit back. His stomach drops a foot without him; the wheels are on pavement but the pavement is half a second behind where his eyes insist it is. And then the worse thing, the thing the briefings never sell hard enough: he knows this corner. He has never been here. He knows it the way you know a hallway from a dream you have had your whole life and never once on purpose — the grey country behind sleep that everyone visits and no one admits to. Behind him Imani makes a sound he has not heard a child make. Not fear. Not pain. Recognition.

[Beat 20]
Warpature, the Network calls it, and the city logs it with the rest of the weather. Chicago always had too much of that — nine months of winter, the lake firing ice sideways, the wind that funnels down the gridded corridors hard enough to lean on. The thin spots are just the newest line on the forecast: the weather that isn't water. This one is the sediment of the mass quantum-entanglement experiments they ran and buried sixty years ago, surfacing now where it likes; the maps mark it LATENT and the insurers won't touch it. Kyle's Neuretics tries to map it and gets back garbage — distances that won't sit still, a clock running three directions — and his own engine note reaches him a half-second late and wrong, like it is coming off a street he already left. For once in his overclocked life he is flying on the one instrument the lattice can't improve: the old animal in the base of the skull that everybody still has. He fixes on a streetlamp on the far rim and obeys the only rules survivors keep: constant speed, eyes on the light, don't look at the edges, don't trust a turn — because in here right is sometimes left and left is sometimes down. He says, "Don't look left." Imani says, "I already wasn't."

[Beat 21]
At the edge of his vision something moves that should not: a shape with one shadow too many, and the shadow arrives a half-beat before the shape does. He does not look at it directly — the dark keeps it blurred, and he understands, the way you understand a drop in pressure, that looking straight at it is not a thing a mind comes back from. Imani's breath catches, his only warning, and he throws the bike right, which is the wrong word for the direction it actually goes. The miss should have been a foot. It is less than an inch. He feels it pass like a current changing in deep water, and then a line of cold opens along his ribs under the coat — clean, deep, not yet bleeding, because in here the blood runs half a second behind everything else. The far lamp is closer. The throttle stays even. A block on, the world remembers its rules, and the pain arrives all at once and warm. And behind him the borrowed calm finally fails her. Imani comes apart — all the way apart, the way an eight-year-old is supposed to — sobbing into his back, both fists twisting in his coat: "It knew my name, it knew my name, I want to go home, I want to go home." And the brace at his kidney picks it up and starts to chirp, fast and climbing, because a six-hour-old heart was never built to run this hard. That, not the thing in the dark, is what can kill her now. Keep her calm, Vance had said. Kyle has no calm in him and no time to counterfeit any, so he gives her the only thing he's got — his voice, flat and low and boring as a read-out, naming the next ordinary thing and the next: the bike, the bridge coming up, the loud part she likes. "You're out of it. It's behind us. It can't come where we're going." The chirp slows. Slows. Steadies. By the next block she has herself back, mostly, in the wrung-out way a kid does after the worst of it. "You're hurt," she says, very quietly. "I'm fine," he says. It is the first time he has lied to her.

[Beat 22]
Twenty-one minutes, and for the first time all night his mirrors are empty: the last fledgling — the one who walked away at the viaduct instead of joining his friends on the asphalt — hung on as far as the thin spot and no farther, because nobody sane follows anybody into one. He has lost them for good. What he has not lost is the Loop. Municipal law has strong opinions about his speed here — except municipal law has been on wildcat strike for nine days and currently has none, which Kyle takes as a gift. The Loop is the one part of the city no corponation can switch off: too public, too packed, the beating heart every zone plugs into, so when it goes wrong it goes wrong in the open with a crowd. Tonight it is going wrong. The fast line to the bay is the Wabash Stack — a six-lane service tunnel the city's lower floors and the corponation's upper floors have feuded over so long the locals named it the Lobby War — and nine days of strike has turned it from standing to hot. Kyle can hear it from the mouth, the flat clap of small-arms under a crowd-roar, and Imani lifts her head off his back. "Is that the loud," she says, a thread of the thin spot still in it. "Different loud," he says. "Just people." Which is no comfort, but the long way costs eleven minutes he's bleeding through, so he goes in. Halfway down the cathedral-tall dark, full of scrolling ads, a corporate security line has a municipal picket pinned behind an overturned cleaning rig — both sides too far gone to clock a motorcycle until it's the most interesting thing in the room. Kyle thumbs Chorus to the flash-bang load and puts a round into the ceiling. The tunnel goes white. He does not get through clean: two seconds of blindness is two seconds of frightened people firing at memory, and one of them is unlucky enough to be lucky — a round furrows the meat across the top of his shoulder, not through, a hot graze that nearly tears Chorus from his grip. He keeps the median. He keeps the throttle. Behind him Imani is watching the rivers of light, not the blood she cannot see. "It's beautiful," she says. Kyle gets his breath back around the burn before he answers, so it lands level. "Don't tell the advertisers," he says. "It'll go to their heads."

[Beat 23]
River North is a thirty-second wedge of gray zone, most of it underwater — the river hasn't been managed in eighteen years, and Lower Wacker is a canal now, black water where a road used to be. Imani turns her head to watch it slide past. "The street's drowned," she says — not scared, just filing it, one more entry in a night that keeps teaching her the ground is negotiable. "Most of them down here are," Kyle says, and takes the upper level: a bridge Tollgate Systems owns and charges credits to cross. There's a barrier across it, a swinging steel boom, the sort of thing that stops a delivery van. He does not slow for it and does not think about it again — it is the least dangerous thing to happen to him in an hour, and it folds off its hinge without comment. What he does think about is the camera that reads his face across the span — not the bike, the bike is nobody, it's *him* the system wants — and bills the only identity it can match: a credit account that hasn't existed since 2207, which Kyle built for exactly this kind of evening and then deleted. The invoice will get an automated rejection, the rejection filed, the filing archived, the archive purged in a routine sweep — six weeks and seventeen credits of Tollgate's money to process a ghost. He laughs, which pulls at the cold line under his coat. "Why are you laughing," Imani says. "Because the most expensive thing I did to them tonight was the paperwork." She says, "Good."

## Streeterville

[Beat 24]
Streeterville is a forest of glass towers in crowns of advertising, and at their feet the brutalist slab of Northwestern Memorial — and tonight the whole district is wall-to-wall people, the strike spilled north into the one part of the city no one gets to lock down. Which is exactly why it's here. Kyle sees the bay's red light six blocks out, and between him and it, wading through the crowd on six legs with the patience of a thing that has nowhere better to be, is an Arcturus crowd-control unit: a crab the size of a panel van, matte riot-grey, a Bulwark. Arcturus builds everything into a crab eventually — low, omnidirectional, impossible to tip — and a crab is what waits at the end of his night. Kyle has put this exact unit down twice. He knows it's the same one because it slows when its optics catch his face, and recalculates, and comes for him first out of a crowd of a thousand easier problems. A machine can't hold a grudge. Kyle stopped believing that the second time.

[Beat 25]
The Bulwark can't do the one thing that would end this fast. It's a crowd unit — built to work shoulder to shoulder with civilians — so it carries nothing that goes bang: no slugs, no rockets, nothing that doesn't care where it lands. What it has is hands. Tanglefoot, kinetic foam, a sonic throw that turns your inner ear to surf, and four hydraulic claws that fold a person up and hold them for collection. It wants Kyle folded up; it does not care about the kid except as part of the package. That is the opening, and it is a thin one. Kyle frees Imani's hands from his coat without setting her down — she stays on the pillion, she stays with the bike — and presses two fat cylinders into her grip off his harness. "It sees in light and hears in sound, and it can't shoot back. Left one's flash — that blinds it. Right one's chaff — that makes it deaf and stupid. Pull the tab, count two, throw at the face." Imani looks at the crab the size of a panel van. Imani looks at the grenades in her hands. "It's a really big crab," she says, in the voice of a kid handed the best and worst job of her life. "It is," Kyle agrees. "Throw at the face."

[Beat 26]
Then it is very loud and very fast. Imani's first flash goes off a half-second early and a yard wide, and the Bulwark flinches anyway, optics whiting out — and Kyle is already moving, not away but under, because the one place a crab cannot reach is beneath its own belly. He lays the bike flat and slings them through the gap between its legs, and as the world tips sideways he draws Silence off his back for the first time all night. The katana has ridden every mile for this. He opens two leg actuators clean on the way through, and the Bulwark drops a quarter of its height with a sound like a building deciding to sit down. Chaff bursts above them, dead on the face this time — Imani, on the two-count — and the thing's remaining legs stop agreeing with one another, hunting for a floor its sensors can no longer find. A claw catches the forearm the chain already opened and widens it to the bone; the bad rib tears further on the next hard pivot and empties half the air out of him; he eats both and stays between the machine and the kid, because there is no version of tonight where he does not. Under the work his lattice runs past every red line it owns, spending him to keep the read fast enough, the cost gathering behind his eyes like weather. He puts the last two actuators down. The Bulwark folds into the intersection — blind, deaf, beached, legs still working and going nowhere anybody can use. The bike does not survive the dismount; it goes down hard and stays down. But the bay's red light is three blocks off, and three blocks is nothing. "Good throw," he tells her. Imani — shaking, fierce, all the way wrung out — says, "I know."

[Beat 27]
The bike is dead and the bay is three blocks off, and three blocks is nothing; he has bled more than this for less. He sheathes Silence, holsters Chorus, and picks her up — harness, battery, the whole small weight of her — because the alternative is to set her down, and he is not going to set her down. He could not tell you the bottom of why. He only knows there was a stretch of his own being-small when somebody could have carried him and chose the easier thing, and that the gap it left never closed so much as taught him which side of it to stand on. So he stands on this side. He carries. Imani's hand fists in his collar. "You're hurt," she says again, and this time he doesn't bother with the lie. "Yeah," he says. "Tell me about the loud," she says, which is the kindest thing anyone has done for him in a year, so he does — the whole way, one foot and then the other, the red light getting bigger.

[Beat 28]
Vance is already in the lane with the gurney — tall, brown skin, hair tied back, scrubs over a body that has run a lot today — and does not waste a syllable. "On the gurney." Kyle is already lowering her. The harness comes off in two practiced motions. Imani's hand stays in his coat a half-second after the rest of her is on the gurney, and when he pries it loose she asks the question every kid eventually has to put to somebody. "Will I see you again?" Kyle does not lie to her; he has been lied to by kinder people than himself, and it never once helped. "No," he says. "Probably not." She takes it the way she has taken everything tonight — straight, no flinch, filing it somewhere. *Good,* he thinks. Better she learns it now, while she is still young enough for the scar to set smooth: that sometimes the person who comes for you is a stranger, who does the thing and is gone, and that this is not the same as being left. He learned it late, and it set crooked. "Thank you for the loud," she says. "Anytime, kid," he says — the gentlest lie he has told all night, and the only one she lets pass. "Tell the heart I said hi."

[Beat 29]
Vance reads the brace, the wires, the cooler — then reads *him*: the hand pressed to his side, the dark spreading at the forearm and the shoulder, the boot that won't take full weight. "Forty-one minutes," she says. "I had you at fifty-five." It is an old argument between them; she has put him back together before, and they both already know how the next part goes. Kyle says, "I cheated." She says, "You're bleeding into my ambulance lane. Stay. Half the people in my bay tonight are in better shape than you." Kyle is already turning away. "Don't worry about me," he says. "I'll just rub some dirt in it."

[Beat 30]
He walks. The bay door drops behind him and its red light goes white, which is the hospital's way of saying they have her, which is the only confirmation he was ever going to get that it worked. No alarm follows him out. No name. That's the shape of the work: you do the thing, the thing closes behind you like there was never an opening you tore wide open, and the city goes back to being the city without ever noticing the hole you came through.

[Beat 31]
He turns south, on foot, the long way, because south is no longer urgent and there's no bike left to make it quick. Behind his eyes the overclocked lattice runs hot and spends another thin slice of whatever he has left, and he carries that the way he carries everything — without complaint, because tonight it bought a stranger's kid a heartbeat she gets to keep. She'll forget his name by spring. That's the deal, and it's a good deal, and he'd take it again before she finished asking. He has somewhere to be. He always does. So he goes — not fast, for once, and not in a straight line, but going, which is the only direction he has ever really had.
