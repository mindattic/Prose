# The One Who Doesn't Stop

_A burned-out freelancer with a dying mind carries a stranger's six-hour-old transplant, and the terrified child it belongs to, in a straight line north across a hostile vertical Chicago: corporate checkpoints, a refinery lockdown, a thin spot where the world stops keeping its promises, and a tunnel war, at a cost greater than the run was ever worth._

## Hegewisch

[Beat 1]
They come for Kyle the way Arcturus Civil Security — the corporate police-for-hire that stands in for the law this city dissolved — does when the invoice runs large: not one man but a kill team — six of them, milspec reflex-ware under the skin, peeling out of two matte SUVs across the Hegewisch crossroads with the bored economy of people who do this for salary and dental. The neighborhood reads it before the squad would like. A noodle cart folds itself shut. A kid on a stoop is hauled indoors by the collar. The street empties the way streets down here have learned to empty, fast and without comment, leaving Kyle alone in the middle of it with his bike and his bad options. He'd known since morning the day had a bad lean to it. A flock of grackles had peeled off the wind over Avenue O and flown a long stretch backwards — against themselves, against the air — the way birds do when The Weather is coming on. He'd figured on a thin spot somewhere ahead; he had not figured on company. Running is the first option his Neuretics throws out — six shooters, open ground, no. Neuretics: bio-compute grown around and through the brain, worked by thought, no jack, no blink-clicking, no poking at empty air. Most people run it to read their messages. Kyle's is a military build, and what it does to a gunfight is what a chess engine does to a club player — it reads shoulders and weight and the hundred tells a body leaks before it fires, and hands him the next half-second a hundred and twenty milliseconds before it arrives. The team leader's finger takes up slack; Kyle is already moving when the first burst stitches the air he left. Cacophony clears its throat off his left hip — the stubby five-chamber revolver-shotgun, a rubber slug, because the math has not gone lethal yet — and the nearest operator folds around his own sternum and sits down in the road. Four in the cylinder. Five shooters still up.

[Beat 2]
The team is good. They don't bunch and they don't panic; they peel to the SUVs and start working angles, and good is exactly the problem, because precognition against one trigger is a gift and against five is a budget. Kyle spends it carefully. An electric-bola round takes the flanker breaking left — the man drops into a private argument with his own nervous system. A breaching slug goes through the door an operator is crouched behind and folds the steel into him. Then Cacophony runs dry on the fourth, the hammer coming down on an empty chamber with a click he feels in his teeth, and there is no time to swing the cylinder out and feed it, so he quits solving the problem with the gun. He draws Silence off his back. The katana is a matte-black length of steel-CNT meta alloy — no edge that takes the light, no trick to it but the hand that holds it, and the hand has had a long time to practice. He crosses the open ground inside the half-second his neuretics buys him, under the muzzle before it can track, and takes the rifle out of the fifth man's hands at the receiver — the man left disarmed, intact, and loudly amazed to still have his fingers. Steel for steel's problems, Cacophony for the rest: the whole of Kyle's philosophy of violence, and it has kept him alive longer than the actuarial tables like. Behind his eyes the overclock runs hot, and in the corner of his mind's eye a small red light ticks over the way it always does when he overdraws — a tally he quit reading years ago. The cost gathers like a debt he'll settle up later. A thin line of blood starts at his nose; he thumbs it off his lip without thinking, and white creeps in at the edges of everything. He ignores both, the way he always does. Five down or out. One left — the leader, who has not moved, because the leader has been waiting.

[Beat 3]
The leader brought the answer to men like Kyle and has only been waiting for the range to clear. It is a Gauss rifle — a man-portable rail driver that throws one hypersonic slug in a line so flat it might as well be drawn with a ruler, and does not care in the least what stands behind the target. It is the one weapon his neuretics cannot beat, because you cannot dodge a thing already past you; you can only fail to be where it was aimed. He puts it on Kyle's chest and fires. Kyle reads the shot the breath before it leaves and is no longer there — and the slug, finding only the air he left, does not stop. It crosses the crossroads dead straight and punches through the windshield of an ambulance running its lights north, and kills the driver where he sits. Steered by no one now, the ambulance never finishes its turn; it T-bones a stalled hauler with the flat finality of a thing nobody's holding, and the impact throws its back doors wide. Kyle is inside the leader's reach before the man can cycle for a second shot — the pommel, then one clean cut — and the Gauss rifle clatters off to begin a quieter life on the pavement. Then it goes very quiet. Kyle stands in the wreck-light looking at an ambulance he tore open by ducking, and a small face inside it looking back — like the universe went to a great deal of trouble to make the introduction. The small face waves. Kyle waves back.

[Beat 4]
"Are you the new one," the small face asks, and Kyle understands the question is not the one she's asking. She's eight, maybe nine, her chest is taped under a flexible biopolymer brace, and she is more composed than the second-tier corponation security officer currently bleeding out a yard to Kyle's left. Kyle steps up into the bay. Driver is dead. Medic in the passenger seat is also not coming back. The patient tag on the gurney reads IMANI OKONKWO-LINDQVIST. CRUCIBLE GENOMICS RECIPIENT. POST-OP, FIRST 6 HOURS. NORTHWESTERN MEMORIAL. Crucible Genomics prints organs to order; the kid is six hours into living on one of them. Kyle reads it twice. The math does itself. He says, "Hi, Imani. I'm Kyle." She says, "I know. My uncle says you're the one who doesn't stop."

[Beat 5]
He thumbs the tag's emergency contact. One ring. "Vance," a voice says, already running. Kyle says, "Your driver's dead. The kid's on the floor of a south-side ditch with a bioprinted heart wired to a battery. I have a motorcycle." A pause on the line — not shock, recognition. "Kyle." Vance says it flat, the way you say the name of a problem you already know by heart. "Of course it's you. What's the brace reading." Kyle looks. "Sinus, ninety-two, paced." Vance says, "You've got sixty minutes before the vascular scaffold throws a clot. Maybe seventy." Kyle does not hear the maybe. The same military grade neuretics that read the kill team a hundred and twenty milliseconds ahead reads the brace now — the battery draw, the kid's color, the math of a six-hour-old heart — and returns one cold number with no maybe in it. Sixty. "Keep her calm," Vance is saying. "A new graft that races can tear off the suture line. Those wires aren't for decoration." Kyle says, "Northwestern." "Streeterville. Trauma Bay Nine. I'll have a slot." "I'm in Hegewisch." Vance does the same math, almost as fast as Kyle's Neuretics had. "Straight line. North. Don't stop for checkpoints. And mind The Weather — there's a thin spot parked over 35th, has been since Tuesday, and your straight line runs right under it. If the read in your skull drops out, you're in it: steady speed, eyes on a far light, don't look at the edges." Kyle says, "See you in a few." Vance hangs up.

[Beat 6]
He lifts Imani onto the pillion, sidesaddle, threads the medical harness through his belt-loop so the battery rides over his kidney instead of swinging, ties her wrists loosely through the back of his coat so she will not fall off if she sleeps. He feeds Cacophony a fresh moon-clip — five new chambers, the old five scattered back across the Hegewisch crossroads — and lets it settle on his left hip. Silence rides across his back where it always rides. Her cheek finds the back of his shoulder like it has been there before. "Hold on," he says, which is a stupid thing to say to a kid whose hands are too small to grip his ribs, but it is what you say. She says, "How fast." He says, "Fast." She says, "Good." The kid is the only new weight, and the only one he is doing math about. The bike starts. Eight cylinders' worth of audacity.

[Beat 7]
The first mile is gray zone — Hegewisch sprawl, no sovereign in residence. Kyle takes Avenue O at eighty, threading two rows of collapsed light poles his Neuretics flags and lines up before his eyes have finished arriving. Imani's breathing is a small warm patch against his back, even, slow, deliberately controlled. He says, "You're not scared." She says, "I'm being efficient with my fear." Kyle almost laughs — then doesn't. "Where'd you learn that." "The lady who got me ready. She said to say it until it's true." "Is it true yet," Kyle says. Imani considers it, honest. "No." "Keep saying it," Kyle says, and does not tell her it never quite comes true. Some lines you say anyway.

## Crucible Genomics

[Beat 8]
The checkpoint is staffed by two guards in soft-helmet armor and a third behind a blast-shield. They have the bored faces of people who expect a manifest. Kyle does not have a manifest. He has a child and a battery and a smile. He brakes hard, throws his hands off the bars in the universal not-a-threat posture, and shouts, "POST-OP TRANSIT, LACUNA COOLER, NORTHWESTERN," before the guards can finish their reach. Left guard looks at the brace. Right guard looks at the cooler logo. The guard behind the glass — Crucible internal, white coat under the armor — runs Imani's face against a phenotype registry, because in a genomics zone the gate reads the person, not the plate; the bike is nobody here, it's the blood they care about. Whatever the registry tells him drains the color out of his evening. "Let him through," he says. "Phenotype clearance." The barrier sinks. Kyle says, "Have a great evening," and means it.

[Beat 9]
A mile and a half of Crucible private artery, manicured, palm-lit, river on the right. Kyle pegs the throttle. The bike makes the unsubtle music of a thing built before noise ordinances. Workers in white smocks look up from the greenhouses and decide not to look long. The corporation has classified Imani as outgoing inventory, which means Kyle is allowed to drive through Eden as long as he keeps moving. It also means the people who want what she can't live without can't follow him through here — they don't have what got him waved in. So they won't be following. They'll be calling ahead. And whoever they wake up won't need her alive to collect — a six-hour heart travels fine in a cooler. He keeps moving. Imani says, "Why are the trees in rows." Kyle says, "Because the company likes to know where things are." She says, "That seems anxious." Kyle has nothing to add. The kid is correct.

[Beat 10]
The north exit is a sally-gate — a slab of layered ballistic shutter that only lifts for a scheduled manifest, and Kyle's manifest is a lie he told a mile ago. He does not slow. His Neuretics reads the gate the way it reads everything — for the part that's load-bearing and the part that's only confident — and paints the hydraulic spine where the shutter meets its track. He draws Cacophony left-handed, thumbs the cylinder to the breaching load, and puts one round exactly there. The actuator gives up its entire career at once; the slab jams a third of the way up, shrieking on a dead track. Kyle flattens to the tank and takes them under it with inches to spare. They drop off Crucible's glass-smooth pavement onto the broken asphalt of a gray zone and the suspension finds out about Chicago all at once. Imani laughs — small, clean, a tuning fork struck somewhere behind his ribs. "Loud part," she says. "Told you," he says.

## The Pullman Seam

[Beat 11]
Pullman Works glows on his right — restored row houses, modern cameras in period housings, an Axiom company town keeping perfect time for people who don't own a minute of it. Kyle threads the gray-zone seam along its edge, the unincorporated strip Axiom never bothered to fence. Heads come up as the engine passes. One kid shouts something the engine eats, but the shape of the word is unmistakably *legend*, and Kyle lifts a hand like he's in a parade. Other heads come up too, and these don't care about the bike. They're fledglings — pledges to the Vultures, the crews that scavenge the dead and the dying, the ones who'll cut the transplant back out of your chest if you miss a payment and sell whatever's left for parts. A fledgling runs salvage on spec — no patch, no blessing — hoping to drag home something worth a place in the flock. A post-op transplant riding pillion through open country isn't a target. It's a winning lottery ticket — the exact score that turns a fledgling into a Vulture. Two of them are already on their bikes and on their phones.

[Beat 12]
Imani's hand finds the side of his coat and clutches. She's not afraid — she's *absorbing*. She's never been outside Crucible's pavement before. Kyle understands this without being told. He says, "First time off the block?" She says, "First time on the ground." He says, "Well. Buckle up." She says, "Buckle up to what?" He says, "Existentially." She thinks about it. She says, "Okay."

## Ashgrave Corridor

[Beat 13]
The Ashgrave Synthesis Corridor announces itself with a wall of haze you can taste before you see it. The perimeter lights glow the wrong color — too yellow, too low — the way a refinery glows when it's having a feeling. This is somebody's commute. A shift-change line of workers in cheap half-masks trudges the shoulder, heads down, lunch pails swinging, walking a thing they've made peace with because the alternative is not eating; a stall at the mouth of it sells filter cartridges and, beside them, a hand-painted board keeps a tally — DAYS SINCE — the number low, the way it's always low here. Kyle has a respirator clipped to his jacket; Imani does not. His Neuretics throws the air up as a number he doesn't like and a clock he likes less. He reaches back without slowing, finds her face with his palm, presses his bandana over her mouth. "Breathe shallow. Through this. Not your nose." She nods against his hand. A masked worker on the shoulder lifts two fingers as he passes — not a wave, just the nod one night-shift gives another — and Kyle returns it, then bites his own respirator's mouthpiece, snaps the seal, and rides into a sky the color of old bruises like he was invited.

[Beat 14]
Two refinery towers exhale orange fire on either side of him, close enough to read rivet patterns. The air pressure shifts as a vent stack sixty feet up blows downward; the bike yaws; he corrects before the yaw finishes, because the correction arrived in his hands a half-second before the problem did. Imani's grip becomes a small steel claw at his belt. Out loud he says, "We're fine." She says, through the bandana, "I know. I can tell from your shoulders." Kyle has met adults who could not read his shoulders. He is starting to take this kid personally.

[Beat 15]
Halfway through, the corridor's throat begins to close. A leak somewhere upwind trips a containment lockdown, and Ashgrave's answer is a blast-rated bulkhead coming down across the only lane on a dead-man timer — slow as a guillotine and about as negotiable — to seal whoever's on the wrong side in with the air they were fleeing. This is not the sally-gate; there's no override to find and no time to find it, because a hydraulic spine already in motion is a different animal than one at rest, and his neuretics says so before he's done asking. He can't beat the door. He can only beat it there. He drops two gears and pins the throttle, and the Neuretics measures the shrinking gap against the height of a man flat on the tank with a kid folded down his spine and hands back a number with no margin in it. He takes it anyway, and the white at the edges of his sight pulses wider for the second he leans that hard on the read, then settles. They go under the falling edge at speed; the lip catches the battery brace and shrieks the length of it in a fan of orange sparks — a hand's width higher and the night ends here — then the bulkhead seats behind them with a boom he feels in the floor of his chest. Through. He throws one look at the brace. Still green; the cooler held. Imani isn't laughing this time — both fists in his coat, face buried, very, very quiet — and Kyle files that with the other things tonight has taught him about her.

[Beat 16]
He clears the Corridor's north ramp at a hundred and ten, rolls off the throttle on the way down, and spits the respirator's mouthpiece out so it swings free on its lanyard. He reaches back one-handed and tugs Imani's bandana down off her face. "You with me?" She coughs once, twice, then says, "I'm with you. Your shoulders said we were okay." He says, "My shoulders are working overtime." She says, "Give them a raise." Kyle laughs so hard he almost loses the line.

## Englewood

[Beat 17]
Englewood at night looks like a city that's been told a joke nobody's laughing at. Kyle means to take 63rd to Halsted, but 63rd dead-ends in a viaduct that finished collapsing sometime this spring — a slope of broken deck and rebar where an underpass used to be. No way through. There is a way *over*: a tilted slab the locals have braced into a ramp, because in a gray zone every disaster eventually becomes infrastructure. He takes it at speed, the bike going light at the crest, Imani's battery thudding once against his kidney, and lands hard on the far side with thirty-four minutes left and a new ache behind his teeth. Two headlights take the ramp a beat behind him. Then a third. His Neuretics tags them, ranges them, and tells him the only useful thing there is to know: they are matching his speed on purpose. He does the only useful thing in response. He goes faster.

[Beat 18]
They come in a flock this time — the ones the phones woke, fresh bikes and no patience, fanning across both lanes to take him at speed. The lead pair don't reach for guns; they reach for their belts and throw, and the air fills with fat little cylinders that burst into white glare and dead static — flashbang, chaff, and the ugly third kind: the EMP-and-interference rounds a salvage crew carries to kill a mark's bike and blind whatever he's running behind his eyes. It is a good plan against almost anyone. Kyle's bike stumbles, coughs, and keeps running on hardened ignition. And the read behind his eyes never so much as flickers — because a military Neuretics is shielded against more than static: against EMP, against the whole spectrum of battlefield interference, and against the low, wrong pressure at the bottom of the band that would fold a civilian rig in half. You do not harden a mind against that last thing unless you already know it is out there. Kyle has never told anyone his is. He files the question of where a pack of fledglings got rounds that carry it, and stops the bike — because running is what they are built for, and standing still is not what they expect. He picks the narrow place under the 63rd Street viaduct where the lane necks down between two dead cars, thumbs the bike into auto-balance, and feels it take its own weight, gyros holding it upright and patient at a crawl with the kid still aboard. "Don't move," he tells her. Imani goes still as furniture. Then he steps off into the road and draws Silence off his back. The first one commits to the chain before he understands the geometry has changed; Kyle reads the swing early, the way he reads everything, and isn't quite where it lands — the chain's tail still opens a line across his forearm, the tax for standing this close — and he takes the kid off the saddle on the way past, shallow and deliberate, and on the same motion cuts the bandolier of grenades off his chest and tosses it back underhand. "Hold these." Imani catches it against herself like a schoolbag. The second tries to simply run him down, which is how the second comes to study the asphalt up close. The third has the sense to draw a pistol — and that is when the Neuretics paints the thing the briefings can't sell: the shot before the trigger finishes it, a hard red line laid from the muzzle straight through the middle of Kyle's chest. So Kyle takes his chest off the line. He drops his weight back off his heels, far enough to feel the round part the air above his sternum and go on to find the fourth fledgling, who had done everything right except stand directly behind the man he was hunting. The shooter stares at what his own hand just did. Kyle takes the pistol, and the fight, off him. The rest of the flock runs the math and doesn't like the product. Two peel away north and don't look back; the ones on the ground will walk when their legs agree to it. Kyle gets back on his bike — still upright, still warm, still holding Imani exactly where he left her — kills the balance, and lets the weight settle into his hands like something familiar. Imani is looking at the bandolier in her lap. "Are these ours now," she asks. "Until some bigger kid wants to try and take them," Kyle says, threading her free hand back through the back of his coat. She thinks about that, and finds it fair — which is, Kyle suspects, the most truthful anyone has ever been to her. He brings them back up to speed, and for the first time since Pullman the mirrors stay empty. They are done being chased.

## The Cinderfall Anomaly

[Beat 19]
Halsted north of the tracks runs over Cinderfall Energy's bones — eleven subterranean thermal installations whose surface is a grid of steam-vent grates and convection waves. Imani says, "Why is the air doing that." Kyle says, "Because the corporation under us is cooking." She says, "Oh" — the *oh* of a child learning the world has a basement. Then, smaller, with none of the cleverness in it: "I don't like it here." "Me neither," Kyle says, and doesn't dress it up, because that one is true. He does not tell her the air is about to do something Cinderfall has nothing to do with. With the road behind him finally empty, his Neuretics has nothing left to track but what waits ahead — and two blocks on, the streetlights stop agreeing on where they are, and the Neuretics, which has had an answer for everything tonight, goes quiet in a way that means only one thing: The Weather, Vance had called it.

[Beat 20]
He should turn west. West is Bridgeport, which is normal. He goes straight, because straight is what the call is for, and at 35th and Halsted the air changes temperature with no source for it — a dry cold, and under it the smell of a room sealed since before he was born — and the world stops being a place. The headlight hits something and the something pushes back. The world dims. His stomach drops a foot without him; the wheels are on pavement but the pavement is half a second behind where his eyes insist it is. And then the worse thing, the thing the briefings never sell hard enough: he knows this corner. He has never been here. He knows it the way you know a hallway from a dream you have had your whole life and never once on purpose — the dark country behind sleep that everyone visits and no one talks about the next day. Behind him Imani makes a sound he has not heard a child make. Not fear. Not pain. Recognition.

[Beat 21]
It has a name on the forecasts and an exclusion flag on the maps, but Kyle knows what it actually is. A thin spot is not bad air and not radiation. It is a wound in the stuff that minds run on — the old, never-closed reach of the Concordance still bleeding through — and it is, faintly and forever, still thinking. That is how it knew his corner. That is why, in a minute, it is going to know her name. His Neuretics tries to map it and gets back garbage — distances that won't sit still, a clock running three directions at once, his own engine note reaching him a half-second late, like it's coming off a street he already left — so for once in his overclocked life he flies on the one instrument no build can improve: the old animal at the base of the skull. He fixes on a streetlamp on the far rim and keeps the only rules survivors keep — constant speed, eyes on the light, don't look at the edges, don't trust a turn, because in here right is sometimes left and left is sometimes down. "Don't look left," he says. Imani says, "I already *wasn't*."

[Beat 22]
At the edge of his vision something moves that should not: a shape with one shadow too many, and the shadow arrives a half-beat before the shape does. He does not look at it directly — the dark keeps it blurred, and he understands, the way you understand a drop in pressure, that looking straight at it is not a thing a mind comes back from. Imani's breath catches, his only warning, and he throws the bike right, which is the wrong word for the direction it actually goes. The miss should have been a foot. It is less than an inch. He feels it pass like a current changing in deep water, and then a line of cold opens along his ribs under the coat — clean, deep, not yet bleeding, because in here the blood runs half a second behind everything else. The far lamp is closer. The throttle stays even. A block on, the world remembers its rules, and the pain arrives all at once and warm, and the white that has been creeping at his edges all night swims in close enough to blur the far lamp before he blinks it down. And behind him the borrowed calm finally fails her. Imani comes apart — all the way apart, the way an eight-year-old is supposed to — sobbing into his back, both fists twisting in his coat: "It knew my name, it knew my name, I want to go home, I want to go home." And the brace at his kidney picks it up and starts to chirp, fast and climbing, because a six-hour-old heart was never built to run this hard. That, not the thing in the dark, is what can kill her now. Keep her calm, Vance had said. Kyle has no calm in him and no time to counterfeit any, so he gives her the only thing he's got — his voice, flat and low and boring as a read-out, naming the next ordinary thing and the next: the bike, the bridge coming up, the loud part she likes. "You're out of it. It's behind us. It can't come where we're going." The chirp slows. Slows. Steadies. By the next block she has herself back, mostly, in the wrung-out way a kid does after the worst of it. "You're hurt," she says, very quietly. "I'm fine," he says. It is the first time he has lied to her.

[Beat 23]
Twenty-one minutes, and the mirrors have stayed empty since the stand at the viaduct. What he has not lost is the Loop. The Loop is the one part of the city no corponation can switch off: too public, too packed, the beating heart every zone plugs into, so when it goes wrong it goes wrong in the open with a crowd. Tonight it is going wrong. Kyle can hear it from the mouth, the flat clap of small-arms under a crowd-roar, and Imani lifts her head off his back. "Is that the loud," she says, a thread of the thin spot still in it. "Different loud," he says. "Just people." Which is no comfort, but the long way costs eleven minutes he's bleeding through, so he goes in. Halfway down the cathedral-tall dark, full of scrolling ads, a corporate security line has a municipal picket pinned behind an overturned cleaning rig — both sides too far gone to clock a motorcycle until it's the most interesting thing in the room. Kyle thumbs Cacophony to the flash-bang load and puts a round into the ceiling. The tunnel goes white. He does not get through clean: two seconds of blindness is two seconds of frightened people firing at memory, and one of them is unlucky enough to be lucky — a round furrows the meat across the top of his shoulder, not through, a hot graze that nearly tears Cacophony from his grip. He keeps the median, keeps the throttle, and adds the shoulder to the rib — two warm lines he will deal with when dealing with them is survivable. Behind him Imani is watching the rivers of light, not the blood she cannot see. "It's beautiful," she says. Kyle gets his breath back around the burn before he answers, so it lands level. "Don't tell the advertisers," he says. "It'll go to their heads."

[Beat 24]
River North is a thirty-second wedge of gray zone where the Loop's grid runs out of nerve at the river. The bridges are still there — old bascule spans, their bridgehouses squatting at either end like chess rooks gone to rust — but the river hasn't been managed in eighteen years, the lift machinery seized a decade back, and they sit permanently down and half-drowned, Lower Wacker a black canal beneath them where a road used to be. The old Hancock still stands over all of it, dark to the eightieth floor, its X-braces holding up a hundred stories of nothing and a crown of advertising nobody's paid to switch off in years. People live here anyway: laundry strung bridgehouse to bridgehouse, a man fishing the canal off a fixed span with a hand line, a kid working a cooler of something cold along a toll-line of cars that never moves. Imani turns her head to watch the black water slide past. "The street's drowned," she says — not scared, just filing it. "Most of them down here are," Kyle says, and takes the upper deck of the Michigan Avenue span, where Tollgate Systems strung a credit-gate across a public ruin because someone always finds the rent in a thing nobody owns. He does not have a Tollgate badge. He has twelve minutes and an opinion about Tollgate. The barrier is a swinging steel boom; it folds off its hinge without comment, the least dangerous thing to happen to him all night. What he thinks about instead is the camera that reads his face across the span — not the bike, the bike is nobody, it's *him* the system wants — and bills the only identity it can match: a credit account that hasn't existed since 2207, which Kyle built for exactly this kind of evening and then deleted. The invoice will get an automated rejection, the rejection filed, the filing archived, the archive purged in a routine sweep — six weeks and seventeen credits of Tollgate's money to chase a ghost. He laughs, which pulls at the cold line under his coat. "Why are you laughing," Imani says. "Because the most expensive thing I did to them tonight was the paperwork." She says, "Good."

## Streeterville

[Beat 25]
Streeterville is a forest of glass towers in crowns of advertising, and at their feet the brutalist slab of Northwestern Memorial — and tonight the whole district is wall-to-wall people, the strike spilled north into the one part of the city no one gets to lock down. Which is exactly why it's here. Kyle sees the bay's red light six blocks out, and between him and it, wading through the crowd on six legs with the patience of a thing that has nowhere better to be, is an Arcturus crowd-control unit: a crab the size of a panel van, matte riot-grey, a Bulwark. Arcturus builds everything into a crab eventually — low, omnidirectional, impossible to tip — and a crab is what waits at the end of his night. Kyle has put this exact unit down twice. He knows it's the same one because it slows when its optics catch his face, and recalculates, and comes for him first out of a crowd of a thousand easier problems. A machine can't hold a grudge. Kyle stopped believing that the second time.

[Beat 26]
The Bulwark is a crowd control unit — built to work shoulder to shoulder with civilians — so it carries nothing that goes bang: no slugs, no rockets, nothing that doesn't care where it lands. What it has is hands. Tanglefoot, kinetic foam, a sonic throw that turns your inner ear to surf, and four hydraulic claws that fold a person up and hold them for collection. It wants Kyle folded up; it does not care about the kid except as part of the package. That is the opening, and it is a thin one. Kyle frees Imani's hands from his coat without setting her down — she stays on the pillion, she stays with the bike — and pulls two fat cylinders from the bandolier she's carried since the viaduct — the fledglings' own flash and chaff, finally good for something. "It sees in light and hears in sound, and it can't shoot back. Left one's flash — that blinds it. Right one's chaff — that makes it deaf and stupid. Pull the tab, count two, throw at the face." Imani looks at the crab the size of a panel van. Imani looks at the grenades in her hands. "It's a really big crab," she says, in the voice of a kid handed the best and worst job of her life. "It is," Kyle agrees. "Throw at the face."

[Beat 27]
Then it is very loud and very fast. Imani's first flash goes off a half-second early and a yard wide, and the Bulwark flinches anyway, optics whiting out — and Kyle is already moving, not away but under, because the one place a crab cannot reach is beneath its own belly. He lays the bike flat and slings them through the gap between its legs, drawing Silence as the world tips sideways, and opens two leg actuators clean on the way through. The Bulwark drops a quarter of its height with a sound like a building deciding to sit down. Then, for the first time all night, the read fails him. Whatever the thin spot did to it at 35th, the overclock picks now to cash the bill — a red light flares in the corner of his mind's eye, and flares again — and the half-second he has leaned on since Hegewisch does not come. So he does not see the claw. It whips back over the fallen flank, closes on the bandolier across Imani's chest, and tears it loose — her half a foot into the air with it before the strap gives and drops her. Something bigger wanted them, just like he told her it would. But she had pulled the chaff's tab a heartbeat before, and its two-count runs out now, in the crab's own grip: it bursts point-blank in the cluster of sensors the claw reached out with. The legs stop agreeing with one another, hunting for a floor they can't find. Kyle gets her down behind the engine block and puts himself between her and the machine, and finishes the last two actuators the slow way — on grip and memory and the bare animal at the base of the skull. A claw widens the forearm the chain opened; the bad rib tears on the blind pivot; he eats both because he never saw them coming. The Bulwark folds into the intersection, blind and deaf and beached, legs working and going nowhere anyone can use. The bike does not survive the dismount. But the bay's red light is three blocks off, and three blocks is nothing. "Good count," he tells her. Imani — shaking, fierce, wrung all the way out, watching the claw that took her grenades and choked on them — says, "I know."

[Beat 28]
The bike is dead and the bay is three blocks off, and three blocks is nothing; he has bled more than this for less. He sheathes Silence, holsters Cacophony, and picks her up — harness, battery, the whole small weight of her. Imani's hand fists in his collar. "You're hurt," she says again, and this time he doesn't bother with the lie. "Yeah," he says. He carries her. The red light in the distance slowly getting bigger.

[Beat 29]
Vance is already in the lane with the gurney — tall, brown skin, hair tied back, scrubs over a body that has run a lot today — and does not waste a syllable. "On the gurney." Kyle is already lowering her. The harness comes off in two practiced motions. Imani's hand stays in his coat a half-second after the rest of her is on the gurney, and when he pries it loose she asks the question every kid eventually has to put to somebody. "Will I see you again?" Kyle does not lie to her; he has been lied to by kinder people than himself, and it never once helped. "No," he says. "Probably not." She takes it the way she has taken everything tonight — straight, no flinch, filing it somewhere. *Good,* he thinks. Better she learns it now, while she is still young enough for the scar to set smooth: that sometimes the person who comes for you is a stranger, who does the thing and is gone. He learned it late, and it set crooked. "Thank you for the loud," she says. "Anytime, kid," he says — the gentlest lie he has told all night, and the only one she lets pass. "Tell the heart I said hi."

[Beat 30]
Vance reads the brace, the wires, the cooler — then reads *him*: the hand pressed to his side, the dark spreading at the forearm and the shoulder, the boot that won't take full weight. "Forty-one minutes," he says. "I had you at fifty-five." It is an old argument between them; he's put Kyle back together before, and they both already know how the next part goes. Kyle says, "I cheated." He says, "You're bleeding into my ambulance lane. Stay. Half the people in my bay tonight are in better shape than you." Kyle is already turning away. "Don't worry about me," he says. "I'll just rub some dirt in it."

[Beat 31]
He walks. The bay door drops behind him and its red light goes white, which is the hospital's way of saying they have her, which is the only confirmation he was ever going to get that it worked. No alarm follows him out. No name. Whatever he tore open to get her here has already closed behind him, seamless, like it was never there.

[Beat 32]
He turns south, on foot, the long way, because south is no longer urgent and there's no bike left to make it quick. The read has come back — slow and shaky, but back — and that is the good news. The white that rode the edges of his sight all night is pulling back now, the bleed at his nose gone tacky and dry; the body, at least, has stopped sending its bills for the evening. The bad news keeps its own count. Where a civilian rig would show a battery bar, the build in his skull shows one small light in the corner of his mind's eye, and every time he runs it past the red, the light goes red with it — one flash, the man who seeded it in a child too young to be asked once told him, for about a year off the far end of his life. He does not look at it during the work; looking never helped. He looks now, and counts what he can stand to of tonight's — the kill-team, the gate, the thin spot, the crab — and quits, because it is the kind of number a man spends his life not thinking about, and he just spent a fistful of it on a stranger's kid. He files it under the cost of the evening and finds — the part that should frighten him and doesn't — that he would pay it again. She'll forget his name by spring. He's counting on it. He keeps a hand pressed to his ribs and does the thing he taught her to do in the dark — names the next ordinary thing, and the next: the curb, the corner, the long block south — until the red light is gone behind him and the street is just a street again.
