# Managed Liability

*Ciro's afternoon appointment. Bone razor on the counter. Amara leaves the reader case locked.*

She had fifty-three minutes between the compliance flag and the meeting.

She used twelve of them at the depot, logging the morning visit under the standard equipment anomaly form. The form had eleven fields. Field seven was NATURE OF ANOMALY. She wrote *Portable reader — secondary exception during sync, data routing issue, may require calibration.* This was technically accurate. She did not write: *Cellvault's security layer caught a strip attempt and filed two entries in a compliance queue. I know what compliance queues do.*

She used the remaining forty-one minutes in transit.

Ciro's office was on the ninth floor of the Cellvault administrative block, which was inside the arcology, which meant the air was cedar and processing and the window behind his desk showed a projected view of the lakefront at early evening — not the current sky, the ideal sky, the one Orison's ambient team had decided best represented productive work. A client chair sat at the correct angle to the desk. The desk had nothing on it that shouldn't be there.

She sat in the client chair.

The razor was on the corner cabinet. Bone handle, fixed blade, laid along the edge of a small towel — the setup she had seen twice before, always when he took a meeting after a workout: he showered, dressed, finished the shave at the office. The blade had a matte finish that meant it had never left the Orison catalog. She had catalogued the razor the same way she catalogued calibration drift: presence, frequency, pattern.

He came in with two coffees and set one in front of her without asking. He had been doing this for six months; her preference was in his system.

"The morning appointment," he said, sitting down. "Dr. Soral's visit went well?"

"Routine," she said. "Tissue stable, follow-up scheduled."

He nodded. He was looking at her the way he always looked at her in these meetings — not searching, not suspicious, just attending. He had a way of making attention feel like good manners.

"The compliance flag." He opened the case file on his screen and turned it to face her. "A routing exception during sync. Two log entries. Secondary system alert." He said it the way you read a list of things that are merely facts. "Do you want to walk me through what happened?"

"The portable reader threw an exception. It was inside the client visit — I was syncing the session data, the routing didn't complete." She kept her hands in her lap, both of them. She did not reach for the reader case. "I documented the anomaly this morning."

"You did." He looked at the form. "I appreciate the prompt filing." He turned the screen back. "The routing issue isn't the flag. The secondary system alert is — field seven routing exceptions under 7.4 go to the compliance queue, not the standard log. That's an automated trigger."

She waited.

"The queue is reviewed on Fridays," he said. "I pulled yours this morning instead. I wanted to understand what I'd be looking at before the review cycle." He picked up his coffee. "The flag says your personal device was connected during the session. That's unusual."

"I had a patient data form open. Cross-reference habit." She said it without emphasis — the same voice she used for every visit note. "I know that's a 7.4 restriction. I'll correct the behavior."

"No harm done." He set the coffee down. "The session data is intact, the client visit is documented cleanly. One flag, pulled before the cycle, no escalation." He said it with the tone of a problem that was already resolved. "I wanted to make sure you knew where it had gone."

There was a pause that she understood was for her to feel reassured. She nodded.

He reached for the razor, unhurried. He drew it across his jaw twice — short strokes, the razor held at the same angle both times, a movement his hands knew without thought. He wiped the blade.

"There's an internal access review next month," he said. "Routine. Sub-contractor portal permissions get audited, outdated tickets get closed." He set the razor down. "I flagged your open permissions ticket — the one from October — so it won't come up in the audit without context. It's logged as a live work item."

She said: "I appreciate that."

"Of course." He picked up his coffee again. "If you need anything from me before the review, let me know directly." He looked at the screen with the measured interest of someone checking whether the conversation was finished. "Anything else on the morning visits?"

"Nothing that isn't in the log."

He smiled — brief, real, the right kind — and stood.

She stood. She picked up her bag. She did not pick up the reader case, because the reader case was under the chair and she did not need it to leave the room, and reaching for it now would look like remembering to reach for it, which was the wrong thing to look like.

She left the reader case under the chair.

She said she'd get it next week. He said not to worry, the secure cabinet was right there. She said thank you. She went out.

As she reached the door, he said: "One more thing. While the October ticket is under review — I've scoped your access down to Level 2 for now. Just to keep things clean for the audit. It won't affect your standard visits. Should clear up in two weeks."

She turned around. "Of course," she said. "That makes sense."

"Good." He was already looking at his screen.

In the elevator she stood with her back against the wall and kept her hands still. She opened her access queue on her personal device.

*Level 3 sub-contractor access: suspended. Reason: open ticket review. Expected resolution: 14 days.*

Level 3 was the clinical infrastructure layer. The batch manifest records lived at Level 3. The comparative sync logs. The secondary exception queue where a pattern would show up if you ran the right search across enough visits.

She could no longer run that search.

He had pulled the flag before the review cycle. He had brought it to her as a small favor — *I protected you from this thing you didn't know was coming* — and then, at the door, when the meeting was already over and she had already thanked him, he had taken something from her in the same even voice.

The elevator opened on the lobby. She walked through it and came out into the corridor.

Left was the depot. Right was Zone 6, the transit line, the relay shop near the Seam crossing where a person could send a message that Cellvault's security layer would not log.

She turned left.

She walked three blocks toward the depot. Then she stopped at a corner and stood for a moment, one hand on the strap of her bag, looking at the corridor ahead.

She turned around and went right.

The relay shop was three blocks past the Zone 6 boundary marker. She found a terminal in the back row — legacy hardware, wire input, the kind of machine that didn't register the sender. She opened the channel and built the message to Seto by hand: the compliance flag, the 7.4 trigger, the access revocation, the October ticket, two weeks.

She sent it and walked back toward the depot.

The reader case was still in Ciro's office. Level 3 access was suspended. She had two weeks to find another way in.
