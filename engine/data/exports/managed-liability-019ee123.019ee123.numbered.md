# Managed Liability

*Ciro calls Amara in. He has her compliance log entries. He handles it personally.*

He was finishing a note when she arrived. He looked up, thanked her for coming, and gestured at the chair across from his desk in a way that made it clear she had been expected exactly at this moment. She sat down.

His office was on the fourteenth floor — no window, which was the tell. The rooms with windows went to client-facing personnel. Ciro worked entirely on internal matters. His desk held a sealed envelope, a bone-handled straight razor, and a desk reader with her session timestamp visible at the top of the page.

She spoke before he opened the file.

"I know why I'm here. I sent a session file to an external address without pre-authorization, and I used a stripping application when the direct route was blocked. I thought the session data fell into the same category as personal wellness records if I was the attending tech. I was wrong about that, and I should have checked the policy boundary before I acted on that assumption."

He looked at her for a moment. Then he picked up the straight razor and opened the sealed envelope — a clean, practiced movement — and set the razor back. He took out a single sheet of paper and read it while she waited.

"The policy language is imprecise on that boundary," he said, without looking up from the sheet. "I've flagged it internally before." He set the paper down, and now he did open the file on his desk reader. "You had two alerts. The first at 09:14, the second at 09:17. The second triggered an additional flag because of the stripping application, which is what made this worth a conversation rather than a standard notice."

"I understand."

"Good." He looked up. "I'm going to record both as inadvertent policy misapplication. That closes them." He made a note on the paper pad beside his reader — fountain pen, handwriting she had never been able to read across the desk. "Was the session you were trying to archive anything I should be aware of? Any concern about the client's progress?"

She put her hand on the reader case in her lap. Theodora's session data was in her local file — the clean version and the layer underneath it. She could open the case and put the tablet on his desk. Show him the motor pathways losing integration since October, the gap between intention and execution that the system had been noting and then not noting. She could show him all of it.

His file was already closed. His questions had already been chosen. He was looking at her with an expression that had no gap in it — not expectant, not suspicious, just present — waiting for her to confirm what he had already decided to record.

She took her hand off the case.

"No," she said. "Routine follow-up. The client's progressing as expected."

"Good." He capped the pen. He stood, which was how he ended meetings — cleanly, without ceremony. "Take care of yourself, Amara."

She said she would.

---

The elevator carried her down fourteen floors through managed air. In the lobby, the desk attendant who hadn't looked up when she arrived was eating the last of the pastry. The news feed had moved to a wildfire evacuation update in a coastal corridor she didn't follow.

She walked out through the air-rights boundary and the Seam hit her sinuses immediately: diesel, a food cart running its exhaust into the unfiltered gap between two corporate zones, the relay mast above the Cellvault hub cycling its test signal. She stood on the pavement.

He had closed both entries. He had not asked why the strip application was already loaded on her personal device. He had not asked why she had spoken first — why she had walked into the meeting with an explanation already prepared, as if she had spent the morning rehearsing it. He had not asked about the photograph.

He had called her by her first name at the end, which he had not done in two years.

She turned left out of the building instead of right. Right was the transit pod back to Zone 6. Left was the Seam boundary into Zone 7 — Seto's routes, six facilities, Tuesday morning windows. She had never gone to Zone 7 without a reason.

She walked until she found a licensed relay shop in the Seam commercial corridor — the kind that carried pre-paid comm cards for gray-zone operators, not logged through any corporate network — and bought one.

She stood outside on the Seam pavement, the card in her hand, and sent Seto a message through a channel Cellvault had no record of.

*I need to meet you. In person.*
