[Beat 1]
# Pressure and Counter-Pressure

*Seto runs a Zone 7 route and finds a new checkpoint procedure at one of the six facilities. He can't read Amara's message until he's clear. The number is 112.*

The new checkpoint was at the receiving bay on his second Zone 7 stop.

Not a new guard. A new process: a secondary scan of the delivery packet before it could be signed off, running through a reader that was newer than anything else in the bay by a factor of at least three years. The reader had an Orison NeoCortex branding plate on the casing. It had not been there on his last run through.

The guard — a woman whose name was printed on a badge he'd seen every delivery for two years — fed the packet through the reader and her jaw tightened for a moment while it processed, the way someone's jaw tightens when they've been told not to answer questions about something. She handed the packet back, ran the sign-off, said nothing about the reader. He signed the confirmation form. He did not ask.

He rode the next leg with the pre-paid card in his jacket pocket. He reached for it once, at the end of the facility block, and stopped himself — the monitoring radius covered approximately three blocks in every direction from the receiving bay, and the relay beacon timing would shift if he opened a gray-zone channel inside it. He rode out of the radius before he stopped.

The message was twelve words: *Found 112 gaps across all zones. Not just our batch. The paper's in my pocket.*

He read it twice. He put the phone in his pocket and sat still for a moment on the bicycle, which was something he never did mid-route.

He was parked beside a relay box on the Zone 5/7 boundary, the bicycle balanced between his knees, watching the Seam traffic work past him. A vendor was arguing with someone about the price of something. A child in a yellow jacket was walking a dog that wanted to go in the opposite direction. The relay box was cycling through its hourly test, the activity indicator making its small green pulse, one per second.

One hundred and twelve.

He had read the manifest by zone and facility. He knew every name in Batch 44-C. If the pattern extended across every batch — if the 112 gaps represented 112 instances of the same suppression protocol running across different batches — then the 847 names he'd read were not the total. They were the fraction he happened to have documentation for. Everyone who had been calibrated at these facilities in the relevant window. Every person whose record had a managed gap in it that they would never see.

He let out a slow breath. The child and the dog had reached some kind of compromise. The relay box pulsed.

He rode the remaining two Zone 7 stops. The receiving bays at both facilities had Orison readers of the older model — the ones that had been there for years. He checked each one without appearing to check. No new equipment.

Just the one.

The new reader was at Facility 4, which was on the eastern side of Zone 7, close to the Zone 6 boundary. It was the facility with the shortest line from Orison's primary calibration center.

He reached his apartment at 17:40. The three drives were in the lockbox under the floor panel near the radiator. He had not moved them in six days. He opened the lockbox, looked at the drives, closed it again. His hands did not shake. He noted this because he had expected otherwise.

He wrote the number on the inside of his left wrist with a pen. Not for permanence — the number was already permanent, was already the only thing in his head. For clarity. *112.* Small, near the pulse point.

He sat with it for an hour.

Then he opened the pre-paid card and sent to Amara's channel: *Same location. Tomorrow. Same time.* He considered adding: *Are you still clear?* He did not. If she wasn't clear she wouldn't answer, and if she answered she was clear.

The relay beacons outside his window were cycling through their hourly test. He had lived in this apartment for four years and he knew their schedule without checking: 18:00 cycle, 19:00, then an extended pause before the 22:00 maintenance window. Tonight the 18:00 cycle ran. Then the 19:00.

At 20:14 he heard a third cycle. Nothing in the beacon schedule called for a 20:14 cycle.

He went to the window. The relay box on the corner was running its activity indicator in the standard pattern. He counted the seconds between pulses. Eleven. The standard was eight.

He pulled on his jacket and went downstairs. He rode to the relay box two blocks east and counted again. Eight seconds. Standard.

Back at the corner box: eleven. The deviation was localized. Someone had parked a monitoring node inside the relay infrastructure of his block, specific to this address.

He went back upstairs. He moved the drives from the floor panel to the inside pocket of his courier bag. He did not go back to sleep for a long time after that. He lay listening to the relay beacons outside doing something they should not be doing, eleven seconds between each pulse, steady as a clock.
