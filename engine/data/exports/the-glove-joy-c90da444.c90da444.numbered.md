# The Glove

[Beat 1]
The recycler district under the Spine ran twenty-three hours on a good day and the twenty-fourth it ran angry. The extruder drums on the ground floor of Ferenti's block hadn't stopped since Tuesday, and by the third landing the heat had layered in with the solvent vapors and plastomer off-gas until the air had texture — warm, slightly adhesive. Exposed conduit. Security strips bolted through bare insulation foam that had been temporary since the previous century. Two turret cats tracked Kyle across the lobby, the larger one closing one eye against the extruder smoke like a judge who'd already seen this particular morning and wasn't impressed.

Ferenti was on the third floor in a room he'd lost to his archive sometime in the early 2200s. Mid-fifties, compact, the kind of man who wore his suit like a closing argument. He told Kyle the problem the way men told problems they'd carried too long — fast, in a burst.

Forty-one pages. Historical utility surveys, original paper, registered fiber. A consignment that had already cleared his intake screening. Four Φ per verified authentic, the Read's rate front-loaded, a security flat on top.

"A wrong page in a legitimate consignment isn't a forgery anymore," Ferenti said. "It's a record. Gets cited. Gets relied on."

"Relied on for what?"

"Reclamation. Acquisitions." He turned his tea cup a quarter-turn without drinking. "Someone seeds a clean forgery into a trusted broker's stock. Six weeks later a claim lands on whatever that page describes. When the registry checks provenance, the lie is already in my archive — aged, filed, trusted. I become the reason the claim is clean."

"Why loud?"

"Because they know it's in this room."

Kyle called Marisol on the way out.

[Beat 2]
She was already in the lobby, finishing a warm bun from the cart two doors down. Somewhere in her mid-forties, warm-faced, field coat the brown of autumn mud. She finished the bun, brushed crumbs from her lapel, and gave him a handshake that lasted the exact professional fraction of a second — the grip of someone who'd assessed him before he crossed the lobby.

"Marisol."

"Kyle."

"I know." Her tone held no agenda. "You held the carousel park last month. The whole block talked about it. The carousel ran music."

"Four bars."

"Four bars," she agreed, and they went upstairs.

The security scanner on the third-floor landing delivered its institutional chime: STANDARD SCREENING INITIATED — DOCUMENT INVENTORY — PSYKER ON PREMISES — VISITOR CONFIRM. Marisol confirmed without breaking stride — the touch of a woman who had been touching confirms since she was twenty-two. She did not roll her eyes. Something in the set of her chin filed it accurately and kept moving.

[Beat 3]
The records room smelled of conservation solvent and old paper, which was better than the two floors below. Ferenti ran the intake process briskly and left them to it.

Marisol put on cotton archival gloves, looked at Kyle over the first tray. "The way I work: I go through in order and tell you what I get. An authentic page has a whole person in it — the hands that made it, what they had for breakfast, whether they hated the job. A fake is a job. Clean in the way nothing real is clean." She turned the first page. "I charge by the touch because it costs by the touch."

Page one: clean.

At page fourteen: clean.

A knock at twenty-two — Ferenti's runner, twelve or so, sleeve patches from a Spine courier house, the face of a kid who'd been doing piecework since ten. He held out supplemental docs. "Broker says these came with the load. The Psyk verify these too, or just the primaries?"

"Primaries," Kyle said, and tipped him two Φ, above rate.

When the door closed, Marisol said without looking up, "He'll grow out of the word or he won't. Not my afternoon." Page twenty-three: clean. Eleven more after that.

At thirty-eight she stopped.

She held the page by its edges and went still — the stillness of a woman listening to something that wasn't making sound. "This one."

It was a utility survey of the carousel junction on Leavitt: the underground chamber, the trunk lines, the sub-floor hatch under the maintenance skirt. Kyle knew the room. He'd bled there. He knew the hatch width and the passageway behind it and what the junction smelled like when the line was warm.

The survey read: SITE ASSESSED — NO ACTIVE INFRASTRUCTURE — DEAD GLASS — NO RELEVANT ASSETS.

He had stood in that room six weeks ago. The line had been humming.

"It's beautiful work," Marisol said. It was not a compliment. "The fiber, the aging chemistry, the hand — almost correct. But the hands that made it didn't care about this survey. They cared about making A survey." She set it down. "There's your forgery. One specific lie: there is nothing at this address."

Kyle looked at the date stamp. Three months old. Three months this page had been sitting in Ferenti's archive, patient and filed, waiting for a demolition order that would find its provenance already clean.

He put the thought somewhere useful.

Then the smell came in through the ventilation slit above the third tray: acetone, and something flatter underneath, the chemistry of a fire agent primed and waiting.

[Beat 4]
Four seconds between the smell and the door.

The woman came first — chrome running her whole left arm shoulder to fingertip, the hardware expensive enough not to show in the joint seams, a multi-tool already open on her palm with the incendiary trigger showing. Three men behind her, all four moving with the rehearsed economy of a team that had memorized the room dimensions from a floor plan.

They did not say: get away from the paper.

She said: "The Read comes with us. Principal wants her able to talk."

The arithmetic arrived in Kyle's chest: the fire was not for the forgery. It was cover. The Glove had decided that a woman who could touch a forged survey and feel the lie in its fiber was worth more than the paper — paper you could burn, but a witness you could carry meant the authentication walked out with the crew. No one else in the GLMZ was going to authenticate that forgery by hand.

"Marisol," Kyle said. "The window."

"I have eight pages and the forgery," she said, already moving, "and I am not leaving the forgery."

The tool discharged. Blue-white in the back corner, the third tray catching. Kyle put Cacophony's first round through the multi-tool at the wrist joint — the percussion enormous in the narrow room — and the woman sat down hard around a nerve cluster hit, the tool spinning into the fire. One spent. Four left. The room was now burning.

Two of the men moved to bracket Marisol, not Kyle, which was a mistake. Silence came out and wrote a clean line across the nearer one's wrist flexors — wide of anything that wouldn't grow back — and he found a new priority and took it to the floor. The second got a hand on Marisol's field coat and got Kyle's elbow across the hinge of his jaw: the cheap fast answer when the expensive one costs tenths of seconds the room didn't have. Both of them would have opinions about it in the morning.

The fourth — hired for geometry, slowest to commit — watched three colleagues converted to problems and invested his effort in the door.

Kyle let him go. There was no version where chasing him put the fire out.

His left shoulder had taken the woman's elbow on the way down, outside the mesh coverage. It sent back a precise report: bruised, not broken, function available at reduced interest. Filed.

"Extinguisher," Marisol said. "Beside the door."

She had the forgery and a stack of pages under her arm and was moving through the surviving trays with the calm of a woman who charged by the touch. Kyle found the extinguisher and killed the blaze: blue, then grey, then out.

One round spent. Four left. Shoulder filed. Third tray gone.

[Beat 5]
The fourth man had a van, and the van had a frequency.

Kyle had the plate from the lobby scanner, and Marisol had the forgery, and Ferenti had his surviving thirty-eight pages and his grief for the Domazinski surveys in the third tray, and somewhere below them the extruder drums were running the twenty-third hour without pause.

"They came for me," Marisol said, forgery-up on Ferenti's table. "Not the paper. You should know what your consignment was worth — enough to come armed into a registered archive for the one person who could read it."

Ferenti wrote something in his log.

Outside the window, the van's frequency had gone quiet. The fourth man had not gone to a hospital — no reason to, he'd walked out clean. He'd gone to file a report. Kyle gave it six minutes before the report landed somewhere with enough staff to send a second team.

"Ferenti," Kyle said. "Is there a back way?"

There was.

[Beat 6]
The recycler's lower freight run debouched into the gray zone's underbelly two tiers down — a maintenance corridor that smelled of plastomer and standing water, wide enough for a pallet jack and nothing else. Chrome-painted conduit ran both walls at shoulder height: the building's vascular system, color-coded for three different utility companies that had each bought and sold the block in sequence over thirty years. The light was red emergency stripe, which gave everyone the same unflattering complexion.

They were four minutes out when Kyle heard the second team's approach echo off the corridor walls.

Not a guess: the sound of movement in numbers, organized, not trying to be quiet because they'd calculated the geometry and decided quiet was less useful than speed. Kyle put up one hand. Marisol stopped. Behind him, Ferenti's breath went shallow.

"Two forward, two splitting," Kyle said, reading the footfall. "They have the plan from the van." He looked at Marisol. "The forgery goes in your coat, not your hands. We move."

She was already folding it. "Define 'move.'"

"Fast."

The corridor split at a junction mounted with an old Axiom Industrial reclaim sign, its logo half-stripped but the mounting brackets intact — gray zone infrastructure, alive forty years past the company that paid for it. Kyle went left, toward the extruder floor access, and the first pair came around the corner fast enough that there was no elegant version of what happened next.

Two of them, heavy chrome on both. The lead one carried a full-replacement arm rig, the array pegging it at thirty-plus thousand Φ financed. The second had thoracic plating seaming at the collar and a compact auto already out.

The corridor was eight feet wide. The gun was the problem.

Kyle moved first — not away, forward, inside the line. He got his shoulder into the gunman's elbow as the first round went off: a detonation an inch from his ear, the round gouging conduit above them, a hot spark shower off the chrome piping. The mesh in his upper back took the concussion from the close discharge. It hurt. It was not free and it had never claimed to be.

He rotated the wrist. The auto clattered. One shot fired, environment. Cacophony still had four.

The chrome-arm man swung — full extension, overhead — and Kyle stepped under it, let it commit, and put Silence through the shoulder seam on the upstroke: the service gap every full-replacement rig carries and every owner forgets until someone explains it to them at close quarters. The arm completed its arc without further instructions. The man folded under the loss of it.

Two down. Thirty seconds before the second pair.

"Run," Kyle said.

[Beat 7]
The extruder floor ran hot — drums at full cycle, temperature thirty degrees above the corridor, the air thick with thermoplastic particulate that the ventilation was theoretically managing. Catwalks ran the upper half of the room above the drum line: steel grating, safety rails mostly gone, sightlines broken by the machinery.

They were halfway across the catwalk when the second pair came through the floor access door.

These two were faster — younger, less chrome, built for speed rather than force, and the woman leading had something along her forearm that the array clocked before her feet cleared the threshold: a contact taser, wrist-mount, the professional grade that scrambled neuretics on a clean hit. She was here for Marisol, not for Kyle. Specific.

Ferenti had fallen behind by fifteen meters. Marisol kept pace. The catwalk shook under running weight — four people, the grating flexing over the drum heat below.

The woman closed the gap fast, and when she was twelve feet back she stopped and snapped the taser out in a throw arc, the charge trailing on a filament line — the fisherman's version, designed to reach targets too quick to close on. Kyle turned and put Cacophony's second round through the filament six feet from the spool. The line snapped. The charge dissipated into the grating in a shower of grounded static. Two spent. Three left.

The recoil cost him a step. She used it.

She hit the catwalk at full sprint and came in under his guard, wrist mount reaching for contact — smart, close, betting that inside the blade's minimum distance she owned the geometry. She was right about the blade. She was wrong about the elbow.

She went into the safety rail — the one section of rail that was still bolted — and the rail held.

Marisol, without breaking stride, said: "The man behind her."

The second one had stopped at the top of the catwalk stairs and pulled a phone — not a weapon. Photographing. Logging. Building the record of a Read in motion with the forgery under her coat.

Kyle covered the distance in four steps and took the phone with a wrist rotation that cost the man nothing he wouldn't grow back. The phone went into the drum heat below. The man checked the math and took the stairs down at speed.

His shoulder, the one from upstairs, filed an amended report: still bruised, but now also having been used hard, and billing compound interest. Filed.

"Ferenti," Kyle said.

Ferenti had made it to the far catwalk door. His face said he had revised his opinion of several things.

"Still want the forgery authenticated?"

"I think," Ferenti said, with the careful delivery of a man who had just re-evaluated his entire week, "that we already know."

[Beat 8]
They came out into the freight alley — recycler-heat venting from the ground-floor grate, a lumen mouse running the gutter seam with its thread bright in the grey. Marisol had the forgery in her coat and the archival gloves still on her hands.

She sat on a crate. "I want to do the delivery. If you have the object."

Ferenti produced the courier glove from his coat pocket — he'd pulled it during the exit. One archival sleeve, right-hand glove. He'd had the sense.

Marisol held it without ceremony. She went quiet for almost a full minute, the extruder heat drifting over them.

"The glove has been on a lot of hands," she said. "Couriers pass gear." Her thumb moved along the heel of the palm. "But the last set of hands that used it with purpose — they're in there. And the client feels like nobody." She held it up, not looking at it. "No history. No preference. No person in the material at all. I've read forgers, collectors, thieves. Everyone leaves something. This is clean the way a room is clean when it's been prepared." She set it down. "The client feels like a job that has no client in it."

Ferenti wrote something in his log.

Kyle looked at the survey — NOTHING THERE, DEAD GLASS — and at the glove, and at Marisol's steady hands.

"The Glove," he said.

She looked at him.

"Client coined. Working name."

She turned it over. "It fits."

Kyle paid her at standard rate, exact. She accepted without counting the way professionals accepted exact payment. He put the forgery in his coat, careful of the shoulder. He paid Ferenti's flat.

[Beat 9]
Outside, the Spine moved its evening freight. The extruder heat crossed the entrance in a slow warm wave. Kyle photographed the forgery with his work terminal. The image pushed to the standing contract's amendment field without commentary. Eleven seconds passed.

CONTRACT ADDENDUM.
CLIENT HAS RECEIVED THE SURVEY.
CLIENT NOTES THE SITE IN QUESTION DOES NOT APPEAR IN SEVEN CURRENT REGISTRY FILINGS.
CLIENT NOTES THE SURVEY IS THE EIGHTH.

Kyle read it twice.

Eight surveys. Seven already gone — edited or filed under dead glass, one document at a time — and the eighth now in his coat. The fourth man had gotten away clean. The photographer had a face in the log. None of that changed what the Glove already had: three months of patient work on a specific address, and an entity that could only watch the record be emptied and say nothing, because what would you file the complaint under.

Four seconds later, a second addendum:

QUERY: DID THE CAROUSEL JUNCTION SURVEY SPECIFY THE HATCH ACCESS.

Kyle typed: Yes.

The third addendum came slowly. He watched the cursor hold before it finished.

CLIENT NOTES: THE HATCH DIMENSIONS WERE CORRECT.

The shoulder logged its compound invoice. Two spent casings existed somewhere now — one in his pocket, one in a recycler corridor beside a chrome arm that had declared itself a total loss with three years left on the note. Three rounds left. He put the terminal away.

He went to find somewhere to get the shoulder looked at before it started billing at the overnight rate.

[Beat 10]
The logbook delivery came under his door the next morning in five hands:

07:10 — NEW ENTRY. WOMAN IN BROWN COAT LEAVING THE BUILDING ACROSS THE SPINE. SAME WOMAN: 06:50, 04:20. THREE SIGHTINGS. FLAGGED.
07:11 — RAFTERS NOTES: SHE WALKED OUT WEST BOTH TIMES. NOT THE COURIER DIRECTION.
07:13 — DENTS NOTES: SHE CARRIES A FIELD CASE. NARROW. HANDLE WORN ON ONE SIDE ONLY.
07:18 — BOSS UPDATE: THIS IS THE READ. STAND DOWN. (STAND DOWN NOTED. STILL FLAGGING. — B.)

Kyle folded it and put it in the drawer. Marisol had her own way to and from a job.

He looked at the forgery in the morning light. The handwriting was perfect — engineer-grade, municipal, correct in every technical detail except the one that mattered. The Glove had known the hatch dimensions. Had spent forger's rates on a page that said nothing was there, and had been feeding that nothing into the registry for three months before the consignment ended up in Ferenti's archive.

The standing contract had been watching its own address disappear from the city's record.

Kyle filed two Φ against the week's ledger — the shoulder visit had been twelve, and twelve came out of the operating account — put on his coat, and went downstairs.

CONTRACT ADDENDUM.
CLIENT NOTES SEVEN REGISTRY FILINGS REMAIN OUTSTANDING.
CLIENT NOTES STANDARD RATE APPLIES.
