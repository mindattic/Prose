# Interference Pattern

_Amara receives the first ambient fragment — not a voice, not a thought, something between._

[Beat 1]
Her body knew the route before she did. Diesel, wet stone, the cold-metal tang of condensate off infrastructure left alone long enough to develop a personality. A two-block gap where neither company managed the air — produce sellers under tarps, a woman behind a card table weighting her laminated price list against the wind with two dead phone batteries. Trade that happened in the open because no corpo held jurisdiction over the dirt it stood on. The smell of cooking oil, someone's boots on corrugated stairs two floors above. She pulled into the loading margin before she'd decided to.

Zone 7/5 Seam. She had worked it for three years. She had never once looked at the battery-swap station on the north corner and seen a choke point, never read the signage as cover, never clocked the relay beacon's thirty-second cycle as a gap-window. A transit corridor with two viable approach vectors and a single choke point. The beacon pulsed through the interchange.

She saw it now, all of it, laid out clean. It took her three seconds to register she was thinking in courier terms. Then it took her a fourth second to register that three seconds was already too long.

She filed it alongside Fragment 10 and put the van back in gear. Today's session with Berenice would be her fifth visit. She had reached 0.9 millimeters every time. She wanted 1.9.

---

Inside the Orison campus the air was twenty-one degrees and would not move off it.

At the front desk, a man was sorting something without looking up. She had been here four times this month. The third time she had stopped and asked whether the temperature was set — just making conversation, a tech passing through. He had told her the campus environment was dynamically optimized for resident well-being, delivering the phrase with the slight over-precision of someone who had said it so many times it had stopped meaning anything to him.

She passed the desk without stopping this time.

The air was the same each visit: twenty-one degrees, handled, arriving through ducts she couldn't locate from any surface she could see. The walls were warm umbers and soft napped textures, surfaces chosen by someone whose entire job was to manufacture the feeling of being safe. She knew this because the feeling worked on her anyway. Every time. Even now, knowing the mechanism, she felt her shoulders come down off her ears in the lobby.

She adjusted the reader case strap on her shoulder without thinking about it.

She stopped at the concourse junction and took the corridor that led past the residential terrace. Same route every time. The deliberate long way. No clocks. She had been inside twenty minutes before she caught herself reading the building's light to find the hour — and finding nothing, because the light came from no source she could point to, uniform and golden, the same at nine in the morning as it would be at four in the afternoon. The campus ran on a schedule. The schedule was simply not hers to read.

The news concourse was running. Zone 8 flooding: aerial footage, a collapsed distribution hub, brown water standing in a corridor that looked like every corridor. A number on the lower third — fourteen hundred residents evacuated. Berenice shifted in the chair beside her as the number appeared, one hand going briefly to the armrest before it released. Cut to an Orison spokesperson, composed, lit warm: *We are monitoring the situation, and our hearts go out to those affected.* Then a card, white sans-serif on deep blue: **RESILIENCE, INSIDE.** Then, without a beat between them, an ad for the restaurant on forty-one, blue ambient light and a reservation link.

She watched the cut happen and understood it cleanly. Here is the disaster. Here is the other CorpoNation that failed the people who trusted it. Here is what to want instead. The whole lesson ran in thirty-two seconds, and she took it in the same way she took in the managed air.

Her client was Berenice. Seventy-three, third generation inside the campus. Berenice's mother had arrived in 2194. Amara staged the calibration probe while she read the intake form, the portable reader pairing itself to the desktop in the corner. Berenice had been born in an Orison clinic in 2201, had worked here and married here and raised two children here.

"Raised two children and never left," Berenice said, not looking up from the compliance form. "People think that's strange."

She had left the campus twenty-two times in her life. She could name each occasion, the reason for it, and what the outside air had smelled like on each one.

"You look tired," Berenice said.

"Zone crossings," Amara said. Mesh depth set to 0.3 — starting shallow, building down by tenths. Berenice's neuretics were the cleanest tissue Amara had ever worked. Not because Berenice was careful with them. Because they had never been asked to do anything hard.

She had read the external contractor service agreement before her first visit. Session equipment data was logged to contractor performance records. Orison QA could request a full review — equipment state, session metadata, reader output — at any time and for any reason.

Berenice adjusted her position in the chair, the compliance form folded neatly on her knee.

---

By 0.9mm she had what she needed for the correction. She spent three seconds looking at the depth indicator.

On her third visit she had caught a 0.003-second discontinuity at depth — the kind of gap that only deliberate work produced, the same signature she'd found in Seto's relay log. Someone had reached into the mesh at that depth and cleaned something out of it. She had designed this session to reach 1.9mm and hold it long enough to characterize the source frequency before it could clear itself again.

She knew the session metadata was logged. She knew what Orison QA did with depth deltas outside protocol range. She turned the wheel toward 1.3 anyway.

At 1.4 millimeters she found it.

Not the bearing housing this time. Not a motion at all. A *state.* A particular quality of attention she had never trained into herself and could not have produced on purpose — an awareness that read the corridor beyond the half-open door as a set of vectors, that rated the exits without hunting for a sign. There were no exit signs in the Orison corridors. There were never exit signs; you were not meant to need one. For perhaps fifteen seconds she became someone who knew where the exits were in any room she walked into.

Berenice was saying something about the flooding. Was it getting worse, did Amara think?

"The grid has redundancy," Amara said.

Her voice came out level. She did not know where she had gotten the steadiness from.

The state was still running under her hands — deeper than her hands, behind her eyes. The kind of attention that walks through a space tasting it for danger, tasting the corridor and getting a clean read: single egress, foot traffic light and consistent, no observation hardware in view. She had never once in her professional life assessed a room this way. She was doing it now with the same idle automation she'd use to check whether a burner was off.

She turned the depth wheel toward 1.9. Deliberate. If she could hold it there long enough — attach it to a source frequency, get enough signal to file it as something more than *anomalous awareness state, unclassified* — she'd finally have something she could work with.

The corridor filled. Not her vision. His. Exit signs that weren't there, load-bearing walls assessed by mass, the freight elevator's hum indexing in her inner ear as an asset, foot traffic resolving into gaps she could move through. A complete operational read of a space she'd entered thirty minutes ago, and it was still running, deepening —

A knock.

Her hand was already on the wheel before the door finished opening — she pulled it back to 1.0mm in a single motion, the reader alarming softly at the unauthorized depth drop. Her own visual field returned in stages: client in the chair, screen, two columns of numbers she needed to read before she could speak.

An Orison staff member stood in the frame, tablet held against her chest rather than extended. Her eyes went to Berenice first before finding Amara. "Technician Osei? This is your three-hour." The slight apologetic uptick on the last syllable, the tone of someone who knows she is interrupting something.

"Thank you," she said. "We're finishing up."

The door closed.

Depth error: 0.4 millimeters, posterior associative, self-corrected. Client unharmed.

She looked at *unharmed* and let it stand. Five sessions. The extraction had not completed once.

She reached for the reader's sync button. Paused. The depth delta was in the transmission log — four tenths below protocol floor, flag-worthy by any automated review. If she synced now, inside the Orison relay, it would land in the metadata header before she reached the lobby. She closed the interface. She would sync from outside.

"My granddaughter's in Zone 7," Berenice said. "Four years now. I keep meaning to go." Her hand moved to her own forearm as she said it — a brief press, the kind of touch a person gives themselves without noticing.

Zone 7. Amara kept her expression steady. The maintenance sector she had flagged in week three, where the feeds had been the cleanest — no events logged in eleven sessions across three contractors. Where the routes on her informal map had gone white first. "Which building?" she asked. A technician's question, even enough.

Berenice named a residential block near the Zone 6 margin. Not the crescent. Amara filed it anyway.

"Outside is where things happen to you," Berenice said. She said it lightly, with the practiced gentleness of a woman long at peace with staying. Not a complaint. A finding. She was inside, and safe, and the news was about Zone 8, which had failed, and the restaurant on forty-one had a table free, and the air was twenty-one degrees and intended to stay there.

Amara re-ran the scan and finished the correction. Her own hands. Her own calibration. The ordinary sense of the small warm room returned in pieces, the way sensation returns after cold.

She filed it and said her goodbyes.

---

At the lobby doors the Orison envelope fell away in a single stride.

Zone 6 hit all at once: warm concrete, diesel, the freight grid riding its pylons off to the west, a relay beacon cycling against a sky the campus didn't permit you to see. She stopped on the threshold with one foot still on Orison tile and one on the public sidewalk and felt the seam between the two airs land in her sinuses — managed versus unmanaged, each pressing its claim from opposite sides of her body.

She was at the van before she noticed she had read the Seam on the way out.

Not on purpose. Not for any reason she could name. The Z5/Z6 margin: the dwell time of the commercial vans idling in the loading strip, the sight lines off the battery station, the two-block window where the relays didn't reach. She had clocked what was available there — what you could move through that gap, and when, if you needed it to leave no record. She'd done it with the same automation she'd use to read a house number while looking for a different address.

Three years on the Zone 5 contract. She had never once needed any of it.

She got in and opened the archive.

*Fragment entry 11 — intrusive awareness state, mid-calibration. Duration ~15s; attempted depth increase to 1.9mm; interrupted by campus welfare check before extraction could complete. Orison campus, Zone 5. Content: spatial threat-assessment — exits, sight lines, freight elevator access. Depth error 0.4mm, self-corrected. Client unharmed.*

She read back the phrase — *interrupted before extraction could complete* — and didn't reclassify it.

*Addendum [T+08m]: unprompted Seam analysis, Z5/Z6 boundary. Courier-pattern spatial read. No training, no known prior onset. ~3 seconds before I caught it.*

She looked at the addendum a while. Three seconds before she caught it. The time before, it had been her hands. The time before that, her proprioception. Each onset reached a little deeper. Each one took a little longer to surface to where she could see it. Each one was harder to explain why she hadn't felt it sooner.

The margin between this and harming a client was not a margin she controlled.

She drove east until the Orison beacon dropped off her tissue's ambient read. Zone 4, four blocks past the boundary. She pulled over by a relay kiosk, opened the reader interface, and synced the session log. Cellvault's confirmation came back in three seconds: *Transmission received. Session record archived.*

She put the reader back in her bag. The delta was in the log — she knew that. But the Orison relay was behind her. Whatever the automated review flagged in her session header, it would route through Cellvault's infrastructure first, not Orison's.

She reached for the burner. The seventh draft, the one she had meant to sit with one more week. She had the subject line filled in.

Not yet.

Another hour, outside the grid.

---

Fragments 12 through 21 she had filed without ceremony — his hands on her routes, his spatial data bleeding into her calibration sessions, the ordinary texture of borrowed awareness. She had classified each one, logged it against the previous, noted the deepening without naming what it meant.

This one was different.

The sister came that evening.

Signal strength 0.4 — below her own threshold for reliable data. She ran it back three times to be sure she wasn't building something out of noise. And a name, riding in under it. *Seto.* Two syllables held low at the back of the throat, worn smooth the way a word gets worn when you've said it ten thousand times and never once aloud.

For forty seconds she received what it was to miss someone you are not permitted to stop missing. Not the event — not the death, or the distance, or whatever the reason had been. The shape the loss took in the ordinary hours. He carried it the way you carry something irreplaceable: both hands, slow, without setting it down. She could not have named the loss. She knew its weight.

She received it the way you receive a smell you didn't ask for — entire and finished, in the lungs before the mind gets a vote on whether it wanted it.

She sat with the notation a long time before she wrote it.

She had been logging clinical fragments for five weeks. Nothing had come across this private. This was not his carrier architecture, or his route patterns, or his courier's read on a room bleeding into hers. This was *him.* A piece of who he was that had nothing to do with how he moved through corridors. She had taken it off his signal without asking, and she could not give it back, and there was no protocol that described what she now held or what she owed for holding it.

*Fragment 22 — familial reference, high confidence, satellite source. Non-local. Duration 40s.*

She closed the laptop.

She lay awake a long while after, holding a dead woman's name that had never been hers to hold.

---

The message had been drafted six days. She had written it seven times. The seventh was good — precise, collegial, unalarmed, citing the literature where the literature would carry her. She had meant to sit with it one more week. More data. A cleaner explanation. A version of the thing that was less a confession.

She opened the sync log at the end of the session. Routine. A formality she ran without reading.

*Timestamp 21:44. Orison Neuretics QA. Assigned reviewer: Fonseca, C. Status: pending interview. Anomalous delta flagged for review — session metadata, service call 019-4471, technician Osei, A.*

Seven hours ago.

She read it three times before it sat still. The delta had surfaced in her session header the instant her reader reconnected to Cellvault's relay — a standard handshake on the Zone 4 street, a thing that happened ten times a day, and folded inside the handshake a variance the protocol had been built to catch. She had thought she was past Orison's infrastructure. She hadn't been past Orison's infrastructure. Cellvault had handed the session log straight through, and the automated review had flagged it before she'd reached Zone 3, and someone called Fonseca had been assigned to it, and there would be an interview.

Then the second thought, arriving the way the others had lately — already finished. Three other session syncs this month. Three other Type C clients. Each one with the same delta riding in its header. Each one tied to a name she had chosen not to file.

She opened her message. The responsible move was to wait — to wait until she knew what Fonseca was going to ask, what the interview would cover, what was already in their file. She read the seventh draft one more time and sent it at 23:19, on the Zone 7 signal.

*I've been receiving ambient fragments off your signal for about five weeks, and logging them for the same period. I have data you should see before you file this as a security incident. I believe I can explain what's happening — and I believe the explanation is more interesting than it is alarming.*

Then she put her hands flat on the desk. The same stillness as the one she'd held in the Orison suite when the welfare check had interrupted and she'd had to read two columns of numbers to find her own hands again. Same stillness, same flat surface. A different reason.

She sat with the question she no longer had the option to defer.

Three other review items. Each one timestamped to a different calibration session. Each one carrying a name she had spent five weeks trying to keep out of the system that had just, without asking her, filed them all.
