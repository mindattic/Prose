[Beat 1]
# Interference Pattern

*Amara has been building toward a message for five weeks. The system moves first.*

She had been crossing zones long enough that her body knew the route without her. Zone 7 to Zone 5: the slip road at the Z7 margin, the Cellvault relay beacon cycling its thirty-second pulse over the interchange, the two-block Seam at the Z5/Z6 line where neither company managed the air and the commerce ran in the gap. Produce sellers under tarps. A battery-swap station with its bays open to the street. A woman behind a card table with a laminated price list for relay-bounce transit passes, weighting the corners against the wind with two dead phone batteries. Trade that happened in the open because no corpo held jurisdiction over the dirt it stood on. Trade with a smell to it: diesel, wet stone, and the cold-metal tang of condensate off infrastructure that had been left alone long enough to develop a personality.

She had worked Zone 5 for three years. She had never once looked at that two-block Seam and seen a transit corridor with two viable approach vectors and a single choke point.

She saw it now. She pulled into the loading margin and sat with it a moment — the choke point at the battery station, the way its signage would screen movement coming up from the south but leave the east approach open, the two-block window where relay coverage thinned to nothing. It took her three seconds to register that she was thinking in courier terms. Then it took her a fourth second to register that three seconds was already too long.

She filed it alongside Fragment 10 and put the van back in gear.

---

Inside the Orison campus the air was twenty-one degrees and would not move off it.

She had been here four times this month, and each time the air was the same: not cold, not warm, just present. Filtered and approved and delivered through ducts buried inside the walls, so there was nothing to look at but the walls themselves — warm umbers and soft napped textures, surfaces chosen by someone whose entire job was to manufacture the feeling of being safe. She knew this because the feeling worked on her anyway. Every time. Even now, knowing the mechanism, she felt her shoulders come down off her ears in the lobby like a hand had pressed them there.

No clocks. She had been inside twenty minutes before she caught herself reading the building's light to find the hour — and finding nothing, because the light came from no source she could point to, uniform and golden, the same at nine in the morning as it would be at four in the afternoon. The campus ran on a schedule. The schedule was simply not hers to read. No clock on the wall, none on the elevator panel, none at the reception desk where a clock would have cost nothing and answered an honest question.

The news concourse was running. Zone 8 flooding: aerial footage, a collapsed distribution hub, brown water standing in a corridor that looked like every corridor. A number on the lower third — fourteen hundred residents evacuated. Cut to an Orison spokesperson, composed, lit warm: *We are monitoring the situation, and our hearts go out to those affected.* Then a card, white sans-serif on deep blue: **RESILIENCE, INSIDE.** Then, without a beat between them, an ad for the restaurant on forty-one, blue ambient light and a reservation link.

She watched the cut happen and understood it cleanly, the way you understand a sentence in your first language. Here is the disaster. Here is the other CorpoNation that failed the people who trusted it. Here is what to want instead. The whole lesson ran in thirty-two seconds, and she took it in the same way she took in the managed air — not with the part of her that decided things, but with the part underneath, the part that just ran.

Her client was Berenice. Seventy-three, third generation inside the campus. Berenice's mother had arrived in 2194. Berenice had been born in an Orison clinic in 2201, had worked here and married here and raised two children here, and had left the campus twenty-two times in her life. She could name each occasion, the reason for it, and what the outside air had smelled like on each one.

"You look tired," Berenice said.

"Zone crossings," Amara said. She had the calibration probe staged, the portable reader paired, mesh depth set to 0.3 — starting shallow, building down by tenths. Berenice's neuretics were the cleanest tissue Amara had ever worked. Not because Berenice was careful with them. Because they had never been asked to do anything hard. You don't need to be careful with tissue that has never been anywhere.

---

The sensation arrived at 1.4 millimeters, in the posterior associative mesh.

Not the bearing housing this time. Not a motion at all. A *state.* A particular quality of attention she had never trained into herself and could not have produced on purpose — an awareness that read the corridor beyond the half-open door as a set of vectors, that rated the exits without hunting for a sign. There were no exit signs in the Orison corridors. There were never exit signs; you were not meant to need one. For perhaps fifteen seconds she became someone who knew where the exits were in any room she walked into. She knew the two service stairs. She knew the maintenance run behind the concourse. She knew the freight elevator that took no resident card. She had never been on this floor in her life. She knew it anyway, the knowledge arriving already finished, the way a smell arrives.

She held the probe still and waited it out.

Berenice was saying something about the flooding. Was it getting worse, did Amara think?

"The grid has redundancy," Amara said.

Her voice came out level. She did not know where she had gotten the steadiness from.

The state was still running under her hands — no, deeper than her hands, behind her eyes. The kind of attention that walks through a space tasting it for danger, and it was tasting the corridor and getting a clean read: single egress, foot traffic light and consistent, no observation hardware she could see. She had never once in her professional life assessed a room this way. She was doing it now with the same idle automation she'd use to check whether a burner was off. Her body simply knew, and did not ask her.

"My granddaughter's in Zone 7," Berenice said. "Four years now. I keep meaning to go."

"It's a good zone," Amara said.

"Outside is where things happen to you."

She said it the way she always said it — lightly, with the practiced gentleness of a woman long at peace with staying. Not a complaint. A finding. She was inside, and safe, and the news was about Zone 8, which had failed, and the restaurant on forty-one had a table free, and the air was twenty-one degrees and intended to stay there.

Amara finished the correction and re-ran the scan. The state had drained out of her by then. Her own hands. Her own calibration. Her own ordinary sense of the small warm room. Depth error: 0.4 millimeters, posterior associative, self-corrected. Client unharmed.

She filed it and said her goodbyes.

---

At the lobby doors the Orison envelope fell away in a single stride.

Zone 6 hit all at once: warm concrete, diesel, the freight grid riding its pylons off to the west, a relay beacon turning over its thirty-second interval against a sky the campus didn't permit you to see. The wet-metal smell of condensate off infrastructure left alone long enough to develop an opinion. She stopped on the threshold with one foot still on Orison tile and one on the public sidewalk and felt the seam between the two airs land in her sinuses. They were not the same substance. She had always known that. She felt it differently now — sharper, the way a temperature reads after a fever, carrying more information than it used to.

She had just spent an hour in a building with no clocks and no exit signs. She had spent fifteen seconds of it being someone who knew exactly where the exits were.

She was at the van before she noticed she had read the Seam on the way out.

Not on purpose. Not for any reason she could name. The Z5/Z6 margin again: the dwell time of the commercial vans idling in the loading strip, the sight lines off the battery station, the two-block window where the relays didn't reach. She had clocked what was available there — what you could move through that gap, and when, if you needed it to leave no record. She'd done it with the same automation she'd use to read a house number while looking for a different address.

Three years on the Zone 5 contract. She had never once needed any of it.

She got in and opened the archive.

*Fragment entry 11 — intrusive awareness state, mid-calibration. Duration ~15s. Orison campus, Zone 5. Content: spatial threat-assessment — exits, sight lines, freight elevator access. Posterior associative depth error 0.4mm, self-corrected. Client unharmed.*

She read back the one word — *intrusive* — and let it stand.

*Addendum [T+08m]: unprompted Seam analysis, Z5/Z6 boundary. Courier-pattern spatial read. No training, no known prior onset. ~3 seconds before I caught it.*

She looked at the addendum a while. Three seconds before she caught it. The time before, it had been her hands — the muscle memory of a bearing housing she'd never held. The time before that, her proprioception. Each onset reached a little deeper. Each one took a little longer to surface to where she could see it. Each one was harder to explain why she hadn't felt it sooner.

The margin between this and harming a client was not a margin she controlled. That was the part she kept turning over. Not whether it would happen. Whether she would be the one to notice when it did.

---

The sister came that evening.

Signal strength 0.4 — below her own threshold for reliable data. She ran it back three times to be sure she wasn't building something out of noise. It was not grief, exactly. It had more mass than grief: the specific texture of how a person carries a loss they have no intention of ever setting down. And a name, riding in under it. *Seto.* Two syllables held low at the back of the throat, worn smooth the way a word gets worn when you've said it ten thousand times and never once aloud.

For forty seconds she received what it was to miss someone you are not permitted to stop missing. Not the event — not the death, or the distance, or whatever the reason had been. The shape the loss took in the ordinary hours. A door he kept propped open inside a self that was otherwise sealed shut. The particular labor of going about your work all day while carrying that, and carrying it carefully, so that no one around you would know it was being carried at all.

She received it the way you receive a smell you didn't ask for — entire and finished, in the lungs before the mind gets a vote on whether it wanted it.

She sat with the notation a long time before she wrote it.

She had been logging clinical fragments for five weeks. Nothing had come across this private. This was not his carrier architecture, or his route patterns, or his courier's read on a room bleeding into hers. This was *him.* A piece of who he was that had nothing to do with how he moved through corridors. She had taken it off his signal without asking, and she could not give it back, and there was no protocol that described what she now held or what she owed for holding it.

*Fragment 22 — familial reference, high confidence, satellite source. Non-local. Duration 40s.*

She closed the laptop.

She lay awake a long while after, holding a dead woman's name that had never been hers to hold.

---

The message had been drafted six days. She had written it seven times. The seventh was good — precise, collegial, unalarmed, citing the literature where the literature would carry her. She had meant to sit with it one more week. More data. A cleaner explanation. A version of the thing that was less a confession.

She opened the sync log at the end of the session. Routine. A formality she ran without reading.

*Timestamp 21:44. Orison Neuretics QA. Assigned reviewer: Fonseca, C. Status: pending interview. Anomalous delta flagged for review — session metadata, service call 019-4471, technician Osei, A.*

Seven hours ago.

She read it three times before it sat still. The system had not waited for her. The delta had surfaced in the session metadata header the instant her reader reconnected to Cellvault's relay — a standard handshake, a thing that happened ten times a day, and folded inside the handshake a variance the protocol had been built to catch. So it had caught it. And someone called Fonseca had been assigned to it. And there would be an interview.

Then the second thought, arriving the way the others had lately — already finished. Three other session syncs this month. Three other Type C clients. Each one with the same delta riding in its header. Each one tied to a name she had chosen not to file.

She opened her message.

*I've been receiving ambient fragments off your signal for about five weeks, and logging them for the same period. I have data you should see before you file this as a security incident. I believe I can explain what's happening — and I believe the explanation is more interesting than it is alarming.*

She sent it at 23:19, on the Zone 7 signal.

Then she put her hands flat on the desk. The same stillness as the one she'd held in the Orison corridor, when someone else's awareness had moved through her and read exits she had never learned and found them all. Same hands, same flat surface. A different reason this time, and she could not have said the new reason was the better one.

She sat with the question she no longer had the option to defer — whether three other review items were already sitting in a queue somewhere she couldn't see, each one timestamped to a different calibration session, each one carrying a name she had spent five weeks trying to keep out of the system that had just, without asking her, filed them all.
