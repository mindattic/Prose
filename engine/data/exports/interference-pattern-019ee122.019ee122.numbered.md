# Interference Pattern

*Amara has been logging the signal for five weeks. Tonight she decides what to do with it.*

[Beat 1]

The air changed four steps past the exit corridor, the way it always did. Orison managed its environmental envelope right up to the property line — their climate, their humidity, their manufactured afternoon — and then it stopped, hard, and the Seam took over with priorities that were no one's in particular. The warmth hit first. Then the smell: recycled cooling runoff from the transit interchange two blocks east, something fried from the cart at the corner, the mineral bite of lakewind pushing down through the gap between buildings. Zone 6 air. Real air. She had been breathing curated nothing for two hours and her lungs registered the difference before she did.

She walked back to her building with her field bag over one shoulder and the calibration report half-filed in her head. Standard quarterly, hippocampal interface, no drift. Her client had shaken her hand goodbye and explained, without embarrassment, that he had not left the building in three years except for medical transfers. *Outside,* he had said, *is where things happen to you.* She had smiled and gone upstairs and done her job and thought about it on the way down and was still thinking about it now, stepping over a Pulse ventilation grate that pushed a column of warm pressurized air up from the transit tunnels below. Somewhere in the Zone 4–6 Seam between them — between her client's sealed world and this one — something had followed her home.

Not that night. That was three days before the first entry.

---

She had forty-seven entries in the log.

The north wall of her apartment carried three colors: blue for Zone 4 relay coordinates, red for anomalous signal clusters she tracked for Cellvault — the licensing outfit that bought her overflow hours and paid in the kind of credits that cleared without questions — and white for everything else. The white section had grown in the last five weeks. Forty-seven entries, their own subsection: time of arrival, duration, inferred location from relay bounce, quality descriptor. She had built the quality-descriptor column herself, because Cellvault's standard monitoring software was not designed for this.

She was adding the forty-eighth timestamp when the bleed arrived.

Not a fragment. Not a signal. Her hands.

She had the calibration probe halfway to its housing, a move she had made two thousand times, and her grip went wrong. Not dropped — her fingers found the housing fine — but the torque was not hers. The heel of her palm seated against the tool with the weight of someone who had been seating that grip into that resistance for years. Muscle memory that was not her muscle memory, distributed down her forearm and into her wrist with a precision that came from repetition she had never done. She felt the component find true-set: not heard, felt, the way a thing running clean transmits through whatever holds it. The satisfaction that followed was not hers either. It was a body register — competence in the palms, the specific pleasure of physical work that had gone exactly right.

She set the probe down.

Her hands looked the same. They felt like someone else's for another forty seconds.

She knew the difference between a feeling arising and a feeling arriving. She had logged enough fragments to know the grain of each. This was arriving. This was embodied knowledge handed across without asking, in through her nervous system, using her hands to demonstrate itself, and she sat still until it cleared and then she opened the log and typed: *fragment entry 11 — working-hands texture, high clarity, proprioceptive intrusion, 40-second duration. Not a feeling. A skill. Someone maintains something mechanical. Not metaphorical — I can still feel the resistance curve of the component. I have never done this work.*

She stared at the last four words.

Then she pulled up the peer-reviewed literature she had been circling for two weeks and read it straight through.

---

Neuretic harmonic resonance required three conditions: base frequencies within 0.8 Hz of each other, a high-density signal environment with at least four active relay nodes in range, and sustained proximity across enough time for the resonance to stabilize. The earliest paper used a tuning-fork comparison she found imprecise but not wrong. Two forks in one room. The sound they made together was not either sound alone.

She knew his schedule. Variable hours, not a fixed shift. Most evenings a Zone 7 relay bounce, one tower on the industrial margin, the one serving the interchange at the southern logistics corridor. Wednesdays were different: further east, longer, irregular rhythm. She had a hypothesis about Wednesdays, had not confirmed it, and had left the column blank.

She knew he maintained something mechanical. Fragments six, nineteen, twenty-three, thirty-five — that working-hands texture, attention present without being effortful, muscle memory running while the mind sat somewhere else. The attention of someone who had kept a thing alive a long time.

The sister was fragment twenty-two.

She had almost flagged it as a misread. Signal strength 0.4, below her threshold for reliable data. She ran it back three times. It was not grief. Not longing exactly. Something with more mass than longing — a pull that arrived from a distance she could not place, the way a tide answers a body it cannot see. She received it without looking for it. She sat with the notation a long time before she committed: *familial reference, high confidence, satellite relationship, non-local.* Then she closed the laptop and went to bed, because there was nothing diagnostic left to do with it and she did not want to keep reading something that private off a stranger.

She was a maintenance tech. She knew what the literature said. She had sat on it for five weeks because she preferred to understand things before she acted — her supervisor at Cellvault called that thoroughness, and she understood it as something nearer to not trusting herself to move on incomplete data.

She understood it now.

---

The message took forty minutes, longer than she needed for technical correspondence. She drafted it in plain text, no formatting, memo style. Mechanism first: harmonic resonance, two neuretic systems, the frequency conditions, three citations by author and year. Not a malfunction. Not a security incident. Not a breach. She was careful with that precision, because the first thing a person does when they receive unexpected neuretic contact is check whether they have been hacked, and she needed to answer that before he could ask it.

She identified herself. Amara Osei, certified neuretic maintenance technician, Cellvault Licensed Service Provider, registration number. She did not mention the anomaly log. She did not mention Orison.

*I've been receiving ambient fragments from your signal for about five weeks, and logging them for the same period. I have data you should see before you file this as a security incident. I believe I can explain what's happening, and I believe the explanation is more interesting than it is alarming.*

She read the last sentence twice. She left it in, because it was true and because she would rather a stranger arrive curious than braced.

She sent it at 9:14. Zone 7 signal, which meant he was near the industrial margin. She felt the message go the way she felt anything go that could not be called back — a state change in the background of her awareness, the frequency now carrying something that had not been in it a moment before.

She opened Mrs. Adeyemi's client report. Zone 4, quarterly, hippocampal interface stable, no drift. She filled in the fields.

The lumen mouse was working at something in the wall behind the data map. She could hear it — the faint dry rustle tracking along the baseboard, pausing, continuing. She had never seen it directly. She knew it from the trace of green it left along the baseboard at night and from the sound. She had stopped trying to work out which part of the wall it preferred.

She was on her fourth client report when the frequency shifted.

She had not been watching for it. She registered it the way she registered everything ambient — peripherally, catalogued and set aside before it was consciously named. This one did not stay peripheral.

*Who are you.*

She set the stylus down.

Not a question. The period was deliberate — she ran the signal twice in three seconds before she would commit to that reading, but the quality was clean and the cadence was unmistakable. A period where a question mark should have gone. He was not asking whether something was there. He was telling it to name itself.

She had predicted this. In the margin of fragment thirty-eight she had written: *if contact is established, expect identification demand before content.* She had been right about the demand. She had not predicted the period.

Outside, the Zone 6 relay beacon pulsed its thirty-second interval through her window. She had stopped seeing those pulses years ago. The light moved across the ceiling and she tracked it now, once across, and opened the plain-text editor.

She wrote her name. Her field, her registration number again. She wrote: *I've been receiving your signal for five weeks. I have a log of forty-seven entries. I understand if that's alarming. It would alarm me.*

She almost deleted the last sentence. It conceded something a technical memo would not. She left it in, because the period had told her who she was writing to. A person who reached for *who are you* without a question mark would not be reassured by someone pretending the situation was normal. He would be reassured by someone who could see it from his side.

She wrote: *I have a theory about why this is happening. It involves a documented mechanism — the citations I sent are real, verify them in the open literature. I'd like you to hear the theory before you file this as a security incident. And I think you were going to file it.*

She wrote: *I also have data you should see. I'm not going to tell you what kind yet.*

She sent it.

---

The wall was quiet. She pulled up the log — the full log, all forty-seven entries, timestamps down the left column, quality descriptors down the right. Fragment 32 was the day after she had first noticed the Type C drift in her client data: four cases, same presentation, none of them logged anywhere they should have been. She had added a second column that day, running parallel to the fragment column.

Two columns, side by side. She had not drawn a line between them in five weeks.

She knew the line was a question. She had not answered it because answering it would require her to do something with the answer, and she had wanted to understand the mechanism first. She understood the mechanism now. The line between the two columns was no longer hypothetical. It was just a line she had not drawn.

She reached for her stylus.

She stopped.

If she drew the line, she could not un-draw it. She could not file the continuity drift as unrelated and hold that position with a straight face once she had logged the connection in her own hand. Her log would become evidence. Evidence of what, exactly, was the part she had not decided how to carry.

Her hand was still over the stylus. It had not moved. She watched it not move and understood that the stillness was the decision — that she was choosing, second by second, not to draw the line, and that choosing not to was its own kind of mark on the page.

She had identified herself to a stranger. She had sent him data she held about his neuretic signature. She had withheld the second column. She did all three knowing the line between the columns was the one piece she had kept from both of them — from him, and from the version of herself that would have to act on it.

She picked up the stylus.

The lumen mouse moved behind the baseboard, north toward the data map, its green light too dim to show through the wall. The relay beacon came around again — thirty seconds, exactly, as it always was. She tracked the light across the ceiling once.

Fragment 32 sat at the top of the second column, waiting for a line.

She set the stylus to the wall and did not close the log.
