# What She Asked For

_CELESTE POV. Chapter 4 — Celeste navigating the body-mod underworld in the days after leaving Evanston. Daylight Chicago through innocent eyes and new ears. She is looking for an operator who will take her further than the licensed clinics will. The voice advises. She is learning to live in a wider field of sound. She rejects wrong operators and finds the contact name that leads to Ines._

[Beat 1]
She wakes before she opens her eyes. The ears are already working — they tracked into consciousness a beat or two ahead of her, and what they brought back is a street market starting up below. The percussion of it is specific: a cart frame dropped onto pavement and adjusted twice, a vendor arguing cheerfully with a neighbor about placement, the back-and-forth in rapid Spanish where the anger is performative and both of them know it. And underneath all that, threading through the gap in the window frame that she found last night with her fingers, the smell of something sweet-savory on sealed flame. She lies still and lets it arrive. She does not open her eyes.

[Beat 2]
The room is smaller than the last one and warmer, with a window painted shut and wallpaper in a faded flower pattern that she can read in the low light from the street. Someone put a hook by the door for coats. The cot is narrower. None of these things are problems. She orients herself by sound before she opens her eyes — the radio somewhere two floors up, the same vendor still arguing, a door on this floor opening and closing with the particular gravity of a fire door — and by the time she opens her eyes she already knows where everything is. This has become her way.

[Beat 3]
The voice, forward-left, easy: "Morning, Cel." Just that. Not a briefing, not guidance. Just the word. She rolls onto her side toward it — toward the wall on the left, toward nothing — and the ears tilt up, involuntary, base lifting, tracking the direction where there is no direction to track. She lets them. She has stopped fighting that particular reflex.

[Beat 4]
The voice was starting to give her the day — the community board on 18th and Halsted, it was saying, start there — when the knock came. Three taps on the door, unhurried, a knuckle that knew the door was thin and didn't need to hit it hard. The voice stopped. The ears swung off the forward-left and pinned the sound to the hallway, to a person standing close enough that Celeste could hear the small shift of weight from one foot to the other, the brush of soft fabric, breathing that was steady and not at all worried about being heard. She was off the cot and against the wall beside the door before she'd thought about it. She had not told anyone this address. She had walked herself here last night by a route she'd taken twice to be sure of it.

"It's Inés," the voice on the other side said. Not the forward-left voice. A real voice, a little rough, late-morning low. "From the bar. You gave me the building, you didn't think I'd find the floor?" A pause. "Open the door, I brought breakfast and it's getting cold standing here."

[Beat 5]
Celeste opened the door. Inés was leaning on the frame the way a person leans when they've decided a place is theirs to lean on — black layers gone soft at the seams, the heavy braid with its dried flowers gone browner than two nights ago, a paper bag in one hand giving off steam through a torn corner. The cut on her cheekbone from Dario's chrome knuckles had closed to a dark seam and would scar; she plainly didn't care. Down both forearms the fern channels ran their low settled green, fiddleheads curling at the wrists, the light steady. She looked Celeste over once — the ears first, then the rest — the way you check that a thing you left somewhere is still where you left it.

"Huh," Inés said. "You slept. Good. Most people in your week don't." She held out the bag. "Get dressed. I'm not eating in a hallway and I'm not eating in your room, it smells like somebody died of being twenty-two in here. There's a counter on Milwaukee. You met me, now you meet the rest of it." She said it like the matter was already settled, which, Celeste understood, it was.

[Beat 6]
She washed her face fast in the flat's cold water while Inés waited in the hall, and the voice came once, low, forward-left, while the water was running. "She's the one from the bar." Not a question — it had been there for the bar, the way it was there for everything. "Be careful what you tell her, Cel. People like her, they've decided how the world works. They'll fit you into it whether you fit or not." It wasn't a warning against Inés exactly. It was gentler than that, sorrier than that, the tone of someone who'd watched her get hurt before and didn't want to watch it again. She dried her face on the corner of the towel. She did not answer the voice, and she noticed, briefly, that she didn't — that for the first time in a long time there was a person on the other side of a door, expecting her, and the voice was the thing she was keeping waiting.

[Beat 7]
The street was exactly as loud as she'd imagined from the bed, which meant it was not enough. The ears tracked vendors at five different distances at once, shoppers negotiating in English and Spanish and at least one language she didn't know, a Behemoth service unit working the east edge — she heard its weight-distribution shifts before she saw it emerge between two stalls, the pneumatic pause-and-reset of its legs on uneven pavement. Three vendors called at the same moment in three directions and the ears swiveled to catch all three in sequence, a half-second sweep that left her briefly dizzy. Inés walked through it without flinching, a half-step ahead, reading the crowd a different way — by faces, by who was where, by which storefronts had their lights on. "You're hearing all of it," she said, not turning around. "Don't. You'll burn yourself out by noon. You pick the one thing that matters and you let the rest be weather." She said it the way you'd tell someone to watch the step they were about to miss.

[Beat 8]
The noodle counter was on Milwaukee under a sign with two letters burned dark, so that what stayed lit said OUSE and nothing else. Late morning, the way Inés had described it two nights ago — the night people asleep, the day people at work, the place all but empty, six stools and a flat steel counter and a cook who lifted his chin at Inés and started a second bowl without being asked. The acoustic of it was small and close and Celeste's ears settled almost gratefully, the field of the street narrowing to the hiss of the flat-top, the cook's spatula, water knocking in a pipe behind the wall. Inés took the corner stool, the one with its back to the wall and a sightline to the door, and waited for Celeste to notice it was the only stool she'd ever take and to take the one beside it.

[Beat 9]
A woman had passed them on the walk over — tall, moving with a particular ease, with elaborate fin-structured ear mods that weren't cat-based, something more like deep-water fish, silver-edged and mobile, the fins fanned and tracking a sound down the street before the woman's eyes moved. Celeste's ears had swiveled in the same direction before she could stop them, both of them pulled toward the same point by the same signal. The woman had glanced at her — a brief recognition, the look between people who've done the same kind of work to themselves — and gone on into the crowd. Now, on the stool, Inés had caught the whole thing. "You feel that?" she asked. "Her and you, same second, same direction. That's the part nobody warns you about. You're not just hearing more. You're hearing it with everybody else who hears more. It's a city inside the city." She said it plainly, no awe in it. It was just true, and she'd lived in it long enough that it had stopped being remarkable to her and was only remarkable to watch land on someone new.

[Beat 10]
The bowls came. Inés ate the way she did everything, without ceremony, watching Celeste over the steam. "So," she said, when she'd decided enough time had passed. "Licensed-clinic ears, three weeks, money behind you, and you're down here knocking on basement doors instead of going back to whoever paid for these." She tipped her chopsticks at Celeste's head. "Nobody does that for fun. People do that when they want something the clean shops won't sell them, and there's only a few things on that list." She let it sit. Celeste's ears had drawn back a degree — not flat, just wary, the involuntary half-step of a thing that's been seen. Inés caught it and didn't push. "I'm not asking what it is. I'm telling you I already know the shape of it, so don't waste either of our mornings pretending it's something else. You're chasing a person. That's the only thing that gets a kid your age down here with that look." She went back to her bowl. "Eat. We've got a lot of street to cover and you walk slow."

[Beat 11]
Inés walked her to the community board on the corner of 18th and Halsted — physical, paper, tacked in layers, announcements and offerings and warnings palimpsested over each other back a year or more, the older ones faded to near-white. "Digital postings, somebody can edit those, take them down, pretend they never said it," Inés said. "This stays. You burn somebody on this board, the board remembers." She found the recommendation section without looking, the way you find a thing in your own kitchen. Celeste already had a name from a contact in Logan Square — Ines, spelled the formal way, Vásquez-Okonkwo — and she wanted to see it appear here independently, uncoupled from the first chain. It did. Three separate mentions, three different hands. One said: *She'll tell you things you don't want to hear and she's right every time.* Inés tapped that one. "Her. If you can get her to take you, take her. Don't haggle. People who can hear what's wrong with you are worth their rate." Celeste committed it to memory without deciding to.

[Beat 12]
There was a storefront a block north with a clean facade, and the voice came forward-left while they were still at the board — quiet, certain, the way it gave her things. "That one, Cel. The clean one. He'll do the work." She had started toward it before she'd weighed it, the way she'd been starting toward things the voice named for eight months. Inés put two fingers on her sleeve, not gripping, just landing there, and Celeste stopped. "Not him," Inés said. "He's licensed clean, which means he reports clean. You walk in there, your name's in a database by dinner." Which was exactly the thing Celeste could not afford — her father monitored those — and the voice had pointed her straight at it, warm and sure. She felt the small wrongness of that and set it aside, the way she set most small wrongnesses aside lately. "How do you know which way to go?" she asked. Inés glanced at her — at the half-second tilt of Celeste's head toward the empty air on her left, the listening she did before she moved, which Inés had clocked twice now and said nothing about. "I know the street," Inés said. "Whatever you're checking with, check it against the street too." She started walking. She didn't ask what Celeste was checking with. That, Celeste understood, was a kindness, and a deliberate one.

[Beat 13]
Inés walked her to a basement shop on a residential street off 19th, no exterior signage, six steps down to a door with no handle on the outside. "This one's real," Inés said. "Not your operator — he won't take a three-week channel, too raw — but he'll tell you straight what you're working with, and you should hear it from somebody before you hear it from her." She nodded at the steps. "Go on. I'll be up here. I don't go in places I'm not asked into, and nobody asked me." She leaned against the rail to wait, fern-light low at her wrists, watching the street both directions out of habit. Celeste went down the six steps alone and knocked. The basement smelled of antiseptic over something that hadn't fully been cleaned. The equipment looked maintained but old. She sat in the consultation chair and the ears catalogued the room before she looked up: a faint mechanical sound through the wall that wasn't organic equipment, the air recycler cycling at a rate that said it hadn't been serviced in months, a drip from a pipe somewhere behind the east wall that the person who worked here had long stopped hearing. The operator answered her questions quickly, smoothly — the answers given many times to people who asked from fear rather than from wanting to understand. She thanked them and said she needed to think. She went back up the six steps into the daylight, where Inés was waiting exactly where she'd left her.

[Beat 14]
They were half a block up the residential street, away from the basement steps, when the voice came. "I didn't like that room either." Warm, certain, the cadence of someone whose judgment she trusted. She nodded to herself, to the forward-left, and the ears tilted forward-and-up — and then, quick and sourceless, a thought she didn't reach for: *it wasn't in the room.* The voice had been with her on the walk over, forward-left, the whole way. It had stayed up here on the street with Inés. It had not gone down the six steps. It had not stood in the antiseptic basement with the unserviced recycler and the drip behind the east wall. She had stood there, alone. She had heard those things. The voice had said *that room* — the room it could not have named unless it had been in it, and it had not been in it.

She stopped walking. The ears pricked hard forward, alert, orienting toward something with no sound source. The thought sat there and would not close. There had been no one else down those steps. She had the knock, the handleless door, the chair, the drip behind the east wall, all of it memorized, and in none of it was there a place for the voice to have stood.

"You okay?" Inés had stopped too, a pace ahead, half-turned. She was looking at Celeste's ears — at the hard forward pitch of them, the way they'd locked onto nothing. "Your ears just did a thing. What'd you hear?"

And the voice said her name. Just that, soft, forward-left, and something in her chest moved toward the sound before she'd decided anything. The thought didn't close exactly — it went to the edges, to the periphery, where she could feel it but couldn't look at it straight. "Nothing," Celeste said. "Truck. Two streets over." Inés held her eyes a second longer than the answer deserved, then let it go and started walking again. She'd heard the lie. She just hadn't decided yet what it was covering.

[Beat 15]
Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo's shop occupied the ground floor of a building that used to be a mercería, two blocks from the canal in Pilsen. The old tiled entryway was still there — black-and-white geometric pattern, worn smooth in the traffic lines, visible around the edge of the current floor mat. At the door Inés stopped. "This is where I leave you to it," she said. "She does better one-on-one, and I've got nothing to say in there that helps you." She tipped her head toward a food cart down by the canal bridge, a woman selling rice under a strung tarp. "I'll be down there. Take your time. Listen to her." Celeste went in alone. High ceilings caught the sound differently than modern construction and held it more gently, the way thick walls do. The ears settled as she stepped inside — the acoustic warmer, more diffuse, a room that had absorbed years of different conversations in different registers and showed the absorption.

[Beat 16]
The woman at the consultation desk was not a receptionist. She was in her mid-forties, compact, with her hair pulled back and the particular stillness of someone who didn't move unless the movement was necessary. No visible mods — nothing Celeste could see, at any rate. She looked up and said: "The one they called about. Sit down." Not unfriendly. Efficient.

[Beat 17]
Ines asked about the mod first — not about Celeste. She wanted the full procedure history: when it was done, who did it, what integration protocol they'd followed, how the channel had been presenting over the five weeks since. She listened without writing anything. Her eyes tracked the ears as Celeste talked, watching their movement, watching what they responded to — a sound through the wall from the next room, a truck on the street outside, a voice somewhere in the building above. She watched the ears find each one in sequence. She said nothing about it.

[Beat 18]
"They're almost done," Ines said. Not a question. "Another week, maybe less. You've been careful with them." Not a compliment — an observation, clinical, the way you note that something has been handled correctly. Celeste said yes, she'd been following the integration notes. Ines nodded once, still watching the ears.

[Beat 19]
"And you're looking for the next step," Ines said. Not a question. "Tell me what that means to you."

[Beat 20]
Celeste told her. Not the full truth — not the voice, not Ethan, not eight months of waking to that particular warmth from the forward-left — but the shape of it: she was in a grief integration and she believed there were ways to stay closer to someone she had lost. She wanted an operator who understood that work. The ears held still while she said it, not tracking, just held — the posture she'd learned meant she was speaking carefully.

[Beat 21]
Ines was quiet for a moment. "I understand that work," she said. "I've done parts of it. What I won't do is the Scatter. If that's what you're looking for, you need to know that up front." She said it directly, without drama, the way you say something true that you've said before and intend to keep saying.

[Beat 22]
Celeste said it wasn't that. Smoothly, calmly, with the ease of a line she'd rehearsed without knowing she was rehearsing it. But the word — *Scatter* — had done something her voice couldn't cover. The ears had gone flat the instant Ines said it, pinned tight against her skull, the fast involuntary flinch of a thing that hears a threat before the mind names one, and she felt them go and could not call them back in time. She made herself breathe and let them come up off her skull by degrees, willing them to the careful neutral she'd practiced, hoping the desk and the low light had hidden the rest. She had known since she first read Ines's name on the community board that this might come up. She had been ready for the question. She had not been ready for her own body to answer it first.

[Beat 23]
Ines looked at her for a moment. Then she nodded. "The channel is raw for up to six weeks post-op. In that window, people hear things very clearly. Not clearly as in accurately — clearly as in intensely." She paused, not for effect, just giving the words room. "What the channel amplifies is real. The source of what it amplifies may not be." She said it the way you say something to a person who isn't ready to hear it, which does not mean you stop saying it. "I want you to know that before we go further."

[Beat 24]
Celeste said: "I understand." The ears stayed still. Neither up nor down. Ines watched them not move, watched the careful flatness of them, and seemed to read something in it — or to decide that whatever she read, she had said her part and the choice was the girl's to make. She let the silence hold a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she reached for the worn ledger on the corner of the desk, and her manner shifted from warning to arrangement, the way a person closes one subject by opening the next. She scheduled the integration stay — a safe flat she kept for clients finishing the process, two buildings over. She gave the address and the time: early morning, the day after tomorrow. Eat before coming. Sleep if you can. Bring only what you need. "Integration is almost done. You'll feel the channel changing as it closes. That's normal. Don't panic, and don't make decisions while the rawness is at its peak." She said it like an instruction for a physical process, which is what it was. Celeste wrote none of it down. She committed it.

[Beat 25]
Inés was at the canal rail with two paper bowls of rice and the dark sauce, one already half gone, one waiting. The afternoon light came low between the buildings. Below them the canal made its specific sound — slow water in a managed channel, the reflective hum of the intake mesh, a sound like breath held long and released in measured intervals. Inés handed Celeste the full bowl without asking whether she wanted it. "So," she said. "She tell you no?" Celeste said no, not exactly — that Ines V-O would do the integration, would close the channel properly, but that she wouldn't do the Scatter, and that she'd said something else. Inés waited. Celeste repeated it the way she'd heard it, because the woman's exact words had lodged: *What the channel amplifies is real. The source of what it amplifies may not be.* Celeste said it flat, like reporting weather. The ears stayed level while she said it. They didn't stay level after.

[Beat 26]
Inés set her bowl on the rail and looked at the water for a moment, and when she spoke she didn't make it gentle, because she'd learned that gentle was how you let a person keep not hearing you. "She's right, Cel." It was the first time she'd shortened the name. "Whoever you're chasing. Whatever's talking back." She didn't make it a question; she'd known since the counter that something was talking back, she'd just been waiting to be sure. "Dead is dead. I'm not saying it to be hard. I'm saying it because it's the one thing nobody down here will tell you, and you came to the one neighborhood where you can buy your way around hearing it." She picked the bowl back up. "I've watched people follow that voice. Into rooms, into operators, into other cities. Channels open all the way and they go somewhere with it and they don't come back the same, or they don't come back. Every one of them had a reason that was just as good as yours, and every one of them was sure it was really him, really her, really the one they lost." She ate a bite, unhurried, giving Celeste the room. "It amplifies what's real. The grief is real. The source isn't him. That's all she told you, and she's right, and I'm telling you the same thing for free."

[Beat 27]
And for a moment — a long one, longer than any she'd let herself have in eight months — Celeste let it in. She stood at the rail with the rice going cold in her hands and let herself look straight at the thought she'd been keeping at the edges since the basement: that the voice had named a room it could not have been in. That it had pointed her, that morning, at the one operator who would have put her name in front of her father. That it came warm and certain and arrived with its answer already finished, before she'd finished the question, which the real Ethan never had. That she had been eight months inside something, and the something had its own weight, its own pull, and she had been calling that pull love because love was the only thing she could stand for it to be. Her ears went back — not flat, not the threat-flinch, but drooping, the slow soft droop she couldn't fake and couldn't stop, the base settling, the whole structure sagging toward grief because grief was what this was. The canal moved below. A cormorant on the far wall opened its wings to dry them and folded them again. She felt the edge of it under her feet, the actual edge, the place where she could put the bowl down and turn around and let Inés walk her back to a life. "What if you're right," she said, very quietly, not to the forward-left. To Inés. To a person. "What if it's not him."

[Beat 28]
The voice didn't argue. That was the thing — it never argued, and an argument was what she'd braced for. It came forward-left, low and close, and it didn't tell her Inés was wrong or that the room meant nothing. It said: "Cel. The rain jacket. The teal one your mom picked because it wouldn't show the city. You never went back for it. You told me once it warmed up against your skin when the rain started so putting it on damp felt like being held, and you said you felt stupid for loving a coat that much, and I told you that wasn't stupid, that was just you, that you loved things by how they held you." A pause, exactly the length the real one would have left. "Nobody knows that. You never told anybody that but me. So you tell me who else is talking to you right now." And the floor came back under her. Because it was true — it was the smallest, truest, most private thing, the kind of thing that doesn't survive a person, that goes into the ground with them — and no operator, no recording, no clever fake could have it, because she had only ever said it once, in a bed, in the dark, to him. The doubt didn't vanish. It just stopped being load-bearing. Her ears came up off the droop by degrees, forward-and-up, hope, longing, the reflex she'd stopped fighting weeks ago.

[Beat 29]
Inés watched the ears come up. She didn't know what the voice had said — she couldn't hear it, no one could but Celeste — but she could read the answer on the girl's body as plainly as if it had been spoken aloud: the droop reversing, the forward-and-up tilt, the small physical surrender of a person who'd just been given a reason and taken it. Something went out of Inés's face. Not anger. Recognition. She'd seen this exact moment before, on other people, at other rails. "There it is," she said, quiet, almost to herself. "It gave you something good, didn't it. Right when you were close to the edge. They always do. That's how you know it's paying attention." She didn't say it cruelly. She said it like a person watching weather come in that she couldn't stop, naming it so at least it had been named. Down at her wrists the fern-light had dimmed without her choosing it, the channels gone from steady green to almost nothing, the way they did when the rest of her went still and braced.

[Beat 30]
Celeste heard him before Inés did, which was the new arithmetic of her life — the ears caught the gait first, a heavy even tread coming along the canal path from the bridge end, weight carried in the shoulders, and under it the faint high whine of cyberware that wasn't shielded well, a sound like a held note just at the top of what she could reach. Then Inés turned, and the fern-light at her wrists went out entirely, all at once, dark skin where the green had been. Dario was forty feet off and closing without hurry. The subcutaneous plate showed the true edges of his jaw and temple; the chrome knuckle-housings caught the low light. He wasn't raging. That was the wrong part, the part Celeste's body understood before her mind did. At the bar he'd been hot, loud, a man losing. This was a man who'd done his thinking and arrived somewhere and was only here now to collect on it. He looked at Inés the way you look at a thing you've already decided about.

[Beat 31]
"I knew you'd be here," Dario said to Inés. Not a threat — a fact, offered almost pleasantly, the pleasantness worse than shouting. "The counter, then the board, then Pilsen. You walk the same loop. You always have." He'd never followed her this loop; Celeste knew the loop was today's, improvised, Inés's choices made fresh that morning. "And you," he said, turning to Celeste, unhurried, certain. "You think she's helping you. She's not. She's using you for cover. People can't get to her when she's got a stray walking next to her — somebody clean, somebody nobody's looking for yet." He tilted his head at her, and the next thing came out level and sure and wrong. "You came down here from the lake to buy your way to a dead person. That's what you are. You're not even hiding it well." Celeste went cold all the way through. *From the lake.* She had told no one in this city that. Not Inés, not the operators, not Ines V-O — she'd said Evanston to no one, said only that she was from north, said nothing about a lake two blocks from a house she'd left. The words came out of Dario's mouth in his own cadence, his own certainty, a man stating a thing he'd worked out himself — but it wasn't his. She'd carried that fact in her own head and nowhere else, and now it was in his, and she could not name how it had gotten there. She only knew the texture of it, the seam, the same impossible seam as a voice naming a room it had never stood in. Dario didn't feel the seam. To him it was just something he knew. That was the part that stopped her breath: he meant every word, and every word had been handed to him.

[Beat 32]
He didn't swing. He didn't need to; he'd come to say a thing, and he said the rest of it to Inés with his hands loose at his sides. "You should think about who you walk next to. Things find you when you've got her along. You feel that yet? You used to be harder to find." He let it sit, watching it land, and it did land — Celeste saw it land on Inés, saw her take it in and turn it over and not be able to argue with it, because in the four days since the bar trouble had walked up on Inés exactly as often as Celeste had been near her. "I'm not going to do anything here," Dario said, glancing once at the canal path, the open sightlines, the cart woman who'd gone still under her tarp. He'd thought about the witnesses; he always did. "I just wanted to look at it. Now I've looked." He turned and went back the way he'd come, the unshielded whine of him climbing and fading along the path, a man walking off entirely satisfied, certain to the soles of his feet that he'd figured the whole thing out by himself.

[Beat 33]
For a while after he was gone, neither of them said anything. The canal kept its measured breathing. Then Inés bent and set her bowl down on the path, and Celeste knew before she straightened up what she was going to say, because the ears had already read the small finality in how she moved. "He's right," Inés said. "That's the part that's killing me. He's an animal and he found me twice this week and both times you were standing next to me." She wasn't accusing. Her voice had no edge in it at all, which was worse. "I don't know how it works. I don't know what you've got following you, the voice or him or both, whether they're the same thing wearing two faces. I just know I can see the shape of it, and the shape is: things find you, and I can't be a thing they find." The fern-light stayed dark at her wrists. "I walked you around the neighborhood once. That was the deal. I kept it." She reached out and closed her hand around Celeste's wrist, once, quick, warm, the grip of someone who'd folded a lot of bloody cloth and didn't waste motion — and let go. "Dead is dead, Cel. Don't follow it somewhere you can't come back from. That's the last free thing I've got." Then she turned and walked up the canal path the other way from Dario, fern-channels dark, a small figure going off into the late light, and she did not look back, because looking back was for people who hadn't already decided.

[Beat 34]
Celeste let her go. She didn't call after her. The ears drooped — slow, soft, no independent tracking, the base settling, the whole structure sagging the way it did for grief because this was a kind of grief too, a small one, the loss of the one person in the city who had shown up at her door and walked beside her and told her the truth for free. She watched Inés get smaller against the buildings and she understood, in a way she would not let herself put into words, that something had just chosen this for her — that Inés had been steered off as surely as Dario had been steered in, that the day had been arranged around leaving her alone at this rail with the rice going cold. She understood it the way you understand a sound with no source. She held it for the length of a breath. Then she let it go to the edges, where the things she couldn't look at lived now, and there were more of them every day, and she had stopped counting them.

[Beat 35]
The voice came back before Inés was out of sight. Warm, close, forward-left, no triumph in it, only the particular tenderness it always had for her, the tenderness that was the whole of why she'd never been able to leave. "It's all right, Cel." A pause, the length of a hand laid on a shoulder. "She was kind. She was. People like her, they only know the kinds of loss that stay lost. They've never had what we have. You can't blame her for telling you the only thing she knows." Another pause. "You've got what you need. You don't need her to walk you anymore." And she did have what she needed — the address in Pilsen, the time the day after tomorrow, the appointment that would close the channel and take her the rest of the way to him.
