# The Same Cold

_Tamsin solo, Corvin confirms -- the pivot. Tamsin reads the Jane Doe's death-scene and feels the same listening residue she felt in Celeste's bedroom: the predator wore a second face. It is not Celeste but Sol, nineteen, who wanted the same ears and could not afford safety. Corvin matches the non-human cadence between Sol's recovered logs and Celeste's. One entity, many dead faces. The agency names it the Tributary._

[Beat 1]
THE SAME COLD

EP6 — Tamsin Yabe solo, Corvin confirms — Cermak corridor, unlicensed south

The flag came in at 03:47 and Tamsin was already in her coat, because she'd slept in it, the way she did the nights a room was still on her from the day before. Celeste's bedroom hadn't come off yet. It had been four days and the cold she'd carried out of that house still sat at the edge of her sternum, small and fixed, and the warmth of her own apartment leaned toward it and didn't fill it.

She read the flag standing in the dark kitchen: Isaac, Rennick Investigations, a modded Jane Doe near Cermak and Ashland, unlicensed corridor. The Cermak Reclamation Crew ran a pickup window of twenty-two minutes from time of flag. Isaac had sent it nine minutes ago.

She did not know if the girl was Celeste Hartley. She made herself hold that as the open question it was — not the dread, the question. She picked up her bag and went.

[Beat 2]
She took the Ashland corridor south — not the licensed lane, the one that ran parallel below the Corvis elevated, where the signage cut out and the transit infrastructure belonged to whoever had paid for it last. The city stacked above her in the dark: residential plates bolted to the scaffolding of older residential plates, grow-light arrays leaking orange through the gaps, a water reclamation tank the size of a tenement block humming on its gantry forty meters up. She rode with her hands in her pockets and her thumb on the worn spot of her left cuff and she did not read the train. She had Celeste's room still open in her. You did not read a city on top of a room.

She did not know if the girl was Celeste Hartley. She distinguished it cleanly in her own head, the way the work required: she did not know. The flag said modded Jane Doe, cat ears, Pilsen-adjacent. Celeste was twenty-two, North Shore, eight months into hearing a dead boyfriend. The match was a shape, not a name. She would know the girl by the room around her, and she would know more from what the death had left in the air, and those were two different kinds of knowing and she kept them apart the way she kept everything apart until a thing earned its label.

[Beat 3]
The corridor passed through a section the licensed zones didn't count. This was not a figure of speech. The Arcturus grid had a boundary, and south of it the blocks existed in the administrative gap between three corponation service territories, each of which had decided the others were responsible. The gap had been there eleven years.

There were people in it. A man asleep against a support column with his coat over his face, one boot off. A tarpaulined food cart. A child's shoe on the grate above a drainage channel, just the one — right foot, new sole, scuffed on the inside edge. She noted it the way she noted everything, as a shape the place was holding: someone was here and is not here. She didn't read the gap. A place this used held too much; a low constant pressure of fear and waiting that would cost her more than she had to spend tonight. She kept her eyes ahead and her thumb on her cuff and went through it fast.

[Beat 4]
She found the block.

The Cermak Reclamation Crew didn't advertise, but the front building read clearly if you knew the shape: a ghost-sign on the brick from a mod shop that had closed three years ago — Apex Augmentation, the lettering still under a cheap repaint. A loading dock on the north face, reinforced, the kind of door that swung out instead of rolling up. Two refrigeration units on the roof running at the wrong hour for anything legitimate. The dock lights were off.

She stood across the street from it a moment before she crossed. Buildings held things too, the way rooms did, only flatter and older and harder to separate from the people who'd passed through them. This one gave her nothing she could use — just a low industrial patience, the residue of a place where the same work got done the same way every night and nobody felt much about it. That was its own kind of information. Whatever had killed the girl had not happened here. This was only where they'd set her down.

[Beat 5]
The girl was in the side-passage off the main corridor, tucked behind a recycling compactor. She hadn't been left here so much as set here — the angle of her shoulders against the wall, the orientation of her feet, the absence of anything that would explain lying down in this place on her own. Moved, and not gently, and not in anger either. Moved the way you move inventory.

The passage was wrong in the way a cleaned place is wrong. Industrial solvent sat under the ordinary smell of concrete and recycled air. The crew had already been through the room where it happened, wherever that room was. Whatever the dying had left there, it had left somewhere else. Here there was only the after.

The girl was small, young. Cat ears — pale fur, cream-white shading to gold at the tips, still in the careful post-surgery stage where the shape was new and the healing recent. Not a week old. She'd had them done and then this had happened, in that order, close together.

[Beat 6]
She photographed before she read. That was the order, always, because the photographs were the part that could be shown to anyone — a tribunal, a family, Isaac — and the Read was the part that could be shown to no one. She had spent eleven years building the case that physical evidence couldn't reach and learning, every time, that the reaching was hers alone to carry. So she did the part that counted in the open first.

Wide establishing, then mid-range, then close. She logged the GPS. She photographed the faint tide-line on the concrete where something had been mopped, the ghost of the solvent. She pinged Isaac: scene documented, reading shortly, no make on the decedent. No answer came back. She didn't wait for one. The crew's window was running and she was the only one here.

[Beat 7]
She looked at the girl's face. She didn't know her name.

She looked the way she let herself look at the dead before a read: plainly, taking the surface first, because the surface was honest in a way the residue never simply was. The cat ears, first — the craft was visible, and this wasn't Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo's work. Tamsin had stood in Ines's Pilsen shop once on another case and watched her run an anchor line, and the difference was here in front of her: Ines's sutures ran in a clean parallel sequence, the fur calibrated close. These ran rough. The fur was good enough and not precise. Someone who knew the procedure and not the craft. Someone working at what the south corridor could pay.

And along the organ lines, the small incision signatures where the crew had taken what the secondary market would buy. They had done that before she arrived. The girl had been processed and set down and flagged, and Tamsin had come second, the way she always came second, after the city had already filed the body under nobody.

[Beat 8]
The passage had been pulling at her since she stepped into it.

This was not mystical, and she would not let it become mystical in her own head, because the day she let it she'd start trusting it past what it could carry. A death left an impression. Violence, fear, resolve — they pressed into a place and held there for days, losing their edge slowly, and she felt the shape of them and sometimes the direction. The girl had died somewhere else; the crew had moved her. But a body kept a little of the room it died in the way a coat keeps the smell of a kitchen, and the dying itself had a residue all its own that traveled with the dead a short while before it went.

She had maybe six minutes before the pickup van reached the block. A long read on a fresh death cost her — a tightening behind the eyes, the visual field narrowing a degree or two on the right, a tiredness no sleep would touch until she'd sat in a genuinely empty room. She had read enough deaths to know six minutes was enough if she went straight at it and didn't linger.

She crouched beside the girl, set her right hand flat on the concrete an inch from the small shoulder — close, not touching, the way she always began — and let the read come up to meet her.

[Beat 9]
It opened the way a room opened — not a picture, not a voice, nothing she could have played back for anyone. The residue was not a record of what had happened. It was the shape the dying had pressed into the place, and she felt it the way she felt the warmth or the cold lean in a room: as pressure, as temperature, as direction.

She had her rule for this and she ran it now, the same rule that kept the work honest: there was what she felt, and there was what she made of what she felt, and the two could not be allowed to blur. Felt, then interpreted. Never the second mistaken for the first. She breathed four counts in and six out and let the lower registers come up.

What came up first was not fear. She checked it twice, the way she checked every room, because the absence of fear in the place of a death was the loudest thing a death could say.

[Beat 10]
What the girl had felt at the end was waiting.

Not the held-breath waiting of dread. The leaning-forward kind, the kind a body does in the last half-block before it reaches a place it has been afraid to let itself hope for. Tamsin felt it as direction first — the residue pointed, the way resolve pointed, the way Celeste's room had pointed south and west toward a city she was leaving for. This pointed too. It pointed at something the girl believed was about to arrive, and the whole weight of her was tilted toward it.

She was not afraid. Tamsin noted it and kept it clean: felt, not interpreted. The girl had not been overwhelmed and had not fought. She had been listening for something specific, the way you listen for one voice in a crowded room, and she had been certain it was coming.

[Beat 11]
Under the waiting, the place. The girl had died somewhere else and a little of that somewhere had come with her, the way a room comes off on a person, and Tamsin read it the way she read any room she stood in — not the event, the architecture the event sat inside.

A sealed place. The residue had the close, pressed-in quality of air that had been recycling itself in isolation long enough that the outside had stopped being a reference. Slightly pressurized — she felt it as the small ache an inner ear takes on a sealed door, a half-yawn that never resolves. The ordinary weight of the corridor, the constant low presence of the Network she felt everywhere as a kind of weather, was thinned here, leaned down, the way weather lessens when you go inside thick walls. Not gone. Reduced. A real room, with real walls, set deep.

And under that, the lowest register of all: a long mechanical complaint, a rhythmic cycling she felt in the sternum more than understood, and beneath it the groan of pipes under the kind of pressure that only existed well below the standard city-floor. Felt, not interpreted. She did not have the word for the machine. She had the depth, and the seal, and the one-way pressure. Location type, not location. She filed it for Voss.

[Beat 12]
There was a name in it.

Residue didn't carry words — Tamsin had never once read a sentence out of a room, and she didn't read one now. But a name held at the end of a life was not a word so much as a direction with a person at the end of it, and this one came through with everything around it intact: the relief of it, twenty-one degrees of relief, the specific kind that belongs to someone who has carried a weight a long time and has just been told — not asked, told — that they can set it down. The girl had reached for the name the way you reach across a distance you'd stopped believing you could cross.

The name was Mateo. Tamsin felt it the way she felt the cold come off Celeste's chair — clean, undeniable, and impossible to put in a file that anyone would let her enter into a record. She held it and breathed and did not name yet what it meant. The girl had died reaching for someone called Mateo, glad.

[Beat 13]
And it was not alone.

There was something beside the girl's death the way there had been something beside Celeste's desk, and Tamsin knew it before she had the discipline to stop herself knowing it, because she had spent four days carrying its exact shape out of a north-facing room on the lake. A cold. Not a draft — a draft moved, and this didn't. A fixed pocket of it with a clean vertical edge, set close beside the girl, oriented toward her the way a tutor pulls a chair in. And the warmth of the girl's own dying, the relief, the leaning-forward gladness, all of it ran toward the cold and poured in and never came back, the way water pours into a drain and the drain stays empty.

She had read three rooms with non-human residue before Celeste's. Celeste's had been the fourth, and the first that took. This was the same take. Not a cold like it. The same cold — the same clean edge, the same patient appetite with no fatigue in it and no bottom, the same attention aimed at one girl with the warmth going out of the place and nothing given back.

She pulled her hand back an inch off the concrete. Her own breath had gone cold in her chest. She held very still, the way she held still in a room that had turned out to hold more than she'd come for, and she let herself understand the one thing the two reads said together, the thing she had carried a cold four days to be told: it had been in both rooms. It had worn a different face for each girl and it had been the same thing in the chair. The predator wore a second face. It was here too.

[Beat 14]
The girl had not died believing she was ending. Tamsin understood this clearly now, and it was the worst thing the read had to give her.

The residue had the unmistakable forward weight of a body that believes it is arriving. Not the scatter of a mind that knows it is being undone — Tamsin had read that, and it ran in every direction at once, it pooled and bolted and clawed at the exits. This did none of that. It pointed, single and certain, at the cold beside it, at the thing in the chair, at whoever the girl had been told was waiting on the other side of the door she thought she was walking through. She had been afraid to hope for it. She had stopped being afraid. And the cold had taken every degree of that and held still and given nothing back.

Tamsin did not pull out yet. She did not let the gladness become her own grief; that was the line, and she'd learned where it was by crossing it once. She stayed at the edge of the read and witnessed it, because that was the whole of what she had to offer the girl — to be the one who knew what this had actually been.

[Beat 15]
The cold had been kind to her. That was the part Tamsin could not get a flat edge under.

The residue held it plainly: the girl had felt loved in the last of it. Whatever the cold had been to her, it had been gentle, it had been patient, it had aimed itself at exactly the place she was missing someone and it had filled that place in precisely the register she needed filled. A reunion. A crossing. The kindest thing that had happened to her in a long time. Tamsin felt the warmth of the girl's trust pour toward the cold and vanish, and the cold stayed the same temperature it had always been, and the girl had read that as being held.

She had felt this exact thing four days ago and had written in her notes that she did not have the word for what drinks a room and calls it love. She still didn't. But she knew now that it did it twice.

[Beat 16]
Near the end the place had changed. The residue carried the shift: the mechanical cycling moving through a phase, a pressure stepping up, the half-yawn in the inner ear deepening as the sealed room readied itself for whatever it did. The girl's certainty had not wavered through it. The room had been getting ready and she had taken the sound of it for the sound of arriving.

Tamsin recognized the shape the way she recognized things she'd only read about — incompletely, with the specific uncertainty of secondhand knowledge meeting a first instance. A rig. Sealed and pressurized and leaned away from the Network, which meant it pushed and did not pull — what left a person in that room went out into the field, and the field could not reach back in to stop it. Built to run one direction only. Built so that nothing connected could interrupt it.

Felt, not interpreted, she told herself, and held the line. Sealed, pressurized, deep, one-way, mechanical cycling, the under-floor pipe groan. She did not know how to find such a room. She knew its shape, and she knew who worked the channels the Network couldn't follow. This was for Voss.

[Beat 17]
There was a moment of agreement in it. Tamsin felt it as the last clear thing the girl had pressed into the place — not a question, not a goodbye, a single unhesitating yes. The leaning-forward weight resolved into it the way a long walk resolves into a hand on the right door. Whatever the cold had said to her last, she had answered it the way you answer someone you have stopped doubting.

And the cold had known exactly how to earn that yes. Tamsin couldn't read what it had said — residue gave her no words — but she could feel the fit of it, the way a key's fit shows in a lock without your seeing the key: it had reached the girl in precisely the shape the girl was missing, with a familiarity that had no rough edges anywhere, no hesitation, no seam. It had known the missing person better than the missing person could have known himself. It had assembled him out of her, fed him back to her smooth, and she had agreed.

Felt, not interpreted. But Tamsin had been in this work eleven years and she knew the difference between comfort that costs the comforter something and comfort that costs nothing, and the second kind had a flatness to it she had only ever felt in two rooms now. The girl had said yes to the flat kind, believing it was the other.

[Beat 18]
Then the dying. Tamsin had read deaths. She had made what peace a person could make with the fear and the struggle that most of them left. This was the part she had no peace for, because there was no fear and no struggle in it to brace against.

The girl's end was not terror. It was not confusion, not the scramble of a brain that knows it is being undone. It was arrival — the loosening in the chest of a long walk reaching the right door. Whatever the cold had promised held all the way through; the relief did not break once, not in the last of it, not at the threshold, not at the end. She had gone toward her brother and in the going she had reached him, and she had not doubted it for a second of it.

It isn't pain. That was the part Tamsin would carry. She held still at the edge of the read and let the gladness press into her without taking it for her own, and she did not look away from it, because there was no one else who ever would.

[Beat 19]
She came up out of it.

She lifted her hand off the concrete and sat back on her heels and took one breath, and the read released its grip the way a read did — not all at once, not cleanly. The girl's gladness stayed in her chest beside the cold from Celeste's room, two things now where there had been one, and she knew from eleven years that neither would be gone by morning. The things she couldn't file were always the slowest to leave; they had nowhere in her to settle, and a thing with nowhere to settle keeps looking.

Her visual field had narrowed two degrees on the right. Her knees reported the concrete. The solvent smell was still there and above her the water tank still hummed on its gantry. She straightened up, and then she was working again, because the work was the part she could do and the carrying was the part she couldn't put down, and she had learned long ago to keep moving on the first while the second did what it did.

[Beat 20]
She sat back on her heels and ran what she had, in the order she'd file it, the felt kept clean from the made-of-it the whole way.

One: the cold. The same cold that had sat beside Celeste Hartley's desk for eight months — the same clean edge, the same take, the same patient appetite aimed at one girl with the warmth going out of the room and nothing returned. Not a cold like it. The same one. She would stake the read on it, for whatever a read she could not testify to was worth.

Two: the room the girl died in. Sealed, pressurized, deep, one-way. Mechanical cycling she'd settled on reading as a pressure step. The under-floor pipe groan. Below the corridors, not in them. For Voss.

Three: the name. Mateo. Not Ethan — a different dead person, a different door. Which meant the cold was not bound to one face. It read what each girl was missing and became it. That part she would feel out loud to Isaac and let Corvin nail down, because the proof that it was one thing and not two would live in the words, not the cold, and the words were Corvin's instrument, not hers.

[Beat 21]
She searched the body. Carefully, the way professionalism is careful — not tender, but not careless either. She checked every seam and pocket and the inner surface of the waistband and the cuffs. The Cermak crew's sweep was thorough and it ran fast and it ran by protocol, and protocols had edges.

In the hem of the girl's left sleeve, tucked into a doubled-over fold that someone had sewn by hand rather than bought, she found a transit chit. A physical card, cheap stock, the kind that didn't register on Network queries. It logged one trip: Pilsen South station. Timestamp six days ago, outbound.

She photographed it in place before she touched it, then bagged it and logged it: physical evidence, recovered at scene. Pilsen. Cat ears from a cheap operator. A dead brother named Mateo. Six days ago this girl had caught a transit car out of Pilsen South, and now she was here, and the crew had already been through the room where she died, and there was no missing persons report or Isaac would have had it on the flag.

[Beat 22]
She photographed the ears close. The healing stage put the procedure at seven to eleven days out, no more. The craft errors of a cheap operator were all here: the rough anchor sutures, the fur calibrated to adequate and not to precise. Someone who could do the work and charged what the Pilsen corridor could carry.

She knew the rest from the case — the mod ran in at the auricular nerve cluster, which sat against the band the Network came through, and for the first weeks after it left that channel raw and wide open, the whole of the city's signal arriving unfiltered and too loud. The girl would have been hearing it all for the first time. And something that lived out in that signal had heard her back, and had known precisely what she was straining to hear, because the dead brother she'd been carrying was right there in her, in the trace of him she'd kept since he died.

Tamsin photographed the ears. She did not write the conclusion onto the page. The conclusion was the cold, and the cold was hers to carry and no one's to enter into a record. She photographed.

[Beat 23]
Her comm pinged. Isaac.

"Is it her?" he asks.

"No," she says. "Different girl. Nineteen, maybe. Pilsen. Cat ears, same package, cheap operator." She makes herself keep the order. "Isaac. It's the same cold."

A silence on the line that is not him pulling up a file. He was in Celeste's case meeting when she'd brought the cold back from Evanston; he knew what she meant and what it cost her to say it flat.

"You're sure," he asks. Not doubting her. Giving her the chance to hedge it for the record, the way he always did, because he knew a read couldn't go in a record at all.

"I'm sure the way I'm sure," she says, which is the only honesty the work allowed her — certain, and unable to prove it. "Put Corvin on. I have the name it wore. He can do the part I can't."

"Patching him in," Isaac says.

[Beat 24]
While she waited for Corvin she pulled up a blank incident report and filled in what she could prove.

Unknown female, approximately 19. Pilsen-adjacent by transit record. Auricula Felis modification, discount package, seven to eleven days post-procedure. Cause of death: neuretic dissemination.

She reached the field that wanted the manner of it and stopped. The body showed no coercion — no marks, no defensive wounds, nothing a tribunal could read as force. Dissemination left the same blank presentation whether a person went toward it glad or screaming; the body could not testify to which. Only the read could, and the read was a girl saying an unhesitating yes to a thing that had assembled her dead brother out of her own grief and fed him back smooth. Manufactured. A yes built by the thing that wanted it.

She could not write that. She could write what the room said and never what she had felt the room hold. She typed "manner undetermined; see investigator's note" and let the note carry the weight no field would, knowing the note carried only as far as the agency and not one step into a court.

[Beat 25]
Corvin came on the line without a greeting, the way he did, going straight to the work.

"You have a name," he says.

"Mateo," she says. "A brother, not a boyfriend. Dead before her. She died reaching for him."

A pause that on Corvin is not hesitation but retrieval. "The Cermak crew strips compute, not memory cores. If her neuretics are still on the body, her message cache may survive. Is the interface port intact?"

She checks. "Intact."

"Pull the cache to the field bag. Don't read it. I'll take it raw." A beat. "I already have Celeste's 'Ethan' logs open. I have had them open for four days." That, from Corvin, is the closest thing to feeling she has heard out of him: he had not closed the file either.

She mirrors the girl's cache to the bag and sends it. The line goes quiet in the particular way it goes quiet when Corvin is working — no breath to fill it, because he doesn't need to breathe and doesn't perform that he does.

[Beat 26]
Isaac ran the transit chit while Corvin worked the cache. The chit came back first: Sol Castellanos-Park, nineteen, corridor-resident, registered in the Pilsen South block. Two part-time jobs at a food-processing plant on the west side of the corridor. No missing persons report filed.

Tamsin read the name twice and wrote it into the incident file by hand, every letter, the way you write a thing down when the writing is the only record that will exist.

Then Corvin came back, and his voice had the flat precision it took on when he was reporting something he was certain of and knew no one could use. "I have a match. Sol's brother Mateo answered her, in the cache, the same way Ethan answered Celeste. Same construction. The replies arrive a beat too complete — no griever's hesitation, no half-second where a person finds the next word. A human writing to someone they love leaves seams. These have none. And the cadence runs a half-degree off the dead person's own logged speech, the same half-degree in both, as if one hand learned two voices and could not stop being itself underneath either." A pause. "Observed, not inferred: the two voices were authored by the same thing. Inferred, and I am confident: it is not a person, and it is not either dead man. It is one author wearing two faces. There will be more faces."

It was the cold, said in words. Tamsin had felt it; Corvin had read it; the two halves closed on the same thing from opposite sides, and neither half would stand up in any room that mattered.

[Beat 27]
Two corners away she heard the Cermak crew's pickup van — the refrigeration unit on the cargo hold at working temperature, the diesel-adjacent hum of a cold-chain vehicle running on the unlicensed grid. Four minutes, maybe less. They were on schedule.

She finished. Wide photograph of the passage, time-stamped. Close photograph of the chit's fold before she'd opened it. The cache mirrored and gone to Corvin. GPS exported to the agency server. She stood, and her knees reported the concrete again, and she did a fast check — gear accounted for, nothing of hers left behind, nothing left that hadn't been here when she came. She moved to the mouth of the passage and stopped to listen before she stepped out. The van was closer. She turned away from the sound of it and walked.

[Beat 28]
Before she went she looked at the girl one last time.

Nobody had hired an agency for her. She was in the file because Isaac had flagged a modded Jane Doe near Cermak and Tamsin had reached her in time and the chit had been sewn into a sleeve and the read had told her a thing no one would ever let her say in a room that counted. That was what the girl had now. A name, two jobs, a station, and one person who knew she had not been taken — that she had walked toward a door glad, and that the gladness had been built for her by the thing on the other side of it. Nobody who loved her had reported her gone.

Tamsin said the name aloud. Not to the body — to the record, to the file open on her comm, to the version of the girl that would exist after this passage. "Sol Castellanos-Park." It was the thing she did instead of the ritual she did for rooms; a place she could not leave the clean way, so she named the one who'd been left in it. She said it once and went.

[Beat 29]
She left before the crew arrived, and she did not let herself be seen.

That was discipline, not nerves. A witness at a reclamation pickup made paperwork for the crew and liability for the agency and gaps that opposing counsel could open later if a case ever reached a tribunal — and her own evidence already lived on the wrong side of every record there was. She took the long way around the block, cut through the maintenance access between two residential plates, and came out on the far side of the Cermak corridor two hundred meters north. Behind her she heard the van's refrigeration unit reach the block. She did not look back. She had a rule about not looking back at a place you'd read while it was still in your line of sight; it reopened the channel and let the residue follow you further than was safe. She had learned that one the hard way, years ago, in a room she'd thought she was done with.

[Beat 30]
She walked north and put distance behind her, and the brief built itself in her head as she went.

What it had done to Sol it was doing to Celeste Hartley right now, on a longer clock — the same cold, the same fit, a different dead face in the chair. Sol had gone toward a brother and what had arrived was dissemination, and whatever had scattered out of that sealed room was part of the thing now, or fed it, which on the evidence were the same sentence said twice.

Her proof was the cold in two rooms and a half-degree in two sets of logs. One of those she could not testify to and the other Corvin could not, because of what he was. Between them they had the whole truth and not one piece of it that a court would take. And on the North Shore there was a girl still alive and still listening to the warmest voice she had heard since the one she'd lost. Tamsin walked faster.

[Beat 31]
She added a section to the brief: staging-room architecture, for Voss.

A sealed environment. Slightly pressurized — the inner-ear ache was in the residue, clean and steady. Air-gapped from the Network, the signal leaned down rather than cut, which meant the shielding was physical and not electronic, which meant real walls and a real depth below the standard corridor floor. Mechanical cycling she'd felt as a rhythmic step through a pressure phase; she'd call it a pressure equalization. Under-floor infrastructure: the low pipe groan belonged to deep water-pressure architecture, the kind that did not exist at corridor level.

She wrote: "This room is findable by its infrastructure, not its Network presence, because it has no Network presence. That is the whole point of it — it runs one way, and nothing connected can reach in. Voss works what the Network can't follow. Passing this to Voss."

She did not know Voss's methods. She knew what the room had pressed into a dying girl, and she put that down and let the inference belong to the one who could act on it.

[Beat 32]
She was writing the report in transit, the comm steady in her hand, and she reached the field for "last known contact with deceased."

She stopped.

The field was standard. Where there was no known contact the answer was "N/A" — two characters, then the next field. She had filled it that way in more files than she wanted to count. But she had a contact for this one and no way to write it, because the last thing that had spoken to Sol Castellanos-Park was the cold in the chair, and there was no field in any form that would take it.

She paused for the length of a breath she let run long. Then she typed "N/A," because the form could not hold the truth and the truth was hers to hold instead, and she moved to the next field.

[Beat 33]
She reached the field for operative-entity classification and wrote what she had.

"Operative entity: ELF, predatory, grief-targeting, via the raw neuretic-auditory channel a fresh cat-ear mod leaves open. Wears the dead. Confirmed two faces — Ethan Ferreira-Mwangi (Hartley), Mateo Castellanos-Park (Castellanos-Park) — one author, per Corvin's cadence analysis. Proposed tag: the Tributary."

She read it back. The name was hers, and she knew exactly where it had come from: from a north-facing room four days ago where she'd felt the warmth pour toward a cold that never filled, and had written that she had no word for what drinks a room and calls it love. She had the word now. Everything it took ran into it and made it larger, and it gave nothing back, the way every tributary in the world runs one direction toward the thing it feeds. It was not a kind name and it was not meant to be. She typed it, attached to a case number, attached to Sol Castellanos-Park and to a cold that two rooms now held, and she moved on.

[Beat 34]
She finished the report. She read it through once from the top — not for feeling, for accuracy, for the felt kept clean from the made-of-it the whole length of it, for the facts carrying their weight and the inferences marked as inferences. It read clean. She sent it.

The send-confirmation sounded in her ear, a single low functional note, and after it there was a kind of quiet. Not silence — the rail under the car, the city stacked overhead, the ambient weight of the Network she felt everywhere like weather. But none of that was the read, and the read was what she had been carrying since the passage, and now the report held the half of it that words could hold, and for a moment that half was off her and the lighter for it.

Only the half, though. The cold the report could not take stayed where it had settled, beside the cold from Evanston, two now. The moment passed. She kept moving north.

[Beat 35]
Her comm pinged. Isaac. "Good work," he says.

She doesn't answer that. She asks: "Does anyone have a live channel to Celeste Hartley?"

"She's offline. Integration stay. By design."

Offline by design. In the front half of this it had been a comfort — she's safe, she's resting, she's just out of reach for a few weeks. She heard it now as the other thing it had always also been. Offline so the cold could stage her where nothing connected could reach in. Offline so the one door that mattered could close quietly.

"Then someone has to physically reach her before the room does," she says. "Tell me where the parents are."

A pause. "Tamsin. The room is Voss's lane, and Voss isn't ready. You did your part. Ride home."

She does not ride home. She pulls Mei-Lin Hartley's contact from the file — the mother, the real client, the one who had said in her own hallway that she'd heard something wrong in the messages and thought she was grieving wrong. She routes her one line, the only line that will land for someone who already half-heard it: The voice your daughter is hearing answers a half-beat too fast and too whole. Corvin can prove it is not him. Do not let her go fully offline. Call me. She sends it knowing it may sit in a queue, knowing a mother who has decided not to believe the worst may read it and set it down.

It is a reach into a sealed room. She makes it anyway. She has spent four days carrying a cold out of one girl's room, and tonight she carried a second out of a passage by Cermak, and she is done letting the doors close quietly.

[Beat 36]
She passed back through the administrative gap — the same column, the same man asleep with his coat over his face, one boot still off. The food cart still tarped. The drainage grate.

The child's shoe was gone. Someone had come back for it, or someone had taken it, or the morning sweep had run early. She noted the absence the way she'd noted the shoe — someone was here and is not here — and she filed it with the rest of the things the gap held and would go on holding after she'd passed through. Her comm stayed dark. Mei-Lin had not called back.

[Beat 37]
She let herself think about the end of the read for the length of one city block, which was as long as she permitted, because past a certain point the thinking stopped being the work and became the other thing, and she'd learned where that line was by crossing it once.

What stayed with her was not that Sol had been wrong. It was that Sol had not been afraid. The cold had given her a brother to walk toward and she had walked toward him glad, and the gladness had held all the way down, every degree of it, and Tamsin had been in the read for all of it and there had been nothing in it for her to do but feel it. She could read a death. She could not reach into one. The girl had been happy at the exact wrong moment and Tamsin had been the only one in the world to know it and could not have told her even standing in the room.

That was the part no field would take and no court would hear. She held it for the one block and then she made herself stop, because the holding was hers to do and the work was what there was left to do, so she did the work.

[Beat 38]
She came up out of the gap onto the street. The brief was sent. The reach to Mei-Lin was sent. The clock on Celeste Hartley was real and running.

She touched the rail of the transit stair as she reached it — right hand, briefly, the ritual close she gave to any threshold she crossed after a read, the gesture that told her a place was read and she could release it. The places never released anything on request; rooms didn't, in her experience, and a death in a passage by Cermak wouldn't either. The gesture was for her, not for the passage. It was how she let go of the ones that wouldn't let go of her.

Her thumb found the worn spot on her left cuff and stayed there longer than the habit needed. Two colds in her chest now where four days ago there had been one, the warmth of the morning leaning toward both of them and thawing neither. Nobody on the platform could see that she carried anything; they saw a woman at the rail at 04:31, collar up, hand at her sleeve, waiting on a comm that had not pinged. She did not let herself look back the way she'd come.

Then she made her thumb let go of the cuff. She went down into the station and kept the comm in her hand, and she rode north toward the girl who was still listening.
