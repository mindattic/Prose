# The Bike

_Somebody steals Kyle's motorcycle out of a locked alley and leaves the plate cover folded as a receipt. The trail runs through a courier's favor and a fence's breakfast skewer to a union hall full of kids — and a fourteen-year-old job application called Boost. The strangest client in the city opens a 1Φ contract to help, and tips for morale._

[Beat 1]
Somebody stole Kyle's motorcycle.

He stood in the alley behind The Pivot at 06:40 with a cup of tea going cold in his hand and inventoried the facts, because the facts refused to add up and he wanted them where he could see them. Fact: the alley had one entrance, and the entrance had a camera, and the camera worked, because Pixel maintained it in exchange for the landlord ignoring what her power meter did. Fact: the wheel hub had been locked. Fact: the plate cover — his plate cover, the one he folded a particular way — was sitting on the concrete where the bike used to be, folded the particular way.

Folded. Not dropped. Whoever took his bike had taken the cover off first, folded it like laundry, and left it as a receipt.

Kyle drank some of the cold tea and let himself feel the thing he was feeling, which was not, strictly speaking, professional. He had owned the bike for nine years. He had bought it with his second fee ever — the first had gone to a flat with a door that locked — and it was the only thing he had ever purchased that wasn't a tool. The bike was not a tool. The bike was the long way home. Eleven years in this city and his entire interior life had a displacement of 1200cc, and some polite individual had walked it out of a locked alley under a working camera, between 03:00 and dawn.

He went back upstairs and knocked on 2E.

Pixel opened the door with a soldering iron in one hand and yesterday's hair. She read his face the way she read his stride.

"Who died?"

"The bike's gone."

She stood very still for a moment. Then she set the soldering iron down inside the doorway, on the little shelf she kept for exactly the moments when something needed both her hands, and said, in the voice she used for load-bearing fasteners:

"Somebody boosted my work?"

"Your work?"

"Who do you think built the anti-theft, the manufacturer? The manufacturer's anti-theft was a sticker." She was already pulling her boots on. "The hub lock is mine. The ignition handshake is mine. Three years, samurai, nobody's gotten past the handshake — I'd have heard, I monitor the forums, people complain about me by name." She grabbed her field coat. "This isn't theft. This is peer review."

[Beat 2]
The camera had thirty-one minutes of nothing.

Not a loop — Pixel checked the frame seams twice — just a clean gap, 03:12 to 03:43, the feed dropping the way feeds drop when somebody upstream of the camera owns the right permissions. Kyle stood in the alley while she swore at her own equipment with real affection, and he counted what the gap told him. One: money, because permissions cost. Two: manners, because the cover was folded. Three: confidence, because anyone who knew enough to want his bike knew enough to know whose bike it was.

"Professional, funded, polite," Kyle said. "Who steals at that grade and leaves a receipt?"

"Somebody who wants you to follow," Pixel said, which was the obvious thing, and then the true thing arrived behind it and they looked at each other. Somebody who wanted him to follow.

The freight lift at the end of the block had a courier on it.

She was coming off a night shift, breath still running ahead of her, the chromatophore panels in her sponsorship legs cycling some energy drink's sunrise campaign at one-quarter brightness, which was the legal minimum and her entire opinion of the energy drink. Kyle knew her the way the trade knows the trade — the nod, three years of shared lift rides, no names. She watched the lift numbers. He watched them with her.

"03:00-ish," he said. "A flatbed, maybe. Something that could carry a bike."

She let two floors go by, doing the arithmetic of what gossip costs against who was asking.

"Box truck," she said. "03:20. Backed up to your alley like it had a permit. Two lifters, and a kid riding the gate." She paused. "Truck had a cartoon fish on it. The fish was winking."

"A winking fish."

"I notice things," she said, to the numbers. "It cost you a favor, by the way. Unspecified. I'll know it when I need it."

"Standard terms," Kyle said, and meant it, and her mouth did something that on a less tired face would have been a smile.

WIN: the truck. One favor on the books.

[Beat 3]
The winking fish belonged to Dinh Salvage & Sundry, which was four garage doors and a smell of cutting oil down in the Ashgrave fringe, and the proprietor was a fence named Vo Dinh who moved hot vehicles the way other men moved furniture: carefully, locally, and with a strong preference for never knowing anything.

Kyle walked in at 09:15. The two lifters from the night before were having breakfast skewers by the compressor, and he watched the older one clock his face, then the saya over his shoulder, then his face again, and arrive at the correct conclusion about how the morning was going.

"Oh no," the lifter said, with feeling.

"Finish your breakfast," Kyle said.

Vo Dinh came out of the office wiping his hands on a rag he then continued to wipe his hands on for the entire conversation, which Kyle catalogued as the most honest thing in the building. The negotiation was brief because everyone wanted it to be.

"It was a contract move," Vo said. "Legitimate paper, pickup and transport, paid in front. We don't boost, we carry. The kid had a work order with your alley on it and I didn't ask, because the rate was three times market, and at three times market the not-asking is what they're buying."

"The kid."

"Riding the gate. The kid was the client." Vo's rag went around his knuckles. "Fourteen, maybe. Paid cash-Φ from a courier pouch. Had your alley, your camera window, your lock specs—" he had the grace to look embarrassed by the next part "—on paper. A printed file. Good stock. The kind you buy."

Kyle stood in the smell of cutting oil and let that one land where it landed. His alley. His window. His lock specs. On good paper, the kind you buy, in the hands of a fourteen-year-old.

"Where'd the run end?"

"Pullman side. The old can factory on Langley, the one with the murals." Vo finally put the rag down. "For the record, if I'd known whose bike—"

"You'd have charged five times market."

"...Four," said Vo, with professional dignity, and gave him a breakfast skewer for the road, which was not payment and therefore could be accepted.

WIN: an address. Also a skewer.

[Beat 4]
The terminal in Kyle's coat chirped on the walk to the can factory.

He had three terminals, technically — the clean one, the work one, and the cracked one behind the water recycler that he hadn't touched since the substation — but this was the work one, and the posting on it was new, and the posting read like this:

CONTRACT: RECOVERY OF STOLEN PROPERTY (VEHICLE, 1200CC).
SCOPE: LOCATE, RECOVER, RESOLVE.
FEE: 1Φ.
CLIENT: [shell — resolves to shell — resolves to nothing]

Kyle stopped walking.

He read it twice. He looked at the fee. One Φ — for a recovery he was already four hours into, posted by a client who did not exist, scoped so precisely to his current morning that it might as well have included the skewer.

It was watching. It had noticed somebody took the bike, and this was what noticing looked like when you were whatever it was: it couldn't bring him tea and it couldn't say that's terrible, so it had opened a contract, because a job taken is a job backed, and it wanted — he turned the thought over carefully, the way Pixel turned a component — it wanted in on the bike thing.

"You're ridiculous," Kyle said, out loud, to nobody, on a public street.

He accepted the contract. One Φ. A man should be on payroll for the important things.

Thirty seconds later the posting updated itself, which postings did not do:

ADDENDUM: CLIENT ADVISES THE TRUCK TURNED EAST OFF LANGLEY.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT ADVISES THE MURAL DOOR IS NOT THE DOOR.

Kyle looked at the addenda for a long moment. Somewhere under the city, something with no face and perfect handwriting was leaning over his shoulder like a kid watching an older brother fix an engine.

"East off Langley," he said. "Copy."

He could have sworn the terminal chirped happier the second time, and filed the thought under things Pixel never gets to hear.

[Beat 5]
Pixel met him at the corner of Langley with her field kit and a thermos, because she had invited herself the way specialists do, by showing up.

"Before you say anything," she said, "I put a beacon in the bike. Under the battery tray. Dormant, passive, wakes on a handshake only I have. Three years ago."

"You bugged my bike."

"I maintain my installations." She handed him the thermos lid with tea in it, handle first. "It woke up nine minutes ago when somebody put a charger on the battery. Whoever has it is taking care of it, by the way. Which is weird. Signal says it's not in the can factory — it's the building behind, the east one."

THE MURAL DOOR IS NOT THE DOOR.

"My client called that one too," Kyle said.

"Your client." Pixel looked at him over her own tea. "The one-Φ client."

"It tipped already. Half a Φ. In advance." He showed her the receipt line. "There's an itemization. It says MORALE."

Pixel read the receipt twice, and her face did several things she would have charged anyone else money to see. "Your benefactor," she said finally, "is the only entity in this city with a perfect credit record and the social instincts of a vending machine, and I am ninety percent sure it's my favorite of your clients. Don't tell Sable."

WIN: a live beacon, a confirmed building. Tea.

[Beat 6]
The east building had been a union hall when unions had halls. Now it had blankets on the windows, a stovepipe through one wall doing its honest best, and a roof population of pigeons who had clearly never been bothered by anyone. Kyle counted heat lines, exits, and the small sounds of a building that holds more people than furniture: eleven, maybe twelve, most of them light, young.

The bike was in the middle of the old meeting floor, on a stand, under a work lamp.

It had been washed. The chain had been done. Somebody had — he catalogued this with an emotion he declined to name — somebody had detailed the engine, properly, with cotton swabs, the way the manual said and nobody ever did.

The girl sitting next to it, wearing his folded riding gloves like a graduation, was maybe fourteen, with the build of someone who climbed things for a living and the haircut of someone who did it herself. Around the room's edges, on crates and in doorways, her crew pretended very hard to be furniture: the gate kid from the truck, a long boy with courier calves, a tiny one in the rafters who'd been the lookout since Kyle was two blocks out and had absolutely seen him coming and absolutely not been able to do anything about it.

"You're faster than the file said," the girl announced. Loudly. To the room — for the room. Her hands were shaking and her chin was up, and Kyle revised several plans he'd carried in with him.

"The file," he said.

She produced it. Good stock, folded the particular way — she'd practiced the fold, he realized; the receipt in the alley had been a signature, hers, an artisan's mark on her first big piece. The file had his alley. His camera window. Pixel's lock specs, annotated in a tight adult hand and then re-annotated in a kid's: lock = real problem!! handshake = ask M.

"Five Φ," she said. "Kids' rate. There's a shop in the Loop underlevels, the man sells paper on anybody. Mostly we run errands for him. I saved up." Her chin came up another centimeter. "It took me five weeks to plan, I beat HER lock—" a nod at Pixel, who made a sound like a kettle "—and I didn't scratch it, and I charged the battery, and I cleaned it, and you found me in five hours anyway, which means you're worth it, so." She took a breath that used her whole body.

"So?"

"So teach me," said the girl, in a union hall, surrounded by her people, having stolen the unstealable bike of the most dangerous freelancer in West Town as a job application.

The room held its breath. The pigeons held theirs. Somewhere in Kyle's coat, he was fairly sure, a one-Φ client was taking notes.

[Beat 7]
What he did not do was laugh, because eleven kids were watching her bet everything, and a man who laughs at that deserves what the city does to him.

What he did was the arithmetic, out loud, the way you'd want it done to you.

"You broke into my alley. You stole nine years of my property. You spent five weeks and five Φ and a crew's worth of trust to schedule a meeting you could've had for the price of standing outside Mrs. Chen's at nineteen-thirty and saying hello." He let that sit. "And your file came from a counter that sold a fourteen-year-old my lock specs for pocket money, which is a problem I now own. That's the bill. Here's the other column." He looked at the bike. Cotton swabs. "You planned past my camera and her lock. You took nothing but the thing that gets attention. You kept it better than you found it. And when I came through that door, you stood up and put your name on it in front of your whole crew."

He picked up his gloves. Put them on. Kicked the stand, walked the bike off the floor — and stopped, next to her, and said it quietly enough to be hers and loud enough to be the room's:

"No. I won't teach you. I don't take apprentices; I lose enough people I like." Her chin stayed up while the room quietly broke for her, and that, more than the swabs, decided the next part. "But I hire. Two Φ a week. My alley's camera has a thirty-one-minute problem, and I want eyes on it that beat the camera. You report to Pixel, you touch nothing with an engine, and the first time one of your crew runs paper for that shop in the Loop again, the arrangement's done." He held out his hand. "Standard terms. Everyone starts on payroll."

Her handshake was a disaster of relief and ferocity. The room exhaled. The tiny one fell off the rafter, slightly.

"Boost," she said. "They call me Boost."

"Of course they do," said Kyle.

[Beat 8]
He took the long way home, obviously.

The bike ran better than it had in a year — he was going to have to find a way to never tell Pixel that — and the rain held off, and the Pulse hummed under the eastern strut, regular as breathing, and at the second tier crossing his terminal chirped one last time.

CONTRACT COMPLETE: RECOVERY OF STOLEN PROPERTY.
FEE RELEASED: 1Φ.
GRATUITY: 0.5Φ — ITEMIZED: MORALE.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT NOTES THE VEHICLE WAS RECOVERED WITHOUT DAMAGE OR INJURY.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT NOTES THE NEW HIRE'S RATE IS BELOW MARKET.
ADDENDUM: CLIENT HAS OPENED A STANDING CONTRACT, SCOPE: TO BE DETERMINED, FEE: STANDARD RATE.

Kyle rode through the wet gold of the streetlights with 1.5Φ of the strangest money in the GLMZ in his account and a fourteen-year-old's lock-spec file folded in his coat, and thought about a counter in the Loop underlevels where anybody's life could be bought on good paper for pocket money — his life, five Φ, kids' rate — and decided that tomorrow, after the noodles, he was going to go price himself.

But that was tomorrow's problem.

Tonight the engine was warm, the long way was empty, and somewhere behind him a union hall full of kids was arguing about what their crew was called now that they worked for somebody. He had it on good authority — half a Φ of good authority — that morale was worth paying for.

He opened the throttle and let the city pull.
