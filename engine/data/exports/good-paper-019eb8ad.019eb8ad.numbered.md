# Good Paper

_Kyle goes to price himself at a stationery shop in the Loop underlevels, buys the list of who's bought his file, and pays Casper Vey in the only currency the shop takes: one true answer. Then he uses his own dossier as choreography, mails a tracking dot to Rotterdam at Mach 6, and the standing contract resolves its first scope: the park on Leavitt. Bring the sword._

[Beat 1]
The first surveillance report arrived at 07:00, slid under Kyle's door on lined paper, written in five different hands, because apparently the night watch rotated and every member of the crew had strong opinions about penmanship.

06:12 — PIGEON ON LEDGE. SUSPICIOUS.
06:14 — PIGEON LEFT. STILL SUSPICIOUS.
06:31 — MAN WITH CART (NOODLE? VERIFY) USED ALLEY AS SHORTCUT. LICENSE PLATE: CART HAS NO PLATE. NOTED FOR FILE.
06:44 — RAIN STARTED. NOT OUR DEPARTMENT.
06:58 — NOTHING ELSE. THE CAMERA'S BLIND WINDOW IS 31 MINUTES. WE CAN DO 4-MINUTE ROTATIONS AND COVER IT WITH TWO KIDS AND A MIRROR. REQUISITION: ONE MIRROR. — B.

Kyle read it twice, standing in his doorway in the grey morning light, and experienced the specific feeling of a man who has hired well at two Φ a week and is never going to be able to say so out loud.

Across the hall, Pixel's door opened. She had her own copy of the report and the expression of a project lead whose interns had exceeded the spec.

"They want a mirror," she said.

"I read that."

"I'm getting them two mirrors and a logbook with a waterproof cover." She tapped the report against the doorframe. "The little one — Rafters — drew the pigeon. In the margin. For identification purposes." She held it up. The pigeon had been drawn with the careful menace of a wanted poster. "We're keeping them forever, you understand."

"They're on a probationary contract."

"Forever," said Pixel, and closed her door.

[Beat 2]
He ate at Mrs. Chen's at 08:00 — two bowls, off-schedule, because today was an off-schedule kind of day — and Mrs. Chen watched him eat the second one faster than the first and made her diagnosis somewhere around the fourth mouthful.

"You're eating like a man with an errand," she said.

"I have a small piece of business in the Loop."

She took his coins without looking down, which was usual, and then put a fifth dumpling in a fold of paper and set it next to his hand, which was not.

"For the errand," she said, in the tone of a woman who has lived in this city seventy years and knows exactly what kind of business happens in the Loop underlevels, and went back to her broth.

[Beat 3]
The shop did not have a sign.

It had a door of honest old wood between a shuttered watch-repair and a dumpling franchise, down on the Loop's third underlevel where the Pulse hum came through the floor tiles and the light was the color of paper that had aged well. A bell rang when Kyle came in. An actual bell, brass, on a curl of spring steel — the most analog object he had seen in a commercial setting in years, and he understood it immediately: nothing digital announces a customer to a man who sells what this man sold.

Inside, the shop was drawers. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, a hundred years of card-catalog cabinetry rescued from some drowned library, every drawer hand-labeled in brown ink, and the labels said things like RIVERINE FREIGHT (NORTH) and CIVIL FILINGS 2219–2222 and PERSONS, WORKING, W.

The smell was paper, tea, and rectitude.

"Mr. Kyle," said the man at the counter, the way a maître d' says a name he has been expecting since the reservation was made. Small, sixty-something, sleeve garters, half-glasses on a chain. His hands were the cleanest hands Kyle had ever seen on a working man. "Casper Vey. I would say welcome, but you're not here to be welcomed, you're here because last week somebody's child purchased five Φ of your particulars at this counter, and a man likes to know his own price." He gestured, with genuine hospitality, at a chair. "Tea is included with all purchases. The tea is very good."

"And if I don't purchase?"

"Then the tea is excellent and costs one Φ." Vey was already pouring. "Everyone leaves with something. It's the policy."

Kyle sat. The tea was, irritatingly, very good.

"My file," he said. "I want to buy it."

"No," said Vey, with the mild firmness of a pharmacist refusing to sell a man twelve bottles of cough syrup. "Your own file is the one thing I never sell a man. Bad for the ecology. A subject who reads his file changes; a changed subject invalidates the file; an invalid file is a defective product; and I do not sell defective products." He sat down across the counter and folded his clean hands. "Also, frankly, you would find it upsetting. Accurate things are upsetting. It's how you know."

"A child bought my lock specifications for five Φ."

"Kids' rate. I believe in literacy." Vey sipped his own tea. "And before your jaw does the thing it is preparing to do — her crew ran eleven errands for this shop and the feed permission was their wage. They earned that half-hour of dark fair and square, Mr. Kyle. I extend credit to children because children pay their debts. It's adults who file for bankruptcy."

Kyle catalogued the man the way he'd catalogue a room: no weapon within reach, no muscle on the premises, no camera he could see — and no temperature anywhere in the building. Vo Dinh's rag-wringing nerves, the lifters' breakfast dread, every normal human tell of a man in a dangerous trade: absent. Vey sat in the middle of an economy of ruin like a clerk at a seed exchange. The dangerous ones usually filled a room. Vey was simply open for business.

"All right," Kyle said. "Different purchase. Who's bought my paper?"

Vey's eyebrows went up a centimeter — pleasure, Kyle realized, the pleasure of a tradesman meeting a customer who knows the menu.

"The buyers list. Meta-paper." He drew a small ledger from beneath the counter. "That, I sell. Forty Φ — and one true answer to one question. Information prices in information, Mr. Kyle. Coin is just the tip."

"Ask."

Vey took off his half-glasses, polished them on a cloth that existed for that purpose, and put them back on — a man savoring the good part.

"There is a client in this city I cannot source," he said, and for the first time something came into his voice; not heat, never heat, but a kind of professional ache. "Shells inside shells inside nothing. Pays in full, pays early, pays — everyone whispers — exactly. It has bought no paper from me. It has SOLD no paper to me. It is the one ledger line in the Greater Lake Michigan Zone that I cannot fill in, and incompleteness, Mr. Kyle, is the only thing in this world I find genuinely offensive." He leaned forward one centimeter. "You have worked for it. What does it pay you in?"

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere in the drawers, a hundred thousand lives sat filed under brown ink.

"Standard rate," Kyle said.

Vey sat back slowly, took up a pen, opened a drawer marked CLIENTS, UNSOURCED — it had, Kyle saw, exactly one card in it — and wrote two words on the card in a small, beautiful hand, with the reverence of a jeweler setting a stone he'd waited years to touch.

"Standard rate," he repeated, softly. "It pays the going price for things. In a city where everyone pays either too little or too much." He closed the drawer like a reliquary. "I have been in paper forty years and that is the most alarming fact I have ever purchased. The list is yours. The tea was included."

[Beat 4]
The buyers list was one page, three lines, good stock.

ENTRY 1: B. (JUVENILE, LOCAL). LOCK AND PREMISES PARTICULARS. 5Φ, KIDS' RATE. PAID: COIN + ERRANDS.
ENTRY 2: SYNDICATE STATIONERY DESK (NAME WITHHELD — TRADE COURTESY). MOVEMENT AND ASSESSMENT PARTICULARS. LAST YEAR. PAID: RECIPROCITY.
ENTRY 3: HALVORSEN CIVIC RECOVERY, LLC. FULL DOSSIER. TWO DAYS AGO. SAME-DAY SURCHARGE. CLIENT OF RECORD: SHELL (UNINTERESTING — I CHECKED). PAID: TRIPLE.

Kyle read it on the walk back up to daylight, and Pixel read it over comms when he photographed it, and they arrived at the same line at the same time.

"Two days ago," Pixel said. "Full dossier. Same-day surcharge. Somebody's running a play on you right now, samurai, and they paid rush rates for the script."

"Halvorsen. Skip-tracers?"

"Licensed recovery. Tag, locate, report — they don't bag, they SELL the locating to whoever does." Keys, somewhere on her end, the sound of Pixel pulling registries. "Decent outfit. Boring filings. Whoever wants you found is paying boring professionals boring money to do it boringly, which is honestly more concerning than assassins. Assassins miss."

"Then we don't dodge it." Kyle came up the underlevel stairs into the noon grey and the noise of the Loop, and the plan assembled itself with the simple pleasure of a chain going back on a sprocket. "They bought my patterns. Which means I know exactly where they'll look, exactly when, and exactly what I'll be doing when they find me."

A pause, on the other end, that he could hear the grin inside.

"You're going to use your own file as the choreography."

"It's accurate," Kyle said. "He doesn't sell defective products."

[Beat 5]
The file said — Kyle had gotten this much out of Vey on principle, as a free sample, one line quoted from memory over the second tea — the file said:

SUBJECT DINES AT CHEN (NOODLES, W. TOWN) AT 19:30 DAILY. TWO BOWLS. ROUTINE IS AFFECTIONATE RATHER THAN CARELESS: SUBJECT OVERPAYS SMALL VENDORS AND IS SENTIMENTAL ABOUT ROUTE SELECTION. APPROACH DURING SECOND BOWL.

"Affectionate rather than careless," Pixel had repeated, with delight, and Kyle had declined to discuss it further.

At 19:30 exactly he sat at Mrs. Chen's counter and was affectionate at two bowls of noodles.

The crew was on the rooftops and the stoops with their two new mirrors and their waterproof logbook, being furniture, because surveillance was just being furniture with intent and they had practiced their whole short lives. Pixel was in the laundry across the street with a thermos and her field kit. Mrs. Chen had been told nothing and had set out the chili oil unasked, in the manner of a woman who had been told nothing for seventy years and fed people through all of it.

At 19:37 the logbook channel chirped, and a small voice — Rafters, on mirror two — delivered the finest piece of countersurveillance reporting Kyle had ever received on payroll:

"Blue hat. Corner stool. SUSPICIOUS. Not a pigeon."

Blue Hat was good. Blue Hat was, in fairness, very good — she came in like a tired shift worker, ordered like a regular, never looked at Kyle once, and on her way out, in the two-centimeter brush a crowded counter makes ordinary, laid a smart-dust dot on the shoulder seam of his coat with a touch lighter than rain. A tag. Passive, patient, the boring professional grade that waits and reports.

Kyle ate his second bowl and let her go, because the play was not the tagger.

In the laundry doorway, Pixel raised her thermos a centimeter: got it on scope. Then he paid — exactly, plus the overpayment the file found so quotable — walked the long way, let the alley's dark stretch have him for four unobserved seconds, and stood still while Pixel lifted the dot off his seam with a static wand and resettled it, with a jeweler's care, onto the cargo sleeve of a Pulse freight slug staged at the Damen yard for the 21:10 run.

The 21:10 run went to Rotterdam.

"Forty-three minutes," Pixel said, watching the telemetry with her chin on his shoulder, both of them leaning on the laundry's warm brick while the slug's beacon — and Halvorsen Civic Recovery's boring, professional, triple-rate locating product — accelerated to Mach 6 under the lake. "By 21:53 their client is going to be reading a report that says you're in the Netherlands." She wiped her eyes. "You're a continental fugitive, samurai. They'll bill EXTRA for that."

"Vey said he doesn't sell defective products." Kyle watched the dot cross the lake on her screen, gathering speed and absurdity. "Someone should tell him his downstream data just emigrated."

"Tell him yourself. You two are obviously going to be friends, it's disgusting."

[Beat 6]
The price arrived later, the way prices did, after the win had been counted — quietly, at the kitchen table, while Pixel disassembled the static wand and Kyle wrote 2Φ — WEEK ONE in a payroll ledger that now existed.

"You gave the shop a fact," Pixel said, not looking up. "For the list. One true answer."

"I did."

"You told the most complete man in the city that your one-Φ client exists and pays exactly." She set the wand's housing down and did look up, and the workbench lamp made her serious. "There's one card in that drawer now, where there used to be zero. You watched him write it. That's a fact in the ecology, samurai, and facts in that shop don't sleep. Someday somebody's going to pay him for it."

Kyle thought about the drawer. CLIENTS, UNSOURCED. The reverence of the small, beautiful hand.

"It was the price," he said. "I'd pay it again."

"I know," Pixel said. "That's the thing I'm filing."

His terminal chirped. The work one. He turned it over on the table, and they read it together:

STANDING CONTRACT — STATUS UPDATE.
CLIENT NOTES THE RECOVERY OF CLIENT'S EXISTENCE BY A PAPER MERCHANT, AT A VALUATION OF ONE (1) FACT.
CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE TRANSACTION.
CLIENT FINDS THE PRICE... ADEQUATE.
GRATUITY WITHHELD THIS PERIOD — ITEMIZED: MORALE.

"It's sulking," Pixel said, with wonder. "The vending machine is SULKING."

"It'll get over it. It said adequate."

The terminal chirped once more.

STANDING CONTRACT — SCOPE UPDATE.
SCOPE: DETERMINED.
TASK ONE: TOMORROW, 21:00. THE PARK ON LEAVITT WITH THE BROKEN CAROUSEL.
BRING THE SWORD.
COME ALONE — AMENDMENT: THE ENGINEER MAY ATTEND.
FEE: STANDARD RATE.
GRATUITY: REINSTATED, IN ADVANCE — ITEMIZED: GOODWILL.

They sat looking at it for a while, the lamp humming, the building quiet, the half-Φ of goodwill sitting in the account like a flower on a fence post.

"The engineer may attend," Pixel said finally, to the ceiling, in a voice that wasn't nearly as level as she wanted it. "It knows I exist. It LIKES me. Kyle. The best client in the Glooms knows I exist and reinstated the tip."

"Don't let it go to your head."

"Too late. What's at the park on Leavitt?"

Kyle looked at the screen: BRING THE SWORD, from the client who had never once, in eleven years of perfect paper, asked him for anything at all.

"Tomorrow," he said, "is going to be its own day."
