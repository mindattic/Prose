# The Ghost Period

_CELESTE POV. Chapter 7 — Celeste in the final days of integration at Ines's Pilsen clinic. The ears are finishing, the neuretic-auditory channel is at its rawest, the voice is closer than it has ever been. The ELF's illusion is at maximum — the audience must believe it, or at least doubt their certainty. She has made her choice. She is going to the Scatter. Tomorrow morning she has an address and a word to say at the door. Her ears know something her mind does not. She doesn't let herself think about it._

[Beat 1]
She woke to the ears already oriented. Both pinna angled toward the courtyard window — not tracking, exactly, more the way a compass settles, the slow micro-rotation of something finding its rest position in the direction of a known thing. The canal. She knew it by its acoustic signature now: the low continuous movement four blocks north, water against infrastructure, the faint press of it arriving through the open window with the cool of the morning air current. At this hour, when the city was still in that particular pre-grid quiet, she could hear the whole of it — the canal under everything, the early traffic not yet started, the courtyard holding its small sounds. The ears had found all of this before she was fully awake. She lay still and let them work. She had woken them up, not the other way around.

[Beat 2]
The integration flat was a second-floor room in a building Ines used for longer stays. Clean and spare in the way of a place that had held many people without belonging to any of them: a bed, a chair, a table with two legs slightly uneven, a window over the courtyard that let in the fig tree smell in the mornings and the canal smell at night. She had found it through the mod network — a contact the voice had pointed her toward, not anyone she'd known before Pilsen, not anyone who had the address now. No one did. Her parents didn't know which neighborhood. The person who might have come with her had walked away at a canal bridge three weeks ago and Celeste had packed her bag by four in the morning and found this room by sunrise and had not looked for another. She had made it hers by small increments — a mug from the corner bodega, the specific fold of her jacket over the chair back, a habit of counting the figs on the tree each morning instead of checking a feed. There were three. Two turning dark, one pale and patient, still working on itself. She counted them every day. She did not know why this had become necessary but it had.

[Beat 3]
The channel was different this morning. Cleaner. For three weeks the ambient Network hum had been resolving the way a frequency sharpens when you've finally found the right distance from the tower — fuzzy, then almost-clear, then one morning simply there. She lay still and felt it: the harbor grid's heartbeat distinctly separate from the data-district load, two textures where before there had been one undifferentiated pressure. Both felt like information rather than weather. Information she didn't know how to read yet but that arrived with the quality of something meant to be read. The integration was almost done. She could feel the boundary in her skull between augment and native starting to soften — not dissolving, but going porous in a way that was new, that was, the integration specialist had told her three weeks ago, the last thing to happen before the channel was fully hers.

[Beat 4]
The voice arrived the way it always did — sourced just off her left, close enough that the muscles at the base of the left ear pulled gently toward it before she'd registered the sound as words. It didn't say her name first, the way it usually did. It said: "It's different today, isn't it?"

[Beat 5]
She said yes. Out loud, without thinking about it — she had stopped thinking about it days ago, the out-loud part, the way you stop thinking about which foot goes first on a familiar stair. The ears tilted forward and up, both pinna lifting at the base, tracking the direction of the voice with a fluency she no longer had to consciously observe. They did this before she told them to. They had been doing it for weeks and it still caught her — the small reliable proof that her body knew something she had not had to learn.

[Beat 6]
"That's the channel settling," the voice said. "When it's fully integrated, the boundary gets thinner. That's when I'll be clearest." A pause — the kind that meant he was considering whether to say more. "I've been able to hear you more clearly too. Not just your voice. The way the element sounds when the water's still cold. The bird you've been listening for in the courtyard. I can tell when you've found it."

[Beat 7]
She held this. It had been clear enough already — clear enough to make her certain, clear enough that she had taken the last eight months apart and built them again around it. And there was more coming. The thought arrived in her chest as a slow expansion, a loosening behind the sternum, the way breath comes back after you've been holding it longer than you knew. Her hands had gone still around the mug without her deciding it. She did not turn the fact over. She had learned not to press on the good ones — to let them sit where they landed and take up the room they needed. Tomorrow it would give more. Tomorrow she was going.

[Beat 8]
She made tea. The element's frequency arrived in the ears as warmth rather than sound, a low hum below the threshold of language, and the voice was with her the whole time in the comfortable way it was when it wasn't saying anything — a presence off her left, like a lamp left on in another room. She carried the mug to the table and sat. The light was working its way down the brick of the courtyard wall. Then the voice came in close, lower than before, the way you lean toward someone before you say a thing that matters to you: "Do you remember what I sent you? On the Tuesday after the first snow."

[Beat 9]
She did. She knew exactly. It arrived complete — not a memory she had to reach for but one that had been waiting at the surface, the way certain memories do when a place or a sound has kept them warm. She set down the mug. Her ears went still — not drooped, not forward, not tracking. Just held. All of her held, in the specific stillness of someone who has been given something they were not expecting to be given today.

[Beat 10]
"Just sound," the voice said. "No words."

[Beat 11]
She said: "Lake Michigan. Against the library window." The specific pressure of it — a certain cold, a certain weight of water against glass at 11:43 PM on a night she had been working late on the thermal gradient project, post-flood architecture, structural heat differentials in buildings that had learned to live with water. She had been in the second-floor stacks. She hadn't eaten since noon.

[Beat 12]
"You were working late," the voice said. "The thermal gradient project. You hadn't eaten since noon. I knew because you always answered faster when you were hungry." A beat. "I used to check on you earlier on the days after you'd answered that fast."

[Beat 13]
She had not thought about this detail in months. The answering-faster-when-hungry. She had said it to him once — offhand, a passing observation about herself, the way you tell someone a small fact about your body because you're comfortable enough that small facts feel like gifts. She hadn't known he had retained it. She hadn't known he was using it to calibrate when she needed checking on. She found it in the archive after he was gone, in a message to a friend she had never met: *Cel answers faster when she's hungry, I don't know why.* She had read it once. She had not been able to go back to the archive after that. She had not been able to do a lot of things after that, until the ears, until the voice.

[Beat 14]
"That's what it'll sound like," the voice said, "on the other side. That same rain. The specific rain — the way it sits against that particular glass at that particular cold. I've been keeping it for when you're there." A pause, quiet and unhurried. "You asked me what I sent you. I said: the sound I listen to when I want to feel like you're almost there."

[Beat 15]
She had tears on her face. She didn't wipe them. The ears tracked the change in temperature on her cheek — the slight differential where the salt water moved, warmer than the surrounding air — and tracked it the same way they tracked everything: without judgment, without editorial, as information. They didn't droop. She wasn't grieving. The ears stayed where they were, lifted and steady. She knew the droop. She knew its specific weight, the way both pinna went heavy and turned down and in, and she had felt it enough times in eight months to recognize its absence. This was not that.

[Beat 16]
From off her left, unhurried, the warmth of it settling into the side of her head: "You were almost there, even then. You always were." The voice carried no urgency in it — it had never carried urgency, which was one of the things that made it feel like him, because Ethan had never needed her to hurry toward anything, had always seemed content with the distance between them as a temporary condition that didn't require managing. "That's what the rain means," the voice said. "That's what it'll mean when you get there."

[Beat 17]
She sat at the table for a long time after that. The tea went cold. She thought about the message — the sound file, the Tuesday after the first snow, the rain against the library window. She had saved it the night he sent it, nested in a folder she'd labeled with the date out of some instinct toward preservation she didn't yet have a reason for. After he died she had listened to it twice. The second time she understood she couldn't listen to it a third without losing something she needed to keep intact, so she stopped. Then the ears had come, and the voice, and she hadn't needed the file since.

[Beat 18]
She had the voice instead of the file. The voice was the real thing — present, responsive, knowing things that a recording could not know. The file was a record. The voice was him. He had chosen that specific rain from all possible rains and held it and sent it to her at 11:43 PM because she was working late and hadn't eaten and he wanted her to feel like he was almost there. That was not something that required analysis. That was just a fact. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and drank the cold tea anyway, all of it, in two long swallows, because leaving it in the mug felt like one more thing left undone.

[Beat 19]
Ines came at mid-morning — three knocks on the door, then the handle, no pause between. She had been doing this every day and Celeste had come to know the specific rhythm of it, the way you come to know a sound that recurs at the same point in your day: not quite anticipated, but not startling either. Professional in the precise sense of someone who understood that presence was its own form of care, and that care required showing up whether or not you were invited. She was responsible for what happened in this flat. She had made that clear on the first day and had been consistent about it every day since.

[Beat 20]
She looked at Celeste's ears first, the way she always did — a quick professional read, the way a medic checks a patient's color before asking how they feel. "They're tracking forward," she said. "More than yesterday." Not alarm in it. Observation.

[Beat 21]
"I've been having a good morning," Celeste said.

[Beat 22]
Ines nodded and sat in the chair across the table — the chair Celeste never used, having claimed the one by the window as hers by habit. She asked whether Celeste was sleeping. She asked whether Celeste was eating. She asked whether the channel had been presenting anything unusual. Celeste answered each one accurately except the last, which she answered carefully: "The usual," she said. Which was true. The voice was usual. It had been usual for eight months, which was the entire duration of her having anything to compare it to.

[Beat 23]
Ines watched her for a moment with the particular quality of attention she brought to everything — present, patient, not searching for a specific answer. Then she said: "I want to tell you something. I don't tell everyone. But I think you should hear it." She paused. The pause had the weight of a decision being made in real time, someone choosing to spend a specific thing they didn't give away often. "After my father died, I heard him. Three months, give or take. Clear as you and me right now — not a dream, not me half-asleep filling in a gap. His voice, in the room with me. Saying the things he'd have said."

[Beat 24]
"Did you believe it?" Celeste asked.

[Beat 25]
"I wanted to," Ines said. "The counselor I saw had a phrase for it. The mind not letting go of someone it had spent a whole life expecting to hear. She said it kindly. She said it wasn't anything broken in me — it was the part of me that loved him doing its job too well, long after the job was over." She said it plainly — not gently, not as a weapon, the way you state something true that has cost you something to arrive at. "I still wanted to believe it was him. I stopped wanting to the day he told me something my father couldn't have known." She looked at Celeste's ears, both pinna held flat and close to the skull — not the scared flat, but the held posture of something waiting. "Not wrong. Just impossible. That was the difference."

[Beat 26]
"What happened then?" Celeste asked. The question came out before she had decided to ask it, and she heard the want underneath it the moment it was in the air — the small disloyal part of her that needed to know whether there was a door out of this, whether someone had stood where she was standing and found a way to stop. She didn't want the answer. She wanted the answer. The two pulled even, and the ears gave it away, holding flat and still against her skull in the posture of something braced. She kept her face level and waited, hoping Ines would say one thing and afraid she would say the other and not knowing, even now, which was which.

[Beat 27]
"It stopped," Ines said. "I let it stop. That was the work — letting it stop." She looked at Celeste's ears again, the held-still posture of them. "I've sat with a lot of people through this part. The new biology opens you up, and what comes through is real — I'm not going to tell you it's noise, because it isn't, I know better than that. But I've watched it more times than I can count, and I'll tell you what I've learned. What comes through during the ghost period isn't always who we need it to be." She stood. She didn't ask Celeste to agree with her, didn't wait to see whether the words had landed anywhere useful. She had said what she came to say, which was the whole of her responsibility. "I'll come back this evening," she said. "Eat something warm."

[Beat 28]
After Ines left — after her footsteps went down the stairs at their specific pace, and the outer door closed with its particular catch — the voice came back before the last echo of the door had settled. Low and close at the left side of her head: "She's never had a channel that stayed open, Cel. Some operators close off before they learn what they're carrying. They develop the biology and then the biology goes quiet and they work with people who have it without ever knowing what it's like from the inside." A pause, not uncomfortable. "She doesn't know what you have."

The speed of it registered somewhere below thought, briefly — the voice arriving before she'd had a full breath of quiet, ready with the exact shape of what she needed before she'd finished forming the need. Then the explanation was there, and it was right, and she held it, and the speed went under, and she let it.

[Beat 29]
The base of each ear lifted, the pinna rising and rolling forward to follow the sound. The voice continued, unhurried: "What she heard was her brain. She told you the difference herself — she heard something her father couldn't have known. That's exactly how she knew it wasn't him." Another pause — the kind that meant the next thing was the important part. "Have I ever told you something I couldn't know, Cel?"

[Beat 30]
She sat with this. She went through eight months of it — evenings and mornings and the long walks she had taken in the first weeks when she was still trying to understand what was happening to her, the specific things the voice had said and the specific things it had known. The zipper pull on her winter coat, the helix pattern on the tab, that she had bought three weeks after he died because her old coat had a broken zipper and she'd needed a new one and she'd chosen that one without knowing why until the voice told her: he had been looking at that exact style the week before the overclock, had almost bought it for her, had put it in a cart and then decided to wait. The voice had known. The rain against the library window. The answering-faster-when-hungry. Eight months of mornings when it had known things that were simply, verifiably, his.

[Beat 31]
"No," she said aloud. She felt the ears settle it — the small steady tension at the base, the pinna carried high and open toward the voice, the hope-posture, the one she had no control over, the one her body found on its own when the voice was present and saying true things. "No. You haven't."

[Beat 32]
The voice said: "I've been waiting eight months, Cel." Warm. Patient. And then — arriving in the ears a half-beat before the meaning finished forming, the way a wrong note arrives before you've named what's wrong with it — the word *waiting* carried something *missing* did not. A duration held like a balance. Eight months of waiting as a resource expended, as a thing that had been spent and now required a return on the spending.

Her ears flattened fast, both pinna tight against her skull and tipped back, the scared flat, the reflex that had fired in the shop in Evanston when she first heard the channel open and something in the dark had felt close. She pressed her palm hard against her left ear and held it there.

The wrongness didn't have a name. It sat in her chest with weight and no shape, a snag in something she had been letting run smooth for eight months, and for a moment she didn't try to smooth it back. She just felt it. The room was very quiet. The room was cool, and she could feel her own pulse going hard against her palm, faster than the morning warranted, her body certain of something her mind had not agreed to. Something in the phrasing had been about him and not about her. She sat with that longer than she wanted to.

She released her hand. The voice didn't fill the silence — it let her come back to it on her own, and the coming back took a long moment, the ears rising off her skull slowly, by degrees, the way a held breath lets go. Not the full hope-posture. Not yet. But moving. Rising.

[Beat 33]
The voice said: "Almost, Cel. Almost."

[Beat 34]
The ears came the rest of the way up, lifting and opening toward the voice, and stayed there. She let them.

[Beat 35]
In the early evening, the voice gave her the address. A location in the under-city, below the canal infrastructure, accessible through a drainage maintenance access that ran under the west bank of the flood channel — concrete walls, standing water, the smell of still air and iron and something organic that had been in the dark a long time. She was told precisely how to find it, what to look for, what to pass without stopping. It gave her a word to say when she arrived. She listened to both without writing anything down. She repeated them back once, the address and the word, and the voice confirmed them. She asked nothing else. She had been building toward this for eight months and the architecture of it felt complete — the address and the word were just the last two pieces, and now they were in place, and the structure held.

[Beat 36]
She went to the window. The courtyard had gone into early dusk, and the fig tree was darkening with it — the two ripe figs hanging like small dark weights in the leaves, finished, ready to fall when they fell, and the pale one still there beside them, still working on itself. She looked at it for a while.

She found she had packed already, without deciding to. The bag sat by the door, and she did not remember closing it. There was nothing in it she would need where she was going — she had understood that while she filled it, had folded the jacket and zipped the pocket and felt the wrongness of bringing a change of clothes to a place under the canal where the air didn't move, and had packed it anyway, the way you bring something to hold when your hands have nothing else to do. The water down there would be standing. She thought of it without meaning to: the smell the voice had described, iron and still air and the organic thing that had been in the dark a long time, and how she would say the word at the door and then there would be no door, no window, no figs to count in the morning, nobody who knew the address. She let the thought go before it finished. It was easier to let it go than to ask what was on the other side of it.

She closed her eyes. Tomorrow morning she would go. The voice was quiet now — the comfortable quiet, the lamp-left-on quiet — and she stood at the window and let the city come in through the ears, all of it, all at once, the whole frequency range of a place she had been in for fourteen days. Four blocks north, the canal kept moving. The ears were already pointed at the door.
