# Work Order

_A dead corponation's demolition automaton wakes up after thirty years and resumes a job the city grew into. The crew needs Pixel's hands more than Kyle's sword — and the sword, for once, is the wrong tool entirely._

[Beat 1]
Pixel knocked on his door, which had happened four times in four years and meant the world had changed shape in some way she intended to explain.

She had a tablet under her arm and her field coat on — the one with the burn scars on the left sleeve, the one she wore to jobs, not to benches — and she walked past him into 2F and put the tablet on his kitchen counter where the kettle should have been.

"I have a contract," she said. "I'm crewing it. I need muscle that listens, and you're the only muscle I know whose listening I've personally calibrated."

On the tablet, in somebody's shaky handheld video, a machine the size of a four-story building was eating a row house in the Ashgrave fringe. Eating was the word. It took the structure down a bite at a time, unhurried, and behind it the rubble of the last block stood in three neat sorted piles — ferrous, mineral, everything else — like a meal it had finished and cleaned up after.

"That's an Axiom D-class," Pixel said. "Demolition and reclamation automaton. It woke up nine days ago, and it is very good at its job, and its job is now four hundred meters from two thousand people who live in the direction it's chewing."

[Beat 2]
The brief, over his kitchen counter, with the kettle eventually involved:

Axiom Industrial had been dead thirty years, which meant the D-class had no owner, no kill-codes on file, and no manufacturer support line — the company died and the substrate stayed. Arcturus Civil Security had assessed the situation, determined that unowned industrial movables outside contract zones were not in their service catalogue, and left a cone. The residents of the Ashgrave fringe had pooled what the Ashgrave fringe could pool: 312Φ, held in a biscuit tin by a woman named Auntie Som, who had sent the video to a parts man, who had sent it to Pixel, because everyone in the salvage trade knew that the only person in the GLMZ who still spoke Axiom bus protocol like a living language worked at a bench in West Town.

"The pay is 312Φ," Pixel said. "Split six ways it's insulting. I'm taking it anyway, and so are you."

"I know," Kyle said.

"You haven't heard the plan."

"I've heard the pay. The plan's not going to make it worse."

[Beat 3]
"You're not going near—" Kyle started, and Pixel set her cup down with a click that ended the sentence.

"Say the rest of that word and my rate doubles," she said. Not heated. The voice she used on a fastener that thought it was load-bearing. "The D-class runs a pre-2200 Axiom control bus. There is no remote interface — Axiom didn't trust radio, they were right, and it died before anyone retrofitted one. The bus has one physical maintenance spur, and the spur is on the chassis, and the chassis is where I will be standing, because the alternative is nobody closes this thing down and the city loses a neighborhood at the speed of careful chewing."

She turned the tablet around. A schematic, hand-annotated in her tight engineer's print, years old — she had owned this schematic before this job existed.

"I have wanted to talk to one of these since I was nine," she said. "You are not protecting me from it. You are buying me ninety seconds in front of it. That's the job, samurai. Price it like one."

Kyle looked at the schematic, and at the annotations, and at his neighbor of four years standing in his kitchen with her field coat on, and did the thing the moment actually called for, which was arithmetic.

"Standard rate," he said. "Yours is double. Specialist on site."

"Correct," Pixel said.

[Beat 4]
The crew came together in thirty hours, because crews came together fast when the job was real and the pay was a joke — the trade's way of sorting itself.

Echo took eyes and comms, which was Echo taking the high ground and becoming a voice in five skulls, the half-step-off voice that had never once been wrong about a sightline. Stash took transport and gear: a flatbed he refused to explain, cases packed for every door in the brief and nothing for any door that wasn't.

The new hands came from the parts man's recommendation and one of Echo's old ledgers. Gantry was a rigger out of the lakefront cranes — a big, slow-moving woman who said maybe forty words a day and spoke to suspended loads in the second person. Furnace was a demolition op with a thermal lance and the cheerfulness of a man whose tool solved most arguments; he had burned scrap in the Ashgrave yards for a decade and knew the ground.

Five of them and Pixel, in the back of Stash's flatbed, driving toward a machine the size of a building, on a contract worth less than the fuel.

"For the record," Furnace said, happily, "I've never worked a job this stupid."

"Stick around," Echo said, from everyone's skull at once.

[Beat 5]
They walked the eaten blocks first, because Pixel wanted to see its work.

The thing was tidy. That was the part that climbed into Kyle and stayed. The rubble of nine days stood in sorted piles at exact intervals — ferrous, mineral, organics — each pile compacted square, each street swept after, the lamppost bases unscrewed and laid in rows like cutlery. It had taken a city block apart the way Pixel took apart a neural reader: with the intention of accounting for every piece.

In the middle of the fourth block, the piles stopped mattering, because the machine was there.

Eleven meters at the shoulder. Four legs, two boom arms, a hull of sintered gray monocoque that had been printed in one piece in a yard that no longer existed — no plates, no rivets, no panel lines, a body with no seams at all. Studding the hull every meter or so sat low black discs, and around each disc the rain steamed faintly off the surface.

"Arc-fence," Pixel said, following his eyes. "Anti-tamper. Axiom yards lost too much hardware to strippers, so the late units came dressed in it — capacitive plates, the hull's one big charge bank. Anything conductive touches the skin, the skin touches back. Forty thousand volts of touches back."

She looked at Silence's saya, and then at Kyle, and made it explicit, because she was the specialist and the specialist priced the risks out loud.

"Your sword is a key for doors, samurai. That thing has no doors. No seams, no cable runs, nothing surface-routed — Axiom buried it all a meter deep in structure. There is nothing on that hull for an edge to mean. And the edge is steel and carbon — conductive. You touch it, you're not cutting, you're grounding. It will cook your neuretics inside your skull, and the burn scar will be the shape of your own blade."

"So I don't touch it," Kyle said.

"You don't touch it," Pixel said. "Tonight your job is everything that isn't it."

[Beat 6]
It kept a schedule. That was the plan's whole foundation, and it was Pixel who had seen it in the video timestamps: the D-class worked a shift. It started at 0600. It stopped at 2200. And at 0300 it walked itself back to the dead Axiom yard and docked against a transformer pylon to charge, the way it had every night for nine days, the way — Pixel said, quietly, looking at the schematic — it had probably been doing in 2196, before somebody powered it down mid-rotation and walked away.

"It docks, the arc-fence drops to maintenance levels, the spur goes live," she said. "Axiom units safe themselves for service. It's in the standard. I get ninety seconds on the spur, I shake hands with the bus, and I find out why it's awake."

"And if the handshake goes wrong?" Echo asked.

"Then it objects," Pixel said. "And the five of you get to be more interesting than I am."

[Beat 7]
The Axiom yard at 0250 was rust and sodium dark and the long shapes of dead gantries against the sky. They took positions the way the brief drew them: Echo on the crane cab with the night optics; Stash and the flatbed at the gate with the engine warm; Gantry up in the rigging of the old loading frame, where two hundred tons of abandoned shipping container still hung from cables she had spent the afternoon talking to; Furnace at the transformer with his lance dark; Kyle on the ground, between all of it, in the place where the geometry said trouble would have to pass.

At 0301 the ground learned a rhythm. Four legs, each footfall arriving through the soles of the boots a half-second before the sound.

At the yard's edge, the D-class stopped. There was a crossing there — two dead lamp poles and the ghost of paint, a pedestrian crossing on a street that had not carried a pedestrian in twenty years — and the machine stood at it, and waited, exactly as long as a signal cycle nobody had powered since before Kyle was born. Then it crossed.

Nobody on comms said anything. There was nothing to say that the waiting hadn't said.

[Beat 8]
It docked. The boom arms folded. The hull's black discs dimmed one by one, the steam off the skin thinning, and somewhere inside the monocoque a contactor closed and the transformer pylon began, gently, to sing.

"Maintenance state," Pixel said, on the channel, already moving. "Fence is down to tickle. Ninety seconds. Count me."

She crossed the yard at a walk — a walk, because running hands shake — with her kit slung and the schematic in her head, and she stepped over the cone Arcturus had left, and she put her bare hand flat on the hull the way she put it between Kyle's shoulder blades. Checking a temperature before trusting a readout.

"Hello, you absurd old thing," she said, too quiet for the channel, and found the spur.

Kyle counted. The whole yard counted. The handshake chattered in Pixel's earpiece, Axiom bus protocol going both ways for the first time in thirty years, and at second forty-one of ninety, a power flicker rolled through the dead grid — somewhere a substation breathed, the pylon's note wavered a quarter-tone — and the D-class's watchdog process, which had kept this machine alive through three decades of abandonment, made the only decision a watchdog knows.

Every disc on the hull lit at once.

"Off the chassis—" Echo said, and the machine stood up out of its dock with Pixel's spur cable still live, and the night went to work.

[Beat 9]
The first thing it did was finish standing, which took two seconds and rearranged the entire yard's geometry. The second thing it did was deploy.

Hatches Kyle would have sworn were not there — the monocoque admitting, after all, to apertures, on its own schedule — opened along the lower hull, and the skirmishers came out: knee-high yard-keepers, six-legged, a maintenance swarm repurposed by the watchdog into a perimeter, two dozen of them flowing off the chassis like spiders off a kicked log.

"Pixel's clear of the envelope," Echo called. "Cable's still in the spur. The spur's still live — if she can get back on it she can finish the handshake. Kyle—"

"I see them."

The skirmishers were everything the hull was not: seamed, jointed, cabled, built to be repaired — built, in other words, to be taken apart. Kyle drew Silence, and the night divided itself into a simple arithmetic: two dozen small machines between Pixel and ninety seconds of work, and a blade that had been the wrong tool for everything tonight except exactly this.

He went into them.

[Beat 10]
It was geometry in the dark, and the geometry was generous: the skirmishers ran a herding pattern, not a killing one — the watchdog wanted the soft intruders off the chassis, not dead — but a six-legged yard-keeper at speed did not distinguish ribs from rebar, and two of them together could take a leg off at the knee through enthusiasm alone.

Kyle worked the seams. Hip actuator on the first, where the cable loom crossed the joint; the second hooked low at his ankle and he gave it the boot it wanted, took the weight onto the planted leg, and opened its lid along the dorsal service line — the blade finding the gap the way a key finds its own lock. Two down. The third and fourth came together and he made them arrive separately, a half-step of timing that cost a slash across his forearm from a manipulator claw — shallow, hot, catalogued.

"Four," Echo counted, flat. "Twenty. Left side, three more, Kyle."

Gantry's voice arrived for the first time all night, slow as a load swinging: "Heads down in the south lane."

The container came off the loading frame on released cables, two hundred tons arriving where Gantry had been promising it to the ground all afternoon, and the south half of the skirmisher line stopped existing as a line.

[Beat 11]
Furnace tried the hull once, because Furnace was a professional and professionals confirm.

The lance ran eleven thousand degrees at the tip, and he laid it against the D-class's foreleg for a full three-count from behind a slag barrier, and when he pulled it away the monocoque wore a glassy scorch the size of a dinner plate. Not a hole. Not a wound. A polish.

"That's me out," Furnace said, cheerfully, packing the lance. "Structure's sintered the whole way through. You'd want a week and a mining permit."

The foreleg turned. A boom arm came around, unhurried — everything the big machine did was unhurried, which was somehow worse — and swept the slag barrier away with Furnace already running, and Kyle was there, between, doing the thing his body had decided before his neuretics could file an objection.

He parried.

Steel-CNT met monocoque for a quarter-second, and the arc-fence did exactly what Pixel had priced. The world went white and sideways. The discharge took him off his feet, through the bones, out the heels — he was airborne long enough to understand he was airborne — and the ground arrived like an invoice. His neuretics browned out whole: the overlay gone, the counts gone, the array's quiet arithmetic gone, eleven years of second heartbeat slapped silent, and Kyle lay in the rust mud of a dead corponation's yard as just a man, with a numb arm and a blade still locked in his fist and the smell of his own scorched glove going up his nose.

Silence was unmarked. Silence was steel; it had no opinion. The burn across his palm was the exact shape of the wrapped grip, printed in.

He got up anyway, because the count in his head still worked even with the hardware down, and the count said Pixel needed thirty more seconds of the watchdog's attention.

[Beat 12]
"Kyle's hit," Echo said, and the channel did not panic — trusted-crew rule, the voice goes flatter as things get worse. "He's up. He's moving. Pixel, status."

"Back on the spur." Her voice had the bench in it — the register she used over his shoulder blades, narrating so the work had a person at both ends. "Handshake's holding. It remembers me from forty seconds ago. Watchdog's split — half its attention on perimeter, half on me. Keep the perimeter busy."

Kyle kept the perimeter busy. He did it without the overlay, reading the skirmishers' tells with his eyes alone — the settle of a leg before a lunge, the way the swarm's flank packed tighter when it meant to push — and Silence went through service seams and actuator looms by feel and training and nothing else, the discipline carrying the body the way it had been built to when the body was all there was. Five more came apart. The forearm slash got a sibling across his thigh. Stash's flatbed came through the gate at second seventy with the plow edge down and herded the herd, and Gantry's second container landed where the survivors had agreed to regroup.

"Seventy-five seconds," Echo said. "Pixel."

"Almost. It's showing me something."

[Beat 13]
"It's not broken," Pixel said, and her voice had changed — the bench gone out of it, something younger underneath. "Nobody corrupted anything. Listen: work order 7741-C, opened March 2196. Block demolition and reclamation, eleven structures, Ashgrave reclamation phase two. It completed ten. Then the yard lost power and somebody walked away and never closed the ticket."

The D-class stood over its dock, boom arms half-deployed, two dozen of its children dead in the mud, and the watchdog watched her read its homework.

"It's been asleep on an open work order for thirty years," she said. "Nine days ago a scavenger crew lit up the yard transformer to strip it, and the wake-on-power did what wake-on-power does. It's not eating the city, Kyle. It's finishing its shift. The eleventh structure is gone — demolished in '08, built over. It can't find it, so it's working the survey grid block by block until it does. The geofence isn't corrupted." A breath. "The city grew into its permit."

"Can you close the ticket?" Kyle asked. His palm had begun, distantly, to inform him about the rest of his week.

"I'm a certified Axiom maintenance bus," Pixel said. "I've been certifying myself for twenty years. Watch me."

[Beat 14]
She closed the work order the way the standard demanded: completion codes, reclamation tallies — she entered the sorted piles, all nine days of them, because they were real work and the ledger should say so — and a sign-off in a dead corponation's maintenance dialect, transmitted down a spur cable by a woman standing in the open ten meters from the thing's foreleg with her hood thrown back.

The D-class went still in stages. The discs dimmed. The boom arms folded, with the particular care of something stowing tools it expected to need again. The hatches took the surviving skirmishers back aboard. Then it walked — past Kyle, past the flatbed, footfalls arriving through everyone's boots — to the center of the yard it had been built in, and turned once, the survey habit of a machine confirming its ground, and folded itself down onto its knees and shoulders.

The pylon's note died. The yard went dark and merely dark.

"Shift's over," Pixel said, to the machine, too quiet for the channel, and coiled her cable.

[Beat 15]
The accounting happened at dawn, at the edge of the fringe, out of a biscuit tin.

Auntie Som counted the 312Φ onto a folding table in front of the whole street, because money counted in front of everyone stays counted, and Kyle took the crew's shares the way the crew had set them: standard rate five ways, Pixel's doubled, which came to numbers small enough to be funny and exact enough to be serious. Furnace laughed at his share and took it. Gantry bit hers, which may have been a joke; nobody had the data to be sure. Echo's went into a dead drop. Stash's went into the flatbed's tank, approximately.

Kyle took his. The fee can be the size of a gesture; it is never nothing; a man who works for free is a man whose work has no floor. Auntie Som watched him pocket 26Φ with the expression of a woman recalibrating a rumor, and put two foil containers of food on top of it without asking, and that was not payment, so it could be accepted without arithmetic.

"The big one," she asked. "It's dead?"

"It's off shift," Kyle said. "It finished."

[Beat 16]
Pixel took her salvage fee in kind, with the residents' blessing and a crowbar: the D-class's master clock — Axiom-sintered, thirty years drift-free, the heartbeat her bench had been improvising around since the relay — and the work-order seal module, which she held up to the dawn like a jeweler.

"Every Axiom unit in the lakes answered to one of these," she said. "There are maybe four left that still validate." She wrapped it in the cloth she saved for things that mattered. "Now there are maybe five."

Kyle stood beside her with his bandaged palm and his browned-out neuretics rebooting in slow stages — the overlay assembling itself back across the world like frost growing in reverse — and looked at the folded machine in the middle of its yard, eleven meters of finished business.

"You talked to it for ninety seconds," he said. "Worth it?"

"Twenty years of wanting to," Pixel said. "Ninety seconds of getting to. That's a better rate than most wants ever pay." She shouldered the clock. "You shook its hand."

"It shook back."

"I told you it would." She started toward the flatbed. "Next time, samurai, when the specialist says don't touch it—"

"Standard rate," Kyle said. "I touched it at standard rate."

[Beat 17]
A week later the laundry came.

Somebody on the fringe ran the first line from a ground-floor window to the D-class's folded foreleg, and by the weekend there were nine lines, sheets and work shirts and a yellowed wedding tablecloth drying in the lake wind off eleven meters of the tidiest demolition in GLMZ history. Kids dared each other to slap the dead arc-fence discs and ran away screaming on the inhale, the way the children had run under the cherry trees. A vendor moved her cart into its shade on hot afternoons.

Kyle rode past it once, the long way, three fragments of skirmisher manipulator still working their way out of his forearm and his palm still wearing the grip of his own sword in scar tissue, and did not slow down, and did not need to.

The piles stayed sorted. Nobody touched the piles.
