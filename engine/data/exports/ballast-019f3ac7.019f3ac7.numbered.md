# Ballast

_Aerobloc Candelaria has forty-two days left in the sky.

The eigenlift frame is failing. The math is public. The descent into The Low is scheduled, legal, and unstoppable — unless forty-one households vote to sell to AshgraveMaterials and take the buyout.

Teo Vasquez is the structural engineer. She knows the weight of every unit, every debt, every quanta owed to the frame. What she can't calculate is whether Priya's daughter's neuretics are worth less than the vote. Whether Wen's grandfather — forty years in a workshop that won't survive the drop — owes the bloc his cooperation. Whether Dagny, eighty-one years old and the heaviest mass in the building, will choose the payout or the floor she knows.

The vote is coming. The frame is failing.

BALLAST is a story about forty-one households and the mathematics that cannot tell them what to do — and the weight of having to choose anyway._

[Beat 1]
The core hummed, and Teo Mamani didn't like it.

[Beat 2]
A healthy frame ran silent. That was the whole of the Hush — sixty years of coherence drivers holding Aerobloc Candelaria in the air so cleanly that nobody in a kitchen four decks up ever thought about physics. When the frame strained, it hummed, low and rising, like a held breath let out too slow. Five years had taught Teo the difference between a hum that meant nothing and a hum that meant the schedule needed changing. This one meant the schedule.

[Beat 3]
She stood on the core's lower gantry with her wrist display open to the stress readings. The bloc was down at the nose by a hair, tilted a quarter-degree toward the east — nothing a resident would feel. Teo felt it. Three months ago the readout had been clean. Now a tremor ran along the main field, a stutter in the coherence lattice, and no repair she could propose would be one the co-op could pay for.

[Beat 4]
Almagre worked beside her. She had stopped trying to fix the automaton in one place years ago; it was everywhere its readouts were. It adjusted trim limits a half-second before she'd have called for them. It didn't speak unless asked, and this morning she hadn't asked. She liked that. It made no case for itself. It just held the ceiling she gave it.

[Beat 5]
"Projected coherence decay," she said, and the wrist display answered with a curve she already knew. Candelaria: built 2166, sixty years old this spring. Forty-one households in a cylinder twelve decks tall, stacked around a core that was failing the ordinary way old things fail. Current altitude, three hundred eighty-three meters. Losing close to a meter a day, and the rate was climbing.

[Beat 6]
Three hundred twenty meters was the number that mattered. Below it lay The Low — no governance line, no Air Tax bracket, no insurer willing to write a policy on a home that had dropped out of the city's counted air. Above that line, Candelaria was a residence. Below it, the bloc was salvage waiting for a claimant. Forty-two days, by her own schedule, until jettison stopped being optional.

[Beat 7]
She had built that schedule herself, week by week, and she never softened it, not even for herself. Every other conversation in Candelaria ran on hope or fear. The schedule only subtracted.

[Beat 8]
She opened the mass ledger next — everything not bolted into a household's fixture rights. The cistern tank on deck nine served six units and could serve four if she moved the piping. The storage floor on deck three had sat half-empty since the Alvarenga family left for a ground-tier block two years back. Forty crates of maintenance stock nobody had counted in years. None of it was anyone's grandmother's chair. It was just mass, sitting where it had always sat, waiting for someone to notice it could sit lighter, or not at all.

[Beat 9]
She marked the storage floor first. Not because it was heaviest — the cistern beat it easily — but because she could move it without anyone downstairs feeling their home shift. The best trim was the weight you could take without anyone noticing it was gone.

[Beat 10]
"First candidate," she told Almagre, and watched it log the mark without comment.
"Provisional. I want two more weeks of readings before it moves."

[Beat 11]
Two weeks she might not have, if the hum kept climbing the way this morning's readout promised.

[Beat 12]
She closed the display and stood a moment on the gantry, listening. Under the Hush, thin as a wire pulled tight, ran the hum, telling her what forty-two days actually meant.

[Beat 13]
Twelve decks up, forty-one households were making breakfast, sending children to the transit lift, painting over last week's number on the commons wall with this week's. None of them knew that a man named Ruslan Adeyinka had already drafted a letter that would land in all their wrist displays within the week. Teo didn't know it either. She knew the number on her display, the number on the wall, and the widening gap between what the bloc weighed and what it could still afford to weigh.

[Beat 14]
She logged her shift, gathered her tools, and went up to walk the spine.

[Beat 15]
The commons wall was the first thing anyone saw stepping off the transit lift, and this Monday someone had already been up a ladder with a brush. Last week's number was gone under a coat of pale grey. In its place, in numerals a hand-span tall: 382. Under that, in smaller letters that had been there since before Teo took the job: ALTITUDE, METERS, POSTED WEEKLY. And under that, added years ago in a different hand: WE ARE STILL HERE. Nobody had ever painted over it.

[Beat 16]
Teo stood in front of the wall until she was sure the number matched her own readout. Then she started her walk.

[Beat 17]
Candelaria ran twelve decks around a central core — cheap lift, honest structure, forty-one households in a shape that kept the trim math simple and the hallways narrow. Sixty years old this spring. She'd read the commissioning plates once, down on deck one where nobody went anymore, and the stamped date — 2166 — had startled her the way old dates did. The bloc had been rising and falling since before her mother was born on a tether platform two hundred kilometers east.

[Beat 18]
She walked deck by deck, the way she did when the numbers wanted checking against the real thing. Deck three: the storage floor, half its crates gone to dust under a skin of condensation, the rest stacked by people who'd stopped believing anyone would come sort them. Deck five: the handholds outside 5-C, worn pale and smooth where a woman nobody remembered had steadied herself for thirty years before she died in her own bed. Everyone since had put a hand in the same place, more from habit than need. Deck seven: an Air Tax notice taped beside a door, three weeks old, the bracket a notch lower than the notice before it. Air Tax was a fee on altitude, and Candelaria had less of it every week — a bloc at 382 meters paid a smaller bracket than the same bloc had owed at 400. The one bill in Candelaria that got lighter as the home sank, and nobody in the building wanted the mercy in it.

[Beat 19]
She knew which households sat over which structural members. It wasn't sentiment; it was the job. If a quadrant had to lose mass — six units on the water main instead of nine, a corridor closed because the frame under it had gone soft — she needed to know whose morning that would rearrange before she ever proposed it to Sigrun. Deck nine held the widow in 8-D, who had outlived her husband and, this year, the shop income he used to bring in. Deck eleven held a family of four crammed into space meant for two, still waiting on a transfer list that hadn't moved in three years. They weren't mass to her. They were households, and she never let herself forget it.

[Beat 20]
On deck four she passed a hand-lettered sign she'd seen a hundred times: CASTELLANOS — MACHINE SHOP, ESTABLISHED 2186. Forty years, near enough. She'd been inside twice, both times for load readings, and both times the man had watched her instruments the way some people watch weather.

[Beat 21]
She got her third reading of the day on deck four, and the number came up wrong. She took it again, holding the instrument flush to the frame the way the calibration wanted, and it came up wrong the same way — the whole deck reading two meters below the wall number, below what the core sensor fed her, below what her feet told her. She worked the frame from the corridor's north end to its south, and the discrepancy held, steady and impossible, a bias that didn't drift the way honest error drifted. Either the deck-four member had already sagged clean out of trim without a single crack to show for it, or the instrument she'd trusted for six years had started lying to her, and she had no way to tell which — not from the corridor, not this morning, not with the number due on the wall Monday next and thirty-eight days behind it. She stood there longer than the reading needed, and the instrument gave her the same wrong answer every time she asked.

[Beat 22]
Near the core on deck two, a boy she didn't know ran past toward the transit lift, late for school. An old man leaned on the rail — a retired machinist off the third floor, out for his morning air — and nodded to her the way people nodded to the person who painted the number on the wall. Not warm, not unkind.

[Beat 23]
She stopped at the outer window on deck six, where the city dropped away in its long grey haze: the lake a smear of light far below, the Gray Zone beyond it, the Spires further still, catching sun that hadn't reached the bloc yet. From here Candelaria looked like exactly what it was — a home for people who couldn't afford to live higher, holding its lift like a tired arm holding a weight too long.

[Beat 24]
Freeboard, she called this. It was an old word for the margin between the water and the rail on a surface hull, back when hulls floated on water instead of holding themselves up against nothing. Candelaria's freeboard was altitude now — sixty-two meters between where the bloc sat and where the law stopped caring what happened to it. Sixty-two meters was not small. At a meter a day and climbing, it also would not last.

[Beat 25]
Three hundred eighty-two meters. Thirty-eight days until jettison stopped being a word on her schedule and became a fact in someone's hallway.

[Beat 26]
She turned back toward the core, already building the week's posting in her head — not the words, the words never changed, just the number. She knew which walls held which lives, and which lives, if it ever came to moving quadrants instead of crates, could bear to be moved without the bloc losing anything but square footage. It was a short list.

[Beat 27]
The letter came on a Wednesday, through neuretics, landing in forty-one inboxes at the same hour of the same morning, so nobody in Candelaria could claim they'd heard it first.

[Beat 28]
Teo read it at the core gantry rail, three days into the week's readings, because Sigrun had forwarded it to her before it went out wide. A courtesy, or a warning — she hadn't decided which. The sender line read AshgraveMaterials Reclamation Division, and under it a name: Ruslan Adeyinka, Senior Broker. She didn't know the name. She knew the company the way anyone who worked mass and structure in this city knew it. AshgraveMaterials bought failing lift assets before the law could seize them for nothing, stripped what could be stripped, and sold the rest as certified salvage. It didn't demolish blocs. It priced them, and then it owned whatever it had priced.

[Beat 29]
The letter was short, which surprised her more than what it said.

[Beat 30]
AshgraveMaterials Reclamation Division is prepared to purchase the structural mass of Aerobloc Candelaria in full, effective upon co-op ratification. Total offer: Φ847,000. Distributed by the co-op's standing allocation formula in proportion to each household's registered mass, this represents an average of approximately Φ20,650 per household. AshgraveMaterials will additionally fund relocation to ground-tier residential blocks for all current residents, coordinated through the co-op chair. This offer does not expire on a rolling basis; it will remain open for the co-op's full deliberation period. A structural assessment accompanies this offer as an appendix and is available to any resident on request.

[Beat 31]
She read the number twice. Φ847,000 wasn't arbitrary. Someone at AshgraveMaterials had run the frame's recoverable mass against current salvage rates and landed on something specific, not rounded up to look generous. It read like one of her own postings. That was what unsettled her — not the amount, but that whoever built it thought in the same units she did.

[Beat 32]
She opened the appendix, out of habit, to check the structural math. To her annoyance, it was clean. Coherence decay curve, current altitude, projected time to the 320-meter line — all of it matched her schedule within a rounding error. Whoever wrote this had either pulled her weekly postings, which were public, or run an independent assessment good enough to land in the same place. Either way, Ruslan Adeyinka wasn't guessing. He'd read the bloc from the outside and reached the conclusion she reached every morning and told no one: the frame was old, and old frames didn't recover. They only bought time against a curve that always won.

[Beat 33]
What the letter didn't say, because it had no column for it, was what Φ20,650 would mean in each of the forty-one households it landed in. It wouldn't mean the same thing twice. Some would read it as a rescue. Some would read it as an insult. She thought, without wanting to, of Prita Guzman's kitchen — the account balance she'd caught once by accident in a projected overlay, the shortfall this number would very nearly close. She thought of the machine shop on deck four, forty years of calibration that Φ20,650 could never buy back.

[Beat 34]
Faint through the shared channel every household kept half-open for co-op business, she could already feel the bloc's attention shift. Nothing on that channel was private; the co-op's whole life ran in the open, postings on a wall, offers read aloud. She felt the change the way she felt a change in the hum — not words yet, just weight moving, forty-one households feeling the same number land in forty-one different places at once.

[Beat 35]
Almagre logged the letter without being asked, filing it under Co-Op Correspondence — Pending Ratification. Teo let it. That was accurate, and the automaton's filing never carried an opinion she'd have to argue with.

[Beat 36]
She sent no reply. It wasn't hers to answer. Her job started after a decision existed, not before. But she stayed at the rail longer than the reading needed, watching the letter sit open beside the week's coherence curve — two documents that agreed with each other completely, and would, she suspected, agree with nothing else in the bloc for a long time.

[Beat 37]
Below, the commons wall read 380, freshly repainted that morning. Someone would have to decide, soon, whether that number was a countdown to a landing or to a sale. Teo closed the overlay, logged her hours, and went to find Sigrun. Whatever the letter turned into, the chair would need the schedule laid beside it before she called anyone into a room to talk about either one.

[Beat 38]
Sigrun called the meeting for the second evening after the letter — not so soon that people would say she'd rushed them, not so late that anyone could say she'd sat on it. The common space on deck one filled the way it always did for something that mattered: fast at first, then slow, then all at once in the last five minutes, until every bench was taken and people stood along the walls with the stillness of a room that already knows what it came to hear.

[Beat 39]
Forty-one households. Not every seat held a full one; some sent a single person, some sent two, a few sent nobody and would get the minutes over the shared channel by evening. Teo counted the room out of habit — near seventy adults, fewer children, most of the children still in school transit or asleep. She stood near the core-access wall, not at the front. This wasn't her meeting. She was there to answer questions about a schedule, not to make an argument. She'd made her argument every Monday for five years, in paint, on the wall outside.

[Beat 40]
She could pick out the names that would matter before the night was over. Prita Guzman sat near the front, her wrist display angled toward her own knee, the way people sit when they already know which number tonight is theirs. Wen Castellanos stood rather than sat, arms folded, near the door he'd have to pass to get home to deck four. Dagny Obuya sat alone near the back, upright, hands folded, giving nothing away. Almagre was only a faint, steady hum in the lighting rig, holding the room's trim without being asked.

[Beat 41]
Sigrun stood at the front with no podium, no notes, her wrist display dark. She was Icelandic-Brazilian, silver-haired, built like someone who'd spent forty years fixing things with her hands before she ever fixed anything with a gavel. She'd chaired the co-op for six of those years by being the person nobody minded hearing hard news from. She let the room settle before she spoke, and when she spoke, her voice didn't reach for anything.

"Two things tonight," she said. "I'll give you both before anyone asks a question, because I want you hearing them together, not one used against the other."

[Beat 42]
She gave them the first plainly. "Current altitude, three hundred eighty meters. Teo's schedule has us losing it at close to a meter a day, and the rate isn't improving. At this trend, we cross three hundred twenty — The Low — inside the year. Before that line, Teo's jettison program begins. Best estimate, forty days from tonight."

[Beat 43]
Nobody looked surprised. That was the strange part. None of it was new — it had been posted all season, every Monday, in paint a hand-span tall. But knowing a thing and hearing it said out loud in a room full of your neighbors were not the same thing at all.

[Beat 44]
"Second," Sigrun said. Her voice didn't change, though the room's did — a held breath spreading bench to bench. "Three days ago, AshgraveMaterials Reclamation Division sent the co-op a written offer. I'm going to read it exactly as it came, because I don't trust myself to say it better, and because nobody in this room should hear it secondhand."

[Beat 45]
She read it. All of it — the total, the per-household figure, the relocation clause, where to find the appendix — in the same flat, careful voice she'd used for the altitude. By the time she reached Φ20,650 per household, it landed with no more and no less weight than three hundred eighty meters had a minute before.

[Beat 46]
When she finished, she let the silence hold longer than most people would have dared.

[Beat 47]
"I'm not going to call a vote tonight," she said. "No show of hands. I'm not asking anyone to speak who isn't ready to. This offer stays open through the co-op's full deliberation. We'll meet again — more than once, probably. Tonight I wanted you to have both numbers in the same room, at the same time, from the same voice, so that whatever you end up thinking, you thought it with the whole picture in front of you."

[Beat 48]
She stepped back, just enough to signal the floor was open. The room did what rooms do after news like that: it didn't speak first. Seventy adults looked at nothing, and for a long moment nobody wanted to be the one who broke the quiet Sigrun had built out of two hard facts laid flat.

[Beat 49]
Teo watched from the wall, and knew the silence wouldn't last.

[Beat 50]
It was Prita who broke it, the way Teo had half-expected someone would — not with a speech, but with a question aimed at the whole room instead of at Sigrun. She raised her hand the way a person does when she's already decided to speak and is only waiting for her nerve to catch up.

[Beat 51]
"I want to ask something plainly," she said, standing, "because if I don't, someone else will ask it less plainly, and I'd rather it came from me."

Sigrun nodded her forward.

[Beat 52]
Prita was younger than Dagny, older than Teo, dark-haired, in the practical clothes of someone who'd come straight from a shift. Her voice held steady. But Teo, watching from the wall, saw the tell before the words: one hand pressed flat against her own sternum, the way you hold a weight in place.

[Beat 53]
"My daughter Kaja needs a corrective procedure on her neuretics integration," she said. "It's not optional and it's not cosmetic. It's the cochlear feeds, and the window for it closes in months, not years. The fund I've built for it is short. Exactly short — Φ8,400 short, as of this week's balance. I've done the math enough times that I don't need to check it again to say the number out loud in front of you."

[Beat 54]
Nobody moved. She'd done this on purpose, Teo understood — put an exact number into a room where every number tonight had been exact, because a vague hardship could be waved past and a precise one couldn't.

[Beat 55]
"I'm not going to pretend the offer solves everything," Prita went on. "I know what people will say about it — that it prices this bloc like it's already dead, that it hands our home to a company that profits either way. I've heard myself think all of that this week. But Φ20,650 a household, if it lands anywhere near the average, closes my daughter's shortfall with room to spare. I don't know how to stand here and pretend that isn't the first thing I think about every time I look at the number on that wall." She paused, and her voice dropped — not from doubt, from care. "So I want to ask honestly, and not as an accusation to anyone: is there another household in this room counting the same kind of shortfall against the same kind of clock? Because if there is, we should know it before anyone decides this is only my problem."

[Beat 56]
The silence after that wasn't the same silence as before. The first quiet had been a room waiting to see who would speak. This one was a room doing math against its own life, all at once, in private. A few heads turned toward wrist displays that weren't showing anyone else anything. Nobody answered Prita aloud. Whether that meant no one else carried a shortfall like hers, or only that no one was ready to say so in front of seventy people, Teo couldn't have told her.

[Beat 57]
Kaja wasn't in the room. Prita had mentioned once, weeks ago, in the ordinary way people mention their children to the person who fixes their water pressure, that her daughter was twelve and hated being talked about more than almost anything. Tonight bore it out. No small figure at Prita's side, no name spent for sympathy — just the bare fact of the shortfall on the table and the girl kept out of the room. It was its own kind of restraint, the kind that cost something to keep up.

[Beat 58]
Near the door, Wen hadn't moved. His arms stayed folded, his weight settled back against the frame. If Prita's number had reached him the way it seemed to reach everyone else, nothing in his stance said so. Teo noticed the stillness the way she noticed a reading that refused to shift when everything around it moved.

[Beat 59]
Sigrun let the quiet run out before she spoke. "Thank you, Prita," she said, and nothing else — no comfort, no agreement, no argument for the offer. That wasn't the chair's job. She was there to hold the room level, not to tip it.

[Beat 60]
Teo watched Prita sit back down, the hand that had been at her sternum now flat on her own knee. Prita had just done something close to what Teo did every Monday on the wall: put an honest number where everyone could see it, and refused to soften it. The difference was that Teo's number only ever subtracted. Prita's, if the vote went her way, would be the first number in this whole slow business that added something back.

[Beat 61]
Φ8,400 belonged on none of Teo's sheets. She wasn't going to forget it either.

[Beat 62]
Wen didn't raise his hand. He pushed off the doorframe he'd leaned against all evening and let his weight carry him a few steps into the room, and the room, which had been turned toward Prita, turned toward him without being asked.

[Beat 63]
"I'm not going to argue with what she said," he said, nodding once toward Prita without quite looking at her. "Her number's real. I believe every part of it." He let it sit. "I want to put a different number next to it, so you all have both."

[Beat 64]
He didn't reach for his wrist display. What he had to say didn't live in a balance.

[Beat 65]
"Household 23. My grandfather bolted a machine shop into that floor forty years ago — 2186, if anyone wants the year exact. Two lathes, three dies, a bank of jigs he built by hand because the ones he could afford to buy weren't square enough for what he needed. I've spent thirty of those forty years learning what he knew about calibrating them, and I'm not finished learning it. That shop isn't furniture. It's fixture. It's bolted into the frame the same way the water main is, and there's no version of moving it that doesn't mean starting thirty years of calibration over on a floor that's never held those machines before."

[Beat 66]
He looked, finally, toward Sigrun, though what came next was for everyone.

[Beat 67]
"I know what happens to fixture mass if this bloc sells. AshgraveMaterials doesn't relocate machine shops. They strip them for the metal and call it certified salvage, and the number they'd give me wouldn't buy back a single jig — because you can't buy back thirty years of a man's hands learning a machine. So when I say no to this offer, I'm not saying Prita's shortfall isn't real. I'm saying mine is real too, and mine doesn't have a number that closes it, because what I'd be losing isn't the kind of thing money was ever built to replace."

[Beat 68]
He didn't explain any further. He didn't say why the shop couldn't be taken apart and rebuilt somewhere else, floor by floor, given time and money and patience. Teo, watching from the wall, understood that he wasn't going to, and she kept that to herself. It wasn't hers to press.

[Beat 69]
She'd been in that shop twice for load readings, and both times she'd noticed the same thing — how quiet the machines sat when they weren't running.

[Beat 70]
Near the back, Dagny hadn't moved, her hands still folded, her face giving away nothing about which number she found more persuasive, if either. Teo had sat through enough meetings with her to know the stillness wasn't agreement and wasn't disagreement. It was just how the woman listened.

[Beat 71]
"That's not a question," Sigrun said quietly when he'd finished — not a correction, an observation, the kind she made when she wanted a thing on the record as exactly what it was.

"No," Wen agreed. "It's not."

[Beat 72]
He stepped back to the doorframe and folded his arms again. The room had two numbers now that didn't fit in the same column — one money could close, one money couldn't touch — and it settled into a different quiet than either that had come before. Teo felt it the way she felt a bloc settle after a jettison: not resolved, just moved, the argument's weight sitting in a new place.

[Beat 73]
Sigrun didn't call on anyone else. She let the two positions stand where they'd landed — Prita's shortfall in plain sight, Wen's refusal plain beside it, neither erasing the other — and closed the evening the way she'd opened it.

[Beat 74]
"We meet again before any decision," she said. "Nothing changes tonight. I wanted you hearing this from each other before you heard it secondhand."

[Beat 75]
The room broke apart slowly, the way rooms do after news that starts something instead of finishing it. Teo stayed against the wall a while, watching Prita gather her things without looking at anyone, watching Wen leave first through the door he'd held up all evening. She had two loads on her sheets now that would never balance against each other.

[Beat 76]
The wall outside the commons read three hundred seventy-seven meters that morning, freshly repainted, lower than last week's by exactly what the coherence readings had promised. Teo had painted it herself the night before, same as every Sunday night. She didn't stop to look now. She already had next week's figure half-built in her head, the way another person carries a grocery list.

[Beat 77]
Her real work that morning was smaller and further from anyone's notice: the household allocation ledger, open on her workroom desk, the cursor on the line for the household two floors down from Wen's. One working adult, one twelve-year-old, not much mass past that.

[Beat 78]
She'd known the shape of Prita's household before she ever ran a number. You learned a household's mass from its water and power draw long before its jettison quota came due, and Prita's drew like a home already trimmed to exactly what it needed — two beds, a kitchen fitted for meals that didn't waste, a closet that didn't hold a season it wasn't wearing. When Teo ran the standard formula — declared mass against total allocation, the same formula every household had agreed to sixty years ago and never revised — the figure came back Φ2,100 in goods, jettison-eligible if an order ever reached that floor.

[Beat 79]
It wasn't a large number. She sat with it before she entered it. Against the Φ8,400 Prita had put into the room three nights ago, precise to the coin, Φ2,100 didn't close a quarter of the distance. Even every jettisonable scrap in that household, sold at the generous end of the salvage schedule, wouldn't touch it. Teo checked the figure twice, hunting for an error she half hoped to find. There wasn't one. Prita's own allocation could never have funded the procedure. The only path that closed that gap ran through Ruslan's offer — Φ20,650 a household if the vote passed, riding on top of whatever a family's own quota carried out the door. Prita's Φ2,100 wasn't the number that mattered. It was the number underneath the one that did.

[Beat 80]
It worked like a lever, and Teo knew levers. A small mass set at the right distance from the center could tip a load a much larger one, set wrong, never would. Prita's Φ2,100 sat close to the center, almost nothing on its own; the Ashgrave payout sat far out on the arm. That was the whole of why it mattered and her quota didn't.

[Beat 81]
She posted the figure anyway, because posting figures was the job. A household's quota went up on the wall every week whether or not it changed a single vote. Five years had taught her not to weight a number by how much it might cost someone to read it. The wall didn't soften the figure for the widow in 8-D, and it wouldn't soften this one for Prita.

[Beat 82]
Her hand stopped over the confirm key. The declared-mass entry on Prita's line didn't match the draw readings she'd carried in her head — someone had revised the household's declaration since the last cycle, upward, and the ledger had swallowed the change without flagging it. Run the formula against the new figure and Φ2,100 became something else entirely; run it against what she knew the household actually held and the two numbers forked, each of them defensible, each of them a different verdict for the same two beds and the same closet. The formula every household had agreed to sixty years ago and never revised assumed the declaration was true. It didn't tell her which one was. She sat there with both numbers open, and neither would resolve into the other no matter how many times she checked the arithmetic — because the arithmetic wasn't the thing in doubt.

[Beat 83]
What she didn't write into the ledger — nothing on it was built to hold this — was the second fact sitting under the first. If the vote failed, if Ruslan's offer went nowhere and Candelaria rode its own frame down past three hundred twenty meters on schedule, Prita's household would still owe its jettison quota. The bloc's mass had to come down either way — that was the frame's physics, not the vote's outcome — except then there'd be no Φ20,650 coming in behind it. Prita would lose the same Φ2,100 no matter how the room voted. The only thing the vote changed, for that one household, was whether the loss arrived with a payment attached or arrived alone.

[Beat 84]
Teo closed the working file and opened the version built for the wall — stripped of names, just floor numbers and totals, the way public postings had always run here. Nobody's shortfall went up there except the one a household chose, out loud, to say first. That had been Prita's choice three nights ago, not the ledger's.

[Beat 85]

Prita took that in without answering right away, and found that she believed every word of it. That made the thanking harder, not easier.

[Beat 301]
"Is there a difference," she asks finally, "between voting yes because the bloc's sinking and voting yes because it closes my daughter's shortfall? I don't mean it as an argument. I mean it plainly, the way you just meant your answer."

[Beat 302]
Dagny looked at her a long moment, with the same stillness Prita had learned to read as neither agreement nor refusal. She didn't answer. Her hand came up and rested on the doorframe, light, the way a person steadies against a weight only she can feel. Prita felt her own throat go tight, the same tightening from the kitchen table three nights ago, and let it sit there this time instead of waiting it out alone.

[Beat 303]
"Thank you," she said again, quieter this time.

Dagny nodded once, the way she nodded to close anything, and stepped back from the door at last — not to let Prita in, only to close it, gently, on a conversation that had finished saying what it was going to say.

[Beat 304]
Prita walked back down the corridor the way she'd come, past doors with nothing posted on them at all. She didn't try to answer her own question. It stayed with her the rest of the day, unfinished.

[Beat 305]
The morning after the vote record went up, Teo opened a new file in the ledger. Not a projection this time, not a split she'd run a dozen times against a maybe, but the actual wind-down schedule — the document that would replace the Monday altitude posting as the thing this bloc measured its time against.

[Beat 306]
Thirty days. Ashgrave's relocation window, the one Sigrun had read aloud twice now, started counting from the morning after the tally. That made today day two.

[Beat 307]
She built the schedule the way she built everything, in columns. Household. Current mass allocation. Relocation date. Receiving address at ground-tier. Structural elements tagged for the strip crew that would follow once every household had cleared its own things out from around them. Forty-one rows. She'd built plenty of documents this year with forty-one rows in them. This was the first one that ended instead of continuing.

[Beat 308]
She ran the driver readings first, the way she did every morning, vote or no vote, because the frame didn't know an offer had been accepted and wouldn't have cared if it did. Driver eleven was still running warm, same as it had for months, the Hum sitting exactly where it had sat at the gantry with Ruslan. She logged the figure in its own column, apart from the wind-down file. The two documents measured different things now. One told her how the frame was aging. The other told her how much longer that aging would matter to the people living inside it.

[Beat 309]
Ashgrave's engineers would take over the coherence drivers on day twenty-eight, two days before the window closed, and step the frame down from there — not letting it fail on its own curve, not racing the six weeks Ruslan had named at the gantry, but bringing Candelaria to the ground on a schedule Ashgrave controlled instead of the frame. Teo entered that line the way she entered every other line, without comment.

[Beat 310]
She worked through the households in order, checking relocation addresses against the ground-tier assignments the co-op had gotten back from the housing authority two days earlier. The widow from 8-D to a unit three blocks from her sister's. Wen's household to an address with, Teo noted, far less floor space than a machine shop needed — a fact nobody had asked her to enter, and she entered it anyway, because the ledger's job was completeness, not comfort. Dagny's line she entered last among the households proper. Address confirmed like everyone else's. Mass allocation the same Φ4,800 she'd posted weeks ago. No note added, none needed.

[Beat 311]
She reached Prita's line and confirmed the closing figure she'd finalized two nights before — the one that no longer rested on the word if — and moved past it the way she moved past every finished figure now, without lingering.

[Beat 312]
She reached her own line last, the way she always did. It had never mattered in what order she entered anyone else's mass, and hers had never mattered to anyone but her.

[Beat 313]
Household allocation: fifty-one kilos. She typed it into the relocation column the same as Wen's exempted zero and Dagny's four thousand eight hundred, and moved the cursor to the next field.

[Beat 314]
The receiving-address field. The one the housing authority had returned filled for forty rows and blank for hers. She checked the return sheet against the roster, then checked it again, because a gap in a document she'd built was the kind of error she caught in other people's work before they'd noticed making it. No blank this time. There was an address entered against her name — she just hadn't been the one to enter it. Ground-tier, but not the co-op's block. It was a decision about where she belonged when this ended, and the ledger, for the first time all year, had asked her a question instead of recording an answer.

[Beat 315]
Then she got up, crossed the small room to the single shelf that held what she owned, and began.

[Beat 316]
It didn't take long. It was never going to take long. The four books first. The two changes of clothes. Then the tools, wrapped in the same cloth she'd carried through every posting before this one, corners tucked the way they always were. She picked up the small gauge she used for driver readings and held it a moment longer than the wrapping took — the same gauge she'd checked against the Ashgrave technicians' instruments that morning — then set it with the others and folded the corners over. The room gave up its whole contents in under ten minutes. The same room that had taken most of an afternoon to survey when it was Dagny's household, before any of this had a vote attached to it.

[Beat 317]
She sealed the crate, checked the latch twice, and set it by the door, in the exact spot where she'd find it in thirty days without having to look. Then she went back to the desk and finished the schedule. Forty-one households, one line each, hers no different in the column from anyone else's. [Beat 318]
Four weeks out, the strip crew's provisional schedule went up on the commons wall, two lines below the descent notice, cross-referenced against the household relocation dates the way everything on that wall cross-referenced everything else now. [Beat 319]

[Beat 320]

Tonight he noticed it on purpose.

[Beat 321]
A drawer of loose tools without the machines they were built for wasn't a shop.

[Beat 322]

Wen ran a hand along the near one's bed the way he did most evenings, habit more than inspection, though tonight he was doing both at once without quite meaning to. Behind it, the bank of jigs he'd carried into the common space three weeks ago sat back on its shelf, returned the morning after, exactly where it had always lived. Six shapes he could have named blind by their weight alone.

[Beat 323]

Then his own beside them, cut a decade later, when the first set had worn shallow from thirty years of the same measurements taken the same way. He'd told himself, cutting that second set, that he was preserving something. [Beat 324]

Nobody was going to. He'd stopped expecting it around the night he laid the jigs out on the vote table and watched the room look at them without arguing.

[Beat 325]

[Beat 326]

[Beat 327]

[Beat 328]
