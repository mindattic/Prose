The hold notice went up on Mrs. Chen's stall on a Tuesday, laminated, official, and entirely fabricated.

Kyle read it standing in the spot where he'd eaten two bowls a night for six years. **COMPLIANCE HOLD — PUBLIC HEALTH (PENDING REVIEW).** Eleven complaints in nine days, it said. Grease-trap violations, an unregistered cold line, a customer who'd filed an allergic reaction with a clinic that, the notice was careful to specify, had a record on file. Pending review meant the stall stayed dark while the review took its time, and reviews, in this city, took exactly as long as someone was paying for them to take.

Mrs. Chen stood beside him with her hands folded, not touching the notice, the way you don't touch a thing that's been put on you by people who want to see if you will. Seventy years in this city had taught her that the notice was not the weapon. The notice was the receipt for a weapon already fired.

"Eleven complaints," Kyle said.

"I have maybe eleven customers a night," said Mrs. Chen. "I know all their faces. None of them is sick." She looked at the dark griddle. "A man came last week. Nice suit. Said the block was being considered for an authentic-dining corridor. Said a name." She said it like a splinter she'd been carrying: **Tessaline.** Operating, on the new frontage two streets over, as *Damen Authentic.*

Kyle had not drawn a weapon in four days and he did not draw one now. This was not that kind of fight. This was the kind the city actually ran on.

He had not always known that. There were years he kept in the same drawer as the other things he didn't take out and look at — years when he'd answered a notice like this one with the only tool he'd trusted — and what they'd taught him, slowly, at a price he was apparently still paying, was that the sword wins the room and loses the building, and that the men who sign the holds are never in the room.

---

The complaints were good work. He'd give them that.

He spent an evening with them — not on the street, on the record, which was where the whole war was being fought. Pixel pulled the filing metadata for him from her bench while she worked on something else, because she could do two things at once and resented being told it was impressive.

The eleven complainants existed. That was the craft of it. They had addresses, citizen indices, clinic relationships. They simply also had a pattern: nine of the eleven had filed their first-ever municipal complaint within the same seventy-two hours, all through the same intake portal, all routed through a filing agent whose license traced — four shells deep, but it traced — to a reputation-management firm that listed exactly one client on its frontage paperwork, which was a holding company, which listed exactly one client, which was Tessaline Foods.

"They didn't fake the people," Pixel said, not looking up from the tiny servo she was seating into a child-sized wrist. "That's the tell. Faking people is hard and gets caught. They rented real people's signatures and pointed them at a portal. The lie isn't in the complaints. The lie is in the *clustering*, and clustering is the one thing paper can't hide once you stack it." She seated the servo. "God, that's clean work. Theirs, I mean. I almost respect it."

"You're calibrating an arm."

"I'm calibrating an arm *and* admiring fraud. Two things." She finally looked up. The arm on her bench was small — a forearm and hand for a kid maybe seven, matte blue, the ulnar line slightly off true. "Gray-zone referral. Kid named Oziel. His last fitting was done by someone who'd never met a child and it's been pinching the growth plate for a year. Nobody to report that to, either." She went back to it. "Eat something before you go be clever at a man. There's broth in the thing. Don't make me say it twice."

He ate the broth. The thing about Pixel was that she fed everyone she could reach, and the list of people she could reach was the only territory she'd ever tried to hold.

---

Mercer received him in the Damen Authentic front-of-house, under a hand-distressed sign and a menu that used the word *heritage* four times. The broth here came out of a flavor cartridge — Kyle could smell it from the door, the too-even sweetness of a profile printed to spec: no scorch, no hand, no forty years, a heritage with a firmware version and a recall notice waiting somewhere in its future. He was the franchise-development man — pressed, pleasant, the specific calm of someone who has never once been in a room where the math could turn around and bite him.

"We're not in a dispute with the individual operator," Mercer said, before Kyle had sat. "We've raised health concerns. Concerns get reviewed. It's all very ordinary."

"It is," Kyle agreed. "That's the part I came about." He set a single folded sheet on the table — not a weapon, a receipt. "Nine of your eleven complaints filed in one seventy-two-hour window, one portal, one agent, traceable to a firm with one client behind two shells. That's not a health record. That's a purchase order, and it's got your frontage name at the bottom of it because somebody four layers down got lazy about the last shell." He left the sheet where it was. "Here's the ordinary part. I don't take this to the street. I'm not built for the street on this one. I take it to two desks. Yours — Tessaline's own compliance audit, the one that exists to make sure regional men like you don't create exactly this kind of liability. And the territory landlord, who leases you the new frontage on a clause that voids for fraudulent interference with a sitting tenant." He let that sit. "You didn't break a noodle stall, Mr. Mercer. You wrote down, in a place that keeps copies, that you would. The stall was never the exposure. *The paper was.*"

Mercer's pleasantness did a small, expensive thing around the eyes — the look of a man recalculating which way the liability flowed and discovering it flowed uphill, at him.

"What do you want."

"The hold lifted by end of day. The complaints withdrawn at the source — your problem how. And a note in your own file that the corridor study found the block's existing operators compliant, because they are, and because a man who writes one true thing in a system is harder to call a liar later." Kyle stood. "I don't want money. I don't want the frontage. I eat two streets over and I'd like to keep doing it. That's the whole ask."

"And the sheet?"

"Is a copy. They're always copies, Mr. Mercer. That's the trouble with paper. The antiques dealer under the Loop taught me that." He didn't explain further.

---

The hold came down at 16:40.

Mrs. Chen lit the griddle without ceremony, the way she did everything, and the block smelled like the block again within the hour — the specific sourness of her broth that three other stalls had tried to copy and none had managed, because the recipe wasn't a recipe, it was forty years of the same hand.

She put a bowl in front of Kyle that night and, beside it, without a word, a second small dish: a soft egg she had not charged him for and would deny, under oath, having given.

"I pay," Kyle said.

"For the noodles," said Mrs. Chen. "The egg is mine to do what I want with. You don't get to pay for everything, Kyle." She wiped the counter that didn't need wiping. "Eat the egg."

Second person to tell him that in a week. He ate the egg.

---

It was past midnight when he got home, and the light was on across the hall, because it always was.

Pixel had the blue arm finished and standing on her bench like a small monument, fingers curled in the relaxed half-fist of a hand at rest — the ulnar line true now, the growth-plate clearance set with two years of a kid's growing built into it. She was looking at it with the particular flat exhaustion of someone who'd done a thing right that nobody would ever know was hard.

"Oziel gets it Thursday," she said. "His mother cried on the deposit, which I hate, because I had to pretend I hadn't seen, because if I see it I have to charge less, and I already charged less." She rubbed her eyes. "Did you fix the noodle thing?"

"With paper."

"Of course you did." She almost smiled. "Whole city's a filing cabinet and everybody's scared of the drawers." She nodded at the door — *go, sleep* — and then, as he turned, the half-thing she did instead of the whole thing: "Your arm's pulling at the sutures. I saw it when you came up the stairs. Come by in the morning and don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You always argue. It's how you say thank you." She turned back to the small blue arm. The door stayed open the width she always left it, which was exactly enough.
