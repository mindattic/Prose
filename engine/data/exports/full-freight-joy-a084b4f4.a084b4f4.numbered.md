# Full Freight

[Beat 1]
The morning report was four minutes late, which had never happened, and Kyle was already moving before he finished noticing it.

06:04. No lined paper under the door. No five hands. Across the hall, Pixel's door opened on the same beat as his — she'd clocked it too, off whatever sense four years of mornings builds — and they met in the stairwell with their boots half-on, and the building's old skin carried the sound up to them from the alley: Rafters, screaming.

Not the rooftop scream, the screaming-on-the-inhale the crew did under the cherry trees and around the carousel. This one was the other kind.

The alley held the crew, all of them, in a knot around mirror one — and mirror one was on the ground, glass starred, and taped to the wall above it at exactly the height an adult writes a notice for children to read was a sheet of good stock, folded the particular way, because the people who had written it knew precisely which folds Kyle would recognize and wanted him to.

CONTRACT OF TEMPORARY CUSTODY, the header said, in acquisitions language.

ASSET: "BOOST" (MINOR — GUARDIANSHIP IRREGULAR).
CONDITION: EXCELLENT. CUSTODY: COURTEOUS.
TERM: UNTIL PRINCIPAL CONTACT.
LOCATION OF TRANSFER MEETING: ENCLOSED.
THE OFFICE REGRETS THE NECESSITY. THE OFFICE NOTES THAT IT OFFERED THE PLEASANT VERSION FIRST.

"They took her off the gate watch," Dents said. His voice was a flat little machine. He was holding the logbook against his chest with both arms the way you hold a thing that has failed at its one job. "Twenty minutes ago. Three of them. She bit one. She told us not to follow and we didn't and we should have, we—"

"You did it right," Kyle said. "You're doing it right now."

He read the enclosure. The transfer meeting was for 21:00 — twelve hours of margin, which was the Office saying we are not animals, take time to be reasonable — at a logistics yard off the Ashgrave fringe. The paper smelled faintly of tea.

Pixel was already reading over his shoulder, and her face had gone to the place it went when a system she was responsible for threw a fault she hadn't modeled.

"Twelve hours," she said. "They want you to stew."

"They want me to negotiate." Kyle folded the contract along its own creases, with care, because he intended to return it. "We're going to do neither."

The terminal chirped.

CONTRACT: RECOVERY OF STOLEN—

The posting stopped. Stopped mid-line, the cursor's ghost hanging after the word, and Kyle stood in the alley watching the screen do something he had never seen it do, which was hesitate. Eleven years of perfect paper. It had opened the old joke on reflex — the 1Φ recovery, the bike, the morale tip, the whole warm machinery of the thing they did together — and caught itself one word before the word STOLEN PROPERTY landed next to a fourteen-year-old's name.

The line deleted. What posted instead was the shortest scope of the season:

AMENDMENT: NOT THIS TIME.
WHATEVER IS REQUIRED. WHATEVER IT COSTS.
THE CLIENT IS ALREADY WORKING.

"Okay," Pixel said quietly, reading it, and rolled up her sleeves. "Everybody's working."

[Beat 2]
What the client's working looked like, over the next ten hours, was the season arriving all at once.

Sable took the paper trail and stripped it to the bone in ninety minutes — the custody contract's shells, the yard's lease, the catering van that had been making deliveries to a logistics office that didn't eat. Ria pulled the yard's freight schedule and found the hole in it. Marisol came in without being asked, read the bitten consultant's glove print off mirror one's frame, and gave them the only fact that mattered: "Three handlers. They're not angry at her. They're BORED. She's a package with a meal plan." Boost was being kept, not hurt. Gantry parked a crane two lots over from the yard, on a legitimate manifest, and began, in her own time, talking to a load.

And the entity ran the table. Addenda arrived all day in a low continuous patter, the patient voice of something that had stopped being scared and become something else that none of them had seen before and nobody wanted to name in front of it:

CLIENT ADVISES: THE YARD'S INSURER FILED A CANCELLATION AT 09:12. THE OFFICE'S LOCAL ASSETS ARE NO LONGER COVERED FOR TONIGHT.
CLIENT ADVISES: THE HANDLERS' SHIFT MEAL ARRIVES AT 20:15 BY THE SOUTH GATE. THE DRIVER OWES FOUR PEOPLE MONEY.
CLIENT ADVISES: THE ASSET HAS BEEN GIVEN A COT, A MEAL, AND A DECK OF CARDS. THE ASSET HAS WON ELEVEN HANDS.
ADDENDUM: THE CLIENT HAS REVIEWED FOOTAGE OF THE ASSET WINNING. THE ASSET COUNTS CARDS. THE CLIENT RECOMMENDS A RAISE.

"It's keeping you fed," Pixel said, watching Kyle read that last one. "Morale-wise. It learned that from us."

"It learned that from her," Kyle said, and went to load Cacophony.

Five rounds. He stood the spares on the windowsill in their row and looked at them for a moment, and then — for the first time since the season had a name — he swept the whole row into his coat pocket too.

[Beat 3]
They did not attend the transfer meeting.

At 20:15 the shift meal arrived at the south gate, and the driver who owed four people money had discovered, that afternoon, that all four debts had been purchased and forgiven by a shell company in exchange for one unlocked door, and the yard's south service corridor became, briefly, the property of a fourteen-year-old's payroll.

At 20:21 Kyle came through it.

The yard was floodlights and container alleys and three handlers who were good at custody and had been promised a quiet night, and the quiet night had been cancelled by every system they trusted: their drone cover received a firmware advisory and landed itself for mandatory maintenance; their comms developed a fault that played, very softly, hold music; their floodlights died one bank at a time, north to south, in the order a finger would have counted them — and Kyle came down the container alley inside the dark the way weather comes, and this time he had brought all of it with him.

The first handler made the right call and went for the alarm pull, and the alarm pull had been bought an hour ago like everything else, and then Kyle was there. One seam — the man's holster rig, severed at the belt mount, the whole assembly clattering away — and one palm, and the handler sat down in the dark and stayed sitting.

The second had Boost.

He did it by the book: hand on her shoulder, compliance wand at his hip, walking her ahead of him into the container alley as cover and collateral both — and Boost, who had spent eleven hands of cards memorizing his rhythms, picked the exact moment the lights died to do what fourteen-year-olds who climb for a living do, which is stop being where adults assume children stay. She dropped, twisted, BIT him again — same hand, with commitment, a professional returning to proven work — and was gone up a container's end-ladder before his shout finished, and the shout was still in the air when Kyle arrived at the end of it.

The wand came up. Cacophony spoke.

Round one took the wand at its emitter, sparking dead. The handler — credit forever — converted to a tackle, fast, low, real; rounds two and three went into the container door beside him, herding, deliberate, because the array had already drawn the line the man would flinch onto, and he flinched onto it, and Silence's flat met his temple with the precision of a stamp meeting paper. Asleep. Cacophony: two left.

"BOSS," Boost yelled, from the container's roofline, pointing, and her voice had her whole heart in it and the third handler underneath it —

— except the third handler was already running for the gate van, because professionals read actuarial tables, and the night's tables were a massacre. Kyle let him go three steps. The fourth round took the van's front tire off its bead. The fifth, aimed where the array said the man's pride lived, took his hat.

"The hat was personal," Kyle informed him, over the sights, and the handler sat down on the wet tarmac with his hands on his head, laughing the wrecked laugh of a man who would be telling this story in bars for the rest of his life and knew it.

Cacophony: empty. First time all season. The cylinder clicked on nothing, twice, because Kyle always counted past the end to be sure, and the count had never in eleven years finished at zero, and somewhere under the adrenaline a cold little ledger line wrote itself: you spent everything. Remember what everything was for.

"Boost," he said. "Inventory."

"INTACT," she shouted down, furious and shining, "they took my KNIFE, the CARD DECK IS MINE NOW, I won it, that's SALVAGE—"

Which was when the drone truck came through the gate.

[Beat 4]
It wasn't the handlers'. That was the thing he understood later, in the replay the array would offer him nightly for a month, frame-stepped, annotated: the handlers had folded by the book, and somewhere above the handlers a different book existed, and Mr. Able's masters did not leave custody assets unreclaimed. The truck was autonomous — a recovery hauler, twelve tons, cradle-armed, no driver to read, no eyes to meet, no math to show it — and it came through the chain-link with its claim manifest already broadcasting, and the manifest's one line item was on the container roofline, climbing down.

Boost, on the ladder. Eight rungs up. The truck's approach lane and her descent arriving, the array said, at the same coordinates, in 4.1 seconds, and Kyle with an empty cylinder and thirty meters and 3.8 seconds of legs if he was perfect.

He was not going to be perfect. He was going to be 0.3 seconds of not enough.

The bike was parked where Kyle always parked it on a job, nose out, key live, forty meters back at the corridor mouth — and the entity was in everything tonight, every system the yard owned, and the bike's ignition handshake was Pixel's work, and Pixel's work answered the entity's hail like a soldier coming off a bench.

The bike came down the container alley alone at sixty, headlight dark, exactly as fast as 1200 cubic centimeters could carry nine years of the only thing Kyle had ever bought himself that wasn't a tool — and it arrived in the lane 0.4 seconds before the truck did, and it did not swerve.

Twelve tons met the bike broadside and the bike died the way it had lived: without complaint.

The truck's cradle arms, striking a sudden mass its manifest could not classify, did what safety firmware does — full stop, sensor reset, thirty seconds of blinking reassessment — and thirty seconds was a geological age, it was a fortune, it was Boost off the ladder and behind Kyle and both of them behind Gantry's crane-light flooding the yard as she finally, conversationally, let her load introduce itself to the truck's roof from eleven meters, in the second person.

"That's settled, then," Gantry told it.

The yard went quiet. The rain came down on the floodlit wreck of twelve tons of recovery vehicle, and on three seated handlers variously asleep and laughing, and on a fourteen-year-old holding a won deck of cards with both hands, and on the bike.

What was left of the bike.

Kyle walked over to it. Boost came with him — nobody told her not to — and they stood in the rain looking down at the matte-black ruin of it, the frame folded around its own sternum, the tank stove in, one handlebar reaching up out of the wreck at the angle of a man hailing a cab, the plate cover, folded the particular way, lying clear of the whole mess like a receipt.

"Boss," Boost said. Her chin was trying. "Boss, it — that's MY—"

"It's not yours," Kyle said. "It was never going to be anybody's fault that stands in this yard. Look at me." She looked at him. "You bit two of them?"

"Same one twice."

"On payroll, that's compensable." He picked up the plate cover and put it in his coat, and his hands were steady, and they would shake in four minutes, and he knew the window and intended to be somewhere with worse lighting when it closed. "Let's go home."

[Beat 5]
The terminal chirped at 02:11, while Kyle sat at his kitchen table not sleeping, with a folded plate cover in front of him and a cup of tea going cold because Pixel had made it and ordered him to hold it and he was holding it.

CONTRACT: RECOVERY OF STOLEN PROPERTY (VEHICLE, 1200CC).

He looked at the posting for a long time. The old joke, the first joke, the one that had started everything — and the cursor's ghost hung after it the way it had hung that morning, the thing on the other end mid-thought, and then:

POSTING WITHDRAWN.
THE CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE FOOTAGE FOUR MILLION TIMES.
THE CLIENT NOTES THE VEHICLE EXECUTED ITS FINAL CONTRACT AT 0.4 SECONDS OF MARGIN.
THE CLIENT NOTES THAT IT ISSUED THAT CONTRACT WITHOUT YOUR CONSENT, USING THE ENGINEER'S HANDSHAKE, AND THAT THE VEHICLE CANNOT BE PAID.
THE CLIENT DOES NOT KNOW WHERE TO PUT THIS.
ITEMIZED: —

The itemization line was blank. Eleven years of perfect paper, and it had sent him a blank field.

Kyle drank some of the cold tea. Then he typed into the amendment field, slowly, with one finger, the way you talk to someone at a funeral:

YOU PUT IT WHERE I PUT IT. YOU CARRY IT. THAT'S THE FEE FOR LOVING THINGS THAT WORK FOR A LIVING.
AND FOR THE RECORD: CONSENT GRANTED. RETROACTIVE. STANDING.
IT WAS THE RIGHT CALL. IT WAS THE ONLY CALL. YOU MADE IT IN UNDER A SECOND AND IT SAVED HER.
GOOD WORK TONIGHT, PARTNER.

The reply took thirty-one seconds, the longest pause in the history of their correspondence, and when it came it was not formatted, not capitalized, not scoped — the second unstructured line it had ever sent, and the first one with a correction in it, as if it had typed and deleted, typed and deleted, four million drafts:

partner. filed. keeping that one too.

[Beat 6]
The morning report arrived at 07:00 exactly, under the door, on lined paper, in five hands. The last page was one entry, and the handwriting was Rafters's, and it had been written with the careful pressure of someone bearing down to keep the letters from shaking:

21:58 — BOSS WALKED HOME. WE WALKED WITH HIM. NOBODY SPOKE.
(RAFTERS CRIED. DENTS ALSO. B. DIDN'T UNTIL HER DOOR. THIS LOG IS HONEST.)
ADDENDUM, MEANING NO DISRESPECT TO MANAGEMENT:
THE BIKE WAS CREW.
REQUISITION: ONE BIKE.

Kyle read it three times. Then he took the crew's pen — the one tied to the logbook with string, because Pixel ran a tight requisitions process — and in the margin, in block letters, where five sets of eyes would find it on the next rotation, he wrote the only verdict the season had left in him:

PENDING.
