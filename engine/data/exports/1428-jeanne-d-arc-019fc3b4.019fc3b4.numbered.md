# Jeanne d'Arc

_Citation-grounded nonfiction biography of Joan of Arc — the documentary record, the trials, and the full spectrum of scholarship on her Voices, told without dramatization or verdict._

[Beat 1]
Before Domrémy, two names and one simple fact, because nothing else in this book will make sense without them. In 1429, France had two men claiming to be its rightful king, and this chapter exists to make clear who they were, why there were exactly two, not three, not one, not none, and which one a teenage girl from a border village was about to spend her life fighting for. The first was Charles, son of the reigning king Charles VI - an adult, an obvious heir by ordinary bloodline, and yet formally disinherited by his own father's government. At this point in his life he is known by his title as heir-apparent, the Dauphin; he is the man Jeanne would spend the rest of this book trying to get crowned, and when that happens, at Reims, this same Charles becomes King Charles VII - the name this book will use for him from that chapter on. The second was a small English child, Henry VI, who had never set foot in France and could not yet read, whose claim rested entirely on a piece of paper his father had signed. That paper is the whole story of how this happened, and it is worth reading in plain terms rather than skipped past [1].

[Beat 2]
The paper was the Treaty of Troyes, signed 21 May 1420. Charles VI of France was mentally ill — chronically, publicly, for decades, prone to episodes so severe he sometimes did not know his own name — and his court had spent years fractured by a civil war between two French noble factions who agreed on almost nothing except that they distrusted each other more than they distrusted England: the Armagnacs, who backed the royal Valois line and the Dauphin, and the Burgundians, a rival branch of the same royal family with their own territory and their own grievance, after the Armagnac faction's men had assassinated the Burgundian duke John the Fearless at a peace conference in 1419 [2]. Into that wreckage, with much of northern France already under English and Burgundian military control, the Treaty of Troyes struck a deal: Henry V of England would marry Charles VI's daughter, be recognized as regent of France immediately, and inherit the French crown outright on Charles VI's death — with the succession passing afterward to Henry V's own line. To make room for that outcome, the treaty required disinheriting Charles VI's own surviving son. A Paris court obligingly convicted the Dauphin of treason in absentia the following year and declared him banished [3]. Two claimants, in other words, were not an accident of confusing bloodlines — they were manufactured, on purpose, by one treaty and one hostile court judgment, to hand a foreign infant a country his own family had never ruled.

[Beat 3]
One common misconception is worth correcting directly, because it makes the whole war look stranger than it actually was: the French throne was not simply empty for a hundred years while this got sorted out. Charles VI reigned, continuously, nominally, from 1380 until his actual death on 21 October 1422 - ill, dominated by rival factions, but never deposed and never absent from the throne. Henry V, as it happens, never got to collect on the treaty's promise at all; he died of dysentery in August 1422, two months before Charles VI himself, so the crown he had arranged to inherit passed instead to his own infant son, who became King Henry VI of England and (in the eyes of the English and Burgundian-controlled north) King of France before he was even a year old [4]. The genuinely acute dispute - two real, competing, crowned-or-crownable claimants, both alive, both with active armies fighting over the actual territory - runs from that 1420 treaty to Charles VII's coronation at Reims in July 1429: a sharp, brutal, roughly nine-year crisis, not a vague century-long vacancy. The fighting dragged on for decades after 1429 too, but the question this book's title asks - was Jeanne's mission worth taking seriously - is a question about those nine years specifically, the ones her arrival helped end.

[Beat 4]
France did not stop functioning while two men argued over who was king of it, and that fact matters for understanding why ordinary people like Jacques d'Arc kept farming, paying taxes, and organizing militia musters throughout. The Dauphin's supporters ran a real, working rival government from Poitiers and Bourges - its own high court, its own accounting office - administering everything south of the Loire except the English-held southwest; the Paris government, recognized by the English and Burgundian regime, ran the north the same way [5]. Two governments, two tax rolls, two law courts, both fully functional, each insisting the other was illegitimate - but neither France, nor the idea of having a king at all, ever stopped. No contemporary source turned up in the research for this book suggesting anyone seriously proposed doing without a monarch altogether; the fight was entirely over which king, never over whether a country needed one. Geographically, the practical border ran along the Loire itself: north and east of it, Paris, Normandy, and the English-Burgundian alliance; south of it, the "Kingdom of Bourges" - the Dauphin's shrunken, unglamorous, but perfectly real state, centered on Bourges and Poitiers, with Chinon and Tours as its court's working residences. Orléans, sitting directly on that river, was the one crossing point that mattered most, which is exactly why an army converged on it in October 1428, and exactly why Jeanne - the teenage girl from a border village two hundred miles away whose story the rest of this book tells - decided, the following winter, that relieving that siege for the Dauphin's cause was where she needed to go.

[Beat 5]
Domrémy sat on the river Meuse, in the Duchy of Bar, close enough to the border with Lorraine that the village split its own loyalty down the middle of the water. Households on the west bank — Jacques d'Arc's among them — owed allegiance through the Duke of Bar to the King of France. Households on the east bank answered to Lorraine, and beyond that to the Holy Roman Empire [6]. This was not a metaphor or a quirk of later storytelling; it was the actual, load-bearing administrative fact of the place a girl born around 1412 grew up in; two families could live within shouting distance of each other and owe their taxes, their musters, and in a real sense their national identity to two different crowns. Domrémy itself, however, held a single, stubborn political character that set it apart from its immediate surroundings: deep inside a broad swath of northern France that had, by the 1420s, gone over to the English and their Burgundian allies, the village stayed loyal to the Armagnac cause — to the disinherited Dauphin, not to the child-king in Paris. It was a loyalist island, and loyalist islands, in a civil war, pay for it.

[Beat 6]
Her father, Jacques d'Arc, was not a lord and not quite an ordinary peasant either. He held the village office of doyen — a post ranking somewhere between the mayor and the provost, responsible for collecting taxes, representing the community's interests, and organizing local defense, muster included, when raiders came [7]. That last duty was not ceremonial. A man who has to call his neighbors to arms is a man the village has decided it can trust with its safety, and the office itself is the best surviving evidence of the family's actual social position: a step above the run of the peasantry, not gentry, but locally notable, moderately land-rich, and used to being listened to. Her mother, Isabelle, came from the nearby village of Vouthon and carried the byname "Romée" — traditionally read as a title given to someone who had made a pilgrimage to Rome, though the record does not nail this down with certainty, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than repeating it as settled fact [8]. Together Jacques and Isabelle held something like fifty acres and a solid house — comfortable, by the standard of the village, not grand by any other standard at all. Jeanne was the fourth of their five children.

[Beat 7]
Loyalist islands get raided, and Domrémy did. In 1425, Burgundian troops drove off the village's livestock and burned part of the settlement, forcing residents to flee — an ordinary atrocity of an ordinary medieval civil war, the kind of event that leaves almost no trace in a chronicle written for kings but reorganizes, permanently, the nervous system of every child who lives through it [9]. It is the same year Jeanne would later say, under oath, that she first heard a voice in her father's garden. Historians are careful, and rightly so, not to hang a whole causal theory on a coincidence of years; nothing in the record lets you say the raid produced the voice, as if trauma were a vending machine. But the two facts sit in the same twelve months of the same short childhood, in the same exposed, frightened village, and a book that pretends the war wasn't already inside the walls of that garden before anything supernatural arrived there would be leaving out something true to make the story cleaner than the evidence allows.

[Beat 8]
One piece of ordinary village custom outlived its innocence entirely, because a court went looking for a reason to burn a nineteen-year-old woman and found one growing at the edge of town. Near Domrémy stood an old beech tree, called locally l'Arbre des Dames or l'Arbre des Fées — the Ladies' Tree, or the Fairies' Tree — standing by a spring that was supposed to cure fevers, around which the village's children, Jeanne among them, were known to sing, dance, and hang garlands in honor of an image of Our Lady of Domrémy. This was not sorcery. It was the kind of low-grade, half-Christian, half-older folk practice that surrounded most rural European children for centuries and troubled almost nobody. At her trial, in testimony given at the Third Public Examination on 24 February 1431, Jeanne acknowledged going to the tree with the other girls, and said she had heard older villagers — including her own godmother — claim to have seen fairies there, while stating flatly that she herself never had [10]. Her judges, assembling a case for heresy and sorcery out of whatever material the record offered, used the tree anyway [11]. It is worth sitting with the specific unfairness of that maneuver for a moment: the very ordinariness of her childhood, the part of her testimony where she sounds most like every other village girl of her generation, is exactly the part a hostile court chose to weaponize. Innocence, in this story, is not a defense. It is evidence, if the people reading it have already decided what verdict they want.

[Beat 9]
Then and Now

A child today who grows up near a national border rarely has to wonder, in any daily practical sense, which army will burn the next field over — that particular species of low-grade dread, the one that organizes a whole childhood around which direction the smoke is coming from, has genuinely receded for most people who will read this book, and it is worth noticing that this is a real change in the texture of being alive, not a small one. What has not changed is smaller and stranger: the way an ordinary custom — a favorite tree, a song, a walk children take without adult supervision — can look, decades later and to the wrong eyes, like the first exhibit in a case nobody has made yet. Every generation has its version of the Fairies' Tree: some perfectly innocent, half-remembered thing a child did, that a later and more suspicious authority decides to read backward as a sign of what they always suspected. Domrémy's tree is one specific instance of a very general and still-active human habit — deciding the verdict first, and only afterward going looking through a life for the evidence.

[Beat 10]
She said she was thirteen. Under oath, two years and a world away from the garden itself, facing men who wanted her dead for the answer, Joan gave the same age every time she was asked: thirteen, midday, summer, her father's garden. She said she was "very much frightened" the first time [12]. That detail is worth pausing on before anything else, because so much of what follows in this book — the crown, the army, the coronation — depends on people forgetting that the very first thing on record is a frightened teenager in a vegetable garden, not a warrior arriving fully formed. Whatever this experience was, it did not arrive as triumph. It arrived as fear.

[Beat 11]
Here is what she said happened, as close to her own words as the surviving record allows: "I heard this Voice to my right, towards the Church; rarely do I hear it without its being accompanied also by a light. This light comes from the same side as the Voice, and generally there is a great light" [13]. Not a face. Not, at first, even a name. A direction — her right side, toward the church, which is itself a detail worth noticing, since a church bell was often what she said she heard it alongside, and a girl raised inside the rhythm of a parish day would have every reason to locate an unexplained voice in the direction her whole life already pointed. Light, and a voice, and fear, and nothing yet she could put a name to. She testified that only after hearing it three separate times did she come to recognize it as an angel's [14]. Whatever name eventually attached to it — and one would, within the same childhood, as the next chapter takes up — the naming came after the experience, not before it. She did not go looking for a specific saint and find one. Something arrived first, unlabeled, and she spent real time inside that not-knowing before the Church's own vocabulary caught up to it.

[Beat 12]
One honest complication belongs here, stated plainly rather than smoothed over: nothing you have just read is a transcript in the modern sense. The Trial of Condemnation survives as an official Latin redaction, compiled after the fact from a French working "minute" taken down day by day by the court's notaries — a summary and a translation, not a recording [15]. Every sentence attributed to Joan in this book has already passed through at least one act of clerical compression before any modern translator rendered it into English. This does not mean the record is worthless — it means the opposite, in fact, since a fabricated confession is usually smoother and more convenient than what survives here, full of deflection, repetition, and answers that plainly frustrated the men asking the questions. But it is worth knowing, once, clearly, before quoting her further: you are reading Joan of Arc's testimony at one remove, filtered through the hostile institution that killed her for giving it. That is simply the shape of the only evidence that exists, and pretending otherwise would be a worse dishonesty than admitting it.

[Beat 13]
One more piece of that first year belongs here, since it answers a question any honest biography owes its reader: did Joan ever have a romantic life, and did she die a virgin? The direct answer, on the documentary record, is yes to the second and no to the first, and the evidence is unusually concrete for a medieval peasant girl. She testified that from around the time of her first voice — age thirteen — she vowed to keep her virginity "as long as it should please God" [16]. That vow was tested in an actual courtroom before any of the rest of this story began: a young man from the nearby town of Toul sued her for breach of a marriage promise, claiming she had agreed to marry him. Joan herself traveled to Toul more than once, at real expense to her family, to contest the case in person, testifying she had made no such promise and had already dedicated her virginity to God. The ecclesiastical judge dismissed the suit, finding no promise had been made [17] — a ruling her own parents reportedly found disappointing, since they would have preferred to see their daughter married like any other village girl her age. No romantic partner of any kind appears anywhere else in the documentary record, contemporary or otherwise.

[Beat 14]
Then and Now

A thirteen-year-old today who reports hearing a voice no one else can hear enters a completely different machinery of response than a thirteen-year-old in Domrémy in 1425 did — a referral, an assessment, a vocabulary of conditions and treatments that did not exist and could not have existed for another five hundred years. That machinery is, in most respects, a mercy; a great many children who hear voices today are helped by it, and Joan had access to nothing resembling it, only a village's inherited categories of angel, saint, and demon. What has not changed nearly as much is the fear itself — the specific, private terror of an experience arriving inside your own head that you did not ask for and cannot make anyone else verify. Every framework a culture builds to explain that fear, ancient or modern, devotional or clinical, is answering the same original human problem: something happened, it happened to me alone, and I need a way to say so that other people can hear.

[Beat 15]
The unlabeled voice from the garden eventually got names, and the names it got were not exotic. Joan identified her three voices as the Archangel Michael, St. Catherine of Alexandria, and St. Margaret of Antioch [18] — the first a warrior-angel already associated, in the popular imagination of the period, with the defense of France itself, and the second two among the most widely venerated virgin-martyrs in fifteenth-century devotional practice, the kind of saints whose images would have looked down from the wall of her own parish church. This is not a small point, and it is one this book will return to from more than one angle: whatever produced these three specific figures, it did not reach for the obscure. It reached for exactly the devotional furniture already sitting in a Domrémy girl's daily life.

[Beat 16]
Michael, she said, came "with a great multitude of Angels" and, at Chinon, walked beside her in the house where she lodged, holding her by the hand [19]. Of Catherine and Margaret she said their faces were "adorned with beautiful crowns, very rich and precious" [20] — royal imagery attached to virgin-martyrs, which is itself a familiar move in late-medieval piety, not an invention unique to her testimony. When her judges pressed for the kind of specificity a hostile court wants — hair, clothing, whether the figures had arms, whether they were unclothed beneath whatever she imagined they wore — she did not perform certainty she didn't have. She said she "had no idea" about such details, and turned the question back on the men asking it: "Do you think God can't find [them] clothes?" [21]. It is one of the sharpest exchanges in the entire trial record, and it is worth noticing what kind of sharp it is — not evasive in the sense of dodging the underlying claim, but impatient with a question she plainly regarded as beside the point. She was not vague about whether the visions happened. She was specifically, pointedly vague about exactly the physical inventory her interrogators wanted, because — on her own account — that inventory had never been the content of what she experienced.

[Beat 17]
On the single largest claim she made about the voices' content — not their appearance but their message — the record shows something a fabricator would have every reason to avoid: a documented change in her own telling. She described an angel bringing Charles VII "a beautiful crown of pure gold," bowing before him, saying "Sire, here is your sign. Take it" [22] — a specific, physical, almost theatrical image. Yet near the end of her life she is reported to have given a more figurative account of that same "sign," describing it in terms closer to her own promise to lead Charles to his coronation than to a literal jeweled crown carried by an angel [23]. Both sides of this book's central question can, and have, read that shift in opposite directions. A judge looking for proof of invention hears a liar caught adjusting her story under pressure. A reader inclined toward authenticity hears something closer to what happens whenever any person tries, repeatedly, over years, to put a genuinely private experience into language for an audience that keeps demanding more precision than the experience itself contained — the account gets more figurative, not less, the longer and harder you're forced to explain it. This book takes no position on which reading is correct. It simply insists that both readings are available, honestly, from the same two sentences.

[Beat 18]
She also said the voices came often — including, she indicated, on days when she was actually being questioned, telling her to "answer boldly" [24]. That detail deserves its own moment of plain attention: whatever this was, on her own account it did not retreat once the stakes became mortal. It is easy, from six hundred years away, to imagine a visionary experience as something that belongs to safety — a garden, a childhood, a world before consequences. Joan's testimony describes the opposite: a presence she says stayed with her, by her own account, into the specific room where men who intended to burn her were asking her to describe it.

[Beat 19]
Then and Now

The specific saints a fifteenth-century French peasant girl's mind reached for — a warrior archangel, two crowned virgin-martyrs — were not arbitrary; they were the devotional inventory already available to her, the way a modern person's private experiences, whatever their ultimate source, still get described using the concepts, images, and categories that culture has already handed them. That has not changed at all: nobody, in any century, invents their inner life from nothing. What has changed is how much interpretive room a listener is willing to give the person reporting it. A modern person describing a comforting presence, a voice of guidance, or a sense of protection has an enormous menu of accepted frameworks to choose from — psychological, spiritual, medical, or simply personal — most of which do not end in a formal accusation. Joan had exactly one available frame that mattered to the men in that room: was this God, or was this the Devil. No third option was on offer, and the entire machinery of her trial existed to force a choice between only those two.

[Beat 20]
Here, plainly, is the question this book was written to sit inside rather than answer: was what Joan of Arc experienced divine communion, or was it common schizophrenia wearing the only vocabulary her century had for the miraculous? Put that bluntly, the question sounds like it wants a winner. It doesn't have one, and any book that hands you one — either one — is selling you certainty the actual evidence does not support. What follows is not a debate with two sides. It is a spectrum, with real people and real publications standing at every point along it, and this chapter's only job is to put them all on the page honestly, in their own terms, without a thumb on the scale.

[Beat 21]
Start with the institution that eventually staked the most on the answer. The 1456 Trial of Nullification did not, technically, rule on whether Joan's voices were authentically supernatural — it nullified the 1431 trial's procedure, finding it "contaminated with fraud, calumny, wickedness, contradictions, and manifest errors of fact and law" [25], a judgment about how she was tried, not a formal doctrinal pronouncement about what she heard. The fuller institutional verdict came later and slower: beatified in 1909 by Pius X, canonized in 1920 by Benedict XV, in a ceremony at St. Peter's attended by some thirty thousand people, including roughly a hundred and forty of her own descendants [26]. It is worth knowing that the canonization process of the period included a formally adversarial role, the Promoter of the Faith — popularly, the "Devil's Advocate" — an official whose job was to argue every unfavorable fact against the candidate before any declaration of sanctity could stand [27]. Canonization is a ruling on heroic virtue and confirmed miracles, not, in strict Catholic theology, a binding doctrinal ratification of the specific content of a private revelation — the two categories are formally distinct [28]. In practice, though, as more than one commentator has noted, the Church does not canonize a person while treating the visions at the center of her entire life's story as fiction [29]. And the historical timing of that canonization is itself worth stating plainly rather than leaving implied: the process, delayed by the First World War, reached its conclusion in a period of active rapprochement between the Vatican and the French Republic, a fact historians treat as a real, if partial, political dimension of when — not whether — she was finally declared a saint [30].

[Beat 22]
Serious secular historians, as a group, decline to adjudicate cause at all, and it is worth noticing that this is itself a position, not an absence of one. Régine Pernoud's foundational modern account treats the visions as reported fact without deifying or diagnosing them [31]. Larissa Juliet Taylor offers practical, grounded context for what Joan's contemporaries called her "miracles" while never reducing her to a case study [32]. Kelly DeVries, writing as a military historian, brackets the entire question, arguing that what mattered historically was the effect of Joan's total conviction on the battlefield, not its ultimate source [33]. Karen Sullivan goes a layer deeper still, reading the trial transcript itself as a text produced by the collision between Joan's lay, oral speech and her judges' clerical, university-trained Latin culture — the "Joan" who survives on the page, in Sullivan's reading, is already partly a product of that collision, which complicates any effort to diagnose the woman using only the document that collision produced [34]. A frequent, if loosely sourced, observation running through this literature holds that Joan's testimony stayed remarkably consistent across many separate, hostile interrogation sessions specifically on the voices — an internal coherence some writers treat as evidence against simple invention. That specific inferential move deserves a flag of its own honesty: it is a broadly repeated synthesis across popular-academic writing on Joan, not a single scholar's precisely footnoted argument, and this book states that distinction rather than borrowing the confidence of a citation it cannot produce [35].

[Beat 23]
It is worth pausing before the medical spectrum to name something the medical framing can obscure entirely: in Joan's own century, hearing voices did not, by itself, mark a woman as ill. It placed her inside a recognized, institutionally regulated category. The medievalist Rosalynn Voaden has traced the doctrine the period actually used to sort these claims — discretio spirituum, "discernment of spirits" — across a continuum of late-medieval women visionaries that includes Bridget of Sweden, Catherine of Siena, and Joan herself, arguing that visionary experience was a culturally available role for a woman to occupy, not an automatic diagnosis of disorder [36]. Some women who claimed exactly this kind of experience were examined, found consistent with orthodox doctrine, and went on to real institutional authority — Bridget of Sweden founded a religious order and was canonized; Catherine of Siena advised popes and was later named a Doctor of the Church. Others, examined under the same general framework, were condemned. What decided the outcome, historians in this tradition argue, was not the presence or absence of a voice, but whether the specifics of what a woman reported, and who was doing the judging, and for what political reasons, lined up in her favor [37]. This matters for the question this chapter is built around, because it means the modern instinct to reach immediately for a medical explanation is itself a later import. The fifteenth century had its own sorting mechanism, and it was not medicine. It was politics dressed as theology, applied unevenly, to a category of experience nobody at the time considered inherently pathological.

[Beat 24]
Now the medical spectrum itself, starting with its oldest entry and — because specialists themselves rank it lowest — the one to get out of the way first. In 1882 the neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot told the French Academy of Sciences that history's religious ecstatics — he named Joan of Arc specifically, alongside St. Teresa and St. Paul — were, in his clinical judgment, "evidently hysterics" [38]. The psychiatrist Georges Dumas folded pathological religious experience into his broader nosology across the 1930s and 1940s, though a careful look at his actual case material turns up a real nuance worth stating honestly: the clearest documented example connecting Dumas to "Jeanne d'Arc" concerns a modern patient who believed herself Joan reincarnated, not a direct retrospective clinical assessment of the historical woman [39]. The schizophrenia hypothesis, as a named diagnosis, carries an almost administrative problem before any clinical argument even starts: the word itself was coined by Eugen Bleuler in 1908, nearly five hundred years after Joan's death [40].

Three specific mismatches are why specialists rank schizophrenia lowest among the medical hypotheses, and they are worth naming precisely rather than gesturing at vaguely. First, onset: Joan dated her first voice to around age thirteen; female-onset schizophrenia, epidemiologically, clusters in the twenties and later, with a documented second peak after forty tied to menopausal hormonal decline — thirteen is not where the condition typically begins [41]. Second, trajectory: the condition is diagnostically defined by functional deterioration, and Joan's own trajectory runs in the opposite direction entirely — an illiterate farm girl who organized a military campaign, navigated the factional politics of a royal court, and out-argued trained theologians under hostile cross-examination, a curve that does not resemble decline by any available measure [42]. Third, content: schizophrenic hallucinations, in the clinical literature, trend persecutory, fragmentary, and derogatory; Joan's voices were, by her own consistent account, named, benevolent, instrumentally accurate, and — this detail rarely makes it into popular retellings — multi-sensory rather than purely auditory. Article Eleven of the Twelve Articles drawn up against her at trial states she showed reverence to the voices by "kissing the earth on which they walk... at times kissing and embracing Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret; she had touched them with her own hands, corporeally and physically" — a specific, textually anchored claim of touch, not just sound or sight, with secondary trial scholarship additionally describing an accompanying pleasant smell [43]. A hallucination that is heard, seen, touched, and smelled at once, consistently, across years and dozens of hostile interrogations, is not the typical clinical picture schizophrenia predicts, whatever else it might be.

[Beat 25]
The best-evidenced medical hypothesis by a wide margin, and the only one with an active, ongoing, peer-reviewed argument still being conducted in real medical journals, concerns epilepsy. The case for ecstatic temporal-lobe seizures was first argued in a 1990 American Academy of Neurology presentation by UCSF neurologist Dr. Lydia Bayne working with writer Elizabeth Foote-Smith, then published the following year in the peer-reviewed literature, pointing to a pattern of onset at thirteen, no aberrant behavior between episodes, a dreamlike ecstatic quality, visions carrying an urgent message, and possible triggering by church bells, which the authors considered typical of complex partial seizures [44]. The hypothesis was then refined rather than simply repeated: neurologists Giuseppe d'Orsi and Paolo Tinuper argued in Epilepsy & Behavior in 2006 for a more specific subtype, idiopathic partial epilepsy with auditory features, which they judged a closer fit to Joan's account than the broader "ecstatic seizure" framing — a stereotyped onset, voices with a constant auditory core and variable visual component, acoustic triggering, preserved consciousness throughout, and no clouding of awareness afterward [45]. A decade later, Nicastro and Picard, of the Geneva University Hospitals, published a competing account in the same journal, concurring with d'Orsi and Tinuper's specific subtype while explicitly rejecting both the looser "ecstatic seizure" label and the schizophrenia hypothesis [46] — which then produced a direct, published, adversarial exchange in 2016, a formal comment from a third specialist and a reply from d'Orsi and Tinuper defending their position [47]. This is worth pausing on for what it actually demonstrates: six hundred years after her death, credentialed neurologists are still arguing with each other, in real journals, about which specific seizure subtype best fits a woman none of them can examine — a genuine, live scientific disagreement, not a settled medical consensus quietly waiting to be reported as fact.

[Beat 26]
A quieter, older, and ultimately self-defeating hypothesis points not at her mind but at her lungs. In 1958, physicians John and Isobel-Ann Butterfield proposed that disseminated bovine tuberculosis, contracted in girlhood through unpasteurized milk, could have produced a temporal-lobe tuberculoma — tying the theory to the real, trial-attested detail that Joan fell seriously ill in prison at Rouen, believing at one point that she was dying, and asked for confession and burial in consecrated ground [48]. The hypothesis found a later defender in R. H. Ratnasuriya in 1986 [49]. But its most rigorous published examination, by Nores and Yakovleff in 1995, arrives at its own hypothesis's funeral: the authors conclude that widespread, serious tuberculosis is difficult to reconcile with the sustained physical activity — riding, wearing armor, campaigning for months at a stretch — that Joan is independently documented to have performed [50]. It is, in its own modest way, an unusually honest moment in this literature: a medical paper that does the math on its own theory and reports, in print, that the math doesn't work.

[Beat 27]
Two more explanations circulate widely enough in popular retellings that their absence from the serious literature is itself worth stating outright, since a reader arriving with either half-remembered is owed a direct answer rather than silence. Ergotism — poisoning by the Claviceps purpurea fungus, well documented as a proposed explanation for the 1692 Salem witch trials — has, on the research for this book, no serious scholarly proponent applying it specifically to Joan of Arc; the association appears to be a popular conflation with Salem, not an actual hypothesis in the Joan literature, and this book declines to manufacture one where none exists [51]. Migraine with aura fares similarly: it is a genuinely serious hypothesis for a different medieval visionary, Hildegard of Bingen, associated in particular with the neurologist and writer Oliver Sacks — but no dedicated, named, peer-reviewed paper proposing it for Joan specifically turned up in the research for this book, and the two women's cases should not be quietly merged into one just because they are both medieval and both visionary [52].

[Beat 28]
A different kind of skepticism altogether, neither ecclesiastical nor clinical, came from the novelist and Nobel laureate Anatole France, whose 1908 biography argued Joan suffered hallucinations "of all the senses, especially hearing," produced not by fraud on her part but by a mind primed through childhood by clerical influence and local prophecy circulating in the countryside [53]. This is a meaningfully different claim from a medical diagnosis — a sincere experience, naturalistically explained, rather than a named pathology — and it did not go unanswered. The Scottish historian Andrew Lang published a rival biography the same year attacking France's carelessness with the sources [54], and the Catholic Encyclopedia's 1910 entry rebutted the "priestly coaching" theory directly: "There is not a shadow of evidence to support this theory of priestly advisers coaching Joan in a part" [55]. Worth noting for calibration, since this book insists on distinguishing serious minority positions from genuine fringe ones: a separate, non-medical, non-ecclesiastical claim — Margaret Murray's 1921 theory that Joan was secretly practicing a surviving pagan witch-cult — has not gained acceptance among academic historians and is treated in current scholarship as pseudo-historical fantasy, not a live position on this spectrum [56].

[Beat 29]
One position belongs on this spectrum mainly by its conspicuous absence. The 1431 trial itself operated on something close to the theory that Joan's claimed voices were either demonic or invented outright — but that was a prosecutorial position built to secure a heresy conviction, not a historian's disinterested finding, and it was itself nullified twenty-five years later as procedurally corrupt. Among the serious modern historians surveyed for this book — Pernoud, Taylor, DeVries, Sullivan, Vale — not one argues that Joan consciously fabricated her voices as a cynical political device. Even Anatole France, the most skeptical writer discussed above, locates their origin in sincere belief shaped by environment, not deliberate deception. The plain fraud hypothesis, in other words, essentially does not exist today as a live scholarly position; it survives only as the losing side's own discredited framing from six centuries ago [57].

[Beat 30]
Which leaves a final position that deserves to stand on its own, not as a hedge tacked onto the others but as a genuine third pole in its own right: the argument that diagnosing the dead is not simply difficult but methodologically illegitimate. The bioethicist Osamu Muramoto has laid out one version of the case formally — an ontological problem (whether a modern disease category is even the same entity as whatever a person experienced six centuries before that category existed), an epistemic problem (a diagnosis that can be neither confirmed nor falsified without the ability to examine the subject is not a diagnosis, it is a narrative wearing a diagnosis's clothes), and an ethical problem (publishing a clinical label on a person who never consented to examination) [58]. The paleopathologist Piers Mitchell makes a related, more source-specific version of the same argument: retrospective diagnosis is only defensible where a historical text gives symptom description specific and detailed enough to support it, and for the large majority of historical illness accounts, it simply does not — a standard this book takes seriously when it comes to a text as adversarial and theologically filtered as Joan's own trial record, built from a hostile interrogation transcript translated into clerical Latin by the same institution seeking her death [59]. It is a telling detail that this caution shows up inside the medical literature itself and not only from historians and paleopathologists defending Joan against it: more than one of the papers surveyed above hedges its own conclusion, and at least one — the tuberculosis hypothesis — undermines its own premise in print. The most intellectually honest position available on this entire spectrum may simply be the refusal to render a verdict at all, and this book takes that refusal seriously rather than treating it as an evasion.

[Beat 31]
One more possibility deserves its own space before this chapter closes, because the question this book's title poses — prophecy or pathology — is usually asked as if only one answer can be true, and that framing is itself worth interrogating rather than simply adopting. Suppose, for the sake of the question, that some named or unnamed neurological condition really did produce the specific sensory experience Joan reported: a voice, a light, a presence, eventually three named figures. Does that fact, if it were somehow provable, actually settle anything about whether God was speaking through it? A great many serious theological traditions — including strands of Joan's own Catholic tradition — have never required that divine communication bypass the physical brain entirely in order to count as real; a mechanism is not automatically a disproof of a message, any more than a telephone line being made of copper disproves the conversation traveling through it. Put the other way: did God speak to Joan, or did God speak to a mentally ill girl, is not obviously a choice between two mutually exclusive sentences. It may simply be one sentence, asked twice, in two different vocabularies — one theological, one clinical — neither of which this book will privilege over the other, and neither of which, on the evidence available, can actually be ruled out.

[Beat 32]
Then and Now

Every era gets to choose its own vocabulary for an experience it cannot independently verify, and every era mistakes its vocabulary for an explanation. The fifteenth century had angel, saint, and demon, and used them to build a court that killed a teenager. The twentieth century had hysteria, and later schizophrenia, and later still a named seizure subtype argued over in peer-reviewed journals — a genuine gain in precision, and a genuine advance in what evidence can actually be checked, but not, in the end, a different kind of certainty about a woman none of these frameworks can examine. What has changed is the machinery surrounding the question: no modern court would burn a person for the content of her testimony about her own inner life. What has not changed is the underlying wish driving every framework on this spectrum, ancient or modern, devotional or clinical — the wish to make a private, unverifiable experience finally, safely explainable, so that the rest of us don't have to sit, unresolved, with the possibility that some things simply cannot be known from the outside.

[Beat 33]
The first time Joan asked anyone official for help, she was refused, and refused in a way meant to end the conversation. In May 1428 she traveled the short distance to Vaucouleurs, the nearest fortified town loyal to Charles VII, and asked its captain, Robert de Baudricourt, for an armed escort to the Dauphin. Baudricourt was unimpressed by a teenage peasant girl claiming divine instruction; he dismissed her outright, and reportedly told the cousin who had brought her simply to slap her and take her home [60]. It is worth sitting for a moment in that first failure rather than rushing past it toward the parts of the story where she succeeds. Whatever this mission was, it did not open any doors on the strength of conviction alone. It took a second attempt, and eight more months.

[Beat 34]
She returned to Vaucouleurs in January 1429 and this time did not leave. She lodged locally, gathered a real following among the townspeople, and kept pressing her case in public rather than waiting for a private audience [61]. What is credited, in varying proportion depending on which source you trust, with actually changing Baudricourt's mind is a specific claim of foreknowledge: Joan is said to have described the outcome of a French defeat near Rouvray — later known as the Battle of the Herrings, fought 12 February 1429 — before any official word of it could plausibly have reached Vaucouleurs [62]. Modern historians handle this the way they handle most of the story's more dramatic single claims: as tradition worth reporting honestly, not as settled fact worth repeating as if it were a police report. What is not in dispute is the outcome. Combined with her sheer persistence, it was enough. By February, Baudricourt relented.

[Beat 35]
He provided her with an escort of six men in all — the esquires Jean de Metz and Bertrand de Poulengy, their own servants, a royal messenger named Colet de Vienne, and an archer, Richard Larcher — a horse, a sword, male clothing, and a letter of introduction to the Dauphin's court [63]. The eleven-day ride to Chinon went largely by night, through territory that could easily have gotten all six of them killed by an Anglo-Burgundian patrol. What survives of that journey survives almost entirely through de Metz's and de Poulengy's own testimony, given decades later at the 1456 Trial of Nullification, and it is unusually tender testimony for a military escort report: de Metz recalled that although he and Poulengy slept beside her on the road, her clothing remained closely fastened and he felt no carnal desire toward her, "by reason of the virtue I sensed in her" — and that she told him, at some point on that ride, that she would rather have stayed spinning wool at her mother's side, "but this thing I must do, for my Lord wills it" [64]. It is one of the only moments in this entire book where the record lets you hear something close to private reluctance, rather than public mission.

[Beat 36]
Then and Now

A young person today who wants to be taken seriously by an institution still, in most cases, has to survive a first rejection before finding the second door — a hearing, an application, an audition that gets turned down before the one that doesn't. What has genuinely vanished is the specific shape Joan's second attempt had to take: showing up, in person, repeatedly, in a garrison town, wearing men's clothes because a woman traveling openly through contested territory was not a safe woman, and simply refusing to leave until the man with the authority to say yes got tired of saying no. Persistence has not gone out of style. The specific physical danger of persisting — the eleven-day night ride through country that could kill you for being on the wrong road — is, for most people reading this, no longer the price of it.

[Beat 37]
Joan reached Chinon on 23 February 1429 and was received in audience two days later, on the twenty-fifth [65]. What happened in that room has become, over six centuries, one of the most retold scenes in the entire story, and also one of the most reliably overdressed. The popular version has Charles hiding among his courtiers, disguised or simply standing apart from a more richly dressed nobleman, waiting to see whether the peasant girl could pick her true king out of a crowd by divine instinct alone — and Joan walking straight to him. Contemporary and near-contemporary sources genuinely disagree about how staged or spontaneous this was, and historians generally treat the most theatrical tellings as embellishment added in the retelling, while still agreeing that some kind of audience took place and left a strong impression on the court [66]. The theatrical version is not fabricated from nothing. It has simply been sanded smooth by five hundred years of people wanting a better story than "an audience occurred, and it went well."

[Beat 38]
Joan was lodged in the château's Coudray tower, where women of the court, acting under the supervision of Yolande of Aragon — Charles's mother-in-law and one of the sharpest political operators of his court — examined her physically to confirm she was, in the language of the period, "a true and entire maid," rebutting rumors already circulating that called her a whore or a witch [67]. This was not incidental to her credibility; a virgin claiming visions occupied a different, more defensible theological category than a woman of doubtful chastity making the same claim, and the court knew exactly what it was protecting when it ordered the examination. Rather than act immediately on an unverified claim from an unknown girl, Charles referred her onward for a formal clerical examination at Poitiers — the subject of the next chapter — before committing any actual resources to her. She returned to Chinon and was received again by the king between roughly 27 March and 5 April 1429, once the theologians had rendered their qualified verdict [68].

[Beat 39]
Somewhere inside these two audiences sits the single most genuinely undocumented moment in Joan's entire recorded life, and this book would rather admit that plainly than paper over it with an invented scene. Joan and Charles spoke privately at some point, and whatever passed between them satisfied him enough to act. Contemporaries and later writers alike called this "the sign" — Joan herself, at trial, described an angel bringing Charles "a beautiful crown of pure gold," bowing and saying "Sire, here is your sign. Take it" [22]; yet near her own death she gave a noticeably more figurative account of that same sign, closer to her own promise to lead him to Reims than to a literal jeweled visitation [23]. Neither Joan nor Charles ever fully disclosed, in any surviving document, exactly what was said in that room. This book will not invent it for you. What can be said honestly is only this: something in that private exchange was decisive enough to move a disinherited, cautious, factionally paralyzed prince from doing nothing to committing an army — and six hundred years later, nobody knows exactly what it was.

[Beat 40]
Then and Now

Every organization still runs some version of the Chinon test: the unproven candidate, the skeptical gatekeeper, the private conversation that decides everything and that nobody outside the room ever gets the full transcript of. What has changed is mostly procedural — a modern equivalent would generate paperwork, references, a formal record of what was said and why it convinced someone. What has not changed is the plain human fact underneath it: the biggest decisions institutions make about which unproven person to trust are still made, disproportionately, in exactly the kind of closed-door, undocumented conversation this chapter has to admit it cannot fully reconstruct, six hundred years or six days later.

[Beat 41]
Charles did not simply take Joan's word for her mission, and it is worth crediting him for that rather than treating his caution as cowardice. He sent her to Poitiers, seat of the university and clergy still loyal to his cause after the loss of Paris, to be formally examined by a commission of theologians convened under Regnault de Chartres, Archbishop of Reims. The examination ran roughly three weeks in March 1429 [69]. Medievalist Deborah Fraioli has shown that even inside Joan's own camp, contemporaries were genuinely divided over whether her mission was divinely or diabolically inspired, working through the era's own formal apparatus for judging exactly this kind of claim — discretio spirituum, the "discernment of spirits" [70]. This was not a rubber stamp. It was live theological risk assessment, conducted by men whose careers and souls were both on the line if they certified a fraud, or a demon, as God's messenger.

[Beat 42]
The commission's actual findings survive only secondhand — the original register is lost, known mainly through Joan's own repeated invocation of it two years later, under interrogation at Rouen, and through later summaries [71]. What those findings amounted to was not the ringing endorsement popular memory sometimes assigns them. The theologians reported a "favorable presumption": nothing in her conduct or speech contrary to the faith, and no good reason to refuse her offered help to the kingdom [72]. That is a considerably more modest verdict than "she is sent by God," and it is worth noticing the gap between the two, because everything that followed — the armor, the army, the campaign — rested on a commission's decision not to say no, not on a commission's decision to say yes.

[Beat 43]
Cleared to help, Joan was equipped for war out of Tours: Charles's household commissioned a custom suit of armor and a small military establishment in her name, and at her own direction a sword was retrieved from behind the altar of the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois — an old, marked blade she said she had never seen but described precisely enough for someone to find it — along with a purpose-made banner bearing the fleur-de-lis [73]. Sources genuinely disagree on whether the physical examination described in the previous chapter happened at Chinon or here at Tours; this book states that disagreement rather than silently picking a side [74]. What is not in dispute is the sequence: by the time Joan left the Tours area for the Loire, she had cleared both the theological screening and the physical one, and was armed, mounted, and attached to a household built for a commander riding to a siege, not a girl riding to a shrine.

[Beat 44]
Then and Now

Every institution still has some version of Poitiers: a vetting process an unproven claim has to survive before real resources get committed to it, staffed by people whose own credibility is on the line if they get the call wrong. What has changed almost entirely is the vocabulary of the risk being assessed — a modern committee is not weighing whether a candidate might be a literal instrument of God or the Devil, and has correspondingly more comfortable ways to say "not yet" or "not sure" than a fifteenth-century theologian facing an army that needed an answer within weeks did. What has not changed is the shape of the verdict itself, once you strip the theology away: not a triumphant yes, but a considered, hedged, career-protecting "we found no reason to refuse her" — the oldest and least glamorous form every real institutional endorsement actually takes.

[Beat 45]
Orléans had been under English siege since 12 October 1428, and both sides expected its fall to open the entire unoccupied south of France to the English regent, John, Duke of Bedford — finishing the conquest Henry V had left incomplete at his own death [73]. The city sat on the last major Loire bridge crossing still in Charles VII's hands, the actual hinge connecting his court to the rest of his own kingdom. This was not a symbolic target. It was the strategic bottleneck of the entire war, and everyone on both sides of it knew it.

[Beat 46]
Joan arrived with a relief convoy in late April 1429 and entered the city on the twenty-ninth. Jean de Dunois, the crown's professional commander holding the city, later testified to an episode he cited for the rest of his life as evidence of her mission: the wind over the Loire, unfavorable for the provisioning boats all day, turned in their favor at almost the exact moment Joan reached the riverbank [74]. Dunois was not a credulous man, and this book does not treat his testimony as proof of anything supernatural — only as proof of exactly how strong an impression she made on a hardened professional soldier who had every institutional reason to be skeptical of an unproven girl arriving at his siege.

[Beat 47]
Within days, French forces stormed the English siege forts ringing the city, most decisively Les Tourelles, the fortified bridgehead, on 7 May; the English abandoned the siege the next day, 8 May 1429, seven months after it began and barely a week after Joan's arrival [75]. Here is where this book has to hold two facts at once without flattening either. The popular version — Joan single-handedly won the siege — is not what the record supports; Dunois held effective command, and captains like La Hire brought real, professional battlefield experience Joan simply did not have. But the opposite over-correction — that she was a mascot, a morale prop with no actual effect — does not survive contact with the record either. Every source agrees the army's aggression increased sharply with her present and urging it forward, and the siege broke in days, not months, once she arrived. The honest account sits in the friction between those two facts, not on either side of it: a professional army, competently commanded, that fought differently, and faster, because of her.

[Beat 48]
Then and Now

Every organization that has ever hired a charismatic outsider to fix a broken effort runs into this same interpretive fork afterward: did the new person actually change the outcome, or did the professionals already in the room do the real work while the newcomer got the credit? Orléans is one of the oldest, best-documented examples of that exact argument, and it has never fully settled, six hundred years on, because both readings are honestly defensible from the same evidence. What has not changed at all is the shape of the dispute itself — a group achieves something faster than expected after a new arrival shows up, and the argument over how much of that speed belongs to the newcomer versus the people who were already capable begins immediately, and rarely ends.

[Beat 49]
Orléans broke the siege; the Loire campaign that followed broke the English field army. Through June 1429, French forces took the remaining Loire strongpoints — Jargeau, Meung, Beaugency — clearing the river of English garrisons one town at a time [76]. This was fast, sustained offensive work, a real departure from years of largely defensive French campaigning, and it did not let up once Orléans was won.

[Beat 50]
It ended at Patay, on 18 June 1429, in a battle that was barely a battle at all in the usual sense. An English relief force under John Talbot and Sir John Fastolf, retreating north after the Loire strongpoints fell, was caught by French scouts and vanguard cavalry under La Hire and the Duke of Alençon — with Joan accompanying the main body — still forming its defensive line of stakes and archers [77]. The French cavalry charged before the longbowmen could plant those stakes, and what followed was closer to a massacre than an engagement: contemporary estimates put English dead, overwhelmingly archers, somewhere between a thousand and several thousand, against light French losses [78]. Talbot was captured. Fastolf, one of the few senior officers still mounted, got off the field alive, which cost him his reputation back in England for the rest of his life.

[Beat 51]
The weapon that broke at Patay is worth naming specifically, because it was not a minor one: the English longbow had won Crécy, Poitiers, and Agincourt, three of the most lopsided French defeats of the entire war, precisely because it could shred a cavalry charge before it closed. At Patay, for one afternoon, that math ran the other way, because the archers never got the seconds they needed to plant their stakes. Patay is, in the driest possible military terms, the clean tactical annihilation Orléans was not — a siege relieved is a reversal; a field army destroyed outright is something closer to an ending. It is what made the next move, a march deep into contested territory toward a coronation nobody had attempted in years, something more than a symbolic gesture.

[Beat 52]
Then and Now

A weapon system that has dominated a battlefield for generations can still lose everything in the specific handful of seconds it takes to deploy it, and Patay is one of military history's cleanest illustrations of exactly that fact — the longbow did not get worse between Agincourt and Patay; the English simply ran out of time to use it. That mechanism has not changed at all, in war or anywhere else: the most reliable advantage a group holds is still only as good as its ability to actually set it up before the moment that needs it arrives. What has changed is almost everything about the scale and speed at which that same principle now plays out — modern engagements are measured in the reaction time of sensors and systems, not the minutes it takes a company of archers to drive stakes into French mud, but the underlying vulnerability, a superior tool caught not-yet-ready, has never gone away.

[Beat 53]
Reims mattered because of a bottle of oil. French royal coronations traditionally took place there, not in Paris, tied to the Sainte Ampoule — a vial believed to hold the oil that had anointed Clovis, first king of the Franks, and by extension every legitimate French king since [79]. In 1429, Reims sat well inside territory aligned with the English and their Burgundian allies, considerably north and east of anything Charles VII actually controlled. Marching there was not a safe victory lap. It was a deliberate gamble, and Joan pressed hard for it specifically because an uncrowned Dauphin was a weaker claimant than an anointed king — she was arguing, in effect, that the war's real center of gravity was not another siege but a ceremony, and that the ceremony itself would do political work no further fighting could [80].

[Beat 54]
The army's momentum, fresh off Patay, carried the argument. Marching through Burgundian-held towns, several of which opened their gates without a fight rather than test the force that had just annihilated an English army, the column reached Reims, which likewise submitted, and Charles VII was crowned in Reims Cathedral on 17 July 1429 [81]. Joan stood near him bearing her banner. Gilles de Rais, then a young royal captain, was one of four lords given the honor of escorting the Sainte Ampoule itself from the Abbey of Saint-Remi for the ceremony, and was created Marshal of France that same day [82]. Jean d'Alençon, "le beau duc," testified decades later that he never left Joan's side from the coronation until the army's subsequent advance on Paris, describing her as "a good and modest woman, living as a Catholic and very pious" [83].

[Beat 55]
This is the high-water mark of the story, and it is worth naming it as such before the book turns, in the next chapters, toward everything that follows a high-water mark. Every earlier claim Joan made under interrogation about her mission's content — raise the siege, see the king crowned — had now been fulfilled, in order, within five months of a nearly friendless girl's arrival at a minor garrison town. Whatever this was, it was not a mission that quietly faded into ambiguity. It kept its own stated terms, in public, in front of an army and a cathedral full of witnesses. What Joan is reported to have said next — that she would only be with the king "one year" — is a detail this book will return to, because the year that followed did not go the way the coronation might have suggested it would [84].

[Beat 56]
Then and Now

A symbol still does real political work today, even in societies that no longer believe a vial of oil confers legitimacy on a ruler — an inauguration, a swearing-in, a first hundred days, all borrow the same underlying logic Joan was arguing for at Reims: that the ceremony marking power is not decoration on top of the power itself, but part of how the power becomes real to the people asked to obey it. What has genuinely vanished is the specific stakes of getting the ceremony wrong or missing it — no modern claimant to office loses his legal standing for failing to reach a particular cathedral by a particular date while an army chases him. What remains, stubbornly, is the underlying human fact Joan understood better than most of the professional soldiers and politicians around her: that being right, and being seen to be right, in front of the correct witnesses in the correct place, are not the same accomplishment, and only one of them ends an argument.

[Beat 57]
The momentum that carried an army from Orléans to a coronation in under three months did not carry it into Paris. Joan pressed for an immediate assault on the capital; the royal court, with its own factional politics and its own caution about overextending a supply line deep in contested territory, hesitated, negotiated, and delayed [85]. This book keeps its treatment of the resulting court politics deliberately brief — the maneuvering among Charles VII's advisors is a subject other books have covered at far greater length, and it is not where this book's own argument lives. What matters for Joan's story is simpler and colder: the single unbroken run of fulfilled predictions that had carried her from a Domrémy garden to a cathedral altar stalled, for the first time, against an obstacle that had nothing to do with voices, saints, or signs. It stalled against a royal council's ordinary reluctance to gamble everything on one more push.

[Beat 58]
An assault on Paris did eventually go forward, on 8 September 1429, and it failed; Joan was wounded in the thigh by a crossbow bolt during the attack on the Saint-Honoré gate, and the army withdrew [86]. It is worth naming this plainly, because the rest of the story is easier to misread without it: this was not a seamless ascent from Domrémy to the stake. It had a real defeat in the middle of it, months before the capture that ended her freedom, and the defeat came at the one target — the enemy's own capital — that would have most decisively finished what Orléans and Reims had started.

[Beat 59]
Then and Now

Momentum is a real, measurable thing in almost any human undertaking — a project, a career, a war — and it is also, reliably, the thing least under the control of the person who built it. Joan generated an extraordinary run of it in a matter of weeks; she did not, in the end, get to decide when or whether it was spent, because that decision belonged to a royal council weighing risks she was not fully positioned to weigh herself. That imbalance — between the person who creates momentum and the institution that decides what to do with it — has not gone anywhere. It is simply less often settled, today, with a crossbow bolt to the thigh at a city gate.

[Beat 60]
By the spring of 1430, Joan's active military career had entered a quieter, more scattered phase — smaller actions, less coordinated support from the court, the war's momentum no longer running in a single clean direction the way it had between April and July of the previous year. Compiègne, a town on the Oise loyal to Charles VII, came under threat from Burgundian forces allied with the English, and Joan entered the town to support its defense [87].

[Beat 61]
On 23 May 1430 she led a sortie out of the town against the besieging Burgundian troops, an attempt to disrupt their siege lines. The sortie met Burgundian reinforcements lying in wait, and as the French force fell back toward the town, the gates were raised or closed before Joan's own party could get inside, leaving her isolated outside the walls [88]. Historians differ on whether that gate closure was ordinary defensive necessity under pressure, panic, or — in largely discredited popular retellings — deliberate betrayal by the town's governor; the more sober modern reading treats it as a standard defensive measure rather than proven treachery, and this book follows that reading while noting the alternative exists [89]. She was pulled from her horse and captured by soldiers under Jean de Luxembourg, a leading captain in the service of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy.

[Beat 62]
Jean de Luxembourg held her afterward, first at Beaulieu, then longer at his wife's family castle of Beaurevoir, before turning her over to the English for 10,000 livres [90]. One detail from Beaurevoir deserves its own sentence rather than a footnote's worth of space: Jean de Luxembourg's elderly aunt, the Demoiselle de Luxembourg, showed Joan real kindness during her captivity there and pleaded with her nephew not to sell her to the English, reportedly even offering to name him her heir if he refused [91]. He sold her anyway. It is one of the plainest illustrations in this entire book of how completely Joan's fate had become a matter of factional transaction rather than personal judgment — a French prisoner, sold by a French vassal of Burgundy, to the English crown, over the direct objection of his own family.

[Beat 63]
Then and Now

A gate closing a half-second too soon, in the middle of a chaotic retreat, is exactly the kind of event that gets rewritten by hindsight into either pure accident or pure betrayal, depending on what the person doing the rewriting already needs to believe about the people involved — and Compiègne is a very old example of a pattern that has never gone out of fashion. What has genuinely changed is the specific mechanism by which a captured person's fate then got decided: a ransom auction between factions, with a family member's plea for mercy weighed openly against a cash sum and losing. Modern captivity still involves cold transactional calculation more often than anyone likes to admit, but rarely anymore as a single, nameable, negotiated cash price, refused kindness included, on the record.

[Beat 64]
Rouen was the capital of English-occupied Normandy and the seat of the English regency government — the strongest, best-defended base of institutional power the English held in France, and the place Joan's captors chose to send her once the sale from Burgundy was complete. Her prosecution was framed, from the start, as an ecclesiastical matter: heresy, a crime the Church tried, not the crown. That framing carried a specific, binding procedural obligation which her jailers simply ignored [92].

[Beat 65]
Canon law and custom required a woman facing spiritual charges to be held in a church prison, under female custodians. Joan instead was held in a secular military cell within the Castle of Rouen, under the authority of the Earl of Warwick, guarded directly by English soldiers and reportedly chained at times [93]. Modern scholars of the trial record — Daniel Hobbins prominent among them — identify this as one of the clearest procedural irregularities of the entire prosecution, and it is worth being precise about why it mattered beyond simple discomfort: an ecclesiastical prisoner under female religious custody had a real measure of protection an English-guarded secular cell did not offer, and everyone arranging her confinement that way knew exactly what protection they were removing.

[Beat 66]
Her male clothing, which had been a practical necessity of campaigning and travel since Vaucouleurs, became a live legal and physical battleground inside that cell — one this book will return to directly in the chapters on her trial and its aftermath, since it is the specific detail that ultimately determined whether she lived or died. For now it is enough to establish the setting plainly: a nineteen-year-old woman, held under armed guard by the army of the country prosecuting her, inside a fortress her own side had no ability to reach, awaiting a trial whose outcome her captors had every institutional reason to want decided before it began.

[Beat 67]
One further examination of Joan's body took place here, at Rouen, before the formal heresy trial even opened, and its purpose was the opposite of hostile. On the orders of Anne of Burgundy, Duchess of Bedford — wife of the English regent — matrons including a woman named Anne Bavon examined Joan and again found her physically intact, confirming the same finding reached at Chinon nearly two years earlier [94]. The Duchess then had it expressly ordered that no one offer Joan violence — the examination's finding was used to place her under a measure of protection, not to clear the way for anything. It is worth stating plainly what this examination was not, since a popular but unverified claim sometimes attaches itself to this moment: there is no primary trial-document evidence that Joan was examined specifically to rule out pregnancy as a legal shield against execution, and no record that any such consideration ever arose in her actual case. That particular idea traces to Shakespeare's Henry VI, Part 1, written more than a century later, where a desperate, cynical Joan falsely claims pregnancy to delay her death — a dramatic invention, not a historical event, and this book will not present it as one.

[Beat 68]
Then and Now

The specific legal fiction Rouen relied on — try her as a Church matter, then quietly strip away every actual protection Church custody would have provided — has an uncomfortably durable shape: choosing the forum that offers a defendant the fewest genuine safeguards while still calling the proceeding by whatever name lends it the most legitimacy. Modern legal systems have real, if imperfect, mechanisms built specifically to catch and correct exactly that kind of forum-shopping. What has not changed is the underlying temptation, whenever a captor gets to choose both which rules apply and who enforces them: to keep the name of due process while removing everything that actually protects the person subject to it.

[Beat 69]
The Trial of Condemnation ran in two phases. A preliminary inquest opened 9 January 1431, gathering depositions — testimony was even sent for from Domrémy, Joan's own home village, presumably in hopes of finding something damning in her childhood — and compiling an initial seventy articles of accusation. The formal trial opened 21 February 1431 before Pierre Cauchon, Bishop of Beauvais, with roughly forty assessors present across its sessions, and Jean Le Maistre, the Vice-Inquisitor of France for the diocese of Rouen, required by canon law to co-preside for the proceeding to carry ecclesiastical validity [95].

[Beat 70]
Cauchon's motives deserve a plainer accounting than "an English stooge," even though the plainer accounting does not flatter him. He was a Paris-trained theologian whose career had run through the Burgundian party into direct English service — banished from Paris in 1413 for Burgundian sympathies, later a counselor to the boy-king Henry VI — and he personally brokered Joan's transfer from Burgundian to English custody before maneuvering to try her at Rouen despite Beauvais lying outside his own diocese [96]. A documented, unglamorous incentive sat behind his zeal: Cauchon angled for the vacant Archbishopric of Rouen during the trial itself, an ambition he abandoned only when the chapter opposed it, settling in 1432 for the see of Lisieux instead — a promotion more than one historian reads as his English reward for securing the conviction [97]. Not every scholar accepts the simplest version of this reading; Daniel Hobbins has argued Cauchon may have seen a procedurally correct trial as Joan's best actual chance of escaping summary execution by her English custodians. Both readings can be true at once, in the sense that a man can be genuinely careful about procedure while still wanting, and getting, the verdict his patrons required.

[Beat 71]
Le Maistre's own later testimony, given at the 1456 Trial of Nullification, shows a man who did not want to be in that room at all: he objected to sitting on the tribunal, argued he had no jurisdiction in occupied territory outside his own inquisitorial seat, and was, by multiple accounts, compelled to attend only after the English threatened his life [98]. The trial's prosecuting officer, Jean d'Estivet, shows the opposite temperament entirely — he personally drafted the original seventy articles, later condensed by the theologian Nicolas Midi into the Twelve Articles that formed the working indictment, barred Joan from attending Mass over the "impropriety" of her male dress, and reportedly threatened at least one assessor who hesitated to render an unfavorable opinion of her [99]. One trial, then, staffed by men occupying every point on a real spectrum of complicity: a presiding bishop with a career-shaping incentive to convict, a co-judge compelled under threat of death, and a prosecutor who needed no compulsion at all.

[Beat 72]
The Twelve Articles, finalized 5 April 1431, covered Joan's claimed visions and voices, the "sign" given to Charles VII, her male dress, her leap from the tower at Compiègne during an earlier escape attempt, her military conduct, and her claim of certainty of salvation, among other counts; she was found guilty on all twelve on 23 May 1431 [100]. It is on the voices specifically that the interrogation returned, session after session, with the same deliberate persistence documented across the whole record — trying, plainly, to catch her contradicting herself on the one subject the entire case actually rested on. She did not. The single most quoted exchange from the entire trial belongs here, because it is the clearest surviving example of what she was actually facing, moment to moment, in that room: asked whether she knew if she was in the grace of God — a question theologians of the day held could not be truthfully answered yes (presumption) or no (despair, itself heretical) — she answered, "If I am not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God keep me in it. I would be the most wretched person in the world if I knew I were not in the grace of God" [101]. Whether that answer reflects quick native intelligence or prior coaching is a live historiographical dispute this book will not resolve for you; what is not disputed is that it is, by any measure, one of the sharpest answers on record to a question designed to have no safe answer.

[Beat 73]
Then and Now

A "have you stopped beating your wife" question — one built so that any direct answer convicts the person answering it — is old enough to have a name in rhetoric, and Joan's judges deployed a version of it with real theological teeth behind it, not as a rhetorical trick but as an actual doctrinal trap with a heresy charge waiting on either side. Modern courtrooms still contain versions of the same maneuver, dressed in different vocabulary, and a competent witness today is coached specifically on how to avoid exactly this kind of forced-choice question. What has not changed is the underlying test such a question is really administering: not "what is true," but "can this person, under real pressure, find the narrow answer that refuses the frame entirely." Joan, apparently without coaching of the modern kind, found it.

[Beat 74]
On 24 May 1431, in the cemetery of the abbey of Saint-Ouen, Joan was brought before the assembled judges and a large crowd to hear a public sermon by Guillaume Erard, a doctor of theology of considerable renown brought in specifically for the occasion. Erard's sermon condemned Joan and, pointedly, named Charles VII himself as complicit in heresy for having trusted her [102]. When the sermon ended, Erard pressed Joan directly to submit and recant, telling her — per the trial record — that she had had ample time to decide and that refusal meant burning that same day [103].

[Beat 75]
Confronted with the stake already built and visibly waiting, Joan — by her own later admission afraid to die — made her mark on a written abjuration formula. Multiple accounts note her hand was guided in producing the signature, since she could not read or write in the conventional sense [104]. The sentence that followed was not death but life imprisonment on bread and water, and it is worth pausing on how narrow the actual space was in that cemetery between those two outcomes: one sermon, one threat of immediate fire, one mark on a document she may not have been able to read for herself.

[Beat 76]
It did not hold. On 28 May, guards found her again wearing male clothing in her cell. Under questioning she stated that her voices had reproached her for having submitted against their will, and that she had resumed the clothing on her own initiative rather than under any renewed compulsion [105]. Historians have long noted the physical logic sitting quietly underneath this moment, whatever the theological framing: a secular prison cell, guarded by men, offered a woman almost no meaningful protection in a skirt that men's hose, doublet, and boots, laced tightly together, at least made harder to simply tear off. Her judges did not weigh that practical reading. They declared her a relapsed heretic — a category with no second abjuration available under canon law — and the trial's outcome, from that point forward, was no longer in genuine doubt.

[Beat 77]
Then and Now

A confession extracted with a lit stake in view and revoked four days later, once the immediate threat had lifted, would today be recognized instantly, by name, as coerced — inadmissible on its face in any court operating under a modern standard of voluntariness. Joan's judges had no such standard to violate, or rather, they had one and simply declined to apply it: canon law of the period did recognize that confessions obtained under duress were suspect, and chose not to notice the duress sitting in plain sight in their own courtyard. What has changed is the vocabulary and the enforcement; the underlying, cynical maneuver — get the signature under maximum pressure, then treat any later retraction as proof of the original guilt rather than proof of the original coercion — remains disturbingly recognizable.

[Beat 78]
Joan was burned at the stake in the Place du Vieux-Marché, Rouen's market square, on the morning of 30 May 1431. She was handed to secular authority the day before, since the Church itself could not carry out an execution directly — a canon-law technicality that let the men who had spent months building the case against her also avoid, on paper, personally lighting the fire [106].

[Beat 79]
Two clergy who had shown her real sympathy through the trial's final days attended her at the stake, and their later testimony at the 1456 Trial of Nullification is the primary source for nearly everything known about her actual last minutes, since the official Latin trial record ends with the sentence rather than a narrative of the burning [107]. Isambart de la Pierre, a Dominican friar who had risked his own safety by treating her with visible compassion throughout, preached to her, recited the seven penitential psalms at her request, and held a crucifix before her at the stake as the fire was lit. Jean Massieu, the trial's usher, who had escorted her into and out of every session since it opened in February, attended her to the end and later recounted her final requests: that the crowd forgive her and pray for her, and that she in turn forgave everyone who had wronged her [108].

[Beat 80]
Her ashes were reportedly thrown into the Seine from the nearby Pont Mathilde, a deliberate final measure to ensure no relic of her body could be kept or venerated — the same institutional apparatus that had spent months building a legal case against her taking one more precaution, after her death, against the possibility that she might matter to people anyway [109]. It did not work. Within twenty-five years her own mother would be standing before papal judges in Paris asking for the verdict to be undone, and within five centuries she would be a canonized saint of the same Church that burned her.

[Beat 81]
Then and Now

Destroying a body specifically to prevent it from becoming a site of devotion is an old, recurring instinct, and it has almost never worked across recorded history, for a fairly simple reason: what people actually venerate is rarely the physical remains themselves, but the story attached to them, and a story survives a river perfectly well. Modern authorities who wish to erase a person's influence still reach for a version of the same instinct — scrubbing records, denying burial sites, controlling the narrative around a death — and it fails for the identical reason, just faster now, since a story today needs no relic at all to keep traveling.

[Beat 82]
Word of Joan's capture reached both camps within days, and the reactions are worth reporting side by side, because they measure exactly how seriously each side actually took her. The Burgundian chronicler Enguerrand de Monstrelet — writing from the opposing side, and no friend of the Armagnac cause — recorded that the English and Burgundians were "overjoyed, more so than if they had taken 500 combatants, for they had never feared or dreaded any other commander" [110]. That is not a hostile chronicler mocking a superstitious enemy. It is a professional assessment, from the losing side of her own campaigns, that she had personally been worth more to French momentum than five hundred ordinary soldiers. The Armagnac response was correspondingly bleak: the news reportedly devastated the French court and army, a mood shift with real practical consequences — no organized, well-resourced rescue effort ever reached her, despite La Hire's own attempts.

[Beat 83]
Charles VII, the man Joan had risked everything to see crowned, went on to reign until 1461 — and it is worth following that reign forward rather than leaving him frozen at Reims in 1429, since the book's own title question deserves an answer to the obvious follow-up: was he worth it? By most measures, yes, though not gloriously. His government pushed through the reforms that actually finished the war: the Estates-General approved the taille as a permanent direct royal tax in 1439, freeing the crown from constantly re-negotiating funding, and between 1445 and 1448 his administration created France's first standing professional army — the compagnies d'ordonnance for heavy cavalry and the francs-archers drawn from the commons for infantry [111]. Those reforms, not another miraculous intervention, are what won Castillon in 1453 and expelled the English from France entirely except Calais, ending the war for good, twenty-two years after Joan's death.

[Beat 84]
History gave Charles VII two nicknames, and the gap between them is the whole verdict on his personal character. "Charles le Victorieux" — the Victorious — is the flattering one, crediting him personally. "Charles le Bien Servi" — the Well-Served — is the honest one, and French historians have long read it as gently ironic: crediting everyone around him — Joan herself, the artillery-modernizing Bureau brothers, chancellor Jouvenel des Ursins, the financier Jacques Cœur, his shrewd mother-in-law Yolande of Aragon, his wife Marie of Anjou, his mistress Agnès Sorel — rather than the king's own initiative [112]. It is, in other words, the same open question this book has asked about Joan's own contribution at Orléans, applied to the man she crowned: how much of a documented success belongs to the person nominally in charge of it, and how much belongs to the capable people surrounding them.

[Beat 85]
His heir did not make the question of good kingship any simpler. Charles VII and his wife Marie of Anjou had roughly fourteen children, several dying young; his mistress Agnès Sorel, the first officially acknowledged royal mistress in French history, bore him three or four daughters. His actual successor, the future Louis XI, spent his own youth in open rebellion against his father: at sixteen or seventeen he joined the Praguerie, a 1440 noble revolt against Charles VII, apparently hoping to be installed as regent in his father's place; the revolt failed, and Louis's continued scheming afterward — including plots against the king's own chief minister — got him banished from court entirely. He set up a rival household in the Dauphiné, married against his father's wishes, and when Charles VII finally sent troops to bring him to heel, Louis fled and was sheltered for years by Philip the Good of Burgundy — his father's old wartime enemy — until Charles VII died in 1461 [113]. Louis XI went on to earn his own nickname, "the Universal Spider," for a reign built on intrigue, secret diplomacy, and an extensive network of informants — a style of rule historians consistently contrast with his father's more institutional, consultative approach [114]. The girl who died insisting God had sent her to secure a crown for one man spent that effort, in the end, on a dynasty whose next act was a son who spied on his own father and fled to the enemy rather than wait his turn.

[Beat 86]
Then and Now

Every founder, in any domain, eventually has to watch what the next generation does with what they built, and it is rarely a clean, flattering continuation — a successor who resents the founder's method even while inheriting its results is one of the most common shapes institutional history actually takes, not a rare tragic exception. Charles VII's own reign is a genuinely useful case study in exactly this, precisely because it is so undramatic: a king remembered as "well-served" rather than brilliant, whose most consequential decisions were arguably which capable people to listen to, followed by a son who rejected that model outright in favor of personal cunning and secrecy. What has not changed, across six centuries of institutions large and small, is the basic asymmetry: the person who does the founding rarely gets to also control what gets done with the foundation.

[Beat 87]
It took Joan's own mother twenty-five years to get the case reopened, and she did it in person. Isabelle Romée, by then elderly, traveled to Paris with two of her surviving sons and a group of supporters from Orléans and appeared before papal representatives at Notre-Dame, tearfully pressing for justice on her daughter's behalf [115]. Newly elected Pope Callixtus III authorized a formal inquiry, and Inquisitor-General Jean Bréhal led the investigation, building on preliminary work that had already begun in 1452. The formal appeal opened in November 1455.

[Beat 88]
Unlike the 1431 trial, which had produced a single institutional narrative under enormous political pressure, the Trial of Nullification gathered testimony from well over a hundred witnesses across France [116]. Some were the professional soldiers and nobility already covered in this book — Dunois, Alençon, the men who had ridden with her from Vaucouleurs. But a great many others were ordinary people who had known her before any of it began: neighbors from Domrémy, childhood acquaintances, villagers who remembered a devout, hardworking, unremarkable girl, nothing like the sorceress the 1431 verdict required her to be. This is one of the quieter but more important documentary facts in her entire story — the rehabilitation trial is where the record of Joan the child, Joan the neighbor, actually comes from, filled in by people with no stake in either her canonization or her conviction, simply asked what they remembered.

[Beat 89]
On 7 July 1456 the tribunal issued its verdict: the 1431 trial was null, "contaminated with fraud, calumny, wickedness, contradictions, and manifest errors of fact and law" [117]. It is worth being precise about what this verdict did and did not claim, since popular memory tends to round it up into something grander than the document itself states. It found the original trial procedurally corrupt — denial of counsel, denial of appeal, coerced participants, a secular rather than ecclesiastical prison, leading interrogation — and it named Pierre Cauchon's own conduct as proceeding from what the record calls a "secular vendetta" rather than legitimate ecclesiastical concern. It did not, on its own, formally rule on the ontological truth of Joan's voices; it ruled that the process used to convict her of demonic invention was itself corrupt beyond trust. Isabelle Romée lived to see this verdict and was present at a banquet held in Orléans on 27 July 1456 honoring Inquisitor Bréhal for the outcome.

[Beat 90]
Then and Now

A wrongful conviction overturned a quarter-century later, on procedural grounds, by a mother who refused to let the record stand, is a story with an uncomfortably familiar modern shape — the gap between "found guilty by a process" and "actually guilty" is one the legal imagination keeps rediscovering, generation after generation, usually only after the condemned person is already dead and the reversal can no longer help them. What has changed is mostly the machinery of appeal — modern legal systems build in review mechanisms Joan's era had to invent from scratch, one grieving mother's petition at a time. What has not changed is the deeper, harder fact underneath both eras: a formal reversal is real relief for the people left behind, and it is never, in any century, relief for the person it was too late to save.

[Beat 91]
Legends about the dead tend to fill in exactly the gaps the real record leaves open, and Joan's death left an unusually large one: a public execution, in a city her own side did not control, with no body left behind to prove anything either way. Within five years, that gap produced a woman.

[Beat 92]
A woman known to history as Claude, or Jeanne, des Armoises surfaced publicly around 1436 claiming to be Joan, somehow escaped from Rouen. She met Joan's own brothers, Pierre and Jean d'Arc, in Metz on 20 May 1436 — and convinced them [118]. That detail deserves to be sat with rather than rushed past: the people with the best possible reason and the best possible knowledge to spot a fraud, Joan's own family, were initially taken in. Des Armoises went on to marry a knight, Robert des Armoises, and had two children with him; her impersonation ran for years and reportedly earned her considerable money, and she nearly deceived Charles VII himself before failing to produce some private detail — a "secret" only the real Joan and the king had shared — at which point she confessed the fraud and was allowed to return to her husband rather than face further consequence [119]. The case is a genuinely useful stress test for this book's whole method: it shows exactly how thin the line was, in the century that actually lived through these events, between a documented historical claim and a convincing performance of one — and it shows that even Joan's own blood relatives were not immune to getting it wrong.

[Beat 93]
For most of the next four centuries Joan functioned mainly as national and religious symbol rather than historical subject — a useful figure for whatever cause needed her, with comparatively little independent scholarly interest in what the actual trial documents said. That changed dramatically in the nineteenth century. Jules Quicherat's five-volume critical edition of both trials (1841-1849) made the full documentary record available to scholarship for the first time in centuries, and a wave of French nationalist enthusiasm — tied to the trauma of the 1870-71 Franco-Prussian War and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine — turned her into a central symbol of French resistance and identity, a role she has never entirely shed since. The canonization process that followed (examined in detail in Chapter 4) moved forward inside this same nationalist and religious current, reaching completion in 1920 during an active period of Vatican-French reconciliation in the war's aftermath.

[Beat 94]
Modern scholarship — Pernoud's documentary rehabilitation, DeVries's military reassessment, Larissa Taylor's revisionist biography, Sullivan's literary analysis of the trial text itself — represents the most recent phase of this same long argument: an attempt to read Joan against the actual surviving record rather than against whatever four centuries of symbol-making had layered on top of it. It is worth naming honestly that this book is itself one more entry in that same tradition, written from a specific vantage point — citation-grounded, deliberately uninterested in royal genealogy for its own sake, centered on the Voices — rather than some final, view-from-nowhere account. Every book about Joan of Arc, including this one, is written from somewhere.

[Beat 95]
Then and Now

A five-year gap between a public death and a documentary record was enough, in 1436, to produce a woman who fooled a dead girl's own brothers — a reminder that misinformation is not a modern technology problem, just a modern-speed one. What has genuinely changed is the clock: des Armoises had years to build her performance and a slow, word-of-mouth information network to spread it through; a comparable fraud today would face both faster detection tools and a faster spread, competing pressures that did not exist in fifteenth-century Lorraine. What has not changed is the underlying vulnerability her case exposes: even the people with the strongest personal stake in getting the truth right — a family grieving a sister, a nation building an identity, a Church deciding a saint — are exactly the people most likely to want a comforting answer badly enough to accept one too quickly.

[Beat 96]
Return, at the end, to the garden. A frightened thirteen-year-old hears something to her right, accompanied by light, and does not yet have a name for it. Everything this book has covered since — Vaucouleurs, Chinon, Orléans, Reims, Compiègne, Rouen, the stake, the reversal twenty-five years later, the canonization nearly five centuries after that — is downstream of that one unexplained afternoon. It is worth ending where the book began, because the battles and the coronations, however factually necessary to tell the story honestly, were never actually the center of gravity. The voice was.

[Beat 97]
This book has laid out, as honestly as the research allows, every serious answer anyone has offered for what that voice actually was: the Church's own considered, if narrower-than-popularly-assumed, judgment that it was authentic; the mainstream historians' studied agnosticism, more interested in what she did with the conviction than in certifying its source; the real, ongoing, peer-reviewed medical argument over temporal lobe epilepsy, the best-evidenced natural explanation by a wide margin; the weaker, mostly discredited or unevidenced alternatives — schizophrenia, which fails on onset age, trajectory, and the sheer sensory richness of what she reported; tuberculosis, which undermines its own premise; ergotism and migraine, which turn out on close inspection to belong to other people's stories, not hers; the naturalistic reading that finds sincere belief shaped by a devotional culture already primed to receive it, rather than any pathology at all; and finally the position that may be the most intellectually honest of all — that diagnosing a woman dead six hundred years, from a hostile interrogation transcript translated by the institution that killed her, is not humility's opposite but its correct application.

[Beat 98]
What this book will not do, at the close, is pretend that laying out the spectrum honestly is the same thing as resolving it — and it will not insult the reader by pretending indifference to the question either. The most defensible position available, on the actual documented evidence, is not a verdict. It is the observation that every explanation on offer requires believing something remarkable: that God spoke audibly and visibly to an illiterate farm girl and then let her die for it; or that an undiagnosed neurological condition produced, with uncanny consistency across years of hostile cross-examination, a set of predictions that mostly came true, in a girl who organized a military campaign while allegedly experiencing it; or that some combination of both is true at once, and the question was never really "which," only "how much of each, and does it matter." None of those options is the comfortable, disenchanted, purely explicable story a modern reader might reflexively reach for. That discomfort is not a flaw in the record. It is the record's most honest feature.

[Beat 99]
Then and Now

Six hundred years on, the question this book opened with — prophecy or pathology — has not gotten any easier to answer, and that fact is itself worth sitting with rather than treating as a research failure. Modern neuroscience can describe, with far more precision than any fifteenth-century theologian possessed, exactly what a temporal lobe seizure does to perception. It cannot tell you whether a girl who reported hearing a warrior archangel and two crowned virgin martyrs, and then went on to do everything she said they told her to do, was having a malfunction or a message, or whether that is even, in the end, a meaningful distinction to draw. What has changed since 1431 is our vocabulary, our instruments, and our tolerance for uncertainty in a courtroom. What has not changed is the fact underneath all of it: a person can report an experience nobody else can verify, act on it with total conviction, and turn out, whatever the actual mechanism, to have been right about the things that could be checked. Six hundred years of better medicine and better history have not made that fact less strange. They have only made it more precisely documented.

1 - The two rival claimants to the French throne

By 1429 France had two claimants to its throne: Charles, the disinherited adult son of Charles VI, and the English child-king Henry VI, whose claim rested on the Treaty of Troyes (1420). Allmand, Henry V (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1992), p. 142.

[Beat 101]
2 - The Treaty of Troyes and the Armagnac-Burgundian civil war

The Treaty of Troyes (21 May 1420) made Henry V of England regent and heir to the French throne, disinheriting the Dauphin Charles, against the backdrop of the Armagnac-Burgundian civil war that began in 1407 with the assassination of Louis of Orléans and continued with the 1419 assassination of Burgundian duke John the Fearless by Armagnac/Dauphinist men. Christopher Allmand, Henry V (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1992), p. 142; Richard Vaughan, John the Fearless: The Growth of Burgundian Power (London: Longman, 1966; repr. Woodbridge: Boydell Press, 2002); Britannica, "Treaty of Troyes" and "Armagnac."

[Beat 102]
3 - The Dauphin's in-absentia conviction

Following the Treaty of Troyes, a Paris court (lit de justice) convicted the Dauphin Charles of treason in absentia in 1421, connected to the 1419 assassination of John the Fearless, formally banishing and disinheriting him. Jonathan Sumption, The Hundred Years War, Volume IV: Cursed Kings (London: Faber & Faber, 2015); Malcolm G. A. Vale, Charles VII (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1974).

[Beat 103]
4 - Correcting the "vacant throne" misconception; Henry V's death

Charles VI reigned continuously from 1380 until his death on 21 October 1422; he was never deposed and the throne was never vacant. Henry V of England died of dysentery in August 1422, two months before Charles VI, so the French crown he was to inherit passed instead to his infant son, Henry VI. The acute dual-claimant crisis runs from the 1420 Treaty of Troyes to Charles VII's 1429 coronation - roughly nine years, not the full 116-year Hundred Years' War (1337-1453). Charles VI of France, World History Encyclopedia; John Matusiak, Henry V (Abingdon: Routledge, 2012), pp. 232, 242-244.

[Beat 104]
5 - Dual governments and the Loire border

Charles VII's supporters ran a rival royal administration from Poitiers and Bourges (the "Kingdom of Bourges"), with its own Parlement and Chambre des Comptes, governing France south of the Loire except English-held Guyenne/Gascony; the Paris government under the English/Burgundian regime governed the north. No contemporary source found in this research suggests anyone seriously proposed abolishing the monarchy itself during the crisis - the dispute was over which claimant, not whether a king was needed. Vale, Charles VII (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1974); Juliet Barker, Conquest: The English Kingdom of France (London: Little, Brown, 2009), p. 123.

[Beat 105]
6 - Domrémy's divided feudal loyalty

Domrémy sat in the Duchy of Bar on the river Meuse, close to the border with the Duchy of Lorraine. Households on the west bank of the Meuse, including Jacques d'Arc's, owed allegiance through the Duke of Bar to the King of France; households on the east bank were reckoned to Lorraine and, through it, to the Holy Roman Empire. Despite sitting deep within a broadly Anglo-Burgundian-controlled swath of northern France during the Hundred Years' War, Domrémy itself remained loyal to the Armagnac/French crown cause. Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, trans. and rev. Jeremy duQuesnay Adams (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 106]
7 - Jacques d'Arc's office of doyen

Jacques d'Arc held the village office of doyen, a post ranking between the mayor and the provost, responsible for collecting taxes, representing the community's interests, and organizing local defense, including militia musters against raiding parties. This placed the family a step above ordinary peasantry — not gentry, but locally notable and trusted with communal responsibility, evidence historians take that the family was moderately prosperous relative to their neighbors. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle: Selected Sources, Manchester Medieval Sources series (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006), a sourcebook translating the relevant village depositions.

[Beat 107]
8 - Isabelle Romée's name and the family's landholding

Isabelle Romée, a native of the nearby village of Vouthon, held with her husband Jacques roughly fifty acres of land and a modest but comfortable house by village standards. The byname "Romée" is traditionally explained as a title given to someone who had made a pilgrimage to Rome, though the precise origin of the name is not settled with documentary certainty and is presented here as plausible tradition rather than proven fact. Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 108]
9 - The 1425 Burgundian raid on Domrémy

In 1425, Burgundian raiders drove off Domrémy's livestock and burned part of the settlement, forcing residents to flee — the same year Joan later testified she first heard her voice. The two facts are presented together as a matter of chronology, not causally linked; no source establishes a causal relationship between the raid and the onset of her visions, and none should be implied. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999).

[Beat 109]
10 - Joan's trial testimony on the Fairy Tree of Domrémy

At the Third Public Examination of the Trial of Condemnation, 24 February 1431, Joan acknowledged going with other village girls to a beech tree near Domrémy known as l'Arbre des Dames (the Ladies' Tree) or l'Arbre des Fées (the Fairies' Tree), standing near a spring reputed to have healing properties, where local children sang, danced, and hung garlands for an image of Our Lady of Domrémy. She stated she had heard older villagers, including her own godmother, claim to have seen fairies there, while affirming she herself never had. W. P. Barrett, trans., The Trial of Jeanne d'Arc (New York: Gotham House, 1932); Daniel Hobbins, trans., The Trial of Joan of Arc (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2005).

[Beat 110]
11 - The Fairy Tree as a component of the heresy case

Joan's judges incorporated her Fairy Tree testimony into the broader case constructed against her for sorcery and heresy, part of the Twelve Articles later drawn from the trial record. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle: Selected Sources (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006), discusses the tree testimony and its use at length.

[Beat 111]
12 - Joan's own dating and description of her first vision

Joan testified consistently that she was thirteen years old when she first heard a voice, in her father's garden, at midday in summer, and that she was "very much frightened" the first time. W. P. Barrett, trans., The Trial of Jeanne d'Arc (New York: Gotham House, 1932); Daniel Hobbins, trans., The Trial of Joan of Arc (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2005).

[Beat 112]
13 - Joan's description of the voice and accompanying light

Joan testified: "I heard this Voice to my right, towards the Church; rarely do I hear it without its being accompanied also by a light. This light comes from the same side as the Voice, and generally there is a great light." Quoted per the trial record translations of W. P. Barrett (1932) and Daniel Hobbins (2005); also rendered in famous-trials.com's compiled account of the trial testimony.

[Beat 113]
14 - Recognition of the voice as an angel's after three occurrences

Joan testified that only after hearing the voice on three separate occasions did she come to understand it as belonging to an angel, prior to her later, more specific identification of St. Michael, St. Catherine, and St. Margaret (see Chapter 3). [Beat 114]
15 - The nature of the surviving trial record

The Trial of Condemnation survives as an official Latin redaction compiled after the fact from a French working "minute" taken down daily by the court's notaries (led by Guillaume Manchon), rather than a verbatim stenographic transcript in the modern sense. The standard critical edition presenting the Latin text and surviving French minute fragments in parallel is Pierre Tisset and Yvonne Lanhers, eds., Procès de condamnation de Jeanne d'Arc, 3 vols. (Paris: Klincksieck, for the Société de l'Histoire de France, 1960-1971). Daniel Hobbins's introduction to his 2005 translation (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press) discusses the record's compilation and dating in detail.

[Beat 115]
16 - Joan's vow of virginity

Joan testified she vowed to keep her virginity "as long as it should please God," dating the vow to around age thirteen, tied to her first vision. This vow was later cited by prosecutors as one of the Twelve Articles against her, framed as improper for having been made without a priest's counsel. W. P. Barrett, trans. and ed., The Trial of Jeanne d'Arc (New York: Gotham House, 1932), Second and Third Private Examinations; Article XI of the Twelve Articles of Accusation (5 April 1431).

[Beat 116]
17 - The Toul breach-of-promise lawsuit

A young man of Toul sued Joan for breach of a marriage promise; she traveled to Toul to contest the suit in person, testified she had made no such promise, and the ecclesiastical judge dismissed the case. Famous-trials.com, "70 Articles of Accusation and Joan's Answers"; cross-referenced with Barrett (1932) and Hobbins (2005) trial translations.

[Beat 117]
18 - Joan's identification of her three voices

Joan identified her voices as St. Michael the Archangel, St. Catherine of Alexandria, and St. Margaret of Antioch. W. P. Barrett, trans., The Trial of Jeanne d'Arc (New York: Gotham House, 1932); Daniel Hobbins, trans., The Trial of Joan of Arc (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2005); trial testimony compiled at famous-trials.com, "The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account."

[Beat 118]
19 - St. Michael's appearance at Chinon

Joan testified that St. Michael came "with a great multitude of Angels" and, while she lodged at Chinon, walked beside her holding her hand. Trial testimony as compiled at famous-trials.com, "The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account," cross-referenced with Barrett (1932) and Hobbins (2005) translations.

[Beat 119]
20 - Description of Catherine and Margaret's crowns

Joan described the faces of St. Catherine and St. Margaret as "adorned with beautiful crowns, very rich and precious." Trial testimony as compiled at famous-trials.com, "The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account."

[Beat 120]
21 - Joan's testimony on physical perception of the voices

Asked whether she saw the saints with her own eyes, Joan answered "I saw them with my bodily eyes, just as well as I see you." Pressed for physical specifics she said she "had no idea" and responded, "Do you think God can't find [them] clothes?" History Today, "The Trials of Joan of Arc," summarizing the interrogation; cross-referenced with Barrett (1932) and Hobbins (2005) translations.

[Beat 121]
22 - The "sign" given to Charles VII at Chinon

Joan testified that an angel brought Charles VII "a beautiful crown of pure gold," bowing and saying "Sire, here is your sign. Take it." Trial testimony as compiled at famous-trials.com, "The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account." The precise content and nature of this "sign" was never fully disclosed by either Joan or Charles VII and remains one of the thinnest-documented moments in her story; see also Chapter 6, "Chinon and the Sign."

[Beat 122]
23 - Joan's later, more figurative account of the sign

Near the end of her life Joan is reported to have given a more figurative account of the same "sign" described in Note 15, describing it in terms closer to her own promise to lead Charles to his coronation than a literal angelic visitation. famous-trials.com, "The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account," summarizing the shift across her testimony; discussed further in Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 123]
24 - The voices' persistence during interrogation

Joan indicated the voices came often, including during her own questioning, telling her to "answer boldly." famous-trials.com, "The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account."

[Beat 124]
25 - The 1456 Nullification verdict's language

The Trial of Nullification's 7 July 1456 verdict declared the original 1431 trial "contaminated with fraud, calumny, wickedness, contradictions, and manifest errors of fact and law." Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d'Arc, vols. 2-3 (Paris, 1841-1849), and Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 125]
26 - Beatification and canonization dates and ceremony

Joan was beatified by Pope Pius X on 18 April 1909 and canonized by Pope Benedict XV on 16 May 1920, at a ceremony in St. Peter's Basilica attended by an estimated 30,000 people, including roughly 140 of her own descendants. EBSCO Research Starters, "Canonization of Joan of Arc"; UPI Archives, "Rome and Paris Honor Memory of Joan of Arc in Church Celebrations" (17 May 1920).

[Beat 126]
27 - The Promoter of the Faith ("Devil's Advocate")

The canonization process then in force, governed since 1588 by the Congregation of Rites, formally included the Promoter of the Faith (promotor fidei), popularly the "Devil's Advocate," an official tasked with arguing every unfavorable fact against a candidate before a declaration of sanctity could stand; the role was reformed in 1983. Britannica, "Devil's advocate"; Aleteia, "Who was the Devil's Advocate."

[Beat 127]
28 - Canonization vs. doctrinal ratification of private revelation

Catholic theology formally distinguishes canonization (a declaration of heroic virtue and confirmed posthumous miracles) from approval of private revelation, a separate, discretionary category never binding on the faithful's belief. Canonization required confirmed miracles attributed to the candidate's intercession, though a dispensation was granted in Joan's case since she had not founded a religious order; three authenticated miracles were accepted for her beatification, confirmed 13 December 1908. stjoan-center.com, "St. Joan's Canonization"; EBSCO Research Starters, "Canonization of Joan of Arc."

[Beat 128]
29 - Practical entanglement of canonization and belief in the visions

Commentary on the canonization notes that, in practice, "the Church never would have canonized St. Joan had they not thought her associated private revelations worthy of belief," even though this is not a strict doctrinal requirement. Catholic Answers Q&A, "The Authenticity of St. Joan of Arc's Visions."

[Beat 129]
30 - Political context of the canonization's timing

Joan's canonization process was delayed by the First World War and completed in a period of active rapprochement between the Vatican and the French Republic; historians treat this postwar diplomatic context as a real factor in the timing of the final declaration. EBSCO Research Starters, "Canonization of Joan of Arc," citing standard historical accounts of the postwar Franco-Vatican context.

[Beat 130]
31 - Pernoud's documentary, non-adjudicating approach

Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin's Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998) is widely described as "respecting Joan's religion, visions, and 'voices,' honoring Joan without deifying her and grounding her story in historical context without dismissing the possibility of God's activity in human life." departments.kings.edu review of Pernoud.

[Beat 131]
32 - Larissa Juliet Taylor's practical framing

Larissa Juliet Taylor's The Virgin Warrior: The Life and Death of Joan of Arc (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2009) offers practical historical context for Joan's reported "miracles" and "voices" while focusing on Joan within her own time rather than through later mythologizing. Yale University Press listing; ResearchGate review of The Virgin Warrior.

[Beat 132]
33 - DeVries's military-focused bracketing of the question

Kelly DeVries's Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999) explicitly focuses on Joan's effect on troop morale and battlefield decision-making rather than the source of her visions. H-Net review by Sankey of DeVries, 1999.

[Beat 133]
34 - Sullivan's discourse-analytical reading of the trial transcript

Karen Sullivan's The Interrogation of Joan of Arc (Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press, 1999) argues the trial transcript is a text produced by the collision between Joan's lay, oral, vernacular speech and her judges' clerical, university-trained discourse, rather than a transparent record of her actual words. The Medieval Review, scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/15616/21734.

[Beat 134]
35 - The "cross-session consistency" argument, flagged as broad synthesis

The claim that Joan's testimony on her voices stayed consistent across many separately hostile interrogation sessions, cited by some writers as evidence against fabrication, is a broadly repeated synthesis across popular-academic sources on Joan rather than one scholar's specifically footnoted argument; this book states that distinction explicitly rather than borrowing false precision. Author's synthesis of the secondary literature, flagged here as such rather than attributed to a single named scholar.

[Beat 135]
36 - Voaden on discretio spirituum and late-medieval women visionaries

Rosalynn Voaden traces the doctrine of discretio spirituum ("discernment of spirits") across a continuum of late-medieval women visionaries including Bridget of Sweden, Catherine of Siena, and Joan of Arc, arguing visionary experience was an institutionally regulated but culturally available category for women, not automatically read as illness. Rosalynn Voaden, God's Words, Women's Voices: The Discernment of Spirits in the Writing of Late-Medieval Women Visionaries (York Studies in Medieval Theology; York: York Medieval Press / Boydell & Brewer, 1999). See also Nancy Caciola, Discerning Spirits: Divine and Demonic Possession in the Middle Ages (Ithaca, NY: Cornell University Press, 2003).

[Beat 136]
37 - Divergent outcomes for women visionaries under discretio spirituum

Bridget of Sweden founded a religious order and was canonized; Catherine of Siena advised popes and was later named a Doctor of the Church; both were examined under the same general discernment-of-spirits framework Joan faced, with different outcomes shaped by politics and specific content as much as by the presence of a reported voice itself. Rosalynn Voaden, God's Words, Women's Voices (1999).

[Beat 137]
38 - Charcot's 1882 "hysteria" characterization

In 1882 neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot presented to the French Academy of Sciences the claim that history's religious ecstatics and demoniacs — naming Joan of Arc, St. Teresa, and St. Paul — were "evidently hysterics." philosophyforlife.org, "Jean-Martin Charcot and the pathologisation of ecstasy"; Barbara Schildkrout, "What Caused Joan of Arc's Neuropsychiatric Symptoms? Medical Hypotheses from 1882 to 2016," Journal of the History of the Neurosciences 32, no. 3 (2023): 332-356, which dates the diagnostic literature's start to this 1882 presentation.

[Beat 138]
39 - Georges Dumas and the reincarnation-patient nuance

Georges Dumas incorporated pathological religious experience into his psychiatric nosology in Nouveau Traité de Psychologie, vol. 2 (Paris: Félix Alcan, 1932) and the posthumous Le Surnaturel et les dieux d'après les maladies mentales (Paris: Presses Universitaires de France, 1946); the clearest documented case material connecting Dumas to "Jeanne d'Arc" concerns a modern patient who believed herself Joan reincarnated, classified under his "realistic supernatural" pathological category, rather than a direct clinical retro-diagnosis of the historical Joan herself — a nuance this book states explicitly rather than eliding. ScienceDirect summary, "Du surnaturel a une theogenie pathologique?"

[Beat 139]
40 - The anachronism of the term "schizophrenia"

The term "schizophrenia" was coined by psychiatrist Eugen Bleuler in 1908, nearly five hundred years after Joan of Arc's death in 1431 — a term that consequently postdates the entire trial record and every contemporary account of her life. Standard history-of-psychiatry reference point, cross-referenced in Barbara Schildkrout, "What Caused Joan of Arc's Neuropsychiatric Symptoms?" Journal of the History of the Neurosciences 32, no. 3 (2023): 332-356.

[Beat 140]
41 - Typical onset age of schizophrenia by sex

Male-onset schizophrenia shows a single peak around ages 21-25; female onset is bimodal, with peaks at 25-30 and again after 45 (linked to menopausal estrogen decline), typically running three to five years later than male onset overall — a pattern inconsistent with Joan's reported onset at age thirteen. Li, R., Ma, X., Wang, G., and Yang, J., Wang, C., "Why sex differences in schizophrenia?" Journal of Translational Neuroscience (Beijing) 1, no. 1 (2016): 37-42.

[Beat 141]
42 - Critique of the schizophrenia hypothesis on symptomatic grounds

Critics of the schizophrenia hypothesis note the trial record shows none of the disorganized thought or functional decline typically associated with the condition, and that Joan is documented as tactically lucid, socially persuasive, and capable of sustained physical command throughout her active career. Barbara Schildkrout, "What Caused Joan of Arc's Neuropsychiatric Symptoms? Medical Hypotheses from 1882 to 2016," Journal of the History of the Neurosciences 32, no. 3 (2023): 332-356.

[Beat 142]
43 - Multi-sensory testimony: touch and smell

Article Eleven of the Twelve Articles of Accusation, drawn from Joan's trial testimony, states she showed reverence to the voices by "kissing the earth on which they walk... at times kissing and embracing Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret; she had touched them with her own hands, corporeally and physically" (per Barrett's 1932 and Hobbins's 2005 translations, cross-referenced via jeanne-darc.info's trial archive). Secondary trial scholarship additionally describes the apparitions as accompanied by a pleasant smell, though this detail is less precisely pinned to a specific numbered trial passage than the touch testimony.

[Beat 143]
44 - Foote-Smith and Bayne's ecstatic-epilepsy hypothesis

UCSF neurologist Dr. Lydia Bayne, working with writer Elizabeth Foote-Smith, first presented the ecstatic temporal-lobe seizure hypothesis at a 1990 meeting of the American Academy of Neurology (reported contemporaneously in UPI, the Deseret News, and the Washington Post, "Saint in a New Light," 2 May 1990), then published it in the peer-reviewed literature the following year: Foote-Smith, E., and Bayne, L., "Joan of Arc," Epilepsia 32, no. 6 (1991): 810-815. The paper cites onset at age thirteen, absence of aberrant interictal behavior, a dreamlike ecstatic quality, visions carrying an urgent message, and possible triggering by church bells as a pattern typical of complex partial seizures. Note: this paper's title is simply "Joan of Arc" — it should not be confused with Nicastro and Picard's differently-titled 2016 paper, "Joan of Arc: Sanctity, Witchcraft or Epilepsy?" (Epilepsy & Behavior 57, pt. B), a distinct, later, unrelated paper by different authors.

[Beat 144]
45 - d'Orsi and Tinuper's 2006 epilepsy paper

G. d'Orsi and P. Tinuper, "'I Heard Voices...': From Semiology, a Historical Review, and a New Hypothesis on the Presumed Epilepsy of Joan of Arc," Epilepsy & Behavior 9, no. 1 (2006): 152-157, proposed idiopathic partial epilepsy with auditory features (IPEAF), also known as autosomal dominant lateral temporal epilepsy, as the best-fitting diagnosis.

[Beat 145]
46 - Nicastro and Picard's 2016 paper

N. Nicastro and F. Picard, of the Neurology Department, Geneva University Hospitals, "Joan of Arc: Sanctity, Witchcraft or Epilepsy?" Epilepsy & Behavior 57, pt. B (2016): 247-250, argued against Bayne's "ecstatic seizure" framing and against psychiatric (schizophrenia) explanations, concurring with d'Orsi and Tinuper (2006) that idiopathic partial epilepsy with auditory features is the best-supported diagnosis given the available data.

[Beat 146]
47 - The 2016 published exchange on Joan's epilepsy diagnosis

Nicastro and Picard's 2016 paper produced a direct published exchange: B. de Toffol, "Comment on 'Joan of Arc: Sanctity, Witchcraft or Epilepsy?'" Epilepsy & Behavior 61 (2016): 80-81, followed by G. d'Orsi and P. Tinuper's reply, "The 'Voices' of Joan of Arc and Epilepsy with Auditory Features," Epilepsy & Behavior 61 (2016): 281 — a live, adversarial, peer-reviewed scientific disagreement over which specific epilepsy subtype (if any) best fits the historical record, not a settled consensus.

[Beat 147]
48 - The Butterfields' 1958 bovine tuberculosis hypothesis

John Butterfield (Professor of Experimental Medicine, Guy's Hospital Medical School) and Isobel-Ann Butterfield proposed a temporal-lobe tuberculoma secondary to disseminated bovine tuberculosis, contracted via unpasteurized milk exposure in girlhood, in "Joan of Arc: A Medical View," History Today 8, no. 9 (September 1958), citing her reported amenorrhea and the serious illness recorded during her imprisonment at Rouen, when she believed she was dying and asked for confession and burial in consecrated ground. historytoday.com/archive/joan-arc-medical-view; Britannica, "St. Joan of Arc: Capture, trial and execution," for the prison-illness detail.

[Beat 148]
49 - Ratnasuriya's 1986 defense of the tuberculosis hypothesis

R. H. Ratnasuriya, then of Bethlem Royal and Maudsley Hospital, London, "Joan of Arc, Creative Psychopath: Is There Another Explanation?" Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine 79 (April 1986), argues for the tuberculoma-based explanation against an earlier "creative psychopath" characterization of Joan; the original proponent of that "creative psychopath" label was not identified in the research for this book.

[Beat 149]
50 - Nores and Yakovleff's 1995 self-undermining reassessment

J. M. Nores and Y. Yakovleff, "A Historical Case of Disseminated Chronic Tuberculosis," Neuropsychobiology 32, no. 2 (1995): 79-80, revisit the tuberculosis/tuberculoma hypothesis and conclude it is unlikely that widespread, serious tuberculosis was compatible with the sustained physical activity — riding, wearing armor, campaigning — that Joan is independently documented to have performed.

[Beat 150]
51 - The absence of a serious ergotism hypothesis for Joan

No serious historian or physician was found, in the research for this book, to have proposed ergotism (Claviceps purpurea poisoning) as an explanation for Joan of Arc's visions specifically. The ergotism hypothesis is well-documented for the 1692 Salem witch trials via Linnda R. Caporael, "Ergotism: The Satan Loosed in Salem?" Science 192, no. 4234 (1976): 21-26; its popular association with Joan of Arc appears to be a conflation with Salem rather than an independent scholarly claim.

[Beat 151]
52 - The absence of a dedicated migraine-with-aura hypothesis for Joan

No dedicated, named, peer-reviewed paper proposing migraine with aura as an explanation for Joan of Arc's visions specifically was found in the research for this book. Migraine with aura is a genuinely serious hypothesis for a different medieval visionary, Hildegard of Bingen, associated in particular with neurologist and author Oliver Sacks's writing on the subject; the two cases should not be conflated absent independent evidence.

[Beat 152]
53 - Anatole France's naturalistic 1908 biography

Anatole France's Vie de Jeanne d'Arc, 2 vols. (Paris: Calmann-Lévy, 1908; English trans. Winifred Stephens, The Life of Joan of Arc, London: John Lane, 1908), argued Joan suffered hallucinations "of all the senses, especially hearing," resulting from religious and hysterical exaltation fostered by priestly influence combined with prophecies current in the countryside, rather than from conscious fraud.

[Beat 153]
54 - Andrew Lang's 1908 rebuttal of Anatole France

Andrew Lang published The Maid of France: Being the Story of the Life and Death of Jeanne d'Arc (London/New York: Longmans, Green and Company, 1908) explicitly in response to Anatole France's biography the same year, attacking its scholarly carelessness and unsupported psychiatric diagnoses; a joint review of both books appeared in the American Historical Review in 1909. Catholic historian Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Ayroles also published a hostile counter-critique, La prétendue vie de Jeanne d'Arc de M. Anatole France (1910), and medievalist Achille Luchaire criticized the work as well.

[Beat 154]
55 - The Catholic Encyclopedia's 1910 rebuttal

"St. Joan of Arc," Catholic Encyclopedia (New York: Robert Appleton Company, 1910), states directly: "There is not a shadow of evidence to support this theory of priestly advisers coaching Joan in a part," rebutting the naturalistic "priestly coaching" reading of her visions. newadvent.org/cathen/08409c.htm.

[Beat 155]
56 - Margaret Murray's witch-cult theory as genuine fringe

Margaret Murray's 1921 theory that Joan of Arc was secretly practicing a surviving pagan witch-cult "has not gained significant acceptance among academic historians" and is characterized in current scholarship as "pseudo-historical fantasy." Michael D. Bailey, review of Witchcraft Historiography (ed. Jonathan Barry and Owen Davies; Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2007), in Magic, Ritual, and Witchcraft 3, no. 1 (Summer 2008): 81-85 (University of Pennsylvania Press) - cited here specifically as an example of a position this book treats as genuine fringe rather than a legitimate pole of the spectrum.

[Beat 156]
57 - The absence of a serious fraud hypothesis among modern historians

No historian surveyed for this book among Régine Pernoud, Larissa Juliet Taylor, Kelly DeVries, Karen Sullivan, or Malcolm Vale argues that Joan consciously fabricated her voices as a cynical political device; even Anatole France's skeptical 1908 account locates their origin in sincere, environmentally-shaped belief rather than deliberate deception. The 1431 trial's own prosecutorial theory of invention or demonic origin was itself nullified as procedurally corrupt by the 1456 Trial of Nullification (see Note 18).

[Beat 157]
58 - Muramoto's methodological critique of retrospective diagnosis

Osamu Muramoto, "Retrospective Diagnosis of a Famous Historical Figure: Ontological, Epistemic, and Ethical Considerations," Philosophy, Ethics, and Humanities in Medicine 9 (2014): article 10, frames the critique of diagnosing historical figures across three challenges: ontological (whether a modern disease category is the same entity as a historical experience centuries removed from it), epistemic (an unfalsifiable diagnosis without ability to examine the subject), and ethical (posthumous reputational harm from a diagnosis the subject never consented to).

[Beat 158]
59 - Mitchell's methodological critique of retrospective diagnosis

Piers D. Mitchell argues retrospective diagnosis of historical figures is only defensible where a historical text provides symptom description specific and detailed enough to support a modern label, distinguishing "social diagnosis" (how a period itself classified an illness) from modern biological diagnosis; for most historical illness accounts, he argues, the evidence is not specific enough to meet this standard. Piers D. Mitchell, "Retrospective diagnosis, and the use of historical texts for investigating disease in the past," International Journal of Paleopathology 1, no. 2 (2011): 81-88.

[Beat 159]
60 - Joan's first, unsuccessful approach to Baudricourt

In May 1428 Joan asked Robert de Baudricourt, captain of Vaucouleurs, for an armed escort to the Dauphin; he dismissed her and reportedly told the cousin who brought her to slap her and send her home. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 160]
61 - Joan's second approach and public persistence at Vaucouleurs

Joan returned to Vaucouleurs in January 1429, lodging locally and gathering a following among the townspeople while continuing to press her case publicly. Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998); Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999).

[Beat 161]
62 - The "Battle of the Herrings" foreknowledge tradition

Joan is reported to have described the outcome of the Battle of Rouvray (the "Battle of the Herrings," 12 February 1429) before official word of it could plausibly have reached Vaucouleurs, a detail credited with helping change Baudricourt's mind; modern historians treat this as tradition worth reporting rather than independently verified fact. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999); Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 162]
63 - The escort assembled for the journey to Chinon

Baudricourt provided Joan an escort including esquires Jean de Metz and Bertrand de Poulengy, their servants, royal messenger Colet de Vienne, and archer Richard Larcher, along with a horse, sword, male clothing, and a letter of introduction, for the eleven-day ride to Chinon beginning around 13 February 1429. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 163]
64 - De Metz and de Poulengy's nullification-trial testimony on the journey

Jean de Metz testified at the 1455-56 Trial of Nullification that although he and Bertrand de Poulengy slept beside Joan on the road to Chinon, her clothing remained closely fastened and he felt no carnal desire toward her, "by reason of the virtue I sensed in her," and recalled her saying she would rather have stayed spinning wool at her mother's side, "but this thing I must do, for my Lord wills it." Trial of Nullification testimony, in Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 164]
65 - Joan's arrival at Chinon and first audience

Joan arrived at the Château de Chinon on 23 February 1429 and was received in audience by the Dauphin Charles two days later, on 25 February. Larissa Juliet Taylor, The Virgin Warrior: The Life and Death of Joan of Arc (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2009); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 165]
66 - The "recognition scene" and its embellishment

Contemporary and near-contemporary sources differ on how staged or spontaneous Joan's recognition of Charles VII at Chinon was; historians treat the most dramatic tellings as embellished in transmission while agreeing an audience took place and produced a strong impression on the court. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006); Larissa Juliet Taylor, The Virgin Warrior (2009).

[Beat 166]
67 - The virginity examination at the Coudray tower

Joan was lodged in Chinon's Coudray tower and examined by women of the court, under the supervision of Yolande of Aragon, Charles's mother-in-law, to confirm she was "a true and entire maid," rebutting circulating rumors. Larissa Juliet Taylor, The Virgin Warrior (2009); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 167]
68 - Joan's return audience at Chinon after Poitiers

Joan returned to Chinon and was received again by Charles VII between roughly 27 March and 5 April 1429, after the Poitiers theological commission delivered its qualified findings (see Chapter 7). Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 168]
69 - The lost Poitiers register

The original register of the Poitiers commission's findings is lost; its content survives mainly through Joan's own repeated references to it at her 1431 trial in Rouen and through later secondhand summaries. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (2006); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 169]
70 - The Poitiers commission's "favorable presumption" verdict

The Poitiers theologians reported a "favorable presumption" regarding Joan's mission, finding nothing in her conduct or speech contrary to the faith and no good reason to refuse her offered help — a qualified, permissive verdict rather than an endorsement of divine origin. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (2006); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 170]
71 - Joan's equipping at Tours: armor, sword, and banner

Charles VII's household commissioned custom armor and a small military household for Joan at Tours; at her direction a sword was retrieved from behind the altar of the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois, and she carried a specially made banner bearing the fleur-de-lis. Larissa Juliet Taylor, The Virgin Warrior: The Life and Death of Joan of Arc (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2009); collated primary references at jeanne-darc.info.

[Beat 171]
72 - Disagreement over the location of the virginity examination

Sources are not fully consistent on whether Joan's physical examination took place at Chinon or at Tours; several accounts place the matrons' inspection, conducted under Yolande of Aragon's supervision, in the Chinon-to-Tours window rather than pinning it to a single site. Larissa Juliet Taylor, The Virgin Warrior (2009); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 172]
73 - The strategic stakes of the siege of Orléans

Orléans had been under English siege since 12 October 1428; its fall was expected by both sides to open the unoccupied south of France to English regent John, Duke of Bedford, and the city sat on the last major Loire bridge crossing held by Charles VII's side. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999).

[Beat 173]
74 - Dunois's testimony on Joan's arrival at Orléans

Jean de Dunois testified at the 1455-56 Trial of Nullification that the wind over the Loire turned favorable for provisioning boats at almost the exact moment Joan reached the riverbank at the Port du Bouchet on her arrival, 29 April 1429, an event he cited as evidence "that Joan was sent by God, and that her behavior in war was a fact divine rather than human." Trial of Nullification testimony, in Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 174]
75 - The fall of Les Tourelles and the raising of the siege

French forces stormed Les Tourelles, the fortified bridgehead, on 7 May 1429; the English abandoned the siege of Orléans the following day, 8 May 1429, seven months after it began. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 175]
76 - The Loire campaign's strongpoints

Following the relief of Orléans, French forces took the remaining Loire strongpoints of Jargeau, Meung, and Beaugency through June 1429. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999).

[Beat 176]
77 - The Battle of Patay, 18 June 1429

At Patay, French vanguard cavalry under La Hire and the Duke of Alençon caught a retreating English relief force under John Talbot and Sir John Fastolf still forming its defensive line of stakes and archers; Joan accompanied the main body. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999); historyofwar.org summary of the engagement.

[Beat 177]
78 - Casualties and outcome at Patay

Contemporary estimates put English dead at Patay, overwhelmingly archers, between roughly a thousand and several thousand against light French losses; John Talbot was captured, while Sir John Fastolf escaped the field, an episode that damaged his reputation in England. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999).

[Beat 178]
79 - Reims as the traditional coronation site and the Sainte Ampoule

Reims, not Paris, was the customary site of French royal coronations, a tradition tied to the Sainte Ampoule, a vial of oil believed to have anointed Clovis, first king of the Franks, and by extension every legitimate French king since. French Moments and historyatlas.com summaries of the tradition; Larissa Juliet Taylor, The Virgin Warrior: The Life and Death of Joan of Arc (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2009).

[Beat 179]
80 - Joan's political argument for pressing to Reims

Joan pressed for the coronation march specifically because it would make Charles VII's kingship a fact recognized by tradition rather than merely a matter of dynastic dispute with the English-backed claim of the infant Henry VI. Larissa Juliet Taylor, The Virgin Warrior (2009); Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 180]
81 - Charles VII's coronation at Reims

Charles VII was crowned in Reims Cathedral on 17 July 1429, after the army moved through Burgundian-held towns that opened their gates without resistance following the destruction of the English field army at Patay. Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998); historyatlas.com summary of the coronation.

[Beat 181]
82 - Gilles de Rais at the coronation

Gilles de Rais was one of four lords chosen to escort the Sainte Ampoule from the Abbey of Saint-Remi for Charles VII's coronation on 17 July 1429, and was created Marshal of France the same day. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999); Britannica, "Gilles de Rais."

[Beat 182]
83 - Alençon's testimony on his time with Joan

Jean d'Alençon testified at the Trial of Nullification that he "never left" Joan from the coronation at Reims until the army's advance on Paris, and described her as "a good and modest woman, living as a Catholic and very pious." Trial of Nullification testimony, in Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (2006).

[Beat 183]
84 - Joan's reported prediction of one year with the king

Joan is reported to have told Charles VII she would "only be with you for one year," a remark later writers have treated as a fulfilled or near-fulfilled self-prediction given the timeline of her capture roughly a year later, in May 1430. jeanne-darc.info/biography/visions/, cross-checked against the general chronology in Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999).

[Beat 184]
85 - The royal council's hesitation after the coronation

Following the coronation, Charles VII's court hesitated over an immediate assault on Paris, pursuing negotiation and delay rather than pressing the military momentum built through the Loire campaign and Patay. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 185]
86 - The failed assault on Paris, 8 September 1429

The French assault on Paris on 8 September 1429 failed; Joan was wounded in the thigh by a crossbow bolt during the attack on the Saint-Honoré gate, and the army subsequently withdrew. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 186]
87 - Joan's defense of Compiègne, spring 1430

By spring 1430 Compiègne, a town loyal to Charles VII on the Oise, came under threat from Burgundian forces allied with the English; Joan entered the town to support its defense. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Stroud, UK: Sutton Publishing, 1999).

[Beat 187]
88 - Joan's capture at Compiègne, 23 May 1430

On 23 May 1430 Joan led a sortie from Compiègne against besieging Burgundian troops; the sortie was met by reinforcements, and as the French force fell back the town's gates were closed before Joan's party could get inside. She was pulled from her horse and captured by soldiers under Jean de Luxembourg. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 188]
89 - Historians' assessment of the gate closure at Compiègne

Historians differ on whether the gate closure at Compiègne was defensive necessity, panic, or deliberate betrayal by the town's governor; the more sober modern reading treats it as a standard defensive measure taken under pressure rather than proven treachery. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 189]
90 - Joan's captivity and sale to the English

Jean de Luxembourg held Joan first at Beaulieu, then at his wife's family castle of Beaurevoir, before turning her over to the English for 10,000 livres. Kelly DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (1999).

[Beat 190]
91 - The Demoiselle de Luxembourg's plea at Beaurevoir

Jean de Luxembourg's elderly aunt, the Demoiselle de Luxembourg, showed Joan kindness during her captivity at Beaurevoir and pleaded with her nephew not to sell her to the English, reportedly offering to name him her heir if he refused; he sold her anyway. Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 191]
92 - Rouen as seat of English regency government

Rouen, capital of English-occupied Normandy, was the seat of the English regency government where Joan was imprisoned and tried after her sale by the Burgundians; her prosecution was framed as an ecclesiastical matter, heresy. Daniel Hobbins, trans., The Trial of Joan of Arc (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2005).

[Beat 192]
93 - Joan's imprisonment in a secular rather than ecclesiastical prison

Although canon law and custom required a woman facing spiritual charges to be held in a church prison under female custodians, Joan was held in a secular military cell within the Castle of Rouen, under the authority of the Earl of Warwick, guarded by English soldiers and reportedly chained at times — identified by Daniel Hobbins and other scholars as one of the clearest procedural irregularities of the prosecution. Daniel Hobbins, trans., The Trial of Joan of Arc (2005).

[Beat 193]
94 - The second, protective virginity examination at Rouen

Before the formal heresy trial opened, matrons including Anne Bavon examined Joan on the orders of Anne of Burgundy, Duchess of Bedford, again finding her physically intact; the Duchess then ordered that no one offer Joan violence. No primary trial document supports the claim (traceable instead to Shakespeare's Henry VI, Part 1) that she was examined to rule out pregnancy as a legal shield against execution. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006), pp. 262-349 (Nullification Trial testimony, including Anne Bavon's deposition on the matrons' examination and the Duchess of Bedford's protective order); James C. Oldham, "On Pleading the Belly: A Concise History of the Jury of Matrons," Criminal Justice History 6 (1985): 1-64 (consulted for the legal-doctrine background, not applied to Joan's case).

[Beat 194]
95 - The trial's two-phase structure and personnel

The preliminary inquest opened 9 January 1431, gathering depositions including testimony sought from Domrémy; the formal trial opened 21 February 1431 before Bishop Pierre Cauchon and roughly forty assessors, with Vice-Inquisitor Jean Le Maistre required to co-preside for ecclesiastical validity. famous-trials.com, "The Joan of Arc Trial: A Chronology"; Citizendium, "Trial of Joan of Arc."

[Beat 195]
96 - Pierre Cauchon's career and motivations

Pierre Cauchon (c. 1371-1442), Bishop of Beauvais, was banished from Paris in 1413 for Burgundian sympathies, became a counselor to Henry VI, personally brokered Joan's transfer to English custody, and maneuvered to try her at Rouen despite Beauvais lying outside his own diocese. Britannica, "Pierre Cauchon"; Daniel Hobbins, trans., The Trial of Joan of Arc (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2005), introduction.

[Beat 196]
97 - Cauchon's ambition for the Archbishopric of Rouen

Cauchon angled for the vacant Archbishopric of Rouen during the trial, abandoning the ambition only when the chapter opposed it, and settling in 1432 for the see of Lisieux instead — read by many historians as his English reward for securing Joan's conviction; Daniel Hobbins offers a competing reading, arguing Cauchon may have seen a procedurally correct trial as Joan's best chance of avoiding summary execution. Britannica, "Pierre Cauchon"; Encyclopedia.com, "Cauchon, Pierre"; Daniel Hobbins, The Trial of Joan of Arc (2005), introduction.

[Beat 197]
98 - Jean Le Maistre's coerced participation

At the 1455-56 Trial of Nullification, witnesses testified that Jean Le Maistre objected to sitting on the tribunal, argued he lacked jurisdiction in occupied territory outside his own inquisitorial seat, and was compelled to attend only after the English threatened his life. jeanne-darc.info, "Motives & Conduct at Joan of Arc's Condemnation Trial."

[Beat 198]
99 - Jean d'Estivet's prosecutorial role and conduct

Jean d'Estivet, the trial's promoter, drafted the original seventy articles of accusation, later condensed into the Twelve Articles; he barred Joan from Mass over the "impropriety" of her male dress and reportedly threatened at least one assessor who hesitated to render an unfavorable opinion of her. jeanne-darc.info, "The Seventy Articles" and "Motives & Conduct at Joan of Arc's Condemnation Trial."

[Beat 199]
100 - The Twelve Articles and the 23 May 1431 verdict

Theologian Nicolas Midi condensed the case into Twelve Articles on 5 April 1431, covering Joan's visions and voices, the "sign" given to Charles VII, her male dress, her leap from the tower at Compiègne, her military conduct, and her claim of certainty of salvation; she was found guilty on all twelve on 23 May 1431. famous-trials.com, "Twelve Articles of Accusation Against Joan of Arc."

[Beat 200]
101 - repeat: see Note 47's companion quotation, the "state of grace" exchange

Joan's answer to the "state of grace" trap question — "If I am not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God keep me in it. I would be the most wretched person in the world if I knew I were not in the grace of God" — is independently corroborated at catholicnewsagency.com and famous-trials.com's compiled trial account, and is widely cited across both confessional and secular sources as evidence of either quick native intelligence or, on more skeptical readings, prior coaching; the sources dispute the cause of her skill in answering, not that the skill was displayed.

[Beat 201]
102 - Guillaume Erard's abjuration sermon

On 24 May 1431, at the cemetery of the abbey of Saint-Ouen, Guillaume Erard delivered a public sermon condemning Joan and naming Charles VII himself as complicit in heresy for having trusted her. jeanne-darc.info, "Sentence and Recantation"; famous-trials.com, "The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account."

[Beat 202]
103 - Erard's ultimatum to Joan

Following his sermon, Erard pressed Joan to submit and recant, telling her she had had ample time to decide and that refusal meant immediate burning that same day. jeanne-darc.info, "Sentence and Recantation."

[Beat 203]
104 - The abjuration signature

Confronted with the stake already prepared, Joan, by her own admission afraid to die, made her mark on a written abjuration formula; multiple accounts note her hand was guided in producing the signature. jeanne-darc.info, "Sentence and Recantation"; goodreads.com summary of the trial account (cross-referenced against Barrett 1932 and Hobbins 2005 translations).

[Beat 204]
105 - Joan's relapse, 28 May 1431

On 28 May 1431 Joan was found again in male clothing in her cell; under questioning she stated her voices had reproached her for having submitted against their will. She was declared a relapsed heretic. famous-trials.com, "Joan's Relapse and Final Adjudication."

[Beat 205]
106 - The execution, 30 May 1431

Joan was handed to secular authority on 29 May 1431 and burned at the stake in Rouen's Place du Vieux-Marché on 30 May 1431; canon law required the Church to hand a condemned heretic to secular authority for execution rather than carrying it out directly. Daniel Hobbins, trans., The Trial of Joan of Arc (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2005); Régine Pernoud and Marie-Véronique Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998).

[Beat 206]
107 - The nullification-trial testimony as source for the execution scene

The official Latin trial record ends with the sentence; the primary sources for the execution itself are the later testimony of witnesses at the 1455-56 Trial of Nullification, principally Isambart de la Pierre and Jean Massieu. jeanne-darc.info, "Trial of Nullification"; famous-trials.com trial account.

[Beat 207]
108 - Isambart de la Pierre and Jean Massieu at the execution

Isambart de la Pierre preached to Joan, recited the seven penitential psalms at her request, and held a crucifix before her at the stake; Jean Massieu, who had escorted her through every trial session, attended her final moments and recounted her requests that the crowd forgive and pray for her, and that she forgave those who had wronged her. jeanne-darc.info, "Trial of Nullification"; stjoan-center.com, "St. Joan of Arc's Trial of Nullification."

[Beat 208]
109 - Disposal of Joan's ashes in the Seine

Joan's ashes were reportedly thrown into the Seine from the Pont Mathilde near Rouen, a deliberate measure to prevent any physical relic from being kept or venerated. Hobbins, The Trial of Joan of Arc (2005); Pernoud and Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story (1998).

[Beat 209]
110 - Monstrelet's assessment of Joan's capture

Burgundian chronicler Enguerrand de Monstrelet recorded that the English and Burgundians were "overjoyed, more so than if they had taken 500 combatants, for they had never feared or dreaded any other commander," on news of Joan's capture. Enguerrand de Monstrelet, Chronique, ed. Louis Douët d'Arcq, 6 vols. (Paris: Société de l'Histoire de France, 1857); English trans. Thomas Johnes, The Chronicles of Enguerrand de Monstrelet (London: William Smith, 1845).

[Beat 210]
111 - Charles VII's tax and military reforms

The Estates-General approved the taille as a permanent direct royal tax in 1439; between 1445 and 1448 Charles VII's administration created France's first standing professional army, the compagnies d'ordonnance (heavy cavalry) and the francs-archers (commoner infantry, exempted from the taille). These reforms are credited with the French victory at Castillon (1453) that ended the Hundred Years' War. EBSCO Research Starters, "Charles VII"; M. G. A. Vale, Charles VII (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1974).

[Beat 211]
112 - "Charles le Bien Servi" as an ironic epithet

Charles VII is remembered both as "le Victorieux" (the Victorious) and "le Bien Servi" (the Well-Served); French historical sources note the latter is used ironically, crediting his entourage — Joan of Arc, the Bureau brothers, chancellor Jouvenel des Ursins, financier Jacques Cœur, Yolande of Aragon, Marie of Anjou, and Agnès Sorel — over the king's own initiative. Herodote.net, "Charles le Bien Servi."

[Beat 212]
113 - Louis XI's rebellion against Charles VII

The future Louis XI joined the Praguerie, a 1440 noble revolt against Charles VII, at sixteen or seventeen, hoping to be made regent; after the revolt failed and further intrigue, Charles VII banished him from court. Louis set up a rival household in the Dauphiné, married against his father's wishes, and fled to the protection of Philip the Good of Burgundy when royal troops were sent after him, remaining there until Charles VII's death in 1461. Paul Murray Kendall, Louis XI: The Universal Spider (New York: W. W. Norton, 1971); Malcolm G. A. Vale, Charles VII (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1974).

[Beat 213]
114 - Louis XI, "the Universal Spider"

Louis XI earned the nickname "l'universelle araignée" (the Universal Spider) for his reliance on intrigue, secret diplomacy, and an extensive spy network, a style historians contrast with his father Charles VII's more institutional and consultative approach to royal power. Medievalists.net, "The Universal Spider: King Louis XI"; The Collector, "Louis XI, King of France: The Universal Spider."

[Beat 214]
115 - Isabelle Romée's petition for a rehabilitation trial

Isabelle Romée traveled to Paris with two surviving sons and supporters from Orléans, appearing before papal representatives at Notre-Dame to petition for a reopening of her daughter's case; Pope Callixtus III authorized the inquiry, led by Inquisitor-General Jean Bréhal, with preliminary investigations beginning in 1452 and the formal appeal opening in November 1455. Pernoud and Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, trans. Jeremy DuQuesnay Adams (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998), pp. 133-135.

[Beat 215]
116 - Scale of the Trial of Nullification's witness testimony

The Trial of Nullification gathered testimony from well over a hundred witnesses across France, including professional soldiers, nobility, and ordinary villagers from Domrémy who had known Joan since childhood. Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 216]
117 - The 7 July 1456 nullification verdict

On 7 July 1456 the tribunal declared the 1431 trial null, "contaminated with fraud, calumny, wickedness, contradictions, and manifest errors of fact and law," naming Pierre Cauchon's conduct as proceeding from a "secular vendetta" rather than legitimate ecclesiastical concern. Jules Quicherat, ed., Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d'Arc, vols. 2-3 (Paris, 1841-1849); Craig Taylor, ed. and trans., Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2006).

[Beat 217]
118 - The des Armoises impostor's meeting with Joan's brothers

Claude (also called Jeanne) des Armoises surfaced publicly around 1436 claiming to be a surviving Joan of Arc and met Joan's actual brothers, Pierre and Jean d'Arc, in Metz on 20 May 1436, convincing them she was their sister. Andrew Lang, "The False Jeanne d'Arc" (originally "The False Pucelle," The Nineteenth Century, May 1895), reprinted in The Valet's Tragedy and Other Studies (London: Longmans, Green, 1903), essay IV; Pernoud and Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, trans. Jeremy DuQuesnay Adams (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998), chapter on the post-execution impostors.

[Beat 218]
119 - Des Armoises's marriage, deception, and eventual confession

Des Armoises married knight Robert des Armoises and had two children with him; her impersonation ran for years and nearly deceived Charles VII himself before she failed to produce a private "secret" known only to the real Joan and the king, at which point she confessed the fraud and was allowed to return to her husband. Andrew Lang, "The False Jeanne d'Arc" (originally "The False Pucelle," The Nineteenth Century, May 1895), reprinted in The Valet's Tragedy and Other Studies (London: Longmans, Green, 1903), essay IV; Pernoud and Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, trans. Jeremy DuQuesnay Adams (New York: St. Martin's Press, 1998), chapter on the post-execution impostors.

[Beat 219]
Anatole France — Skeptical/naturalistic biographer (1908); Nobel laureate novelist

Anatole France was a French novelist and essayist (Nobel laureate, 1921), member of the Académie Française. His Vie de Jeanne d'Arc (Calmann-Lévy, 1908, 2 vols.) offered a deliberately naturalistic, skeptical biography, arguing Joan suffered from hallucinations 'of all the senses, especially hearing' originating in childhood, and portraying her at points as a manipulated figurehead ('mascot') of political factions around her rather than a self-directed agent of providence. The book triggered significant contemporary backlash: Scottish historian Andrew Lang published a rival biography the same year, The Maid of France (Longmans, Green, 1908), explicitly written in response, attacking France's scholarly carelessness and unsupported psychiatric diagnoses — a rebuttal documented in the American Historical Review's joint 1909 review of both books. Catholic historian Jean-Baptiste-Joseph Ayroles also published a hostile counter-critique (1910), and medievalist Achille Luchaire criticized the work as well. His approach is skeptical/naturalistic-literary — an early and controversial example of applying secular-medical explanation to Joan's voices; the reception controversy itself (France vs. Lang vs. Ayroles) illustrates the early 20th-century battle lines between secular-skeptical and devotional-traditional readings.

[Beat 220]
Bertrand de Poulengy — Vaucouleurs esquire; escorted Joan to Chinon alongside Jean de Metz

Bertrand de Poulengy, an esquire of the Vaucouleurs garrison alongside Jean de Metz, had met Joan before her formal commissioning and was already persuaded of her sincerity; he later testified he offered to escort her at his own initiative (Trial of Nullification depositions, 1455-56, in Craig Taylor, Joan of Arc: La Pucelle, 2006). He rode with her from Vaucouleurs (c. 13 February 1429) to Chinon (23 February 1429), part of a six-man party that also included de Metz, servants, the royal messenger Colet de Vienne, and an archer, Richard Larcher, traveling largely by night to avoid Anglo-Burgundian patrols (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999). His nullification-trial testimony, given decades later, corroborates de Metz's account of the journey — that Joan's conduct and dress gave the escort no cause for impropriety, and that she expressed reluctance about leaving her family behind but insisted the mission was not her own choice but a duty she 'must do' (Trial of Nullification testimony, in Taylor, 2006). Along with de Metz, his deposition is one of the primary sources historians rely on for this otherwise thinly documented stage between Vaucouleurs and Chinon.

[Beat 221]
St. Catherine of Alexandria — One of the two female voices Joan identified alongside St. Margaret

St. Catherine of Alexandria was one of the two female saints Joan identified as her voices, alongside St. Margaret of Antioch. Joan testified their faces were 'adorned with beautiful crowns, very rich and precious' (famous-trials.com, 'The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account'), and, pressed on physical reality, asserted 'I saw them with my bodily eyes, just as well as I see you' — a direct, unhedged claim of literal sensory perception. When judges pushed for physical specifics — hair, clothing, whether the saints had arms, whether they were naked — she resisted or deflected, answering she 'had no idea' about such details and returning barbed rhetorical questions such as 'Do you think God can't find [them] clothes?' (History Today, 'The Trials of Joan of Arc'). Catherine and Margaret, both venerated virgin-martyrs of the early Church, were popular devotional figures in fifteenth-century French piety generally, which is itself part of the spectrum of explanation this book takes up rather than resolves (see Chapter 3, 'Catherine and Margaret,' and Chapter 4, 'What Was Heard: A Spectrum').

[Beat 222]
Charles VII — Disinherited Dauphin of France, later crowned king at Reims through Joan's mission

In 1429 the man Joan of Arc called 'the Dauphin' held a severely diminished position. The Treaty of Troyes (21 May 1420) had disinherited him, naming Henry V of England and his heirs successors to the French throne upon Charles VI's death, and later tradition holds that his own mother Isabeau of Bavaria's public conduct fed a case against his legitimacy — though the treaty itself does not state this explicitly, and the claim is contested by specialists and should be read as later tradition rather than flat fact (Treaty of Troyes, 1420; Britannica, 'Treaty of Troyes'). In 1421 a Parisian court, acting under Anglo-Burgundian authority, convicted the absent Dauphin of treason and pronounced him banished and disinherited. By 1429 his authority was confined to territory south of the Loire, his court centered at Bourges, earning him the derisive nickname 'King of Bourges' (Vale, Charles VII, 1974). Malcolm Vale's biography portrays a cautious, anxious prince, uncertain of his own legitimacy and pulled between competing factional advisors. Joan first met him at Chinon, arriving 23 February 1429 after an eleven-day journey from Vaucouleurs, received privately on 25 February amid his courtiers — the encounter later mythologized as her 'recognizing' him despite his hiding among the crowd (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). What passed between them in a private conversation — sometimes called 'the sign' — was never fully disclosed by either party and remains one of the thinnest-documented moments of the whole story; a second, more formal audience followed after the Poitiers theologians examined her.

[Beat 223]
Chinon (approx. 47.17°N, 0.25°E)

The Château de Chinon, on the Vienne in Touraine, was the Dauphin Charles's residence when Joan arrived there on 23 February 1429 after her escorted ride from Vaucouleurs. She was received in audience two days later, on 25 February — the episode later mythologized as the 'recognition scene,' in which Joan is said to have picked Charles out from among his courtiers despite his having disguised himself or stood apart from a more richly dressed nobleman (L.J. Taylor, 2009; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). Contemporary and near-contemporary sources differ on how staged or spontaneous this recognition was, and historians treat the most dramatic tellings as embellished in transmission, while agreeing an audience took place and produced a strong impression on the court (Craig Taylor, 2006). Joan was lodged in the château's Coudray tower, where she was also examined for her virginity by women of the court under the supervision of Yolande of Aragon, Charles's mother-in-law and a key political patron (L.J. Taylor, 2009). Rather than act on Joan's claims immediately, Charles referred her to a formal clerical examination at Poitiers; she returned to Chinon and was received again by the king between roughly 27 March and 5 April 1429, after which preparations began to send her to the relief of Orléans (Pernoud & Clin, 1998).

[Beat 224]
Compiègne (approx. 49.42°N, 2.83°E)

Compiègne, on the Oise north of Paris, was where Joan's active military career ended. By spring 1430 the town, loyal to Charles VII, was under threat from Burgundian forces allied with the English; Joan had entered the town to support its defense. On 23 May 1430 she led a sortie out of the town against besieging Burgundian troops — a bid to disrupt their siege lines — but the sortie was met by Burgundian reinforcements lying in wait, and as the French force fell back toward the town, the gates were raised or closed before Joan's own party could get inside, leaving her isolated outside the walls (DeVries, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). Historians differ on whether the gate closure was a defensive necessity, panic, or (in some, largely discredited, accounts) deliberate betrayal by the town's governor; the more sober modern reading treats it as a standard defensive measure taken under pressure rather than proven treachery (Craig Taylor, 2006). Joan was pulled from her horse and captured by soldiers under Jean de Luxembourg, a leading captain in the service of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy. She was held by the Burgundians for several months before being sold to the English for 10,000 livres, ending her free military career and beginning the sequence of imprisonments that led to Rouen (DeVries, 1999).

[Beat 225]
Craig Taylor — Editor/translator of the standard English primary-document sourcebook

Craig Taylor is a British historian of medieval history at the University of York. His Joan of Arc: La Pucelle (Manchester University Press, Manchester Medieval Sources series, 2006) is a sourcebook, not a biography — the first English-language collection assembling major primary documents on Joan's life and contemporary reputation, translated from Latin, French, and Italian. It is organized in five sections: the life of Joan beginning with the 1420 Treaty of Troyes, the Trial of Condemnation, 'Debating Joan of Arc' (contemporary polemic), the Nullification Trial, and the later Memory of Joan of Arc. His role is documentary/editorial, not argumentative — his contribution is curatorial and translational, giving readers direct access to the range of contemporary voices (favorable, hostile, and theological) rather than advancing a single interpretive thesis. Cite Craig Taylor when a claim needs to be traced to a specific translated primary document rather than to a historian's argument.

[Beat 226]
Daniel Hobbins — Translator of the modern standard English trial edition

Daniel Hobbins is an American historian, professor at the University of Notre Dame, specializing in medieval France. His The Trial of Joan of Arc (Harvard University Press, 2005) was the first new English translation of the condemnation trial transcript in fifty years, based on the Latin text established in Pierre Champion's 1920 edition. Hobbins's introduction argues, per recent scholarship he cites, that this Latin record can be dated to the year of the trial itself (1431), giving it a stronger claim to contemporaneity and authority than earlier assumed. His approach is textual/documentary — translation and codicological/dating argument rather than biographical interpretation; he is the standard modern citation for the trial transcript in English-language scholarship, superseding Barrett's 1932 translation for academic use.

[Beat 227]
Deborah Fraioli — Historian of the earliest contemporary theological debate over Joan

Deborah A. Fraioli is an American medievalist whose Joan of Arc: The Early Debate (Boydell Press, 2000) is a focused study of the period between Joan's emergence and her capture at Compiègne (May 1430), examining how she persuaded the dauphin, his court, and his theologians of her divine mission. Fraioli's central contribution is showing that even within Joan's own camp, contemporaries were genuinely divided over whether her mission was divinely or diabolically inspired — she reconstructs this through the theological framework of discretio spirituum ('discernment of spirits'), a formal Catholic doctrinal apparatus for judging claimed revelations, examining contemporary theological tracts debating Joan's authenticity. Her approach is intellectual/theological-historical — treating Joan's reception as a live discursive contest among theologians and propagandists from the very beginning, rather than assuming either credulous acceptance or later-imposed skepticism. Valuable for characterizing how contested Joan's legitimacy was in real time, before either the trial or the myth solidified.

[Beat 228]
Domrémy (approx. 48.44°N, 5.69°E)

Domrémy (now Domrémy-la-Pucelle) was a small farming village on the river Meuse, in the Duchy of Bar near its border with the Duchy of Lorraine — a region with genuinely divided feudal loyalties: households on the west bank of the Meuse, including Joan's family, owed allegiance through the Duke of Bar to the King of France, while households on the east bank were reckoned to Lorraine and the Holy Roman Empire (Pernoud & Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, 1998). Despite sitting deep in a broadly Anglo-Burgundian-controlled swath of northern France during the Hundred Years' War, Domrémy remained loyal to the Armagnac/French crown cause — an exposed loyalist enclave with direct consequences: in 1425, Burgundian raiders drove off the village's livestock and burned part of the settlement, forcing residents to flee, the same year Joan later said she first heard her voices (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999). Village life c. 1412-1428 was ordinary agricultural life — small households, communal fields, the parish church of Saint-Rémy — under real, recurring threat of raid. One piece of local folk custom became evidentially significant at Joan's 1431 trial: a large old beech tree near the village, called 'l'Arbre des Dames' (the Ladies' Tree) or 'l'Arbre des Fées' (the Fairies' Tree), standing near a spring reputed to have healing properties, around which local children — Joan among them — were known to sing, dance, and hang garlands for the image of Our Lady of Domrémy. At her trial (Third Public Examination, 24 February 1431), Joan acknowledged going to the tree with other girls and hearing older villagers, including her own godmother, claim to have seen fairies there, while stating she herself never had; her judges used this local custom as part of an attempted case for sorcery and heresy (Trial of Condemnation, Third Public Examination, 24 Feb. 1431, Barrett 1932 and Hobbins 2005 translations; discussed at length in Craig Taylor, 2006).

[Beat 229]
Gilles de Rais — Royal captain who fought alongside Joan in 1429-1430; later infamous for unrelated 1440 crimes

Gilles de Montmorency-Laval, Baron de Rais (c. 1405-1440), was a young Breton nobleman serving as a royal captain during the 1429 campaigns. He fought in Joan's company through the relief of Orléans and the subsequent Loire operations, and was one of four lords chosen for the honor of escorting the Holy Ampulla from the Abbey of Saint-Remi for Charles VII's coronation at Reims on 17 July 1429, the same day he was created Marshal of France (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999; Britannica, 'Gilles de Rais'). Contemporary chronicles place him at the same engagements as Joan but describe no special personal relationship between them; the popular image of Rais as her devoted, near-inseparable protector is a much later, largely 19th-century literary construction (traceable substantially to historian Eugène Bossard's 1886 study) rather than a feature of the fifteenth-century record (DeVries, 1999). His documented contribution to Joan's story is as one professional captain among several fighting in the same campaigns during 1429-1430. He is separately and infamously known to history for his 1440 trial and execution for the torture and murder of dozens of children — a matter entirely outside Joan's own lifetime and narrative, noted here only for reader orientation, not treated further in this book.

[Beat 230]
Guillaume Erard — Doctor of theology who preached the abjuration sermon

Guillaume Erard was a doctor of theology of considerable renown, brought in specifically to deliver the public sermon preceding Joan's sentencing and abjuration in the cemetery of the abbey of Saint-Ouen on 24 May 1431 (jeanne-darc.info, 'Sentence and Recantation'). Before the assembled judges and a large crowd, Erard's sermon condemned both Joan and, pointedly, King Charles VII by name as complicit in heresy for having trusted her. When his preaching concluded, Erard pressed Joan directly to submit and recant, telling her — per the trial record — that she had had ample time to decide and that refusal meant immediate burning that same day. Confronted with the stake already prepared, Joan, by her own admission afraid to die, made her mark on a written abjuration formula, though multiple accounts note her hand was guided in producing the signature (jeanne-darc.info; goodreads.com). Beyond this single, pivotal appearance, independent biographical detail on Erard's broader career is thin in the sources consulted for this book — a genuine gap rather than a fact to paper over. His role is best documented through the trial minutes themselves and their translations (Hobbins, 2005; Barrett, 1932).

[Beat 231]
The Trial of Condemnation — Primary source — the 1431 heresy trial transcript

The Trial of Condemnation unfolded in two phases at English-occupied Rouen in 1431. A preliminary inquest opened 9 January 1431, gathering depositions — including testimony sent for from Joan's home village of Domrémy — and compiling an initial seventy articles of accusation. The formal trial opened 21 February 1431 before Bishop Pierre Cauchon and roughly forty assessors, with Vice-Inquisitor Jean Le Maistre required to co-preside for ecclesiastical validity. On 5 April 1431 the theologian Nicolas Midi condensed the case into Twelve Articles, covering Joan's claimed visions and voices, her 'sign' to the Dauphin, her male dress, her leap from the tower at Compiègne, her military conduct, and her claim of certainty of salvation; she was found guilty on all twelve on 23 May 1431. On 24 May, confronted by Guillaume Erard's sermon and the threat of immediate burning, Joan signed an abjuration and was sentenced instead to life imprisonment. On 28 May she was found again in male clothing in her cell — she stated her voices had reproached her for having submitted against their will — and was declared a relapsed heretic; she was burned at the stake in Rouen's Place du Vieux-Marché on 30 May 1431. The proceedings were recorded contemporaneously by notaries Guillaume Colles (Boisguillaume) and Guillaume Manchon, producing a running French-language 'minute'; Cauchon later directed Manchon and theologian Thomas de Courcelles to compile an official Latin redaction (five certified copies survive). The standard modern critical edition is Pierre Tisset and Yvonne Lanhers, Procès de condamnation de Jeanne d'Arc (3 vols., 1960-1971); the first unabridged English translation was W.P. Barrett's The Trial of Jeanne d'Arc (1932), based on Jules Quicherat's 1841 edition; Daniel Hobbins produced the first new English translation in fifty years (Harvard University Press, 2005). Its well-documented procedural defects — denial of counsel, suppression of Joan's appeals to the Pope/Council, detention in a secular rather than ecclesiastical prison, leading interrogation — were later formalized as grounds for the trial's 1456 annulment.

[Beat 232]
The Trial of Nullification — Primary source — the 1455-56 posthumous rehabilitation proceeding

The Trial of Nullification was the posthumous retrial that formally reversed the 1431 condemnation. It was initiated by Joan's mother, Isabelle Romée — who, with her two surviving sons and supporters from Orléans, petitioned newly elected Pope Callixtus III for redress, appearing before the pope's representatives at Notre-Dame to request justice for her daughter — aided by Inquisitor-General Jean Bréhal. Investigations began in 1452, with a formal appeal in November 1455; the tribunal gathered extensive eyewitness testimony from Joan's Domrémy childhood acquaintances and family, from fellow soldiers and commanders who served alongside her, and from clergy involved in the original proceedings. The final summary (June 1456) characterized Joan as a martyr and implicated the deceased Bishop Pierre Cauchon in heresy for having pursued a politically motivated conviction of an innocent woman in violation of proper canon-law procedure. On 7 July 1456, the tribunal formally declared the original trial invalid due to procedural fraud and deceit, nullifying the condemnation. Jules Quicherat's 1841-1849 edition (Volumes II-III) is the founding published source for this material; Craig Taylor's 2006 sourcebook devotes a full section to it in English translation.

[Beat 233]
Isabelle Romée — Mother of Jeanne d'Arc; drove the 1455-56 petition to reopen her daughter's case

Isabelle Romée (also recorded as Isabelle de Vouthon, c. 1377-1458) was Joan of Arc's mother, a native of the village of Vouthon near Domrémy who married Jacques d'Arc and, with him, held roughly fifty acres of land and a modest house — comfortable landholding by village standards (Pernoud & Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, 1998). The byname 'Romée' is traditionally explained as a title given to someone who had made a pilgrimage to Rome (or, in some accounts, another notable shrine), though its precise origin is not settled with documentary certainty and should be presented as plausible tradition rather than proven fact. She outlived both her husband (d. 1440) and her daughter, and her most historically consequential act came in the 1450s: together with two of her surviving sons, Jean and Pierre, and with the support of Inquisitor-General Jean Bréhal, she petitioned the Church to reopen her daughter's case. This petition led to the Trial of Nullification, authorized by Pope Callixtus III, which examined whether the 1431 Trial of Condemnation had been conducted lawfully. Isabelle, then elderly, is recorded as having appeared in person before the papal judges at Notre-Dame in Paris to press her plea for her daughter's justice (Rehabilitation Trial records, 1455-1456; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). She lived to see the tribunal's final verdict on 7 July 1456, which annulled the condemnation and declared Joan innocent, and was present at a banquet held in Orléans shortly afterward, on 27 July 1456, honoring Inquisitor Bréhal for the outcome. Historians regard her as the driving personal force behind the rehabilitation effort.

[Beat 234]
Isambart de la Pierre — Dominican friar who showed Joan sympathy during the trial and at her execution

Isambart de la Pierre was a Dominican friar attached to the priory of Saint-Jacques in Rouen who showed Joan conspicuous sympathy during the trial's final days, a stance that put him at personal risk from the English faction. At the execution on 30 May 1431 he is recorded as having preached to her and recited the seven penitential psalms as she asked, and — in the best-known image from the eyewitness record — held a crucifix before her at the stake at her request as the fire was lit (jeanne-darc.info, 'Trial of Nullification'). He was one of seven key witnesses summoned in a preliminary 1450 inquiry into the trial's conduct and testified again at the full Nullification proceedings of 1455-56, where, alongside fellow Dominicans Toutmouillé, Ladvenu, and Duval, he described the coercive atmosphere of the original trial.

[Beat 235]
Jacques d'Arc — Father of Jeanne d'Arc; doyen (village official) of Domrémy

Jacques d'Arc (c. 1380-1440) was Joan of Arc's father, a landholding peasant of Domrémy who occupied the local office of doyen — a village post ranking between the mayor and the provost, responsible for collecting taxes, representing the community's interests, and organizing local defense, including militia musters against raiding parties (Pernoud & Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, 1998; the role is sometimes rendered in English as 'dean' or village headman). This placed the family a step above ordinary peasantry — not gentry, but locally notable and trusted with communal responsibility, evidence historians take that the family was 'somewhat prosperous' relative to their neighbors (Craig Taylor, Joan of Arc: La Pucelle, 2006, a sourcebook translating relevant depositions). He is known chiefly through testimony given by neighbors, relatives, and village acquaintances at the 1456 Trial of Nullification, since he did not testify at the 1431 Trial of Condemnation and died in 1440, before that later proceeding. Those depositions describe him as protective of his daughter's reputation and initially resistant to her claimed mission — several witnesses recalled that he had dreamed of Joan going off 'with men of war' and was reported to have said he would rather see his sons drown her than see that come to pass, a remark historians read as reflecting ordinary peasant anxiety about a daughter's chastity and reputation, not necessarily literal intent (rehabilitation trial depositions, 1456, as summarized in Pernoud & Clin, 1998, and Taylor, 2006). He married Isabelle Romée of nearby Vouthon and with her raised five children in Domrémy.

[Beat 236]
Jean d'Estivet — Promoter (prosecutor) of the Trial of Condemnation

Jean d'Estivet (also recorded as 'Benedicite') held the office of promoter — the trial's prosecuting officer — and personally drafted the original bill of accusation, seventy articles condensed from the preliminary inquest, later distilled by the theologian Nicolas Midi into the Twelve Articles that formed the working indictment (jeanne-darc.info, 'The Seventy Articles'). Nullification-trial testimony describes him as one of the most personally hostile figures in the proceedings: he barred Joan from attending Mass, citing the 'impropriety' of her male dress, and witnesses in 1456 stated that, unlike some assessors who claimed coercion, d'Estivet and Cauchon pursued the case voluntarily and with evident zeal — d'Estivet reportedly threatened at least one assessor who hesitated to render an unfavorable opinion of Joan (jeanne-darc.info, 'Motives & Conduct at Joan of Arc's Condemnation Trial'). Later tradition holds he met a grim end after the English lost Rouen — reportedly found dead in an open sewer or ditch outside the city — a detail widely repeated in popular accounts but less rigorously documented than the trial record itself, and should be treated as folklore-adjacent rather than settled fact.

[Beat 237]
Jean de Dunois — Professional commander at Orléans; key nullification-trial witness

Jean, illegitimate son of Louis, Duke of Orléans (hence 'the Bastard of Orléans'; later legitimated as Count of Dunois), was the crown's senior professional commander at besieged Orléans in spring 1429, holding effective command as lieutenant-general there in Charles VII's name (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999). He was roughly 26 at the time. Dunois met Joan on her arrival before Orléans on 29 April 1429; a much-repeated episode from his own later testimony describes the wind over the Loire turning favorable for provisioning boats at almost the exact moment Joan reached the riverbank at the Port du Bouchet, an event he cited decades afterward as evidence 'that Joan was sent by God, and that her behavior in war was a fact divine rather than human' (Dunois testimony, Trial of Nullification, 1455-56, in Craig Taylor, Joan of Arc: La Pucelle, 2006; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). He worked alongside her through the raising of the siege and the subsequent Loire campaign, and became one of the nullification trial's most credible witnesses precisely because of his professional standing and proximity to events; he also testified to the unusual discipline and chastity of the army in her presence. Dunois went on to a long military and political career under Charles VII well beyond Joan's death, but his documented value to her story is concentrated in the Orléans campaign and his sworn later recollections of it.

[Beat 238]
Jean de Luxembourg — Burgundian lord whose men captured Joan at Compiègne; sold her to the English

John II of Luxembourg, Count of Ligny and lord of Beaurevoir (1392-1441), fought in Duke Philip the Good's service at the siege of Compiègne in 1430. It was a soldier in Jean de Luxembourg's own company who physically pulled Joan from her horse and captured her outside the town on 23 May 1430 during a sortie (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). Jean de Luxembourg held her afterward, first at Beaulieu and then for a longer period at his wife's family castle of Beaurevoir, before turning her over — in exchange for 10,000 livres — to the English, who were determined to have her tried (DeVries, 1999). A frequently cited, documented human detail is that his elderly aunt, known as the Demoiselle de Luxembourg, showed Joan real kindness during her captivity at Beaurevoir and pleaded with her nephew not to sell her to the English, reportedly even offering to name him her heir if he refused; he sold her anyway (Pernoud & Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, 1998). This transaction — a French vassal of Burgundy selling a French prisoner to the English crown — is one of the plainest illustrations of how the Armagnac-Burgundian civil war subordinated Joan's fate to factional politics.

[Beat 239]
Jean de Metz — Vaucouleurs esquire; escorted Joan to Chinon alongside Bertrand de Poulengy

Jean de Metz (sometimes recorded as Jean de Nouillonpont) and his fellow esquire Bertrand de Poulengy, both of the Vaucouleurs garrison, were Joan's actual escorts on the journey that made her a public figure. Both had met her before her formal commissioning and had already been persuaded of her sincerity; both later testified they offered to escort her at their own initiative (Trial of Nullification depositions, 1455-56, in Craig Taylor, Joan of Arc: La Pucelle, 2006). The party that left Vaucouleurs around 13 February 1429 and reached Chinon on 23 February included, besides Joan, de Metz, de Poulengy, their servants, the royal messenger Colet de Vienne, and an archer named Richard Larcher — six men for an eleven-day ride through contested territory, much of it by night to avoid Anglo-Burgundian patrols (DeVries, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). Joan traveled in male dress, which de Metz said he provided for her. Both men testified decades later at the nullification proceedings, and their depositions are among the most-cited primary sources for this stage of her life: de Metz stated that although he and Poulengy slept beside her on the road, her clothing remained 'closely fastened' and he felt no carnal desire toward her, 'by reason of the virtue I sensed in her.' De Metz also recalled her telling him she would rather have stayed spinning wool at her mother's side, 'but this thing I must do, for my Lord wills it' (Trial of Nullification testimony, in Taylor, 2006).

[Beat 240]
Jean II, Duke of Alençon — Young prince of the blood; close ally, extensive nullification-trial witness

John II, Duke of Alençon (1409-1476), was a young prince of the blood whom Joan affectionately called 'le beau duc' ('the fair duke'). Captured as a teenager at the disastrous Battle of Verneuil in 1424, he had spent years as an English prisoner, only securing his release — at an enormous ransom of 200,000 saluts d'or, forcing him to sell part of his patrimony — shortly before Joan's arrival at Chinon in early 1429 (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999). He met her soon after his release, quickly became one of her most devoted supporters among the high nobility, and accompanied her through the Loire campaign, serving as nominal commander of the army at the taking of Jargeau in June 1429 (DeVries, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). At the 1455-56 Trial of Nullification, Alençon gave detailed testimony: that Joan had reassured his anxious wife, 'Lady, have no fear; I will bring him back to you safe, or in even better condition than he is now'; that at Jargeau she warned him away from a spot where a cannon shot moments later killed another man standing there; and that she showed a marked, pious aversion to soldiers' swearing and blasphemy in her presence (Trial of Nullification testimony, in Craig Taylor, Joan of Arc: La Pucelle, 2006). He testified he 'never left her' from the coronation at Reims until the army's advance on Paris, and described her as 'a good and modest woman, living as a Catholic and very pious.'

[Beat 241]
Jean Le Maistre — Vice-Inquisitor of France; nominal co-president of the trial, reluctant participant

Jean Le Maistre served as Vice-Inquisitor of France for the diocese of Rouen and was required by canon law to co-sign the proceedings against Joan for them to carry ecclesiastical validity — inquisitorial and episcopal jurisdictions had to act jointly in a heresy case. The documentary record, drawn from the 1455-56 Nullification testimony, shows Le Maistre was a deeply unwilling participant: multiple witnesses testified he objected to sitting on the tribunal and initially refused, citing that he had no jurisdiction in what was English-occupied territory outside his own inquisitorial seat, and that he was ultimately compelled to attend only after the English threatened his life (jeanne-darc.info, 'Motives & Conduct at Joan of Arc's Condemnation Trial'). His participation was thus a matter of legal necessity rather than conviction, and his presence — obtained under duress — became one of the specific defects cited when the trial was annulled in 1456. Le Maistre co-signed the sentence and was present at the final sessions, but the eyewitness testimony gathered a quarter-century later paints him as a reluctant instrument of a process he understood to be improperly constituted from the start.

[Beat 242]
Jean Massieu — Usher and executor of the trial court; attended Joan at the execution

Jean Massieu held the institutional role of usher (huissier) and executor of the trial court — the officer who formally conducted Joan into and out of the trial sessions from its opening on 21 February 1431, and who attended her at the execution (maidofheaven.com, 'Jean Massieu Court Bailiff'). At the Nullification trial he gave detailed testimony on the coercion applied to Vice-Inquisitor Jean Le Maistre and recounted Joan's final requests before the pyre — that the crowd forgive her and pray for her, and that she in turn forgave those who had wronged her (stjoan-center.com, 'St. Joan of Arc's Trial of Nullification'). Massieu and Isambart de la Pierre's testimony, given decades after the events, remains one of the principal eyewitness sources historians use to reconstruct the execution scene, since the official Latin trial record itself ends with the sentence rather than a narrative of the burning.

[Beat 243]
Jeanne d'Arc — Central figure — peasant visionary and military leader, tried and executed for heresy, later declared innocent and canonized

Jeanne d'Arc was born in the village of Domrémy, in the Duchy of Bar on the border with Lorraine, in or around 1412 — the year is the scholarly consensus, but the exact date is not independently attested in any document; she stated at trial that she did not know her own age precisely. The commonly cited date of January 6 (Epiphany) derives from a 1429 letter by Perceval de Boulainvilliers describing portents at her birth, not from a parish record, and should be treated as later literary tradition rather than certainty (Boulainvilliers letter, 1429; Pernoud & Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, 1998). She was the fourth of five children of Jacques d'Arc and Isabelle Romée, a family of moderately prosperous peasant landholders. Physical descriptions come almost entirely from trial and rehabilitation-trial testimony rather than any portrait made from life: witnesses describe her as short (roughly 5'2"/1.58m), physically strong, dark-complexioned, with black hair cut short in a masculine style, and a notably compelling speaking voice; claims about eye color or a birthmark are far less firmly attested and vary by source (trial and rehabilitation testimony; Taylor, The Virgin Warrior, 2009; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). She reported first hearing voices around age thirteen (c. 1425, the same year Domrémy suffered a Burgundian raid), left home in 1429 to reach the dauphin Charles at Chinon, helped relieve the siege of Orléans, accompanied Charles to his coronation at Reims, was captured by the Burgundians at Compiègne in May 1430, sold to the English, tried for heresy at Rouen by a French ecclesiastical court under English influence, and burned at the stake on 30 May 1431 (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999; Trial of Condemnation, 1431). A posthumous Trial of Nullification cleared her name in 1456; she was beatified in 1909 and canonized in 1920.

[Beat 244]
John of Lancaster, Duke of Bedford — English regent of France; chief political/military opponent

John, Duke of Bedford (1389-1435), third son of Henry IV of England, served as regent of France for the child king Henry VI following the death of Henry V in 1422, and was the chief English political and military authority Joan's campaign confronted. It was Bedford's forces besieging Orléans whom Joan's arrival forced to abandon the siege in May 1429, a reversal that Bedford's own reinforcing army compounded weeks later when it was destroyed at the Battle of Patay (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999). Bedford's contempt for Joan is preserved in his own words: in a letter to Charles VII he described her as 'a woman of disorderly and infamous life, dissolute in conduct,' dressed as a man, calling this 'abominable in the sight of God' (Bedford correspondence, quoted in DeVries, 1999). After her capture at Compiègne and transfer to English custody, it was Bedford's administration that arranged and oversaw her ecclesiastical trial at Rouen, using clergy sympathetic to the English/Burgundian cause, ending in her execution on 30 May 1431 (DeVries, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, 1998).

[Beat 245]
Journal du siège d'Orléans — Contemporary chronicle sources on the siege of Orléans and Joan's campaigns

Several distinct contemporary chronicles corroborate and cross-check the trial record and later testimony. The Journal du siège d'Orléans is an anonymous 15th-century French-language chronicle covering 1428-1429, focused on the siege of Orléans, providing granular operational detail including discussions of military tactics between Joan and Jean de Dunois; it received a dedicated scholarly edition by Paul Charpentier and Charles Cuissard (1896). The Chronique de la Pucelle (also called the Chronique de Cousinot) is traditionally attributed to Guillaume Cousinot, Seigneur de Montreuil, a diplomat and contemporary of Joan who wrote some years after the war's end; first published in 1661 by Denis Godefroy, with the standard critical edition by Auguste Vallet de Viriville (the Cousinot attribution, while generally accepted, is not fully settled in modern scholarship). Perceval de Cagny's chronicle was written by a career servant of the house of Alençon, compiled in 1436, only six years after Joan's death; because Cagny wrote from within Alençon's own household, with direct access to a commander who fought at Joan's side, it is regarded as among the best and most reliable contemporary chronicle accounts of her campaigns. Enguerrand de Monstrelet's chronicle (covering 1400-1444) is a Burgundian-aligned source; historians assess his treatment of Joan as 'about as fair as can be expected from a Burgundian,' recording that her capture left the Armagnacs devastated while the English and Burgundians were 'overjoyed, more so than if they had taken 500 combatants' — a valuable cross-check from the opposing political side on how seriously Joan's opponents took her military threat. Standard edition by Louis Douët d'Arcq (1857); English translation by Thomas Johnes (1845).

[Beat 246]
Jules Quicherat — 19th-century archivist who first published the critical edition of both trials

Jules Quicherat (1814-1882) was a French paleographic archivist, archaeologist, and historian; a graduate of the École des Chartes (1835), he later returned there and became its director in 1871. He was an active member of the Société de l'histoire de France (from 1841) and the Société nationale des antiquaires de France (from 1845). His five-volume Procès de condamnation et de réhabilitation de Jeanne d'Arc, dite la Pucelle, publiés pour la première fois d'après les manuscrits de la Bibliothèque royale (Paris, 1841-1849) was the founding critical edition of the trial documents: Volume I contains the condemnation trial materials, Volumes II-III the rehabilitation (nullification) trial, and Volumes IV-V a gathered selection of contemporary testimonies about Joan. This edition made the full trial record available to scholarship for the first time and remains the base text (directly or via Champion's later 1920 edition) for virtually every subsequent translation, including Barrett's and, indirectly, Hobbins's. He is not an interpretive historian of Joan so much as the archivist who made the entire modern historiographical tradition possible by publishing the primary record.

[Beat 247]
Karen Sullivan — Literary/discourse-analytical scholar of the trial transcript

Karen Sullivan, then associate professor of literature at Bard College, wrote The Interrogation of Joan of Arc (University of Minnesota Press, 1999), a literary and intellectual-historical analysis of the trial transcript itself rather than a biography. Sullivan's central argument locates the conflict between Joan and her interrogators not primarily in political allegiance (Armagnac vs. Burgundian/English) but in a deeper clash between clerical, university-trained culture and lay, oral, vernacular culture — she reads the 'Joan of Arc' who emerges from the transcript as a persona substantially shaped by that cultural collision rather than a transparent record of a single historical woman's speech. Her approach is literary/discourse-analytical, treating the trial record as a textual artifact produced by an adversarial cross-cultural encounter rather than as straightforward biographical evidence — useful for framing claims about how the trial documents should be read critically, as distinct from historians (Pernoud, DeVries) who use the same transcript as relatively direct evidence of fact.

[Beat 248]
Kelly DeVries — Military historian specializing in Joan's generalship

Kelly DeVries is a military historian at Loyola University Maryland, specializing in medieval warfare. His Joan of Arc: A Military Leader (Sutton Publishing, 1999) is explicitly a corrective work: it deliberately sets aside the 'proto-feminist or saint' framings that dominate popular biography and instead evaluates Joan strictly as a field commander, using maps, siege schematics, and contemporary military documents to place her campaigns (Orléans, the Loire campaign, Reims, Paris, Compiègne) within the broader operational context of the Hundred Years' War. His approach is military-specialist — DeVries treats the trial and hagiographic material as secondary to tactical and logistical reconstruction, asking what Joan actually did as a commander (troop deployment, siege strategy, her relationship with professional captains like Dunois and Alençon) rather than why contemporaries believed she was divinely sent. His work is the standard citation for any claim about Joan's specific battlefield decisions or generalship, as distinct from her spiritual claims or political symbolism.

[Beat 249]
La Hire — Veteran mercenary captain; commanded a vanguard role at Patay

Étienne de Vignolles (c. 1390-1443), known universally by his nickname 'La Hire' (possibly from a form of the Latin ira, 'wrath,' reflecting his reputation for a violent temper), was a veteran mercenary captain of the Armagnac cause long before Joan's arrival. He was among the relatively small number of professional captains at Orléans and in the Loire campaign willing to work with her and credit her mission, commanding a vanguard role at the Battle of Patay (18 June 1429), one of the campaign's most complete French victories (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999). His documented, contemporary role is that of a capable, battle-hardened captain fighting alongside her during the 1429 Loire campaign — nothing more exotic is attested in the sources closest to the events. The colorful image of La Hire as a foul-mouthed brawler who supposedly prayed 'God, I pray you today to do for La Hire what you would have La Hire do for you, if you were a captain and La Hire were God,' is a much later literary embellishment with no contemporary documentary basis (DeVries, 1999; Craig Taylor, Joan of Arc: La Pucelle, 2006). After Joan's capture, La Hire was among the few captains who attempted a rescue effort; he was himself later captured and imprisoned by the Burgundians.

[Beat 250]
Larissa Juliet Taylor — Historian emphasizing Joan's agency and political acumen

Larissa Juliet Taylor is an American historian, Professor of History at Colby College. Her The Virgin Warrior: The Life and Death of Joan of Arc (Yale University Press, 2009) is a full biographical narrative that pushes back against the 'naive peasant girl manipulated by nobles and clerics' framing. Taylor's Joan is self-confident, charismatic, and strategically minded from the outset — 'a pucelle with a plan' rather than a passive vessel — tracing her transformation into 'a strong-willed, bold, and gifted captain of war' (per Journal of Military History review). Her approach is revisionist-biographical, emphasizing Joan's agency and political acumen over her passivity or mysticism, while still working from the standard documentary base (trial records, chronicles, letters). She serves as a modern counterpoint to both the purely hagiographic tradition and DeVries's narrower military framing, integrating political, military, and personal-psychological readings into one narrative arc.

[Beat 251]
Malcolm Vale — British historian of Charles VII; Joan-minimizing causal reading

Malcolm G. A. Vale is a British historian whose Charles VII (University of California Press, 1974) remains, per later reviewers, the most recent full academic English-language biography of the monarch. It is not a Joan of Arc biography but a study of Charles VII built around courtly context, devoting substantial space to Joan's rise, her condemnation, and the delayed nullification process. Per secondary review commentary (this characterization is drawn from reviews rather than a direct quotation from the book itself, and should be flagged as such), Vale's distinguishing move is a deliberately Joan-minimizing reading of the reign: he argues Charles's real recovery of his kingdom (1449-53) had comparatively little to do with Joan's intervention, situating her within — rather than as the hinge of — Charles's fortunes. This makes Vale useful as a military/political-context historian whose approach is skeptical of Joan's causal centrality to the war's outcome, a counterweight to Joan-centered biography.

[Beat 252]
St. Margaret of Antioch — One of the two female voices Joan identified alongside St. Catherine

St. Margaret of Antioch was one of the two female saints Joan identified as her voices, alongside St. Catherine of Alexandria — the two most frequently named of the three across her testimony, appearing together in the trial record's descriptions of crowned, radiant figures whom Joan insisted she saw 'with my bodily eyes, just as well as I see you' (famous-trials.com, 'The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account'). Like Catherine, Margaret was a popular virgin-martyr saint of the early Church with an established cult in fifteenth-century French devotional practice, a fact both sides of this book's central spectrum treat differently — the ecclesiastical reading takes it as evidence of authentic sanctity meeting a receptive vessel, while several naturalistic readings (see Chapter 4) treat the specific choice of these two widely known local devotional figures as evidence of a visionary experience shaped by the devotional culture already surrounding her, without settling whether that shaping is itself evidence against authenticity.

[Beat 253]
St. Michael the Archangel — First of the three voices Joan identified; recurring figure in her testimony

St. Michael was the first of Joan's voices she named at trial, and the one she said came 'with a great multitude of Angels,' appearing to her at Chinon in the house where she lodged, walking with her and holding her by the hand (famous-trials.com, 'The Trial of Joan of Arc: An Account'; jeanne-darc.info trial index). She testified she recognized the voice as an angel's after hearing it three times, and that it was usually accompanied by a great light coming from the same direction — to her right, toward the church (quotescosmos.com, quoting the trial record). At trial she described an angel — understood as Michael — bringing Charles VII 'a beautiful crown of pure gold,' bowing and saying 'Sire, here is your sign. Take it' (famous-trials.com), though near her death she is reported to have given a more figurative account of this same 'sign,' describing it closer to her own promise to lead Charles to his coronation — a shift later readers have taken in opposite directions (hostile judges as proof of invention, sympathetic readers as clarification under duress). This entry names the tradition's identification only; whether her experience of this figure was a literal apparition, a devotional impression, or something else entirely is the subject of this book's central, deliberately unresolved question (see Chapter 4, 'What Was Heard: A Spectrum').

[Beat 254]
Orléans (approx. 47.90°N, 1.91°E)

Orléans, on the north bank of the Loire, had been under English siege since 12 October 1428, and its fall was widely expected — by both sides — to open the entire unoccupied south of France to the English regent, John, Duke of Bedford, fulfilling the ambition Henry V had left unfinished at his death (DeVries, 1999). The city sat on the last major bridge crossing of the Loire held by the Dauphin's side, making it the strategic hinge between English/Burgundian-controlled northern France and the still-loyal south. Joan arrived with a relief convoy in late April 1429 and entered the city on 29 April. Within days French forces, with Joan present and urging aggressive action, stormed the English siege forts ringing the city — most decisively Les Tourelles, the fortified bridgehead, on 7 May — and the English abandoned the siege on 8 May 1429, seven months after it began and only about a week after Joan's arrival (DeVries, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). The relief of Orléans reversed the momentum of the war and is treated by military historians as its turning point, opening the Loire campaign that culminated weeks later at Patay.

[Beat 255]
Tours (approx. 47.39°N, 0.68°E)

Tours, in Touraine, was where Joan was equipped for war after clearing the Poitiers examination: Charles VII's household commissioned a custom suit of armor for her and established a small military household in her name, and it was from this area that she sent for the sword later recovered, at her direction, from behind the altar of the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois — an old, marked blade she carried alongside a specially made banner bearing the fleur-de-lis (L.J. Taylor, 2009). Sources are not fully consistent on whether the physical virginity examination itself took place at Chinon or at Tours; several accounts place the matrons' inspection — carried out under the supervision of Yolande of Aragon, to confirm Joan was 'a true and entire maid' and rebut hostile rumors — in this Chinon-to-Tours window rather than pinning it to a single site (L.J. Taylor, 2009; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). What is firmly documented is that by the time Joan left the Tours area for the Loire campaign, she had passed both the theological screening (Poitiers) and the physical/moral screening, and was outfitted, armed, and attached to a household befitting a commander riding to Orléans (DeVries, 1999).

[Beat 256]
Patay (approx. 48.05°N, 1.68°E)

Patay, a small town north of Orléans on the road to Paris, was the site of the decisive field battle of the Loire campaign on 18 June 1429. Following the relief of Orléans and the fall of Loire strongpoints at Jargeau, Meung, and Beaugency, an English relief force under John Talbot and Sir John Fastolf attempted to retreat north; French scouts and vanguard cavalry under La Hire and the Duke of Alençon, with Joan accompanying the main body, caught the English still forming their defensive line of stakes and archers (DeVries, 1999). The French cavalry charged before the English longbowmen — the weapon that had won Crécy, Poitiers, and Agincourt — could set their stakes, and the result was a rout rather than a battle in the usual sense: contemporary estimates put English dead, overwhelmingly archers, in the range of over a thousand to several thousand, against light French losses (DeVries, 1999). Talbot was captured; Fastolf, one of the few senior commanders still mounted, escaped the field, an episode that damaged his reputation in England. Patay effectively destroyed England's field army in the region and, unlike Orléans (a siege-relief), was a clean tactical annihilation — the engagement that made the subsequent march to Reims militarily possible rather than merely symbolic (DeVries, 1999).

[Beat 257]
Philip the Good — Duke of Burgundy; English-allied opponent, later reconciled with Charles VII

Philip III 'the Good,' Duke of Burgundy (1396-1467), ruled the powerful Burgundian state allied with England against Charles VII for most of Joan's active life, a continuation of the Armagnac-Burgundian civil war he inherited on his accession in 1419 after his father John the Fearless was assassinated by men in the Dauphin's circle (Vale, Charles VII, 1974; DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999). He was not present in person at Orléans or in the Loire campaign; his direct connection to Joan's story is limited to the aftermath of her capture — his troops besieging Compiègne took her prisoner on 23 May 1430, and it was ultimately with his assent that she was sold to the English for 10,000 livres (DeVries, 1999). Philip's Burgundian-English alliance did not survive Joan's death by long — he broke with England and reconciled with Charles VII in the 1435 Treaty of Arras, which annulled the Treaty of Troyes.

[Beat 258]
Pierre Cauchon — Bishop of Beauvais; presiding judge of the Trial of Condemnation

Pierre Cauchon (c. 1371-1442) was a Paris-trained theologian (rector of the University of Paris, 1403) whose career ran through the Burgundian party into direct English service. Banished from Paris in 1413 for his Burgundian sympathies, he attached himself to John the Fearless and then to the English regency of John, Duke of Bedford, becoming Bishop of Beauvais in 1420 and a counselor to the boy-king Henry VI (Britannica, 'Pierre Cauchon'). He personally brokered — and profited from — the transfer of Joan from her Burgundian captors to English custody, then maneuvered to try her at Rouen despite Beauvais lying outside his own diocese, since Joan had been captured within his jurisdiction at Compiègne (Hobbins, The Trial of Joan of Arc, 2005, introduction). A documented incentive sat behind his zeal: Cauchon angled for the vacant Archbishopric of Rouen during the trial, an ambition he abandoned in the face of chapter opposition, settling in 1432 for the see of Lisieux instead — a promotion many historians read as his English reward for securing the conviction. Modern scholarship is not unanimous on his inner motives: Daniel Hobbins complicates the simple 'English hireling' reading, arguing Cauchon was procedurally meticulous and may have seen a correctly-run trial as Joan's only chance of escaping summary execution by her English custodians (Hobbins, 2005). Both readings agree the 1456 Nullification verdict formally implicated the dead Cauchon in fraud and deceit, naming his conduct as proceeding from a 'secular vendetta' rather than legitimate ecclesiastical concern.

[Beat 259]
Poitiers (approx. 46.58°N, 0.34°E)

Poitiers, seat of the university and clergy still loyal to Charles VII after the loss of Paris, was where the Dauphin sent Joan for formal examination before committing any resources to her. Over roughly three weeks in March 1429, a commission of theologians and clerics, convened under the direction of Regnault de Chartres, Archbishop of Reims, questioned her on doctrine, her claimed visions, and her intentions (Craig Taylor, 2006; DeVries, 1999). The commission's findings — recorded in a now-lost register that Joan herself repeatedly invoked two years later at her trial in Rouen, surviving only through summaries and secondhand references — did not certify her visions as divinely inspired outright; instead the theologians reported a 'favorable presumption' regarding her mission, finding nothing in her conduct or speech contrary to the faith, and concluded there was no good reason to refuse her offered help to the kingdom (Craig Taylor, 2006; Pernoud & Clin, 1998). This qualified, permissive verdict — not a ringing endorsement — was what cleared Joan to join the army bound for Orléans and to wear men's dress as a practical necessity of campaigning.

[Beat 260]
Régine Pernoud — Foundational modern rehabilitative historian of Joan of Arc

Régine Pernoud (1909-1998) was a French archivist-paleographer and medievalist, a curator at the Archives Nationales and founder of the Centre Jeanne d'Arc in Orléans. She is widely regarded as the foundational figure of modern Joan of Arc rehabilitative scholarship — the historian who insisted the trial transcripts, letters (three survive dictated by Joan herself), and contemporary chronicles be read on their own terms rather than through centuries of accreted legend. Her signature English-language work, Joan of Arc: Her Story (St. Martin's Press, 1998), co-authored with Marie-Véronique Clin and translated/adapted by Jeremy duQuesnay Adams, is a revision of her earlier French studies. The book builds its portrait almost entirely from primary documentary evidence — trial testimony, ecclesiastical records, eyewitness accounts — presenting Joan as a coherent historical actor rather than a mystical abstraction. Her approach is sympathetic-but-documentary — not devotional legend, but a historian's reconstruction that treats the trial record as fundamentally reliable and Joan's own testimony as basically trustworthy. Her stance set the template that most subsequent English-language biographies (DeVries, Larissa Taylor) work from or react against.

[Beat 261]
Reims (approx. 49.26°N, 4.03°E)

Reims, in Champagne, mattered because it — not Paris — was the customary site of French royal coronations, a tradition tied to the Sainte Ampoule, a vial of oil believed to have anointed Clovis and, by extension, every legitimate king of France since. In 1429 Reims lay well inside territory aligned with the English and their Burgundian allies, considerably north and east of Charles VII's Loire-valley strongholds, so marching there was a deliberate political gamble as much as a military one: an uncrowned Dauphin was a weaker claimant than an anointed king, and Joan pressed hard for the coronation march specifically because it would make Charles's kingship a fact recognized by tradition rather than merely a matter of dynastic dispute with the English-backed claim of the infant Henry VI (L.J. Taylor, 2009; Craig Taylor, 2006). The army moved through Burgundian-held towns after Patay, several of which submitted without a fight as French momentum built, and reached Reims, which opened its gates, allowing Charles VII to be crowned in Reims Cathedral on 17 July 1429 with Joan standing near him bearing her banner — the high-water mark of her career and the fulfillment of the mission she said she had been given (Pernoud & Clin, 1998).

[Beat 262]
Robert de Baudricourt — Captain of Vaucouleurs; sent Joan to Chinon after initial skepticism

Robert de Baudricourt (c. 1400-1454) was captain of the royal garrison at Vaucouleurs, a small fortified town loyal to Charles VII on the eastern frontier of his shrunken kingdom. Joan first approached him in May 1428, asking for an armed escort to the Dauphin at Chinon; Baudricourt, unimpressed by a teenage peasant girl claiming divine instruction, dismissed her and reportedly told the cousin who brought her to slap her and send her home (DeVries, Joan of Arc: A Military Leader, 1999; Pernoud & Clin, Joan of Arc: Her Story, 1998). She returned in January 1429 and pressed her case again. Two things are credited, in varying combination across the sources, with changing his mind: her reported foreknowledge of a French reverse — the 'Battle of the Herrings' near Rouvray — before news of it could plausibly have reached Vaucouleurs, and the sheer persistence of her public insistence that God had sent her to save the kingdom (DeVries, 1999; Craig Taylor, Joan of Arc: La Pucelle, 2006). By February 1429 Baudricourt relented, providing her with an escort of several men-at-arms, a sword, and a letter of introduction to the Dauphin's court. He did not testify at the 1455-56 Trial of Nullification (he had died the previous year); his role is attested through the testimony of the men who rode with her, notably Jean de Metz and Bertrand de Poulengy.

[Beat 263]
Rouen (approx. 49.44°N, 1.10°E)

Rouen, capital of English-occupied Normandy and seat of the English regency government, was where Joan was imprisoned and tried after her sale by the Burgundians. Although her prosecution was framed as an ecclesiastical matter — heresy — she was held not in a church prison under female custodians, as canon law and custom required for a woman facing spiritual charges, but in a secular military cell within the Castle of Rouen (the English citadel, under the authority of the Earl of Warwick), guarded directly by English soldiers and reportedly chained at times; Daniel Hobbins and other scholars of the trial record identify this as one of the clearest procedural irregularities of the entire prosecution, one weighed heavily at her 1456 posthumous rehabilitation (Hobbins, 2005). The trial proper, directed by the pro-English bishop Pierre Cauchon, opened 9 January 1431 and, after prolonged public and private interrogation, ended in a conviction for heresy; found a 'relapsed' heretic after briefly recanting, she was handed to secular authority on 29 May and burned at the stake on 30 May 1431 in the Place du Vieux-Marché, Rouen's market square, with her ashes reportedly thrown into the Seine from the nearby Pont Mathilde to prevent any relics being kept (Hobbins, 2005; Pernoud & Clin, 1998).

[Beat 264]
Vaucouleurs (approx. 48.60°N, 5.67°E)

Vaucouleurs, a royal fortress town on the Meuse a short ride from Domrémy, was where Joan first pressed her mission on Robert de Baudricourt, the Dauphin's captain there. She approached him twice: a first attempt in May 1428, which Baudricourt dismissed outright, and a second, longer stay beginning January 1429, during which she lodged locally, gathered a following among the townspeople, and continued to press her case in public (Pernoud & Clin, 1998; DeVries, 1999). Baudricourt's skepticism reportedly broke when Joan is said to have foretold the outcome of a French reverse near Orléans — the 'Battle of the Herrings' at Rouvray (12 February 1429) — before official word of it reached Vaucouleurs, a detail recorded in later testimony and treated cautiously by modern historians as tradition rather than settled fact (DeVries, 1999). Baudricourt ultimately outfitted her with a small escort, male clothing, and a horse, and sent her on the roughly eleven-day ride across contested territory to Chinon, departing mid-February 1429. Vaucouleurs was thus the hinge between Joan as a village visionary and Joan as a recognized claimant to a mission at the Dauphin's court (Pernoud & Clin, 1998; Craig Taylor, 2006).

[Beat 265]
W.P. Barrett — Earlier English translator of the trial record

W.P. Barrett's The Trial of Jeanne d'Arc (Gotham House, New York, 1932) was the first unabridged English translation of the condemnation trial from the original Latin and French documents, and included an essay and biographical sketches by French historian Pierre Champion (translated from French by Coley Taylor and Ruth H. Kerr). Barrett's translation drew on Jules Quicherat's 1841 critical edition as its base text, supplemented by consultation of additional manuscripts. It was reprinted by the Notable Trials Library (1991) and later by Routledge (Routledge Revivals series). His role is documentary/translational, historically important as the standard English trial text for most of the 20th century, now largely superseded for academic citation by Hobbins (2005) but still widely used and reprinted — useful for marking citations from mid-20th-century English-language scholarship on the trial before Hobbins's retranslation.
