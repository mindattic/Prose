# The Intake

_Ensemble, Isaac Rennick POV. Douglas and Mei-Lin Hartley climb to the agency walk-up -- his organ-harvest theory versus her daughter's research feed and three words, find her. The four partners and the agency are established; Douglas reveals he also retained Meridian Consulting. Isaac reads which parent is the real client; the four claim their angles._

[Beat 1]
EP1 — THE INTAKE

Rennick Investigations. Third floor, Gray Zone edge, walk-up above a Pulse relay shop that smelled permanently of solder and ozone. The buzzer on the street door worked two times out of three, which Rennick had decided was adequate for a firm that did not advertise.

[Beat 2]
The intake desk was Tamsin's by informal treaty — she was the one who kept the intake log current, who remembered which clients needed a hand-written receipt versus a transfer confirmation, who never let a referral chain go cold. Corvin had once suggested they formalize it. Tamsin had said she'd think about it and had not thought about it. The desk remained hers. Nobody argued.

[Beat 3]
All four were in that morning, which happened less often than you'd think for a firm with no required hours and four independent case loads. Corvin had the smallest desk, pushed to the corner, and was doing something on a screen that he would describe if asked and that no one had asked about in three weeks. Voss was in the armchair with a paper notebook — actual paper, which he used because it left no network trace and because Voss had opinions about what constituted a trace. Tamsin had the intake desk and the building's whole sound coming up through two floors of concrete, and the look of someone listening to all of it while she squared the morning's receipts. Rennick stood near the window over the Gray Zone buffer, reading the block below from elevation the way he read everything, by the order things sat in.

[Beat 4]
The buzzer sounded once. Rennick's optical overlay flagged the street-level camera: a man, late fifties, silver-haired, the build of someone who used to be athletic and had not quite admitted the tense had changed. Behind him, a woman a year or two younger, arms crossed at her sternum. Not cold. Rennick noted the distinction.

"North Shore," he said, to the room.

From the window he could already see the car below it, a private hire idling at the curb with its hazards on, the driver not getting out.

[Beat 5]
They came up all three flights without the elevator, which was working that day. Rennick registered that: a man who climbed stairs when he didn't have to either wanted to feel the building or wanted to feel his own effort inside it. The woman climbed because she was keeping pace with her husband, which told Rennick something about their arrangement. She was reading the walls. He was looking ahead.

Rennick opened the door before they knocked.

[Beat 6]
Douglas Hartley stopped inside the doorframe and took in the office with the expression of a man recalibrating. He had expected something — less, probably, or different, or both. The office was not the thing you apologized for, but it also was not the thing that matched the Evanston tax bracket he'd arrived from. Three flights of walk-up, a view of the Gray Zone buffer, intake desk that had seen use. Tamsin's tea on a coaster. Voss in the armchair with his notebook, not looking up.

Rennick said: "Mr. and Mrs. Hartley. Come in."

He gave Douglas the better chair. He handed Mei-Lin the better cup — handle first, coffee, not tea, which was an inference from the way she'd held her arms crossing the threshold, the small muscular inventory of someone who'd been moving since early morning and hadn't sat yet.

His own cup he set down and turned a quarter-turn on the desk so the handle faced the same way as hers — a thing his hands did with any cup in reach, had always done, that read nothing and proved nothing and that he had stopped noticing he did. Tamsin had noticed years ago. She had never mentioned it.

[Beat 7]
Douglas produced the briefing before Rennick asked for it. Printed on paper — actual paper, home printer, collated, paper-clipped at the upper left corner. Rennick accepted it without comment and set it on the intake desk and did not open it yet.

The paper was information before the paper's content was information. Paper meant either old money or theater; the briefing's format — careful, organized, the act of a man who believed the effort demonstrated the seriousness — ruled out theater. Old money, then. Or at least old habit, which was close enough for intake purposes.

[Beat 8]
Douglas Hartley had the posture of a man settled in his conclusions. Not arrogant — Rennick had been in rooms with arrogant clients and this was a different shape. The conclusions had been poured and cured. The meeting was, in Douglas's architecture, a formality: he had already decided what had happened to his daughter; he needed a professional to confirm it and act on it.

He said: "Celeste is twenty-two. She came to us six days ago and said she wanted to pursue cosmetic genework. Cat ears — functional ones, not cosmetic-only. A melanin shift. Some expression work. We discussed it." A pause that was not uncertainty. "Four days ago she went offline. She isn't responding to contact attempts."

Rennick said: "Neuretics ping?"

"Not responding."

"Location services?"

"Off. Or blocked."

"What's your read on why?"

Douglas's expression moved in a specific direction. He said: "That's why I'm here."

[Beat 9]
Rennick opened the briefing.

Douglas had done his research. The pages were organized the way a man organizes information when he is afraid and intelligent: news items, community alerts, a Pulse Network investigative segment — eighteen months of documented cases where unlicensed modification corridors had fronted for organ operations. The articles were real. The facts inside them were real. The selection had been assembled, item by careful item, to support a conclusion that had arrived before the assembly started. A man this organized had decided what he was afraid of, then gone looking for the evidence that proved it.

He read each page at the pace it deserved. He kept his face neutral. Across the room he was aware that Tamsin had set down her pen and gone still at the intake desk, reading the two clients the same way Rennick was reading the briefing.

[Beat 10]
Douglas said: "There's a corridor in Pilsen. Unlicensed shops, no provenance checks, clients who disappear into the system and don't come out. She went looking for a modification and she found something that found her first. That's the theory."

He said it carefully, using the word theory the way people use it when they mean fact.

Rennick set the briefing down. He said: "Mr. Hartley, before your daughter went dark — was there anyone she'd been in contact with regularly? Someone outside your immediate circle?"

Douglas said: "She had friends. Her generation has the feeds."

"Anyone specific? Someone she'd been talking to for several months, maybe longer?"

A silence. Not long. Douglas said: "There was a boy. Ethan. He passed away — eight months ago, nine. A Network accident."

The room did not change. Rennick noted that it didn't.

[Beat 11]
Mei-Lin moved then. Not urgently — not the performance of someone who had been waiting for permission — but with the directness of someone who had been waiting for an obstacle to clear. She set her cup on Tamsin's coaster, pulled out her own device, and held it out flat so Rennick could read it.

"I found this through a friend of a friend of hers," she said. "It's semi-private. Eight months of posts."

Rennick read.

What he was looking at was not what a young person looked like when they discovered something new. No breathless enthusiasm, no overclaiming, no this has been hidden from me energy. What he was reading was a research log. Careful, specific, incrementally building — the language of someone who had spent months learning how to talk accurately about something they'd been thinking about for longer. Baseline drift. Willing flesh. Integration stays. The unlicensed corridor. Ghost period. Motor map formation.

Eight months of documented research. Which meant the thinking had come before the documentation.

[Beat 12]
Rennick handed the device back. He said: "She knew what she was doing."

Mei-Lin said: "Yes."

A beat. Then: "Find her."

She said it the way a professional gives an instruction — flat, complete, the kind of sentence that doesn't have qualifications attached because the qualifications were already handled internally before she opened her mouth. Rennick heard it that way. He also heard the fact underneath it, which was a mother who had read eight months of her daughter's research and understood it and was still afraid, and whose fear was a different shape than her husband's — not the fear of the wrong story, but the fear of the right one.

He said: "We will." He meant it as a professional statement and as a fact. He said it the same way for both.

[Beat 13]
Douglas said, almost in passing — the way you mention something you assume everyone already knows — that he had also retained Meridian Consulting. Corporate investigative firm. Comprehensive coverage. Full team deployment, two days ahead on the case. He said this the way people mention insurance: redundancy, the responsible thing when the stakes are high enough.

Rennick kept his face in the same register.

From the armchair, without looking up from his notebook, Voss said: "Two days ahead, working which angle?"

Douglas said: "The Pilsen corridor. The organ operation theory. They have contacts in Arcturus organized crime."

Voss made a small sound that was not agreement or disagreement. He wrote something. What he wrote was not for Douglas.

[Beat 14]
Douglas said: "I assume that's not a problem. Parallel investigation. The more coverage the better."

Rennick said: "No problem."

He was looking at Douglas when he said it. He was aware of Mei-Lin when he said it — aware that she had gone very still in the way of someone who had just had a hypothesis confirmed. Her husband had retained Meridian two days ago, working the organ-harvest angle, and had come here this morning. The sequence was legible if you knew what you were reading: Douglas had coverage on the conclusion he'd already reached, and had come to Rennick Investigations — hedging without calling it a hedge — for the other outcome.

[Beat 15]
Tamsin stood from the intake desk. She said: "What did she say about the cat ears? When she came to you and said she wanted them."

Douglas said: "She said it was something she'd wanted for a long time."

"Did she say why functional? Why not cosmetic-only?"

A pause. Rennick watched Douglas locate the answer in memory and then locate something uncomfortable adjacent to it.

"She said she wanted to hear the way Ethan heard," Douglas said. "He'd had them since he was sixteen. She said the world sounded different to him." He stopped. "I told her it was grief talking. That she'd feel differently in a few months."

The room absorbed this without comment. Tamsin sat back down.

[Beat 16]
Rennick stood and poured himself water from the carafe on the side table — not because he was thirsty but because standing gave him the moment and the geometry to look at both clients together without making it a thing.

Douglas sat forward in his chair, hands on his knees, the posture of a man ready to receive a briefing. Mei-Lin had her coffee. She had not moved from where she stood near the window; she had declined the chair. She was watching her husband in a way that he had not noticed, which told Rennick something about how long she had been doing it.

He set the water down. He said: "We're going to ask you a few more questions, and then we're going to take the case. Before we do — can you send me Celeste's neuretics ID, full contact records for the six weeks before she went dark, and anything you have on Ethan Ferreira-Mwangi? Full name, last known, any surviving contacts."

Douglas said: "I can send the neuretics and her contact records. I don't keep information on her friends."

Mei-Lin said: "I have Ethan's last address and his cohort from the university. I'll send them."

Douglas looked at her. It was the look of a man learning something in public.

[Beat 17]
He sent them out with the receipt — twenty-five hundred Φ retainer, terms standard, the invoice formal and paper-correct. Douglas accepted it with the naturalness of a man for whom invoices were the language of a resolved transaction. Mei-Lin folded her copy once and put it in her bag.

They left the way they'd come. The buzzer sounded once at street level, then the door.

Rennick listened to the footsteps diminish three flights down. He gave it thirty seconds after the street door. Then he said: "Two jobs in one contract. Who's reading what."

[Beat 18]
Voss said: "He hired Meridian to confirm the organ angle. He hired us because some part of him doesn't believe the organ angle." He turned a page in his notebook without writing on it. "Which is not the same as him being right."

"He's not right," Corvin said. He said it without inflection, as a data point. "The research log the mother showed you. Did it reference any of the documented front operations in the briefing?"

Rennick said: "No. The language was different. Practitioner vocabulary, not fear-based consumption of news."

"Someone who had done the research," Corvin said, "would avoid exactly the shops that had been in the news. She would be following a specific trail toward a specific operator. The briefing is a map of where she didn't go."

Voss said: "If she's following a known-good trail, she's probably fine. Probably."

"'Probably' is why we're taking it," Rennick said.

[Beat 19]
Voss had his notebook open on his knee. He said: "She went offline by design. That's the most useful datum in the file." He said it with the deliberateness of a man choosing each word because there is no autocomplete to catch him. "An integration stay. The standard duration at a reputable unlicensed shop is seventy-two hours, maybe ninety-six for a complex bilateral like functional hearing. She went dark four days ago. She might already be out."

Tamsin said: "She's not out. She would have pinged her mother."

A silence.

"Yes," Voss said. "She would have."

[Beat 20]
Tamsin had been quiet, the way she got when a room handed her more than she could file yet. She said now: "The boy. Ethan. Network diver, died eight months ago. She got his ears so she could hear the way he heard."

Rennick said: "The father called it grief talking."

"It isn't," Tamsin said. "Grief talking is wanting him back. This is wanting to stand where he stood. That's grief working." She squared the receipts under her hand without looking at them. "She had a plan. You don't get a functional bilateral hearing mod on a whim. Months. Money. A practitioner. She built toward it."

She said it without editorializing. She called it a plan because it was one.

[Beat 21]
Tamsin said: "I want the room."

Everyone looked at her. She said: "Celeste's room. Before anyone else goes in. If she planned this over months, if she was building toward it — rooms hold that. Resolve looks different from panic. I can tell you which one was in that room."

Voss said: "You think the father would let you?"

Tamsin said: "I think the mother would."

Rennick said: "We'll ask the mother." He wrote it. Actual paper, because Voss kept a spare pad on the armchair side table and it was closer than his screen. He wrote: Tamsin — bedroom, Evanston, Mei-Lin's permission. He underlined Mei-Lin's.

[Beat 22]
Corvin said: "Send me the feed. All eight months." He said it to Rennick, not to the room. "I want the message provenance on the Ethan thread. Who she was writing to after he died."

Voss said: "She was writing to a dead person?"

"People do," Tamsin said.

"She was writing to something," Corvin said. He distinguished the two words precisely, the way he always did — not dramatically, as a technical correction. "Whether that something was a memorial account, an automated response system, a third party managing the channel, or something she built herself from his message history, I can't say yet. The feed will tell me. The cadence of the replies will be the first diagnostic."

Voss said: "That's a very specific list of options."

Corvin said: "Those are the options."

[Beat 23]
Voss closed his notebook over one finger. He said: "Father's organ-harvest theory. The corridor part of it. I'll take that."

Rennick said: "You don't think it's right."

"I think the harvest is real and the girl isn't in it," Voss said. "Those are two findings, not one. There's an active reclamation trade working the edge of the Pilsen mod shops — I've heard it in the analog channels for a year, the part that doesn't ping. Meridian will hunt it the loud way, through Arcturus contacts, and the corridor will go quiet around them the moment they breathe on it." He said it with the deliberateness of a man choosing each word because there is no autocomplete to catch him. "I don't ping. I can walk the offline part of that corridor and find where an offline person actually goes — and find out whether her trail crosses the harvest or steers clear of it."

Rennick said: "And if it steers clear?"

"Then that's a clean negative on the father's theory," Voss said. "Still a finding. The forensic read on the operation itself — who runs it, whose signature's under the grime — that's yours. I find the door. You read the room behind it."

[Beat 24]
Voss had been writing in his notebook. He said, without looking up: "Meridian's team is connected. Every checkpoint they pass, every channel they ping, leaves a signature. If Celeste is offline by design at a reputable shop, their approach will look exactly like an aggressive corporate investigation to everyone on that block, and the shop will go dark." He looked up. "They're going to scare off the one person who knows where she is."

Rennick said: "We need to be faster than they are."

"I can move without leaving a trace," Voss said. "You know that."

"I know that."

Voss said: "Give me the physical description and her last known location and I'll find the shop before Meridian makes enough noise to close it."

He said it as a logistical statement, not a boast. Voss rarely boasted. He simply stated the geography of what he could reach that others couldn't, the way you'd describe a passage that was too narrow for anyone over a certain width.

[Beat 25]
The four of them sat with that for a moment. The solder-and-ozone smell came up from the relay shop below, ambient and mild, the building's baseline.

Rennick said: "What the room holds. What she wouldn't do. What the offline corridor says. What the feed provenance says." He said each line once, flat, the shape of four different readings aimed at one girl. "We don't share working theory until we have a result. If the readings contradict each other, that's what I want to know first."

Voss said: "What are you taking?"

Rennick said: "The physical trail. The briefing is a map of where she didn't go. I'll start with where she did — and with whatever runs the corridor you find."

[Beat 26]
Corvin said: "One administrative note."

Rennick waited.

"Anything I find on the Ethan feed is inadmissible," Corvin said. He said it without affect, as a known fact being entered into the shared record. "I want that on the log before we start, not after."

Tamsin said: "It's always on the log."

"It should be said each time," Corvin said, "because each time the temptation is to assume it won't matter this time." He turned back to his screen. "It will matter. It always does. Note it and we proceed with clear terms."

Nobody argued with this either. Rennick added it to the paper: Corvin — feed, provenance, message cadence — inadmissible, logged.

[Beat 27]
Tamsin said: "You want to tell me what you read in there? In the room. While they were still in it."

Rennick said: "You first."

Tamsin said: "He hired for a conclusion. She hired for a fact. If the conclusion is wrong, he'll pay us and be unhappy and that'll be the end of it. If the fact is bad, she'll pay us and—" She stopped. "She'll pay us."

"Yes," Rennick said.

"Two separate invoices, then. Eventually."

"Eventually."

Tamsin straightened the manila folder square to the edge of the desk, the small alignment her hands did when a room had left a weight on her. She said: "The cat ears. She wanted to hear the way the dead boy heard. That's not a cosmetic modification. That's a memorial written into bone."

"I know," Rennick said.

[Beat 28]
Voss set his notebook on the side table, open to the page he'd been writing. From across the room Rennick could see the columns: locations, timestamps, what connected each to the next, three blank rows at the bottom labeled ? in Voss's block-letter hand.

"She went offline by design," Voss said again, as if returning to the sentence to check its weight. "Offline by design means she planned the window. She knew exactly how long she'd be unreachable and she chose it. That's not someone who was afraid of being stopped." He picked up his notebook. "That's someone who was afraid of being talked out of it."

Rennick looked at the paper on Tamsin's desk. The briefing, the paper clip at the upper left corner, Douglas's fear made legible in eighteen months of collected news.

"Start this afternoon," he said. "All of us."

[Beat 29]
They dispersed by ones and twos. Corvin had not moved from his screen; for him the case had started the moment the feed arrived, and leaving the building would have changed nothing about where his work was. Voss took the stairs with his notebook open to the next page, the offline corridor already laid out in it as a problem of doors. Tamsin stayed at the intake desk, where she could see the whole morning of the case in the order it had arrived. The relay shop's solder smell rose and held.

[Beat 30]
Rennick stood at the window over the Gray Zone buffer. The strip below ran east to west — unclaimed concrete between two corponation territories, neither willing to pay for it and neither willing to cede it. A parts dealer. Two food stalls. A woman selling handmade neuretics-charging cords from a folding table. All of it working. None of it registered.

He had read Douglas as a man managing fear through certainty, and Mei-Lin as a woman managing grief through attention. One of them had walked in wanting confirmation. The other had walked in wanting the truth. He knew which job he'd taken, and it was not the one on the invoice.

[Beat 31]
The problem with two jobs in one contract was not the second job. It was that the two clients did not know they had hired for different things. Douglas thought Mei-Lin wanted what he wanted: the organ theory confirmed, the daughter recovered, the story closed. Mei-Lin knew better and had not told him. She might be right to wait. She might also have been wrong for eight months while a girl she loved built something out of grief and went offline by design.

That was the part he'd hold for later. He'd work the physical trail first, because the trail would tell him where she'd gone, and where she'd gone would tell him whether later was a luxury they still had.

[Beat 32]
Tamsin capped her pen. She said, without looking up from the intake log: "If Mei-Lin says yes tonight, I go to the room tomorrow. Before Meridian thinks to send anyone to Evanston."

Rennick said: "They won't. They're working Pilsen."

"Then I have the room to myself," Tamsin said. "Rooms keep resolve longer than fear. If she was afraid in there, I'll know which kind." She said it as a logistical fact about what rooms held and how long they held it. "Call you after."

"Call me after," Rennick said.

"I always do."

[Beat 33]
He picked up the briefing from the intake desk. Eighteen months of fear, paper-clipped at the upper left corner, every article real and the selection wrong. He put it in the case folder — the manila one Tamsin kept for clients whose documents needed to exist in a form that couldn't be subpoenaed from a server.

The briefing would be useful for its negative space. Every news item Douglas had chosen was a place his daughter had specifically, deliberately not gone — a map of absences, the terrain she'd been navigating around for months while her father read about it and assembled his conclusions.

Rennick put on his jacket. He had the feed from Mei-Lin, Celeste's contact records from Douglas, a dead boyfriend's last address, and a briefing that told him more about the father's fear than the daughter's plan.

He went downstairs. The buzzer sounded once on his way out, which was the usual ratio.

[Beat 34]
On the street he stopped.

The woman at the folding table was packing up her charging cords — end of her day. Eight sold, four left, Φ12 a cord at the low end of what the parts dealers charged for the same thing with provenance. She counted the four back into her bag and zipped it without looking at her hands.

He noted the count the way he noted every count. Eight sold. It meant nothing yet. None of his numbers meant anything yet, and one of them was a girl four days offline by her own design.

He went south, toward the trail Douglas's briefing had drawn around in eighteen careful pages and never once crossed.
