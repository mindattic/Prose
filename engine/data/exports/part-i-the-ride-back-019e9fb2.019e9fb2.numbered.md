# Part I: The Ride Back

_Carrying three teeth, three rounds in his back, and a night too heavy to put down clean, a dying freelancer rides the long way home to the medic who has spent four years tuning her ear to the sound of his motorcycle. A quiet hour of forceps and warm tea between one job and the next._

## Part I: The Ride Back

[Beat 1]
He rode north out of the Gray Zone through the last of the rain. The Pulse line that ran the eastern strut threw its low-frequency hum up through the road surface — a sound below sound, the kind of vibration you stopped noticing after a year of riding the surface routes and never stopped feeling. It kept a rhythm under everything, too deep to hear and regular as breathing, and when a slug ran the line the whole platform off his left shoulder unloaded a quarter inch at once, every waiting spine on it dropping together and rising again. The bike took it without complaint. The bike never complained.

[Beat 2]
The towers leaned over him as he climbed out of the strut. From the bottom of the canyon you could not see the sky; the sky was a private resource at this depth, available only to the residents of the upper hundred floors and to the VTOL traffic that crossed between them. From the elevation Kyle was now at — sixteenth-floor surface road, the tier they had cut into the side of the structure when the original road grid drowned in 2174 and the city had decided to keep going — the sky was a slot. A vertical slot of slate-grey weather between two buildings whose names he had never learned. You only needed the names if you worked in them. He didn't.

[Beat 3]
He rode the long way. The short way would have taken him up the West Town freight ramp and dropped him at the Damen lift in twenty minutes. The long way ran him through three tier crossings he did not strictly need to make, through the Wicker boundary surveillance grid he made a point of crossing with different plates each time, through the kind of residential block where the windows above the alley had laundry strung between them and somebody was always cooking something that did not smell like ration paste. The first crossing was an Arcturus Civil Security gate now — the thing that had replaced police, scanning for biometrics instead of plates, which made his different-plates habit cosmetic. He kept it up anyway. The second crossing wore a fresh corponation flag he did not recognize over a substation whose mounting collars were pure Axiom Industrial, the bolt pattern that was everywhere because the company that made it had been dead for thirty years and the substrate did not care who flew the flag above it. The long way was forty-eight minutes. He had never once regretted them.

[Beat 4]
The long way had pockets like this — a corner under a freight-band antenna junction, between the second crossing and the residential block, where the city's coverage had gone thin because the corponation overhead had rotated three times since the cameras were last provisioned. The rain came down different here, falling through the synthesis corridor a half-territory east before it reached the street, and it carried the corridor with it: solvent and pear drops up front and under that something cold and mechanical, the smell of an engine that had never been warm. Two of the kids below the stoop had their faces tipped up to catch it on their tongues. A man on a stoop. Beside him, a kid who could have been twelve or could have been fourteen, the way kids in this tier tended to be ambiguous about it, with a handful of small packets in a belt-clip organizer. Blue-dot stamped, Gray Zone origin — the standard Noopharma singles that moved by the half-Φ at every school-adjacent corner from here to the Spine. Two younger ones stood below the stoop with the patient attention of customers who had no reason to be anywhere else. The transaction took six seconds. The man watched neither his hands nor the kids; he watched the freight band. The kids looked at what they'd bought, pocketed it, and walked away with the easy economy of people conducting entirely routine business.

Kyle rode through it. He filed it in the part of his array that did not ask what to do with it — the same section as the abandoned surveillance cameras, the same section as the corponation flag he didn't recognize, the same section as the thirty-seven other things the long way had shown him tonight that he was not, in any immediately actionable sense, responsible for. The residential block was a thousand feet ahead. Someone up there was still cooking.

[Beat 5]
There was a stretch of the long way, between the second crossing and the residential block, that he had never gotten the measure of. A block that took eleven steps when the geometry said nine, and under it a hum at nineteen hertz from no direction he could name — the same note the Pulse threw up through the ferrocrete, except the Pulse explained itself and this didn't. Some nights, beneath the rain, he could pick out one more sound under the hum: the long hollow note of water striking something vast and metal, far below, slow as breathing. He noted it. He did not slow down. The long way had a few of these, and they were not the kind of thing you investigated.

[Beat 6]
Tonight the work was a twelve-year-old's hands.

He kept arriving back at them. Cupped, steady, holding three teeth that were still warm from a dead jaw rig — and then the scream, high and thin, bouncing off every hard surface in the dock and coming back changed. She had screamed and her fingers had closed anyway. That was the part. Not the chrome on the floor, not the boss's hand going dark around its katana. A child who screamed at what he handed her and did not drop it, because it was her father's, and she had already done the math about which things you get to keep.

He could still feel them in his palm. Three small weights, body-warm, lighter than the rounds that had paid for them.

The work was not the loading dock. The work was carrying that home without having to put it down.

[Beat 7]
He did not put it down. He carried it. He rode.

[Beat 8]
The bike found the Damen lift at 22:01. On the way in he caught a thread of open comm traffic bleeding off the freight band — somebody asking after Stash, somebody else saying Stash had the Spine route locked for the rest of the month and to take a number — the ambient chatter of a half-dozen freelancers working the same stretch of city Kyle worked, none of them his problem tonight.

A courier was already at the lift gate when he rolled up, one boot on the rail, her breath still running ahead of her. Sponsorship legs — the full rebuild, hips down, better hardware than anything else she was wearing — and the chromatophore panels in her calves were finishing an ad rotation her stride had triggered somewhere back down the strut: fifteen seconds of amber liquor pouring itself for an audience of rain. She watched the lift numbers and not her own legs, with the practiced neutrality of someone keeping count of a number much larger than eighteen floors.

"CONDUCTOR's running them slow tonight," she said, to the numbers, the way people talked about rain.

Kyle made the sound you make when the weather is somebody's fault.

Under both their boots the Pulse came up through the gate-plate, deep and even, and when a slug passed the line the platform settled a quarter inch and lifted again, the courier's boot riding the rail down and up without her looking at it. Kyle gave her the nod the trade gives the trade. She returned it and went back to the numbers.

He took the lift up to the eighteenth-floor surface (The Pivot's tier — somebody had hand-painted PIVOT in matte black on the lift's interior at some point in the building's earlier life and the corponation maintenance crew had never quite gotten around to scrubbing it). The doors opened on rain that had been waiting for him at this elevation specifically, a heavier rain than the strut had been throwing, the kind that hit the lift's threshold in flat percussion before he had even rolled the bike out onto the deck.

[Beat 9]
He rolled the bike out anyway. He walked it the last hundred and thirty feet to the alley behind The Pivot. Habit. The bike could ride that distance under its own power and Kyle was the only one who would know he had walked it instead, but the habit was the habit, and the habit was that you arrived home the way you wanted the room to know you were arriving. Quietly. With your weight off the engine. Without telling anyone above the third floor that anyone had come back.

[Beat 10]
The Pivot was a converted insurance archive that had stopped being an insurance archive in 2189. The building had a flat brick face and a service alley that ran along its east side and a fire-escape lattice that was older than half the operating buildings in West Town and structurally sounder than most of them. The window on the second floor at the back of the alley belonged to apartment 2E. The light was on. The kind of soft yellow light that came from the workbench lamp Pixel kept aimed at her current project. Music inside, very faint through the building's old skin, something with strings.

[Beat 11]
He kicked the stand down. He locked the wheel hub. He covered the plate. He walked the bike through the back service door and into the freight elevator, and the elevator's gate had not finished clattering on the second floor when Pixel's door opened.

[Beat 12]
Her window looked down the alley. The workbench lamp was on, the bench faced the glass, and a man walking a matte-black motorcycle past her sightline at 22:40 was not a mystery she needed instruments for. He had stopped trying to sneak in around the third month.

[Beat 13]
"Get in here."

[Beat 14]
She had said it before she saw the back of his shirt — before he was properly off the elevator. Not the bike; the bike only told her he was home. Him. The stride coming down the hall a half-step short. The left arm carried a few degrees off his side. The turn toward her door taken with the whole body instead of the waist, the way a man turns when his lower back has stopped cooperating. Four years of calibrating his hardware had given her his baselines, and tonight he was off all of them.

[Beat 15]
He went in.

[Beat 16]
Apartment 2E was three rooms and a fire escape: a main room with the workbench under the window, a galley kitchen, a small bedroom, and a bathroom that had been retrofitted by someone who had not entirely thought through the plumbing run. The main room was where Pixel worked. The kitchen was where Pixel fed people, including Kyle, on the rare occasions she could trick him into being fed. Tonight the kitchen was where she did her field-medic work, because the kitchen table was the only flat surface in the apartment large enough to lay a man across, and the kitchen had the best light.

[Beat 17]
She pointed at the chair. He sat. She put on gloves. She turned the music down, not off — Pixel did not work in silence, Pixel worked with the music doing the thinking she did not need to do consciously — and she came around behind him.

[Beat 18]
"Coat off, shirt up."

[Beat 19]
He took the coat off and laid it across the next chair. The coat was wet. The kitchen floor did not care. He took the shirt off entirely because he had learned, over four years of these visits, that *shirt up* and *shirt off* produced the same result when Pixel was the one doing the work and the shirt off was thirty seconds less negotiation.

[Beat 20]
She let out one long breath through her nose. Not commentary. Inventory. "Three?"

[Beat 21]
"Two for sure. The third might be a graze, might be in there."

[Beat 22]
"Lie down."

[Beat 23]
He lay down on the table. The table was warm because Pixel had been working at it before he arrived; her current project was scattered along its far edge, a half-disassembled neural reader pulled apart in the specific organized disorder of someone who intended to return to it. The wood was old. Pre-2200 oak, oiled to a depth that nothing the building's atmosphere did to it could ruin. Kyle had bled on this table before. The table had not held the stains.

[Beat 24]
She got out the forceps.

[Beat 25]
The forceps were the long pair. She had four pairs of forceps; the long pair was the pair she used for Kyle specifically, because Kyle's musculature ran lean enough that the entry track on most rounds was deeper than a normal-bodied retrieval, and the long forceps reached. The short pair was for the regular clientele Kyle did not ask about. Kyle had stopped asking about Pixel's regular clientele years ago, around the time he had stopped asking why she lived across the hall from him. He liked having her there. He left it alone.

[Beat 26]
She started talking. This was always the way. Pixel did not work in silence on a person; she narrated, low, the running commentary doing the work of telling him she was there and the work was happening and the next ten minutes had a person at both ends of it.

[Beat 27]
"Upstairs bird's been making sounds again. Three this morning. Same minor key, three different intervals, I think it's learning the building's HVAC."

[Beat 28]
"Yeah."

[Beat 29]
"There's a new noodle stand opened on Damen. Five blocks west of Chen's. I went past it on Saturday. They have a poster up, full corponation logo, and they are calling themselves *Damen Authentic* in three languages, and I do not need to tell you that an authentic anything that has a corponation logo printed on the poster is not authentic in any of the three languages."

[Beat 30]
"No."

[Beat 31]
"I think they are trying to do to Chen what they did to the old man on Ashland."

[Beat 32]
"They will fail."

[Beat 33]
"I think they will, but they will hurt her some first. You should know."

[Beat 34]
"I do now."

[Beat 35]
He felt the first fragment come out. She had it. She held it up where he would not see it directly but where his peripheral vision could register that something had been removed, because she had learned years ago that he hated the moment more if he did not have a visual confirmation that the procedure had progressed. The fragment hit the small metal dish at the edge of the table with the small bright tick of metal on metal he had heard a dozen times at this table.

[Beat 36]
Her palm settled flat between his shoulder blades for a moment — not procedure, calibration habit, the way she checked a machine's temperature before trusting its readouts.

"Your array's running warm," she said. "Warmer than a fight explains. Like it's been busy after hours."

"It bills me in calories. Maybe it's doing inventory."

"Mm." She didn't move her hand right away. "Tell it the shop's closed."

The hand lifted. The work went on.

[Beat 37]
"There's a song I like," she said, sliding the forceps in for the second one. "I'll play it for you. After. Not during. During is yours."

[Beat 38]
"Mm."

[Beat 39]
He let the array route the pain. Not off — the facility had been clear that fully partitioning pain was a long-term injury — but down, attenuated, the channel volume dropped to a setting where it was information rather than emergency. The trick was old. The trick was the trick the facility had built him to do. He used it the way other men used coffee.

[Beat 40]
She got the second one.

[Beat 41]
"Damen's bird theory," she said, transitioning without seam to a topic she had been waiting to get to all evening, "is that the building's HVAC plays it specific tones at specific times of day depending on which apartments are running their kettles. The bird memorizes the tones. The bird then sings the tones back when the tones are not playing, which is, if you really think about it, the bird trying to negotiate with the building. The bird is trying to bring the kettle back."

[Beat 42]
"That is a theory."

[Beat 43]
"I think the bird is smarter than the building. I think the building's HVAC is not actually responding to the kettles, but the bird thinks it is, and the bird is doing field experiments. There are worse epistemological projects." She moved the lamp. "Hold still."

[Beat 44]
He held still. She found the third fragment. The third was the graze — half-buried, the depth ambiguous until she had it under the light. She took it out clean. The fragment hit the dish. Three.

[Beat 45]
"Done."

[Beat 46]
She set the forceps down. She removed the gloves. She put a strip of biotape over each entry, slowly, with the patience she saved for things that mattered — the project on her bench, the bird, Kyle's back — and then she stepped back and washed her hands at the sink for longer than the procedure had required.

[Beat 47]
"Hey." She came around to where he could see her without turning. The gloves were off. "Whatever it was tonight — it followed you up the stairs. It's sitting in your shoulders."

"It's fine."

"I didn't ask if it was fine." She let that stand exactly as long as it needed to. "Drink the tea when it comes. Both hands."

It was the closest they ever came to the other conversation.

[Beat 48]
He sat up.

[Beat 49]
She was at the counter when he turned around. She had a kettle going. She had been heating it the entire time, he realized, timed, without announcement, to be ready exactly when the procedure ended. She poured two cups. She handed him one. The handle was on the side without the chip.

[Beat 50]
"Drink it slow."

[Beat 51]
He drank it slow.

[Beat 52]
He left her apartment at 23:47. He did not feel the time pass. The kitchen had been warm and the tea had been hot and Pixel had played the song she had mentioned — a thing he did not recognize, sung in a language he did not speak — and she had not said anything for the last six minutes of the song because the song had been doing what it needed to do without help.

[Beat 53]
He crossed the hall to 2F. He unlocked the door. He set Silence on the rack inside the entryway and Cacophony next to it, the holster open, the cylinder still loaded with all five rounds he had not fired at the dock. The bird's-head grip caught the light from his apartment's one functional ceiling fixture and threw a small red glint off the crosshatch texture, the only color in the room besides the matte black of everything else Kyle had brought home in his life.

[Beat 54]
He did not turn the light off. He did not undress. He went into the bedroom and lay down on the bed in the clothes he had been wearing since the apartment with the camphor smell, and he reached for Silence on the rack across the room without standing up, because the rack was where Silence lived but the bed was where he lived, and he did not leave that gap open on nights like this one.

[Beat 55]
He brought Silence to his chest. He held the saya across his sternum. The blade was steel. It had no opinion about whose chest it lay across or why. He held it there anyway — through the camphor smell, through the daughter's hand closing around the teeth, through Mrs. Chen's offer of forever — and the cold and the weight stayed put when nothing else did. That was enough.

[Beat 56]
He sat up. He felt the three biotape strips on his back move with his shoulder blades. He felt the cut on his left forearm where the boss's follow-through had grazed him — a thin line, scabbed clean, no infection signal from the subdermal mesh. He felt the array come up from its overnight low-power state and find him in the room and start cataloguing things that did not need to be catalogued. He let it.

[Beat 57]
He stood. He put Silence back on the rack. He put a kettle on. He looked out the bedroom window at the alley below, which was full of the same rain it had been full of when he had come up the night before, the alley itself unchanged in any of the ways that mattered. On the transit ledge across the gap a dozen pigeons sat hunched and grey against the wet, and among them one bird was lacquer orange and white and black, jewel-bright and alone in the row, koi-marked the way nothing in the row had any business being. The bike was where he had left it. The bird from upstairs was, as Pixel had reported, singing — a low minor-key thing Kyle had now heard with his own ears for the first time, three different intervals, three different tries at coaxing something out of the building's HVAC. The bird's epistemological project was ongoing. Kyle could respect that.

[Beat 58]
The kettle clicked. He poured. He held the cup in both hands and stood in his kitchen and did the thing he did most mornings, which was sit with the awareness that he was alive and uninjured beyond the catalogued, and that the day in front of him was going to be its own day, and that the work he did would not save the world and was not trying to, and it was still his to do.

[Beat 59]
He drank the tea.

[Beat 60]
The phone in his coat — left on the chair across the kitchen — chimed once. He set the cup down. He walked over and read the message. Sable's pipeline. A new contract had landed. The terms read clean. The pay read fair. On the surface it was a normal job, the kind the pipeline had been delivering for two months without giving him a reason to question it.

[Beat 61]
The bird upstairs sang again. The HVAC did not answer. The bird tried a different interval.

[Beat 62]
Kyle filed the contract for later. He finished the tea. He went to put on a clean shirt.

[Beat 63]
The day was going to be its own day. He had no idea, walking across his apartment to the closet, what the day was going to be. He did not know that he would be at Mrs. Chen's by 19:30 that evening for the second bowl of his usual two. He did not know that a hooded woman would be at the counter three stools down, watching him pretend not to watch her, until she tilted her head *come*. He did not know that the contract on his phone had been routed to him for reasons the contract's own metadata could not legibly describe, by a pipeline he trusted because the pipeline had not yet given him a reason not to. He did not know that *not yet* was about to become *eleven hours ago*, in a shielded room he had not yet agreed to walk into.

[Beat 64]
He did not know any of that. He buttoned the shirt. He put the cup in the sink. He locked Silence into its saya and Cacophony into its holster. He shrugged the coat on, the one that had been wet last night and was now dry from sitting against the radiator, and walked out of his apartment into the day the day had become. Down on the corner where the alley met the freight track a brown dog crossed the street, unhurried, at the same minute it crossed it every day, the same line it had walked for years, gone around the corner before the light at the crossing had decided anything about it.

[Beat 65]
Pixel's door was closed. The hallway smelled the way hallways smelled. The bird upstairs was still working on the kettle.

[Beat 66]
He took the long way.
