# Black Spot

*Seto's model has a hole in it. He finds what fills the hole. Then Amara does.*

[Beat 1]

The model he had been running for three weeks had a hole in it the size of a door.

He knew where it was. It showed up in the aggregate as a smoothness that wasn't real, the way water lies flat over a submerged ledge. The aggregate told him how many neuretics clients had registered suppression events in the corridors he'd been mapping, and when. It didn't tell him why the timing clustered the way it did — not quite random, not quite patterned. He'd marked the model incomplete and sent Amara the underlying geometry anyway. The geometry was real even with the explanation missing.

He had the explanation now. He just wasn't ready to say what it meant.

Eleven days of pulling IA scheduling data — the Integrated Assessment logs every licensed neuretics client generates, timestamped by the calibration authority each time they come in for maintenance. Public the way everything in the gray zones was technically public: if you knew the right relays, paid the access fees, waited out the response cycles. He'd done all three.

Forty-seven facilities' worth of scheduling windows. The hole was gone.

Outside the window the relay tower annex ran its amber service indicator on a forty-five-second cycle. Below, the Zone 7 transit corridor was working — freight crews changing shifts at the tower's base, night relay operators arriving with thermoses, the canteen two doors down already venting frying onions into air that nobody filtered here, because here was the Seam. The gap between two arcologies where neither corp ran edge management and you got the actual city instead of a managed approximation of it. Somewhere west, Orison's atmospheric perimeter pushed mediated air into its own jurisdiction — the arcology-state that licensed Cellvault's neuretics across the eastern Seam corridor, the interior where the corridors smelled of synthesized cedar and the windows never showed weather. Here the air tasted of exhaust particulate and cooking fat and the faint ionized sting off the relay mast, and that was what outside smelled like when it was real.

He'd been at the numbers since before the relay operators came on shift. The githeri on the burner had gone cold at dawn.

The suppression events — his name for the gaps in information flow, the contacts who should have pattern-routed a relay and didn't, the dead corridors — were not correlated with the aftermath of calibration visits. Not residue. Not delayed effect.

*During.*

During the visits.

He sat with that, then opened the model and rebuilt the timing layer from scratch. The mast's amber indicator cycled. Down in the corridor a freight worker shouted something at the canteen window and got laughter back.

He was aware of Amara at the edges of his working attention the way he was aware of the weather through a wall — not from any signal she sent, but as a change in the pressure he worked inside. Her density was specific: compressed, careful, the set of someone carrying something they don't want to spill. He checked without deciding to check, a habit three weeks old that he had not named, because naming it meant deciding what it was. The sealed system had been the condition that let him keep operational distance, the distance that kept clients safe. The sealed system no longer fully held. He could tell when she was afraid. He could tell when she was sitting with something settled versus working it through. He hadn't signed up to know this about anyone, and she hadn't signed up to give it. The channel ran both directions and neither of them had built a door.

Today the texture was narrower. The specific quality of someone who had found the edge of a thing and was deciding whether to look over it.

He noted it and went back to the model.

The revised timing layer loaded clean. He ran it against the silence geography — the corridor map built from eleven years of route intuition and eight months of deliberate tracking — and against the facility corridor data he'd pulled in month three, when he'd started to understand the silence wasn't random but organized around specific infrastructure nodes.

Three layers. He had never had three layers that matched before.

The crescent they'd confirmed in week six — him from the outside, Amara from inside the tissue records, both arriving at the same curve by different routes — sat in the facility corridor layer. He had the facilities. He had the corridors. He had the windows. The windows matched.

He was looking at that when she sent the data set.

Not the batch documentation. Something smaller — a protocol document nested inside the Orison-accessible tier of a Cellvault record system she should not technically have been able to reach. He didn't ask how she'd gotten there. She was a neuretics assessor. She had access he didn't, and she was using it.

*I found what the visits are for.*

He read the protocol.

Labeled *Secondary Calibration*, under a project code he recognized — she'd named it in week four without a definition, the way you name a thing you don't yet have a handle on. The code sat in the document's header like a clearance flag.

The procedure ran on associative memory integration nodes. Not the memories — he read that line twice, because the distinction mattered more than almost anything else he had read in three months — not the memories themselves, but the way they were fitted together. The architecture under the content. He had a picture of it: a filing system where every folder is intact and every document present, but the index cards that tell you which folder holds what have been pulled. The thing is still there. You just can't find it from where you used to stand.

The procedure went in through the maintenance interface — the same interface every licensed calibration specialist used on every visit — and applied a targeted inhibition pattern to one cluster of associative nodes. Same cluster. Every visit. Every client.

The same cluster Amara had flagged in her Type C drift cases.

He set the protocol in a window beside the model. Morning light came in sideways off the building across the alley. He added it as a third layer over the silence geography and the facility corridor map.

The crescent was gone. What lay under it was not a crescent.

It was a plan. The geography was not the shape of the intervention — it was the delivery logistics. Where the facilities were, where the workforce was, the service-sector infrastructure of the GLMZ: the people who moved things and maintained things and calibrated things and needed licensed neuretics to do jobs that required licensed neuretics. People who came in on schedule because the license required it, because the calibration window was the price of access. Orison ran the maintenance schedule. Orison ran the facilities. The visit was the delivery mechanism. The maintenance was the procedure.

He could see it whole for the first time. Not a crescent, not a cluster of anomalies. A program.

Running right now, through the maintenance infrastructure of the gray zones, at a scale he had not let himself put a number to. Putting a number to it meant committing to the number.

He let himself put a number to it.

The model took nine minutes. He watched it run and listened to the mast tick through its amber cycles. Down in the corridor the shift change had finished; the freight crews were gone and the Seam had dropped into its midmorning register, quieter, the canteen vents still pushing onion smell into the unfiltered air. He had been at this table since before any of it started.

The model resolved.

He looked at the number.

The affected-population estimate he'd been working with — several hundred, inside eighteen months, if the rate held — had been built on pre-procedure data. Before the timing windows. Before he understood the events were concurrent, not consequential. He had been modeling a leak. Working backward from damage.

The procedure data changed the model. He wasn't modeling a leak.

He was modeling a maintenance schedule.

The number was in the hundreds. Not *will be*. Not *projected*. Current. Now. Given the scheduling windows, the geographic scope, the cluster target confirmed by both his route data and Amara's tissue records.

His shoulders had come up. He noticed it and did not lower them.

Through the ambient, Amara went still.

He hadn't sent her the number. He didn't know whether she could read him that clearly at this distance or whether she'd run her own version of the calculation. Probably the second. She was someone who ran calculations. This was the stillness he had learned meant she was holding something steady — not falling, holding it.

Then his right hand closed on nothing.

The memory came up through the wrist the way it always did now: cool aluminum under his palm, a bearing housing seating to a tolerance he had never machined, the small resistance just before the seat found true and then the clean run of the rotation. The satisfaction of work done right, registering in a body that hadn't done the work. Her hands. Her bench, some afternoon, some facility in the eastern corridor he had never set foot in. It arrived whole and uninvited. He didn't pull back from it. Then it left the way it came, and his own hand lay open on the table again, fingers half-curled around the shape of a tool that wasn't there.

He looked at it. His hand. The number on the screen. Neither had changed.

He stayed present in the ambient without sending anything — a thing he had worked out over three weeks with no precedent and no model for it. Presence as texture rather than content. What she needed in these moments was not information.

It was a long quiet.

She sent: *The four clients in my log. I know which facility they were calibrated at.*

He knew the answer before she gave it. The facility would be in the crescent, because all of them were — that was what the crescent was, the delivery network, the thing that had looked like geographic coincidence before he had the protocol.

She sent the facility identifier.

It was in the crescent.

He sent: *How many people in Cellvault's rotation had maintenance at that facility.*

The response took four minutes. He waited the way he waited on routes — without impatience, because impatience was noise and noise cost.

She sent the number.

One facility. One rotation. Dozens. He had mapped at least nineteen facilities in the crescent.

He did not do the multiplication aloud.

Then: *I need to know if I'm on the list.*

He didn't answer at once — not because he was weighing what to tell her, but because he didn't know yet. He needed to look first.

He pulled up everything he could reach on batch 44-C. The project code from the protocol. The scheduling windows. The facility corridor that overlapped the crescent's eastern arc. He pulled his own route data from that corridor in months five and six, back when he was still only mapping silence.

Here was where the decision sat. He could look and not tell her what he found. He could send her the raw data and let her read it herself. He could close the inquiry entirely — mark it operational risk and stop. Every option was open. He had promised her nothing.

He pulled the batch 44-C scheduling manifest and started cross-referencing facility assignments.

He had promised her nothing, and he had started looking anyway. That was a decision, and he let it be one.

The mast outside cycled through amber. Seam air moved through the window he'd cracked at dawn, still carrying onions, still carrying exhaust, still carrying the faint ionized bite off the relay. The model sat open on the screen. He read down the list of names and facility codes, looking for hers.

When he found it, he did not move for a long time. Then he reached for the ambient.
