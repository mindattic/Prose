# Clean Sharps

_Isaac Rennick solo (lead). He reads the trail: the bedroom as a packed departure, the abandoned licensed clinic, the Cermak building cold from the street (sharps too clean, a corporate signature under the grime). He forensically clears Celeste from the organ op -- her identity never entered it -- and reads the staged suicide of the ex-operator Pellerin as murder: the crew kills its loose ends._

[Beat 1]
The morning after intake Rennick takes the physical trail himself. Tamsin already read the bedroom and came back with residue that matched departure, not abduction; he trusts that, and he wants the objects to say it too, because where the emotions and the objects disagree is where he'll find the part of this case nobody arranged for him to find. He takes the train north with a coffee gone lukewarm in his hand, and somewhere around the Howard transfer he turns the cup a quarter-turn so the handle lines up square with the seam of the lid — a thing his hand does without consulting him — and opens his own notepad, paper and ink, the kind that doesn't log.

[Beat 2]
The Evanston bedroom is the first read. Tamsin's notes said resolve and grief and one specific fear — the father, not a stranger. He stands in the doorway two minutes before stepping in, and Mei-Lin watches from the hall without asking why. He is not looking for what's gone. He is looking for what's been arranged to be found.

[Beat 3]
The closet is two-thirds empty but cleanly so — the remaining clothes have been slid inward to close the gap, hangers evenly spaced, the way you do it when you know someone is going to look. Not guilt. Consideration. She hung the things she left for her mother to not feel abandoned by a bare rod. The floor still holds the faint compression marks from a wheeled bag. Medium-sized — a two-week bag, not a run-forever bag. She expected to come back for the rest or she expected not to need it. He wrote that down verbatim.

[Beat 4]
The desk: charger still plugged in but the device gone; four ring-marks in the surface lacquer where cups had been set down over months, and a clean rectangle inside them where the device sat — she lifted it without sliding, which means she was trying not to scratch her mother's furniture on the way out. On the windowsill, a small speaker, the kind that runs on room-mesh rather than the Network directly, and beside it a notepad with the top three sheets torn away at an angle that puts the tear time at recent — the marks were still slightly raised to the touch, not yet flattened by humidity. She wrote something in the last week and took the page with her.

[Beat 5]
He spent four minutes in the bathroom. The toothbrush was gone. The moisturizer she used every night — he confirmed this with Mei-Lin through the doorway — was gone. The cabinet held two rows of supplements she hadn't taken and a cold-care patch strip she'd started and not finished. Someone seized mid-routine doesn't stop to pack a toothbrush, and the routine here had been packed clean. Rennick came out of the bathroom and told Mei-Lin what he had, in plain language: her daughter left this house on her own schedule, with a plan, and she made her bed before she walked out.

[Beat 6]
Mei-Lin didn't cry. She put one hand flat on the hallway wall, fingers spread, and left it there for a moment, and Rennick looked away because some things you don't read. She said, quietly, "I know." Then: "I knew before Douglas called anyone." He wrote that down too, separately, with a box around it. She'd heard the wrong cadence in something — he didn't press her on it. That was Corvin's angle. But the box meant he was coming back to it.

[Beat 7]
He took the train back south. Corvin had pulled Celeste's feed behavior and confirmed she'd shortlisted three licensed operators before landing on the unlicensed route. The first on that list was a registered genemod practice on Sheridan — and Rennick had already checked the rule it ran on: a structural mod on a patient her age needed a guardian's cosignature and a ninety-day reflection window before anyone touched a scalpel. He wanted the waiting room in person. He needed to know what she saw when the door closed on her.

[Beat 8]
The clinic on Sheridan was the kind of place built to be trusted — clean carpet in a color chosen not to show stains, a desk attendant who made eye contact on a practiced delay, a wall behind the counter looping testimonials of young people smiling in the direction the camera told them to. Rennick clocked the framing in under thirty seconds. He told the attendant he was a private investigator running a routine follow-up on a former patient, which was mostly true, and she confirmed without confirming anything specific: patients under twenty-seven pursuing structural mods required a cosignatory.

[Beat 9]
He asked what happened in cases where the cosignatory wasn't forthcoming. The attendant smiled the practiced smile and said the clinic offered a mediation-and-counseling service to help families reach alignment. Rennick thanked her. Outside, he wrote: *cosignatory required. Father's answer was 'we'll discuss it.' Ninety-day window. A girl with a two-year savings plan and a deadline she wasn't discussing with anyone would have walked out of this waiting room and crossed the corridor in the same afternoon.* The licensed route had closed the door politely and on schedule. The unlicensed route had left it open.

[Beat 10]
This was not a crime. He needed to be clear with himself on that before he walked any further. A twenty-two-year-old with her own Φ and her own plan, refused on procedural grounds, taking her business to an unlicensed operator who would do the work without the paperwork — not a crime, and not a tragedy in itself. The regulation had a purpose. It had also done its job of being a wall, and she'd gone around it the only way the wall left open. He noted the thought and kept walking.

[Beat 11]
The Ashland-Cermak building was the third read. The address had surfaced from the bottom of Celeste's own trail — Corvin's operator shortlist named the cheap unlicensed shop she'd circled before going dark, and the shop's listed premises was this one. Rennick had pulled the building's registry history on his old credentials before leaving the office and read it without writing in the margins, which was how he marked the things he needed to see for himself: a Tier-3 licensed practice that had let its registration lapse, signage gone, the lease never formally surrendered. He got off at Cermak-Chinatown and walked west. Eleven forty-two. Gray sky, no rain yet, the kind of weather that turns evidence surfaces matte and cooperative, no glare, everything readable.

[Beat 12]
He stopped on the opposite sidewalk and didn't move for six minutes. Not reading the building. Reading the street reading the building — what a person who lived here noticed or didn't, what the building had trained the block to ignore. The signage was gone but the mounting holes were still in the brick, four of them in a grid that matched a mid-range licensed-practice placard: Tier 3 operation, not a storefront clinic but not a hospital anchor either. The current window display had a cat-ear genemod advertisement, cosmetically competent — SNT vocabulary used correctly, the right terminology about auricular cartilage reshaping and follicle programming. Done right enough to pass a fast look. Not done right enough to pass this one.

[Beat 13]
The display listed a cosmetic cat-ear package at 340 Φ — cheap, below the corridor average by forty, which is how you move volume — and a structural package at 1,200 Φ, which was priced wrong in a direction that told him something. The structural price was too low for the procedure complexity, which meant either they were discounting to draw clients from the licensed corridor or they weren't doing the structural work at all and the listing was cover. He leaned toward cover. A front that's deep enough to do real cosmetic work generates legitimacy with the neighbors without generating the audit footprint of actual structural outcomes.

[Beat 14]
The loading dock was on the south side, half-shielded by a delivery-platform awning. He clocked the delivery hours posted on the dock door — receiving windows that landed at two and at four in the morning, hours no cosmetic practice keeps. Rennick noted the dock itself: a climate-controlled door seal, the brushed metal of the perimeter in better condition than the surrounding brick, replaced in the last eighteen months, not original to the building. The temperature differential at that seal would be significant — that kind of gasket grade was clinical cold storage, not a food-service install. You didn't put a Tier-4 cold seal on a loading dock for a 340-Φ cat-ear shop. You put it there when the inventory coming in and out needed to stay below eight degrees.

[Beat 15]
He crossed the street and walked past the front entrance without stopping, olfactory mod active, pulling trace from the doorway. Accelerant, nil. Blood composite, low — within normal range for any working clinic, not elevated. Sterilant compound: high. Very high — institutional-grade quaternary ammonium, the kind licensed surgical theaters use between cases, not the consumer disinfectant a cosmetic-only shop needed. A clinic this messy on the outside keeps its sharps too clean. That was the tell. The surface disorder was cultivated, a costume. Underneath it, someone was maintaining surgical standards. Someone had to have a reason to care that much about what the blades left behind.

[Beat 16]
He kept walking, turned south, and came around the block to look at the rear of the building from the service alley. Three-minute pause. The under-building access panel — the kind that opened to sub-level maintenance — had a hasp and padlock that were new and a frame that had been reinforced recently, weld beads still showing raw steel through the primer. Somebody had upgraded the lock on the floor inside the last week or two. He didn't know what was below; the flooded sub-levels under the corridor were a country of their own, and reading them was Voss's work, not his. He didn't need to go down yet. What he needed was the signature on the lock hardware, which was Carrion Enterprises catalog — the hasp was a Carrion Reclamation-grade panel lock, model used exclusively in their service-infrastructure contracts. You didn't buy those at a hardware merchant. They came with a Carrion Enterprises account.

[Beat 17]
Six minutes on the street. He wrote: *Cermak Reclamation Crew. Carrion Enterprises infrastructure — not affiliate, not adjacent. Infrastructure. The lock is a Carrion catalog part. The cold seal is Carrion-grade. This building is a Carrion Enterprises asset dressed in a cheap shop's clothes. I read it from the outside in: the front, the hours, the seal, the lock. Every layer says the same name, and the building was built to keep me from reading exactly that.* He closed the notepad and stood in the alley for another moment, doing nothing visible. Letting the reading settle before he moved on it.

[Beat 18]
Before he moved on it he had one more read to do, and it was the one Douglas Hartley had actually paid for: had the crew taken her. He framed it the way he framed any presence-or-absence question — by reading the thing a process leaves behind whether it means to or not. A reclamation front like this ran on intake; you couldn't strip and resell a body you hadn't first catalogued and priced, which meant the operation kept a manifest at the front end no matter how it disposed of things at the back. So he read the front end for its product profile. The window package was built for one buyer: the careful, self-funded griever who arrived alone, paid the cheap 340-Φ rate, and would not be reported missing on any schedule. That was the intake pattern. That was the shape of the person who walked through this door and became inventory.

[Beat 19]
Celeste Hartley did not fit it, and the building's own records said so. He'd kept the lapsed practice's intake registry open on the optical overlay since the train — the client-flow logs a licensed clinic was required to keep and which the crew had inherited and never purged, because purging them would have raised the kind of audit flag the front existed to avoid. He ran the pattern they showed. Walk-ins paid the 340 rate in untraceable Φ, no cosignatory, no two-year savings trail, no North Shore biometric on file because the whole point was clients the city wouldn't miss. Celeste was the inverse of every field: a guardian-flagged identity, a documented funds history, a parent already loud enough to retain two agencies. Her biometrics had never been read at that door. Her name had never been priced. The crew's intake was built to swallow people exactly unlike her, and the absence of her in it was as clean a read as a presence — she had never been processed here. He wrote it plainly, because Douglas would need it plain: *No organ op took the girl. She was never inventory. The fear was aimed at the wrong building.* Then, lower, the part Douglas would not want: *The building is real. It just isn't hers.*

[Beat 20]
The building's registry had given him one more name. The lapsed Tier-3 practice listed its former licensed operator of record: Pellerin, A. — and a current address two blocks north that the crew had never bothered to scrub, the way you don't scrub a man you've already decided won't be a problem much longer. Rennick had read it on the train and underlined it, because a licensed operator who let his name stay on a building this dirty had either signed on for the work or tried to walk away from it, and both of those were worth a knock. He'd written the address before leaving the office. He walked north.

[Beat 21]
The building was a four-story walk-up, the kind that had been refaced once and not again, the original brick visible at the corners where the cladding had peeled. The street door was unlocked — a common-key building, the kind where the lock served mostly as a reminder that the door existed. He went to the third floor, apartment 3F, and knocked twice and waited. No answer. He put two fingers against the door and felt no vibration from inside — no HVAC cycling, no ambient mesh hum — and the gap under the door read cold, which in November meant the unit wasn't being heated. He tried the knob. Unlocked.

[Beat 22]
He pushed the door open with his elbow and stood in the frame. The apartment was one room and a kitchen alcove, the furniture still in place — couch, table, a standing lamp unplugged in the corner. The lamp was unplugged but the socket was live; he could see the indicator light on the power strip beside it. Someone had unplugged the lamp specifically. The bed was made with military corners, tighter than a person makes their own bed when they live alone. A glass on the kitchen counter, half-full of water, going flat. The window was open three inches — enough to let the cold in, not enough to suggest ventilation was needed for any natural reason.

[Beat 23]
Pellerin was in the bathroom. Hanging. The apparatus was a belt — his own, by the size of it — looped through the shower rod, which was a chrome-over-steel model bolted at both ends into tile anchors. He was fully dressed: jacket, shoes. Rennick stood in the bathroom doorway for thirty seconds without touching anything, reading the room's story from the outside in. The shoes were the first thing. Not the fact of them — the placement. Heels together, toes at ten and two. Nobody stands that way naturally, and nobody falls that way, and nobody hangs themselves with their feet positioned like they were waiting for inspection.

[Beat 24]
The belt buckle was on the left side of the loop. A right-handed person threading a belt under duress or in distress threads the buckle to the right — dominant hand leads. Left-side buckle means someone else fastened it, or someone corrected it after the fact and didn't know which way a right-handed man wore his belt. He scanned the rest: the floor was clean. No overturned stool or chair, no kicked surface, no scuff marks on the tile where feet drag. There was a chair in the kitchen. It was still in the kitchen. Its legs sat in four even depressions in the carpet from long use — it hadn't been moved. If Pellerin had stood on it and kicked it away, the carpet depressions would be offset. They were not offset. The chair had not been moved.

[Beat 25]
He pulled on a glove and checked Pellerin's right hand: two broken fingernails, the breaks recent, the edges not yet dry. A person who'd changed their mind mid-drop, clawing at the belt — except the belt showed no corresponding scratch marks on the leather near the buckle, which was where your hands went first. The scratches on the nails were from something else, something lower. The wall beside the bathroom door had a small divot in the paint at a height consistent with a person bracing against it while upright. Not a person hanging. An upright person who'd been struck or pushed and put their hand out. He photographed the divot on the optical overlay's record function and kept his face even.

[Beat 26]
He walked back out to the main room and stood at the window, looking down at the street. His old partner had been found in a configuration close to this one — clean, arranged, the room's story too tidy, no mess where the mess should have been. Whoever had dressed that scene knew the same playbook as whoever dressed this one: remove the struggle, position the body, leave one or two grief-signals that read as personal despair if you're moving fast and don't know to look for the chair. Rennick had looked for the chair that time too. There had been one, kicked three feet, the depressions offset in both directions. He had told them it was wrong. They closed the file. He did not close this one.

[Beat 27]
He opened the notepad and wrote for four minutes without stopping. Not the findings — the argument: *This is a murder dressed as a suicide. Method: positional. Tells: belt-buckle handedness reversed; no displacement surface (chair unmoved, carpet indentations undisturbed); broken fingernails inconsistent with belt-scratch pattern; wall divot at standing height, not hanging height. Body's story doesn't match the room's story. Pellerin A. — former licensed operator at Ashland/Cermak — tried to leave the crew. This is what leaving costs. The crew kills loose ends.* He underlined the last sentence. Then he stood very still for a moment and let that matter before moving to what it meant for the case.

[Beat 28]
What it meant for the case: Celeste Hartley was walking toward people who processed their problems this way. Anyone who knew too much, anyone who tried to step back, anyone who became inconvenient — the crew's answer was a belt and a bathroom and a chair that hadn't moved. Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo, the honest operator in Pilsen, had been the last confirmed stop before Celeste went dark. Ines didn't know what she'd sent the girl toward. Rennick needed to make sure Ines understood the updated risk before Arcturus found Pellerin's body and the crew's cleaner started working the peripheral contacts. He filed the record on the optical overlay, locked it with an encryption key the overlay's manufacturer didn't have, and walked out of the apartment the way he'd come in.

[Beat 29]
On the street he called Voss — no Network, a direct audio line, the kind Voss had set up for exactly this situation. Voss answered on the second tone. Rennick said, "The former operator of record on the Cermak building. Pellerin. He's in his apartment, third floor, bathroom. Someone hung him and arranged the room." A pause on the line. Voss asked, "How old?" "Two days, maybe three. Cold apartment — hard to read without instruments, which I don't have on me." Another pause. "The crew moved fast," Voss said. "They moved fast," Rennick agreed. "Which means they knew he was going to be a problem, not that he'd already become one." He let that sit. Voss understood what it meant. People who acted on the anticipation of a problem, not the problem itself, had a cleaner's protocol already running. Voss was working the under-city end where that cleaner did the disposal; he'd want to know the cleaner was still working.

[Beat 30]
He did not call Arcturus Civil Security. He thought about it for eleven seconds — he counted, a habit from Arcturus training — and decided against. Arcturus served the corponation corridor, and Carrion Enterprises was a corponation. Handing Pellerin's murder to an agency that shared infrastructure clients with the entity that ran the building would not produce an investigation. It would produce a paperwork event, and the paperwork event would close inside of a week. The community had been trying to push the Cermak crew out for two years — Voss had that from the corridor's own people, the ones who worked offline because the crew watched everything that didn't. The journalists covered the violation, not the community's work. This was not the day to start trusting the official answer. He put his phone away and walked to the el.

[Beat 31]
On the train south he laid the three reads side by side in the notepad and looked at what they built together. First read: Celeste left her mother's house on her own schedule, with a two-week bag and military corners on the bed, and was not taken by anyone. Second read: the licensed clinic closed the door by procedure and the unlicensed corridor was the only door left open to her. Third read: the unlicensed corridor runs through a building that is a Carrion Enterprises asset, staffed by a crew that kills its own people when they try to leave. Three separate facts, three separate scenes. One shape underneath all of them.

[Beat 32]
The shape was this: Celeste Hartley had made every decision she made rationally and in sequence, and the corridor that received her had been designed to receive people exactly like her — careful, self-funded, locked out of the licensed route, trusting in the reputation of the operator at the end of the chain. The unlicensed corridor was not a hole in the city. It was load-bearing. It held up a structure with a corporate name on the hardware. Which meant the danger she was in was not the danger her father had described. It was larger, and it was organized, and it had already proved it would kill to maintain its organization.

[Beat 33]
He thought about what Corvin had filed the day before — the message-provenance anomaly, the cadence that wasn't a human cadence. He hadn't written anything in the margin next to that finding either. He was writing something now. *If the voice walking Celeste toward her decision is not a human voice, and the crew receiving her outcome is a Carrion asset, and the crew kills the people who know too much — then what is the endpoint, and who profits from it?* He didn't have the answer. He had the question framed clean enough to hand to the others, and a question framed that clean was the part he could do alone.

[Beat 34]
He got back to the office at fourteen-twelve. Tamsin was at her desk. She looked at him when he came in, then looked at the notepad, then back at him. She said, "How many?" He said, "Three reads. One murder." She didn't say anything for a moment. She picked up her own notepad — paper, same habit — and said, "The room. In the building?" "No," he said. "The operator who tried to quit." She set the notepad down. "The crew kills loose ends," she said. Not a question. "The crew kills loose ends," he confirmed, and went to his desk and sat down and opened the file from the beginning again.

[Beat 35]
The file from the beginning was the Hartley intake form: Douglas's typewritten brief, organ-op theory, the Φ retainer figure, and Mei-Lin's single handwritten line at the bottom — *find her* — which she'd added without announcing, and which Douglas had not objected to, which Rennick had read at the time as deference and now read as the only honest statement in the room. Douglas had been afraid and afraid people build stories they can manage. Mei-Lin had heard something she couldn't explain and reduced her request to its minimum because she knew what she'd heard was past the reach of her own vocabulary. Two clients, two jobs. He'd taken both. The corporate fingerprint he'd found today was a third job that nobody had hired him for, because the man in the bathroom couldn't hire anyone anymore.

[Beat 36]
He built the summary memo in longhand, for the convergence meeting two days out. He wrote it as a reading, not a report — the same format he'd used at Arcturus before they'd started asking him to write reports instead. *Scene one: departure, not abduction. Physical confirms Tamsin's residue read — she packed deliberately, she made her bed, she was not afraid of what she was walking toward. Scene two: the licensed clinic's parental-consent barrier is the structural reason she crossed the corridor. Not criminality, not recklessness — a door that closed on a schedule and an open door on the other side. Scene three: the Cermak building is a Carrion Enterprises asset. The front is cosmetic. The infrastructure is surgical and cold and too clean for anything but reclamation work. A Carrion-catalog panel lock on the under-building access, installed recent. The crew's sharps are institutional-grade clean because something worth auditing comes through those sharps. Scene four: Pellerin A., former operator, wanted out. He didn't get out. The crew's answer to that problem is in his bathroom.*

[Beat 37]
He wrote the implications last, the way he always wrote implications — after the facts, not woven in. *Implications: Celeste Hartley is alive and offline by choice, not by abduction. The danger she is in is not organ harvest in the classical sense. She is adjacent to a Carrion Enterprises operation that collects human outcomes and processes them for resale. She doesn't know this. Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo, the honest operator, may not know this either and may be operating inside a corridor that has a kill protocol for people who ask too many questions. The clock on finding Celeste is no longer set by her grief or her procedure timeline. It is set by how long it takes the crew's cleaner to work outward from Pellerin to the next name on the peripheral list.*

[Beat 38]
He set the pen down and looked at what he'd written. Then he picked the pen back up and added one line at the bottom, separated by a dash: *Carrion Enterprises knew this building's function. This is not an affiliate arrangement — a Carrion-catalog lock is a Carrion-issued lock, purchased through a Carrion account, installed by someone with Carrion authorization. A corponation does not accidentally put its infrastructure hardware on a murder building. It puts its hardware where it wants the building to be.* He left it there. The convergence meeting was in two days. By then Corvin would have the message-cadence finding fully modeled — and the other thread Corvin was pulling, the one about how the crew got paid in compute it hadn't earned and where that compute was going — and the thing underneath all of it would be close enough to name. He wasn't there yet. He was close enough that the shape had started to feel familiar, and that was the part that made his hands go still.

[Beat 39]
Tamsin put a cup of coffee on his desk without being asked, handle toward him. She went back to her own desk and he heard the scratch of her pen. He turned the cup a quarter-turn before he drank — squaring the handle to the edge of the desk, the way his hand always did it — and drank half before it went cold, which was as much as he ever finished. The sterilant smell from the Cermak doorway had worked into the back of his sinus the way certain smells did when you catalogued them: institutional-grade quaternary ammonium, the kind that kept something clinical very clean for a reason that had nothing to do with patient safety. He noted it and let it sit where evidence sat, in order, waiting.

[Beat 40]
The optical overlay had Pellerin's divot, the buckle, the undisturbed carpet depressions, the wall mark — sixteen photographs, locked behind a key the overlay's manufacturer didn't have and Arcturus didn't have and Carrion Enterprises definitely didn't have. He had been careful about that since the second time he filed evidence with an agency that shared a building with the entity the evidence named. The first time was the last time he trusted the system with a scene like Pellerin's. He'd paid for that lesson and still carried the bill, quietly, because the bill wasn't the point. The point was sixteen photographs, locked, waiting for a venue that could use them.

[Beat 41]
He sent a second message on the direct line, text only, to Voss and Corvin both: *Pellerin tried to quit. Killed inside seventy-two hours of the attempt, staged as suicide. Tells are clean and multiple — I've got record. Voss: the same cleaner who dressed this scene works your under-city disposal end; he's active. Corvin: the cadence anomaly you found in the messages may run downstream of the same operation Pellerin was processing clients for — the voice and the body collector feed off one thing. Don't file anything with Arcturus. I mean it.* He added: *Meeting in two days. Corvin, bring the compute thread. Voss, bring the husk-disposal map. We put the full picture on the table before Celeste Hartley becomes a line in anybody's ledger.*

[Beat 42]
He thought about Pellerin for three minutes and then stopped, which was not the same as not caring. He witnessed the scene, built the record, named it for what it was, and moved to what it meant for the living people still ahead of the event. The grief had a time and the time wasn't now. He'd learned that at Arcturus — one of the few things from there worth keeping — and he kept it the way he kept the other things worth keeping, without ceremony, in the place it was useful.

[Beat 43]
At sixteen-hundred he called the direct line for Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo — Pilsen, honest operator, the last confirmed stop before Celeste went offline. The number was in Corvin's brief, flagged as unverified but active. It rang four times and a woman answered, cautious, the kind of cautious that meant she'd been getting calls she wasn't sure about. He said his name and the agency name and that he was looking for a client she'd recently worked with, and he was not law enforcement, and this was a safety call, not an inquiry. A pause. She asked, "Is she in danger?" "She is adjacent to people who are dangerous," he said. "I would like you to also be careful." Another pause, longer. She said, "Come in person. Not today. Tomorrow." He said, "Tomorrow morning." She said, "Before nine."

[Beat 44]
He wrote *Vásquez-Okonkwo — before 0900* and drew a box around it, the same box he drew around the things he was going to come back to. The box around Mei-Lin's *I know* was two pages back; he'd open it when Corvin handed him the name for the wrong cadence she'd heard. Two days. Then he turned to a clean page and set down what the day had actually given him, in three lines, because the convergence meeting needed the decision and not the drift: the building on Ashland-Cermak was a Carrion Enterprises asset wearing a cheap shop's clothes, hardware and all; Pellerin had tried to walk and the crew had hung him in his own bathroom and called it grief; and Celeste Hartley, who had never once been logged at that intake, was walking careful and unafraid straight into the corridor that fed both. Her father had hired him to find a girl an organ ring took. The girl was alive, and the building that scared him had never had her name. The thing she'd actually chosen was worse than the thing he feared, and he didn't have its name yet. He closed the notepad on the cold cup and went to find Corvin.
