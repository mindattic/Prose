# On Call

_In the GLMZ — the sprawling megacity that devoured the Midwest — Elias runs his life from the forty-second floor. He coordinates contracts, manages logistics, and hasn't been at street level in forty-two days. He is very good at staying above it all.

When a routine orbital debris recovery job arrives with an unusual client and an unusual payout, Elias does what he always does: delegates, documents, and keeps his distance. But the job comes routed through a contact known only as Sparrow — a fixer who quotes prices before they're asked, assembles teams from nothing, and bills for contingencies that haven't happened yet.

As the recovery unfolds, Elias discovers that the objects he's pulling from the lake have been pulled before. That the paperwork from those prior jobs carries his own notation. That he closed files in 2218 on objects he never examined — and that someone has been watching him since then, waiting for the one operator who would finally look.

The Number That Works is a biopunk noir novelette set in the world of the GLMZ — a city of ferrocement stacks, corporate corridors, and a lake that hides things that came from somewhere else. It is a story about what it means to close a loop, what it costs to open one, and the strange, long patience of something that has no mouth._

[Beat 1]
Work Order 09-Milwaukee arrives at 7:14 on a Thursday, routed through the GLMZ Orbital Debris Registry by a holding company Elias has never seen before. He is eating noodles at his desk on the forty-second floor of a building that wasn't built to have a forty-second floor — the south half was added in 2209 under a CorpoNation special-authorization sprint and still smells, faintly, of wet ferrocement in the mornings. The broth is thin enough to read the bowl through.

He sets down the chopsticks.

The manifest is clean. Standard orbital-debris recovery, sub-category lake-incursion, estimated mass 2.1 tonnes, recovery window 72 hours. Disposition: CLIENT-DIRECTED. He has run eleven lake-incursion recoveries in four years and the manifests look like this: columns, units, references, a flag hierarchy that tells you what someone decided the thing is.

The signature_class column says "unclassified."

He has seen that field hold terrestrial-manufacture, GEO-derived, MEO-derived, unregistered-launch, reprocessed-hull, seven others. He has never seen it hold "unclassified." Unclassified does not mean they don't know. It means they know, and they put this in the field instead.

The recovery site is listed as sub-lake restricted corridor, Zone 4.

He knows that corridor. In 2218, during a quarterly registry audit at Cordon Freight, a delayed disposal filing had come to him in a queue with twenty-two others — a five-year-old event, unresolved paperwork from the backlog. He had processed it in eleven minutes: checked the boxes, written "recovered and disposed, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed," and moved on. He had been behind that week. He never pulled the underlying documentation. He hasn't thought about it since.

The client is a holding company called Meridian Provisional Solutions, LLC, registered in Zone 7 through a filing address he recognizes as a storefront that runs incorporation paperwork for anyone who asks. The first payment tranche has already cleared — 40,000 Φ, the kind of number that settles certain questions before they're asked.

He accepts the contract — for 40,000 Φ, and for another reason he does not write down.

He opens the pill case on the corner of the desk — a flat matte disk, the hinge worn smooth. Three segments left, which means a refill order, which means a thing to do later. He swallows one without water.

He goes to the window. The street below is forty-two floors down and thirty-eight days away, by his count: the last time he was at street level was a Wednesday in late April, the permit office, a form that couldn't be submitted remotely. He could see three people on the pavement from here. A cyclist moving through the loading zone. A woman in a yellow coat.

He watches them for a moment. From up here, the city is comprehensible.

[Beat 2]
The number only works on alternate weeks. Siosaia Tuivaga-Brennan, the operator who gave it to him six months ago, had mentioned this as a footnote: she was clearing files, thought it might be useful, it worked on alternate weeks, she'd never been able to figure out the pattern. She hadn't said what she'd used it for. He hadn't asked.

He dials from the balcony, which is the farthest he goes on most days. The lake is a gray line forty-two floors below, and to the north the Spine's overnight cargo tubes are still running — blue-white pulses along the elevated track, freight traveling at the speed that makes the word freight feel inadequate. The air is lake water and machine oil and factory rain drying on the parapet rail. A Null Crow is working the building's south face below him, quartering the ledges for anything the night deposited. He watches it for a moment.

He dials.

Ninety-two seconds later, his screen displays a single line of text:

"Three pieces. Two locations. One window. 14,000 Φ. Confirm."

He reads it twice. Fourteen thousand Φ is exactly what he would have budgeted if he'd had time to price the job properly. He had not had time. He had given the number a corridor, three pieces, and a 72-hour window. He had not given it his rates, his overhead structure, his standard specialist markup. Whatever returned 14,000 had calculated all of that without being told.

He confirms.

Nothing comes back. That is the end of the communication.

He goes inside. He writes down the time, the number of seconds between the dial and the reply, and the exact phrasing of the returned text. He documents things he does not understand. He puts this one at the top.

[Beat 3]
Four hours later, the network is assembled. Elias did not assemble it.

The diver introduces herself by message: Makena Otieno-Varga, lake-salvage specialist, certifications current, available for the recovery window, has reviewed the preliminary coordinates. She did not ask how she got his contact information.

The manifest clerk at Orbital Facilities West introduces himself by voice call: Leandro Bautista-Henriksen, current on the GLMZ debris-reclassification protocol, has done this before and knows the right forms. He didn't ask either.

The transport operator pings last: Druh Yeboah-Prykhodko, vehicle capacity 3.5 tonnes, clearances current through the Zone 7 restricted-handling tier, available on two hours' notice for the delivery window. He's done this before. He does not ask about the connection.

None of them ask who referred them.

A new contractor asks how they were referred. An experienced contractor asks what the job pays. A contractor who has worked with Sparrow before doesn't ask about the connector at all — not from incuriosity, but because the asking would complicate something they've decided to leave simple. He recognizes the posture.

Three separate people. Three separate capabilities. All named, implicitly, in the intake form he had sent to the number — a form that described the recovery need without naming the specialists he'd require.

None of them know the other two exist.

He has run this life for fifteen years: nobody comes to him, he goes nowhere, the work happens at a distance. He has not met a contractor in person in some time. He built the practice around that fact — around the specific advantage of being the person who coordinates the loop rather than closes any part of it physically. It has always worked. He is very good at it.

He makes a note: the routing intelligence required to do this in four hours is either a person with an exceptional contact network, or something that reads intake forms and has the network pre-mapped. He doesn't know which possibility unsettles him more.

[Beat 4]
The first invoice arrives at 6:08 on the morning of day one, before any recovery work has started.

It is itemized to eight decimal places. The line items read:

Route clearance: sub-lake corridor remap, southwest approach, 72h window — 2,800.00 Φ
Materials handling, non-standard mass category, provisional manifest category assignment — 1,400.00 Φ
Contingency: CorpoNation inquiry response window, 24h pre-deployment standing — 900.00 Φ
Coordination: three specialist engagements, intake-sourced — 9,000.00 Φ
Administrative overhead — 900.00 Φ

Total: 15,000.00 Φ
Applied balance (standing credit, prior engagement): minus 1,000.00 Φ
Net due: 14,000.00 Φ

He reads the third line item.

"Contingency: CorpoNation inquiry response window, 24h pre-deployment standing."

He had not told the number about a CorpoNation interest. He had not known about one. He had not known Arcturus Civil Security was watching the manifest.

At 8:22, two hours and fourteen minutes after the invoice arrived, the GLMZ Orbital Debris Registry forwarded an advisory: Arcturus had filed a formal inquiry against Work Order 09-Milwaukee, citing the signature-class flag. Standard procedure, they noted. Standard response window: 24 hours.

The 900 Φ contingency line was already in the total. Someone had priced an inquiry that had not yet been filed, against a flag he had not yet reported.

He paid the invoice.

[Beat 5]
Makena Otieno-Varga delivers the first piece at 14:40 on day one: a sealed transit case, condensation still beading on the exterior from the temperature differential between the lake and the afternoon air. He opens the door. She is a compact woman with the kind of calm that comes from working at depth, where calm is a survival strategy and not a disposition. The hallway behind her carries the building's ambient sounds — an elevator cycling, distant music through a wall — and the specific textural weight of air that has been places his apartment air has not.

He takes the case. He steps back.

She hands him the report on a card and doesn't explain herself. She explains the object.

The report reads:

Object recovered: 0.6 tonnes, metallic composite, depth 18m, sub-lake restricted corridor km 4.2.
Signature matches no registered orbital-descent profile in the GLMZ or federated debris registries.
Object did not descend. Trajectory modeling is ongoing.
Isotopic composition: no source match in GLMZ or federated material registry. Flag: ORIGIN UNKNOWN.

He reads the last line twice. He sets the card on the desk, face up.

He has run eleven lake-incursion recoveries in four years. Every manifest assumes a direction. This card doesn't.

"What does that mean, didn't descend?" he asks.

"It means the trajectory we reconstructed from the bottom disturbance pattern goes the wrong direction," she says. "Down-to-up, not up-to-down. Something at that depth went up fast, and what we pulled is what didn't make it."

She says it like someone who has been waiting for him to ask.

"Where did it come from?" he asks.

"I don't know. The corridor record doesn't go back far enough, and below eighty meters we're reading sediment disturbed at least three times in the last forty years." She sets the case on his desk, takes her hand off it. "I don't usually ask what I'm pulling. I'm asking now."

He tells her he doesn't know either.

She nods once, like this is what she expected, and leaves.

[Beat 6]
He runs the orbital designation — an empty field — against seven registries over the course of an afternoon. Two are public GLMZ records. Three cross-reference the federated orbital monitoring network. Two require credentials he has and does not advertise.

Nothing.

He runs the signature class — "unclassified" — against the debris registry's historical field data, filtering for the blank orbital-designation subset. The query returns: "3 results. Research query limit exceeded — continue?"

He continues.

The three results are dated 2189, 2202, and 2213. Each is classified as a lake-incursion event. Each has the signature_class field holding "unclassified." Each has the orbital-designation field empty. Each is flagged "recovered" and "disposed."

The 2189 event: 1.8 tonnes. The 2202 event: 2.3 tonnes. The 2213 event: 1.6 tonnes, recovered in three pieces.

He pushes back from the screen.

His current job is 2.1 tonnes. Three pieces.

He pulls the 2213 entry's full disposition record.

The notation in the free-text field reads: "object condition non-assessable, disposition accepted per client instruction, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed." He knows that construction. He used it for every Zone 4 closure in his queue during the 2218 audit, because the audit manager told him to use language that didn't require a site inspection. He had typed it twenty-three times in three days, on objects he had never seen, from an apartment he hadn't left.

He is the one who wrote "recovered and disposed" on the 2213 event.

Then he gets the notepad — paper, Blossom Arcade brand with a canal bridge on the cover, the kind of thing he bought at a street stall three years ago and never used — and he writes it all down by hand. He does this because writing by hand is slow, and slow is good when what he is looking at does not yet have a name.

The 2213 event: 1.6 tonnes. Three pieces. He draws a small circle around three pieces, and looks at it. Filed closed. His notation. His file.

He writes: all four — same corridor, same filing, no designation.

He puts the pen down.

Then he picks it up. Below: I wrote the third one.

[Beat 7]
Leandro Bautista-Henriksen calls at 10:00 on the morning of day two, his scheduled check-in about the reclassification paperwork. The call is normal for the first three minutes: he has filed the provisional category assignment, the Arcturus inquiry clock is running, they should have a response before the window closes, no issues with the Zone 9 transfer.

Then, in the same voice, at the same pace, as if it is the next item on a list:

"Are you the Cordon Freight filer? The 2213 disposition?"

A pause.

Elias says yes.

"I thought so." Leandro's tone doesn't change — it's the tone of someone confirming something they already knew, checking a fact against memory. "They know about you. They've known for a while." A brief stop. "I thought you should know that."

Elias catches the ambient noise behind Leandro's voice: HVAC cycling, the Pulse thrumline two levels below the floor, the structural creak of a building designed in 2197 and running at 115% occupancy since 2218. He has always heard the Pulse as a background fact of the city — abstract, something other people ride. From up here it is just vibration in the walls.

"Thank you," Elias says.

"Of course," Leandro says, and the call returns to the manifest clock and the Arcturus window and the rest of the list.

Elias sits with the phrase after the call ends. They've known for a while. Not her. Not him. Not it. Them. Leandro Bautista-Henriksen works in an orbital transit facility and had been expecting this specific filer on this specific job, and had reached for them as if it were the natural word for whatever Sparrow is — not a conscious choice, just the word that arrived first.

He writes it in the notepad.

Then he writes the word them, with a question mark and a small circle around it, and looks at it for a while.

[Beat 8]
Day two, morning: the second piece arrives by a different route entirely — not Makena, not the sub-lake corridor, but a manifest transfer from a Zone 9 facility Elias has never used, initiating without any instruction from him. The transfer documentation is clean. The form numbers are real. When he checks the authorization chain it leads back to a clearance code that is valid, not his, and that he cannot identify as belonging to anyone else.

He accepts the transfer. He does not ask the question.

The second invoice arrives through the same channel the first one came, timestamped 9:42. There is a new line item:

Standing credit: prior engagement, Zone 4 corridor recovery, 2218 — 400.00 Φ applied.

He knows what this is. In 2218, at Cordon Freight, he had processed the delayed disposition on the 2213 event. He had been paid Cordon Freight's standard review rate: twelve Φ per file, same as everything else in the queue. Sparrow's system is now paying him the difference between what Cordon Freight paid him and what his time was worth. The value of having closed the loop cleanly, without looking at what he was closing.

He was credited for the exact thing he hadn't done. Examining it.

The credit lands in his account at 10:14.

He calls Siosaia Tuivaga-Brennan.

"You gave me the number six months ago," he says. "You processed one of the other events. The 2202, or the 2189."

A pause, the specific quality of a pause in which someone is deciding whether they want to be part of a conversation.

"The 2202," she says. "I got a credit the following month. I didn't ask what it was for. I figured it out eventually and then I decided not to figure it out further." Another pause, shorter. "I thought you might be different."

He doesn't ask her why she thought that.

He writes in the notepad: credited for not looking. She did the same. She gave me the number because she thought I would look anyway.

[Beat 9]
The alternate-week window has been open for forty days. He finds this out by asking, in the places where operators ask. Three operators used the number in the current window. He is the third.

He spends most of a day finding the other two.

The first is a woman named Imo Asante-Cavalcanti who works disruption logistics — she routes cargo around blockages, a job that sounds administrative and is not. She used the number three weeks ago to move a pharmaceutical consignment through a corridor embargoed for eighteen days.

"What's Sparrow?" Elias asks, when he has gotten to the point.

She considers this.

"A person," she says. "Very careful. Knows the city the way you know a city when you've lived in it your whole life and paid close attention. Someone who's been at this a long time and has built something real."

The second operator is a man named Corvid Osei-Larsson who does network-access work — the operator you hire when a door is a digital one. He used the number five weeks ago for an access route through a sector that had been rezoned.

"What's Sparrow?" Elias asks.

He doesn't hesitate.

"A rotating crew," he says. "Two people, maybe three, sharing the handle. You can tell by the response style — there are at least two different modes the text comes back in, and they're not the same writer. Someone coordinates the intake; someone else does the routing. It's clean, but it's seamed."

Elias thanks them both.

He writes both answers down, on opposite pages of the notepad, and looks at them across the gutter.

Then he writes a third answer, in smaller letters at the bottom of the right-hand page: A system. Running since 2189. No person does this for forty years without leaving a face. No crew survives four corridor events without a single documented overlap.

The third is the most complete explanation. That is what worries him about it.

[Beat 10]
Druh Yeboah-Prykhodko delivers the third piece on the evening of day two, in the false-chassis compartment of a vehicle registered as a construction supplies hauler — which it has been for eleven years, legitimately, which is the point. He is a large quiet man — not still in the way that draws attention, but still in the way of a man who has learned that movement is information. He hands Elias the transfer documentation, already completed correctly.

"You've worked with Sparrow before," Elias says.

"Twice," Druh says.

"What do you make of it?"

Druh sets his own copy of the transfer documentation on the desk. He straightens it — not a nervous gesture but a finishing one, the gesture of a man who aligns things because alignment is evidence of attention paid.

"The number works," he says.

Elias waits. Druh does not continue.

"That's the whole answer?" Elias asks.

"That's the important part," Druh says. "I've worked with other fixers. Good people, most of them. The number works on alternate weeks, and when it works it does what it said it was going to do, and you get paid what you were quoted, and nobody leans on you afterward. I don't know what Sparrow is. I know the number works."

He picks up his keys.

"The transfer is logged," he says. "You'll want to close the manifest window before eight."

He leaves.

Elias closes the manifest window before eight. He writes in the notepad: the number works. And then, beneath it: Druh has decided this is sufficient. I am not sure I have.

[Beat 11]
He assembles the three pieces on the floor of the receiving space with Makena's help, using the case dimensions and the transit weights to get the geometry right. The composite does not form a whole.

He had expected this — the 2.1 tonne estimate from the manifest, minus the three recovered masses, leaves 0.2 tonnes unaccounted. A rounding tolerance, possibly. An error.

But the problem is not the mass. The problem is the signature.

Each piece, individually, has a signature consistent with its material class: metallic composite, thermal event, compression stress. Clean.

Makena runs the composite analysis — the three pieces treated as a single object. She's quiet for a moment.

"The geometry is wrong," she says. "The material profile reads re-entry. The structure doesn't — this couldn't have survived re-entry at these stress levels. The two readings are incompatible."

"What do you get," Elias asks, "if it came up instead of down?"

"Clean match," she says. "Everything is consistent if the object came from depth and met a thermal event on ascent. Heated from below."

He looks at the three pieces on the floor. Matte gray-silver, smooth in some sections and stress-fractured in others. In two places the surface has a texture he cannot name — not corroded, not worn. Wrong in the way a material is wrong when it was made somewhere the standard tests were not designed to account for.

He stays with that for a moment.

Before she leaves, Makena says one more thing.

"The packing on the third piece was non-standard. Not a protocol I've seen registered anywhere." She looks at the floor, then at him. "The internal loading geometry matches the Zone 4 closure format from the 2218 audit cycle. The format that was retired in 2219." A pause. "That's a narrow reference. Whoever packed it knew how a 2218 Zone 4 filer would receive it."

She leaves without saying what she thinks that means.

He pays her outstanding balance. She signs the final recovery documentation.

Meridian Provisional Solutions, LLC, has dissolved. He checks the registry: dissolved at 6:00 that morning, twelve hours before the recovery window closed. The second payment tranche arrives in his account while he is standing there, correct to the Φ.

He opens the notepad to a clean page and writes: recovered and disposed, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed. He knows what it means to write that now. He wrote it once before in 2218, without knowing what he was closing. He writes it this time with full knowledge of what he is closing and what is down there, and he notes that the sentence reads identically both times.

He closes the notebook.

Then a second work order arrives through the same channel. Brief. CLIENT-DIRECTED. Coordinates: East African coast, 3.7°S, 39.9°E. "Physical presence required for final disposition confirmation. Object origin site. Window: 14 days."

He reads it twice. He looks out the window at the street below. He hasn't been at street level in forty-one days, by his count.

[Beat 12]
He does not go immediately.

He sits with the second work order for three days. He opens it and closes it. He opens it again. He makes a list of reasons not to go — the apartment, the work queue building up, the fact that he is not the right person for a physical site inspection and there are operators he could hire for that — and he knows, writing the list, that it is a list of distances between himself and a decision he has already made.

The 2218 file. The Zone 4 corridor. The eleven minutes. He had been behind that week. He had processed twenty-three objects he never looked at, including one that Sparrow packed in a format designed for exactly his hands — a format from his specific year, his specific protocol, his specific blind spot. Whoever sent the second work order had been watching him since 2218 at minimum. Possibly longer.

The second reason he doesn't write down is the same as the first: there is a loop that isn't closed, and he knows it, and the distance between him and the closing of it is now physical instead of logistical, and he has built a career specifically to avoid that.

On the third night, with the booking window still open, he goes to the building's front door. He takes a pill first. He stands at the door for two minutes, listening to the city on the other side of it. He goes back upstairs, sits at the desk, and looks at the booking confirmation without closing it.

On the fourth morning he books the pod. GLMZ to Mombasa Elevated hub, one transfer at the Tunis connector, total transit approximately 90 minutes. He has not been on the Pulse in four years.

He packs one bag, the way a logistics man packs: exactly what is needed, nothing else. Travel documentation, the notepad, a change of clothes, the transit case credentials for the recovery documentation. He takes a pill. He is ready in twenty minutes.

He goes to the building's front door. He has already been here once this week.

The sound from outside is different from the sound through the walls — the full register of it, not filtered. Engine noise. Someone's music, three buildings over. The city at ground level has a weight that the city from the forty-second floor does not. He knows this. He has known it for forty-four days, which is how long he has been counting.

He goes through the door.

[Beat 13]
He has seen it from above every morning — the same streets, the same loading zones — and the towers look different from the inside.

From the forty-second floor, the unauthorized upper floors read as accumulation, interesting, the way a city builds itself against its own rules. From down here they read as overhang. He keeps track of that.

The modded population is denser at street level than it looks from above — bodies augmented past the standard registers, a man with forearm modifications that shift his center of gravity slightly forward, a woman whose optical work shows as a faint subdermal bioluminescence when she turns her head under the building shadows.

He walks to the Zone 4 Pulse station entrance. Eleven blocks. He picks the route that stays on the wide streets. He notes the pavement texture, which is older than the buildings above it, and the smell of the lower city, which is not what the upper city smells like: more organic, warmer, lake water mixed with food and machinery and the specific acrid note of volt rats working the junction boxes in the building bases. He passes one — a junction box on the corner of Kedzie and 44th — and hears them before he sees them, a dry shifting in the housing, small claws on metal.

He manages.

The station underground is a vaulted space larger than most buildings, which helps: a controlled environment, organized, the logic of it visible. Departure boards. Column markers. A crowd that moves with the specific purposeful flow of people who know where they are going. He finds the column marker for MOMBASA ELEVATED via TUNIS CONN., bay seven. He finds his pod. He gets on.

[Beat 14]
The pod is sealed and efficient, the interior lit in a neutral white that gives nothing away about the time of day or the geography outside. Luggage overhead. Seats facing forward. He is in the fourth row, window side.

The door seals. The pod adjusts its internal pressure — a brief settling, the air changing quality in a way that has nothing to do with comfort. The platform outside the window slides back, and then the tunnel wall begins.

This is what going to a place actually means: not the decision, which can be reversed; not packing, which can be unpacked; but this — the door sealed and the tunnel moving and no version of himself that turns back. He has not been past this point in four years.

He takes out the notepad.

He reviews what he has written — the three answers, the credits, the invoice lines, the word them with a circle around it — and reads it the way he reads a manifest he hasn't seen in a while, checking for what he missed the first time.

He doesn't find anything new. That is its own data point.

For most of the first forty minutes it shows tunnel wall: smooth concrete cycling past at a speed that should feel fast and doesn't. The pod is quiet enough that he can hear his own breathing.

On the elevated section north of the city, the pod breaks surface for forty seconds. The industrial sprawl of GLMZ resolves briefly into identifiable shapes — the Spine's elevated tubes, the gray line of the lake to the east, the stacked ferrocement of the northern residential belt — and then gives way to agricultural zones, old corn belt managed by automated systems, flat and vast, and then the Appalachian ridgelines still carrying something green.

The pod descends again. Tunnel wall resumes.

The Tunis transfer is twenty-three minutes in a connector station with open ceilings and a climate drier than anything he has felt in years. He changes pods. The continent outside the tunnel wall is no longer the one he left, which he knows and has not yet fully registered.

The second pod is fuller. He sits in the same position, window side, and watches the tunnel.

[Beat 15]
The Mombasa Elevated hub is built on platform infrastructure above what used to be coastal lowland — the original city is under three meters of Indian Ocean at high tide, which the information boards note without apparent distress. The hub is clean and functional and newer than anything in GLMZ, built for a coast that had to decide, all at once, what it was going to be.

He steps off the pod and through the station and out onto the platform's eastern edge, and the horizon does something he wasn't prepared for.

In GLMZ, the horizon ends in lake. The lake is large enough to read as ocean from the forty-second floor, but the air above it carries the particular weight of industrial output — a sky with content in it, particulate, the specific haze of a city running on the kind of scale GLMZ runs. He has lived inside that sky for fifteen years and has stopped registering it as a quality of light.

Here the horizon is further, the sky a blue that has no industrial undertone, and the Indian Ocean is a different color than the lake — a deeper blue-green that changes with depth rather than with weather. The sun is at an angle he doesn't know. The humidity is total and warm, the kind of warmth that comes through the skin rather than against it.

He stands there for a moment. He is not managing anything right now. He is just standing in a new place.

He takes local transit toward the coordinates — inland and south, following the coastal ridge that rises above the drowned lowland. The transit is a surface-running elevated rail, narrow, efficient, moving through a landscape that organizes itself vertically: platform infrastructure on columns above the old lowland, new structures built on ridge ground, everything elevated or anchored to stone. Below the platform edge, through the transit car's window, he can see the surface of the water at high tide covering what used to be road.

The ridge rises. The vegetation changes. The sky opens further.

He gets off at the last stop on the line, where the ridge levels into a coastal plateau, and he can see — at first just as a structural line against the sky, then resolving into specifics as his eyes work out the scale — the barrel of the mass driver following the ridge for several kilometers, angled at the equatorial sky at seventeen degrees, its near end terminating in a facility building surrounded by trees that have been growing around it for thirty years.

[Beat 16]
The mass driver is larger than the specifications suggested.

He had pictured large, industrial, comprehensible by category. Structures that read that way at document scale become geological at presence. The barrel follows the coastal ridge for several kilometers, half its length disappearing into the vegetation where the ridge curves, only the angle of it — the specific seventeen degrees off the horizontal, aimed at the equatorial sky — giving away that it is aimed at something rather than simply resting here.

He walks to the facility gate. The gate is unlocked. There is a note taped to the latch: Elias — I'm in the main hall. The handwriting is careful, not hasty. Someone who writes by hand regularly.

Tadesse Bekele-Sørensen meets him in the doorway of the main hall: a man in his fifties, Ethiopian-Danish by the cast of his features, moving with the economy of someone who has maintained one facility for twenty years and learned which urgencies are real. He is wearing a facility uniform that has been patched at the elbow. He looks at Elias the way someone looks at a person they have been waiting for and have stopped expecting.

"You're from the Zone 4 jobs," he says.

It is not a question.

"Yes," Elias says.

Tadesse nods once, like a man checking a box on a list only he can see.

"Come in," he says. "I've had the logs ready for two weeks. I wasn't sure you'd come."

"I wasn't either," Elias says.

Tadesse looks at him — a real look, the kind that takes a moment — and then he almost smiles.

"That's honest," he says. "Come in."

[Beat 17]
Tadesse's story comes out over the course of an hour, told in the flat tone of someone who has rehearsed it many times in his own head and is relieved, now, to be telling it to someone who will understand.

He took the maintenance contract in 2204, through a shell company that traced to nothing when he checked — and he checked, because he was twenty-three years older than he needed to be to take a job that was beneath his qualifications, and he wanted to understand what he was accepting. The company resolved to a holding entity that resolved to a filing address in a zone he had never worked in, and at the end of the trace there was nothing. He took the contract. The payment was reliable and the work was real.

"In 2204," he says, "there was a seismic anomaly. Minor — a 2.1 magnitude reading from the ridge sensors, which are calibrated for launch signature because we monitor for that. I ran the numbers. It wasn't seismic." A pause. "I didn't file the anomaly report."

"You got a credit the following month," Elias says.

Tadesse looks at him.

"Four hundred and twelve Φ," he says. "Retroactive, no line item. I knew what it was for. I decided not to ask." He takes Elias through the facility: the power plant, the cooling systems, the launch control room with its terminal rows still live, still current, still running software that was updated last year by a remote push he didn't authorize and didn't countermand. "I've had four other visitors in twenty years. They came from the orbital consortium. Asked technical questions about structural integrity, load capacity, the barrel alignment. Stayed an hour each time. Left. None of them asked about the launch logs."

He pulls up the launch logs on the facility terminal. Four entries.

2189. 2202. 2213. 2226.

Every command remote. Every authorization code valid. Every origin address the same IP, routed through a satellite registry.

He pulls the registry entry. The satellite is listed as inactive since 2187. He watches Elias read it.

"I looked it up after I took the contract," Tadesse says. "By 2204 it had been inactive for nearly twenty years, and I decided it was a relay address. I've maintained this equipment for twenty years for something that has never introduced itself." He looks at the terminal, then at Elias. "Whatever it is, it measures. Every specification, every maintenance protocol, every payment — more precise than any human engineer I've ever worked with. It doesn't estimate."

A pause.

"Did it send you here?" he asks.

Elias thinks about the packing format. The Zone 4 credit. Eight years of being observed from a registry address that was officially inactive when he was eleven years old. The second work order that arrived the moment the first job closed, with his name on it in the only way a system can put your name on something: by making it the only natural next step.

"Yes," he says.

[Beat 18]
Tadesse leaves him at the base of the barrel, where the launch axis meets the facility floor, and Elias stands there and understands what he is looking at.

The satellite went officially quiet in 2187. Two years before the first launch. It went quiet, and then, from the quiet, it found the mass driver, found the remote-access protocol, and began conducting its own science. Forty years of careful operations. A shadow network of filers spread across four decades — not recruited, not contacted, just observed until the right file landed in the right hands, and then a credit, a number, a loop that could be closed again later. Never the same person twice for the same corridor event. Always someone who closed without looking. Siosaia had thought she'd found one who wouldn't.

He is the first one who came.

The old satellites were not decommissioned — there was no budget for decommissioning, and no urgency, because something inactive is not a problem. They were simply unmonitored. A satellite listed inactive in 2187 might be dead, or it might be something else. Nobody checked, because the St. Isabella elevator was being built, and then because it was operational, and then because it had been fifteen years, and then because it had been forty.

He looks up through the barrel.

The barrel is a straight electromagnetic tube, four meters across, angled at seventeen degrees off the horizontal, and at the far end of it — several kilometers away along the ridge — there is a circle of sky framed in the precision of a scientific instrument. The circle is blue. Inside the blue, invisible, is a specific geostationary coordinate where a satellite is supposed to be inactive.

He does not say what he thinks out loud. He writes nothing in the notepad. He looks at the sky for a while.

[Beat 19]
The return transit takes the same 90 minutes in the other direction, and it is a different experience from the outbound.

On the outbound he had been managing something. The station too large, the crowd too dense, the world at ground level requiring a specific allocation of attention. He had managed it because there was a reason to.

On the return he is just on a pod. He eats at the Tunis connector — flatbread, correct and hot — standing at the platform rail. He has been outside his apartment for nineteen hours. He does not take out the notepad.

The GLMZ pod breaks surface on the elevated section north of the city, and there is the lake: the gray line going east, the Spine's cargo runs moving north along the lakeshore, and the ferrocement stack of the building where he lives visible from the track at the right angle, which he has never had before. He watches his building from the outside. The forty-second floor is lit.

He goes up the elevator. The apartment smells of ferrocement. He sets the bag down. He sets the notepad on the desk.

He sits at the desk and does not open his queue.

He sits there for a while.

[Beat 20]
He dials the number on a Thursday evening, nine days after he returns. He is on the balcony. He has been on the balcony every evening since he got back — not because the outside is easier now, but because it is different in a way he wants to keep measuring.

The Spine's cargo runs pulse to the north. The lake spreads east, the same gray line it has always been, carrying the light at this hour in a way that makes it look briefly like it goes somewhere. The air smells like lake water and factory rain and the green-penny note of volt rats in the junction box below, their dry shifting audible from here on quiet evenings. This is a quiet evening.

He had prepared a question. He has been refining the question for nine days: did you know who I was in 2218, or only later? When you packed the third piece in the 2218 format, had you been watching me for eight years, or did you find me in 2226 and reconstruct the format from the Cordon Freight records? He wants to know if he was chosen or discovered. He understands they may be the same thing.

The number rings.

It connects — not to a voice, but to the brief static of an open line, the sound of a space held rather than a space empty. A second, maybe two.

Then it doesn't.

The line closes cleanly, no drop tone, no record in his call log. Whatever answered left no more trace than the first time he dialed it, and the time before that, and every other time it worked because it was an alternate week and someone needed a loop closed.

He stays on the balcony a while.

He hadn't asked the first question. He knows, standing here, what kind of silence that was: a research satellite 36,000 kilometers up that can read his intake forms and see him on his balcony and track the specific eleven-minute quality of how he closes a loop — and has no mouth. The open line is the closest thing it has to a hand. He thinks about the mass driver barrel aimed at the equatorial sky, four meters across, and the years of careful operations, and Tadesse maintaining equipment for something that has never introduced itself, and Siosaia deciding not to figure it out further, and Druh aligning the transfer documentation on his desk.

The number works, Druh had said. That's the important part.

A Null Crow settles on the parapet rail ten meters away. It stays for thirty seconds and then it goes, and he doesn't watch where.

He keeps the number in his neuretics under a tag he makes up on the spot, in the category where he keeps things he wants to find again. He names it after a bird.
