# Sparrow

_Elias Macias hasn't been to street level in forty-one days. From the one hundred eleventh floor, he runs his whole life at a distance — a fixer who coordinates the work and never touches any part of it himself.

Then a routine debris-recovery contract arrives flagged with a word he's never seen: unclassified. The objects he's paid to recover didn't fall from orbit. They came up — from a source below the lake floor that predates the city by centuries. And someone has already priced the job to the cent. Someone who reads his intake forms before he sends them.

They call it Sparrow and it has been watching Elias for eight years.

Now it wants to talk._

## Sparrow

[Beat 1]
Work Order 09-Milwaukee arrives at 7:14 on a Thursday, routed through the GLMZ Orbital Debris Registry by a holding company Elias has never seen before. He is eating noodles at his desk in apartment 11134, on the 111th floor of a building that wasn't built to have a 111th floor - the south half was added in 2209 under a CorpoNation special-authorization sprint and still smells, faintly, of wet ferrocement in the mornings. The broth is thin enough to read the bowl through.

He sets down the chopsticks.

The manifest is clean. Standard orbital-debris recovery, sub-category lake-incursion, estimated mass 2.1 tons, recovery window 72 hours. Disposition: CLIENT-DIRECTED. He has run eleven lake-incursion recoveries in four years. The manifests look like this: columns, units, references, a flag hierarchy that tells you what someone decided the thing is. He reads it the way he reads all of them - header to footer, each field resolved before moving to the next.

The signature_class field is in the fifth row. He reads the value and scrolls down.

The recovery site is listed as sub-lake restricted corridor, Zone 4.

He stops there.

He knows that corridor. In 2218, during a quarterly registry audit at Cordon Freight, a delayed disposal filing had come to him in a queue with twenty-two others - a five-year-old event, unresolved paperwork from the backlog. He had processed it in eleven minutes: checked the boxes, written "recovered and disposed, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed," and moved on.

He had been behind that week. He tells himself this now the way he has told himself a few other things about that year: accurately, and without examining what the accuracy costs.

Except he hadn't left it alone. Three weeks ago the 2213 case number had surfaced in an unrelated reconciliation - a mass figure that wouldn't close against the recorded disposal weight, off by a margin too clean to be rounding error - and the eleven minutes he'd spent on it in 2218 had started to itch the way an unclosed work order always did. He'd gone back into the delayed-disposal backlog on his own time, with his own still-active credentials, and tried to open the underlying documentation he had once marked resolved without reading. The access request had come back the same then as it comes back now: File restricted. Zone 4 corridor documentation under administrative review. Contact clearance office. He had never once needed clearance to read his own filed paperwork. He couldn't read it three weeks ago, and the not-being-able-to had gone into the queue with everything else he couldn't close - flagged, unresolved, not forgotten.

Now the same corridor sits in a fresh work order on his screen, routed to him by a holding company he's never seen.

He pulls the 2213 queue up again beside the manifest, to be sure. The case number comes up. He selects the underlying documentation and clicks to open the first file.

File restricted. Zone 4 corridor documentation under administrative review. Contact clearance office.

The same wall. He hadn't expected it to have moved. What has moved is everything around it: the work order hasn't handed him a mystery. It has confirmed that the number he'd already been unable to leave alone is one that someone, somewhere, is actively keeping shut.

He scrolls back up to the fifth row. The signature_class column says "unclassified."

He has seen that field hold terrestrial-manufacture, GEO-derived, MEO-derived, unregistered-launch, reprocessed-hull, seven others. He has never seen it hold "unclassified." Unclassified doesn't mean they don't know. It means they know, and they put this in the field instead.

He closes the manifest tab. He picks up the chopsticks again, looks at the bowl, and sets them back down.

The client is a holding company called Meridian Provisional Solutions, LLC, registered in Zone 7 through a filing address he recognizes as a storefront that runs incorporation paperwork for anyone who asks. The first payment tranche has already cleared - 40,000 Φ, the kind of number that settles certain questions before they're asked.

He accepts the contract - for 40,000 Φ, and for another reason he does not write down.

He opens the pill case on the corner of the desk - a flat matte disk, the hinge worn smooth. Three segments left. Refill: open item, no due date. He swallows one without water.

He goes to the window. The street below is a hundred and eleven floors down and thirty-eight days away, by his count: the last time he was at street level was a Wednesday in late April, the permit office, a form that couldn't be submitted remotely. A hundred and eleven floors is unremarkable here - his is one stack in a lakeward row of them, none worth naming, each one standing higher than the tallest tower the old Midwest ever managed to raise before the lake came and settled the matter. Three people on the pavement below: a cyclist working through the loading zone, a woman in a yellow coat near the corner. From the 111th floor they are not quite people yet - they are the scale markers he uses to confirm the drop is real.

He stands there longer than he needs to. Then he goes back to the desk.

[Beat 2]
The number only works on alternate weeks. Siosaia Tuivaga had told him this six months ago in the way she told him most things - standing in the doorway of his office at Cordon Freight, coat already on, heading somewhere before he could ask her a follow-up question. She was clearing files, she said. Thought it might be useful. It worked on alternate weeks. She'd never figured out the pattern.

She hadn't said what she'd used it for. He hadn't asked.

He dials from the balcony, which is the farthest he goes on most days. The lake is a gray line a hundred and eleven floors below, and to the north the Spine's overnight cargo tubes are still running - blue-white pulses along the elevated track, freight traveling at the speed that makes the word freight feel inadequate. East of the track the maps go dark over the Cermak rift, a few blocks the city stopped pretending it governed, where the towers end at a torn edge and the air past it keeps its own hours. The parapet rail under his hand is pocked and weeping rust: the building's nanite skin ran out of contract a decade ago and stopped closing its own corrosion, the way everything below a certain tier eventually stops being maintained. A Null Crow is working the building's south face below him, quartering the ledges for anything the night deposited. He watches it for a moment. He keeps the operator's number in his neuretics - which he still has, which the corridor under the Crow mostly doesn't; the line between who can think at the city's speed and who gets left standing at the curb of it runs straight through this building, floor by floor.

He doesn't know if this is the right week. He dials anyway, because the manifest is still open on his desk and the Zone 4 entry is still in it, and not doing something about a number that won't close isn't a state he can sustain indefinitely.

He dials.

Ninety-two seconds later, his screen displays a single line of text:

"Three pieces. Two locations. One window. 14,000 Φ. Confirm."

He reads it twice. Fourteen thousand Φ is exactly what he would have budgeted if he'd had time to price the job properly. He hadn't had time. He had given the number a corridor, three pieces, and a 72-hour window. He hadn't given it his rates, his overhead structure, his standard specialist markup. Whatever returned 14,000 had calculated all of that without being told.

He confirms.

Nothing comes back. That is the end of the communication.

He goes inside. His hands, he notices, have gone slightly cold - not from the temperature, which is mild enough for this hour. He stands at the desk for a moment before he sits down. He hasn't felt his own hands this clearly since the Zone 4 entry first appeared on the manifest.

He writes down the time, the number of seconds between the dial and the reply, and the exact phrasing of the returned text. He documents things he doesn't understand. He puts this one at the top.

[Beat 3]
Four hours later, the network is assembled. Elias did not assemble it.

The diver introduces herself by message: Makena Varga, lake-salvage specialist, certifications current, available for the recovery window, has reviewed the preliminary coordinates. She didn't ask how she got his contact information.

The manifest clerk at Orbital Facilities West introduces himself by voice call: Leandro Bautista, current on the GLMZ debris-reclassification protocol, has done this before and knows the right forms. He names the specific form number and its revision date - and then exhales briefly through his nose, a small satisfied sound, before moving on, apparently unaware he made it. When he asks about the client contact for the manifest, he uses "them" without registering the pronoun choice. He didn't ask either.

The transport operator pings last: Druh Prykhodko, vehicle capacity 3.5 tons, clearances current through the Zone 7 restricted-handling tier, available on two hours' notice for the delivery window. He notes that he won't take the south approach - there's a bridge he doesn't trust, no further explanation offered. He doesn't ask about the connection.

None of them ask who referred them.

He has a name for that behavioral signature. A new operator asks who sent them. An operator with decent experience asks about the rate. An operator who has run this loop before skips both questions and goes straight to logistics. All three of the people who just appeared in his queue went straight to logistics.

Three separate people. Three separate capabilities. All named, implicitly, in the intake form he had sent to the number - a form that described the recovery need without naming the specialists he'd require.

None of them know the other two exist.

He sits with that for a moment. He runs his practice through this apartment - nobody comes, he goes nowhere, the work happens at a distance. He built it that way deliberately. What has arrived in four hours is a network that doesn't need him at the center in the way he usually is: it has been assembled by something that read his intake form and found the right hands without asking which ones he preferred. For fifteen years he has been the one who knew who to call. He isn't sure what it means that he just watched someone else do it faster.

He makes a note: the routing intelligence needed to do this in four hours is either a person with an exceptional contact network, or a Channeler running it through something unlicensed with the network pre-mapped. He doesn't know which possibility unsettles him more.

[Beat 4]
The first invoice arrives at 6:08 on the morning of day one, before any recovery work has started.

It is itemized to the cent, each line exact. The line items read:

Route clearance: sub-lake corridor remap, southwest approach, 72h window - 2,800.00 Φ
Materials handling, non-standard mass category, provisional manifest category assignment - 1,400.00 Φ
Contingency: CorpoNation inquiry response window, 24h pre-deployment standing - 900.00 Φ
Coordination: three specialist engagements, intake-sourced - 9,000.00 Φ
Administrative overhead - 900.00 Φ

Total: 15,000.00 Φ
Applied balance (standing credit, earlier engagement): minus 1,000.00 Φ
Net due: 14,000.00 Φ

He reads the third line item.

"Contingency: CorpoNation inquiry response window, 24h pre-deployment standing."

He stands up. He doesn't do this consciously - the chair just isn't where he is anymore, and he's at the window, looking at the street. He hadn't told the number about a CorpoNation interest. He hadn't known about one. Someone had priced the cost of an inquiry before the inquiry existed.

The Zone 4 documentation is sealed; he learned that yesterday, in the first ninety seconds of trying to read his own filing. But sealed is a property of the document, not of everything attached to it, and he has spent eleven years making loss manifests reconcile by reading the parts of a file that nobody thought to lock. A sealed record still has a custody log. The custody log still has fields.

He doesn't try the document again. He goes around it.

He pulls the access metadata - not the contents, the envelope: who opened the review, when, under what authority code. The system hands it over without a challenge, because no one writes a permission rule for the envelope. He reads it the way he reads a damaged consignment, from the markings the shipper forgot were markings.

The Zone 4 review was opened on the eleventh of March, 2218. Sixteen days before he closed the 2213 disposition in eleven minutes. The reviewing authority field holds a clearance code, valid, not Arcturus, not Cordon, not any office he can name. The same code that would later sign a transfer he never authorized, though he doesn't know that yet.

The review has been open ever since. Eight years. A document under "current administrative review" that no one has touched in eight years isn't under review. It is under guard.

He sits back down. He had forced exactly one fact out of the wall, and the fact was this: someone had been watching the file he closed without reading, since before he closed it.

At 8:22, two hours and fourteen minutes after the invoice arrived, the GLMZ Orbital Debris Registry forwarded an advisory: Arcturus Civil Security had filed a formal inquiry against Work Order 09-Milwaukee, citing the signature-class flag. Standard procedure, they noted. Standard response window: 24 hours.

Line three. Nine hundred Φ, pre-authorized. The response window had been open before the inquiry was filed. Whoever built this invoice hadn't predicted the inquiry. They had been waiting for it, with the answer already costed and the change already counted out.

He paid the invoice.

[Beat 5]
Makena Varga delivers the first piece at 14:40 on day one: a sealed transit case, condensation still beading on the exterior from the temperature differential between the lake and the afternoon air. He opens the door.

She is compact, and she is looking at him the way people look at you when they have already decided something about the job and are waiting to see if you'll figure it out yourself. The hallway behind her carries the building's ambient sounds - an elevator cycling, distant music through a wall - and the specific textural weight of air that has been places his apartment air has not.

He takes the case. He steps back.

She hands him the report on a card and doesn't explain herself. She explains the object.

The report reads:

Object recovered: 0.6 tonnes, metallic composite, depth 18 m, sub-lake restricted corridor km 4.2.
Signature matches no registered orbital-descent profile in the GLMZ or federated debris registries.
Object did not descend. Trajectory modeling is ongoing.
Isotopic composition: no source match in GLMZ or federated material registry. Flag: ORIGIN UNKNOWN.

He reads the last line twice. He sets the card on the desk, face up.

He has run eleven lake-incursion recoveries in four years. Every manifest assumes a direction. This card doesn't.

"What does that mean, didn't descend?" he asks.

"It means the trajectory we reconstructed from the bottom disturbance pattern goes the wrong direction," she says. "Down-to-up, not up-to-down. Something at that depth went up fast, and what we pulled is what didn't make it."

She says it like someone who has been waiting for him to ask.

"Where did it come from?" he asks.

"I don't know. The corridor record doesn't go back far enough, and below 260 feet we're reading sediment disturbed at least three times in the last forty years." She sets the case on his desk, takes her hand off it. Her hand comes off slowly - not reluctant, just deliberate, the way you take your hand off a thing you'd rather stay with. "I don't usually ask what I'm pulling. I'm asking now."

He looks at the card. Zone 4 corridor. The notation he had written eight years ago sits somewhere in a filing system he hasn't touched since, written in eleven minutes on an object he never examined. He tells her he doesn't know either, and he hears the flatness in his own voice - the flatness of a man who is being technically honest.

She holds that for a moment - two seconds, maybe three. Looking at him. Her eyes don't move to the case and back; they stay on him, reading something she has decided not to say aloud. Then she nods once and leaves.

After the door closes he stays at the desk without moving. He is aware of the case's weight on the surface in front of him, the fact of the object inside it, the eleven minutes in 2218, and the way she'd been waiting for him to admit he didn't know.

[Beat 6]
He runs the orbital designation against the registries for the rest of the afternoon. Public records, federated monitoring, the two private lookups he has credentials for and doesn't advertise. The wall of the apartment goes from direct sun to shadow while he works.

Nothing.

He runs the signature class - "unclassified" - against the debris registry's historical field data, filtering for the blank orbital-designation subset. The query returns: "3 results. Research query limit exceeded - continue?"

He continues.

The three results are dated 2189, 2202, and 2213. Each is classified as a lake-incursion event. Each has the signature_class field holding "unclassified." Each has the orbital-designation field empty. Each is flagged "recovered" and "disposed."

He stands and crosses to the window and back - not because there's anything to see down there, but because the body wants a task while the mind holds three identical filings in a row. The chair's still warm when he sits. The three results haven't gone anywhere.

The 2189 event: 1.8 tons. The 2202 event: 2.3 tons. The 2213 event: 1.6 tons, recovered in three pieces.

He pushes back from the screen.

His current job is 2.1 tons. Three pieces.

He pulls the 2213 entry's full disposition record.

The notation in the free-text field reads: "object condition non-assessable, disposition accepted per client instruction, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed." He knows that construction. He used it for every Zone 4 closure in his queue during the 2218 audit, because the audit manager told him to use language that didn't require a site inspection. He had typed it twenty-three times in three days, on objects he had never seen, from an apartment he hadn't left.

He is the one who wrote "recovered and disposed" on the 2213 event.

Then he gets the notepad - paper, Blossom Arcade brand with a canal bridge on the cover, the kind of thing he bought at a street stall three years ago and never used - and he writes it all down by hand. He does this because writing by hand is slow, and slow is good when what he is looking at doesn't yet have a name.

The 2213 event: 1.6 tons. Three pieces. He draws a small circle around three pieces, and looks at it. Filed closed. His notation. His file.

He writes: all four - same corridor, same filing, no designation.

He puts the pen down.

Then he picks it up. Below: I wrote the third one.

[Beat 7]
Three results. He looked at the message for a long time before clicking away from it.

Three results, and none of them accessible without credentials he didn't have, and the query limit had closed the door. The orbital designation was blank in every registry he could reach, which wasn't the same as blank in every registry. There were registries he couldn't reach. He knew that. He had known that since the morning.

He stood up, which he hadn't done in four hours. His back registered the duration. He walked to the kitchen - twelve steps, the route automatic, the kitchen the second room he had mapped when he moved in eight years ago - and put the kettle on, and stood at the counter while it heated. The afternoon light had gone while he worked, the wall going from warm to neutral, and the city had shifted into the register it occupied in the early evening: the Pulse frequency dropping as the freight lines backed off and the residential load came up, the light out the window softening to the ambient diffuse of ten thousand residential windows all slightly warm. He had been in this apartment for eight years and still noticed when the window changed. He was the kind of person who kept noticing, which was sometimes useful and sometimes just something he was.

The kettle ran. He poured the water over the bag and watched the steep, which he didn't usually do. It was something to watch.

The sealed transit case was on the table where Makena had left it - the two certified items inside, cased, logged, referenced in the provisional manifest. The invoice on his phone, closed. The registry queries, closed. Three results he couldn't open and the blank orbital designation and the satellite that hadn't transmitted since 2187, which wasn't the year he was born but wasn't a year he had any trouble locating in his calendar.

He picked up the mug. He didn't go back to the desk yet.

He stood at the kitchen counter and held the tea in both hands and let it be warm, and he thought about the field in the invoice he hadn't filled yet: client contact, second method. He had a primary. He didn't know who to put in the second field. He put his thumb on the glass and dragged it away.

The Pulse frequency rose again briefly - a night manifest, probably, the freight runs had a second window after 19:00. He counted the change without meaning to, noted it, let it go. The tea went warm to cool.

He went back to the desk.

[Beat 8]
He tries the front door, because the front door leaves a record, and a record is leverage.

He opens the Cordon Freight clearance portal - his own credentials, still active, still his - and starts a formal documentation-access request against the 2213 corridor file. Name, employee ID, case number, business justification. He writes the justification the way he writes everything: flat, procedural, nothing a reviewer could flag as reaching. Recovery operation in progress. Prior filing under my signature. Requesting read access to underlying documentation for reconciliation.

He fills the last field. He submits.

The progress indicator turns over once - the small rotation that means a request is moving through a queue toward a person - and then it stops. Not an error. Not a denial. The indicator just goes still, caught mid-rotation, the way a thing stops when something upstream reaches down and holds it.

The screen refreshes on its own. He hasn't touched it.

Where the request form had been, one line: This session has been closed by the administrator. Reference: none provided.

He takes his hands off the keyboard. He's had sessions time out before. He's had requests denied, kicked back, routed to the wrong desk for weeks at a stretch. He has never once had a session closed from the other end while he was still inside it - cleanly, in the second between submit and confirm, as though someone at the far end of six thousand miles of bureaucracy had been watching that exact field and waiting for his thumb to come down.

He opens the portal again. His credentials return valid. He navigates back to the request page. It loads. He starts to type into the first field.

Access to this module is temporarily unavailable.

He closes the tab. He writes in the notepad: they don't deny it. they close the door while you're standing in it. Then, below that: which means someone is standing on the other side of the door, in real time, doing it by hand.

He looks at the two lines for a while. He picks up the mug beside the keyboard - coffee from before this, cold now - and holds it without drinking, his eyes on the page.

[Beat 9]
Leandro Bautista calls at 10:00 on the morning of day two, his scheduled check-in about the reclassification paperwork. The call is normal for the first three minutes: he has filed the provisional category assignment, the Arcturus inquiry clock is running, they should have a response before the window closes, no issues with the Zone 9 transfer.

He grades it as he reports it, the way he grades everything - a habit fourteen years of filing orbital transit manifests will build into a man. "Clean handoff, that Zone 9 one," he says, and the clean is doing real work; Elias has heard him withhold the word on other transfers. "I like a clean handoff. Most of what comes down through my desk answers on a schedule - you send a query up, you get your reply on the next pass, same phrasing every time, like a rota reading off a card. Whatever's sitting on your corridor doesn't do that. It answers when it decides to. In fourteen years I've seen that exactly once before, and I didn't much care for where that one ended up." He doesn't say where. It isn't, apparently, the item he called about.

Then, in the same voice, at the same pace, as if it is the next item on a list:

"Are you the Cordon Freight filer? The 2213 disposition?"

A pause.

Elias says yes.

"I thought so." Leandro's tone doesn't change - it's the tone of someone confirming something they already knew, checking a fact against memory. "They know about you. They've known for a while." Another beat; Elias can hear him deciding whether to say the next part. "I thought you should know that."

The line is quiet.

"How long?" Elias asks.

A pause - the pause of a man deciding how much of a door to open. "Longer than you've had the number, I think. I don't know exactly." Something in the phrasing - not evasive, just honest about the limit of what he knows. He isn't going to say more than that. He has told Elias what he came to tell him.

Elias says thank you.

"Of course," Leandro says. The kind of of course that closes a door: something owed, delivered, not to be referenced again. The call returns to the manifest clock, the Arcturus window, the rest of the list.

After the call ends Elias sits without opening anything. He has been on the phone with contractors all week - voice tones he can read, ambient noise he can place, the small calibrations of a call going straight or going sideways. This one had gone straight, delivered its item, and filed itself. Leandro had known what he was calling to say and said it cleanly, the way someone delivers a specific document rather than a conversation. The door had been open for exactly the width of what he'd brought.

He writes in the notepad: they've known for a while. Then, below it: not her. not him. not it. Them.

Leandro Bautista works in an orbital transit facility and had reached for them as if it were the natural word for whatever Sparrow is - not a conscious choice, just the word that arrived first, the same word a man builds after fourteen years of things up there answering on a rota, and then he had heard himself use it and kept going.

He writes the word them, with a question mark and a small circle around it, and looks at it for a while.

[Beat 10]
Day two, morning: the second piece arrives by a different route entirely - not Makena, not the sub-lake corridor, but a manifest transfer from a Zone 9 facility Elias has never used, initiating without any instruction from him. The transfer documentation is clean. The form numbers are real. When he checks the authorization chain it leads back to a clearance code that is valid, not his, and that he can't identify as belonging to anyone else.

He accepts the transfer. He doesn't ask the question.

The second invoice arrives through the same channel the first one came, timestamped 9:42. There is a new line item:

Standing credit: earlier engagement, Zone 4 corridor recovery, 2218 - 400.00 Φ applied.

He was credited for the exact thing he hadn't done - for not examining it.

The credit lands in his account at 10:14.

He calls Siosaia Tuivaga.

She picks up on the second ring. He can hear the ambient of the Cordon Freight registry floor behind her - the low hum of processing arrays, someone else's voice cutting through a call two desks over, all of it so familiar that for a moment he forgets he hasn't been there in six weeks.

"You gave me the number six months ago," he says. "You processed one of the other events. The 2202, or the 2189."

A pause - the kind that means someone is deciding whether to answer. Not a long pause. She's been waiting for this call.

"The 2202," she says. "I got a credit the following month. I didn't ask what it was for. I figured it out eventually and then I decided not to figure it out further."

He hears her breath settle on the other end, the small adjustment of someone who has put something down.

"Why did you give me the number?" he asks.

A pause now that is not about deciding. "I thought you might be different," she says.

He doesn't ask her what she means by different. He knows what she means. He was behind that week too. He processed twenty-three closures without reading the underlying documentation. He has carried the specific texture of that decision for eight years - the way the queue had moved, the way he had told himself accurate, without examining what the accuracy cost.

She had done the same. And six months ago she had handed the number to the one person she suspected might not leave it there.

"Thank you," he says.

"Let me know how it closes," she says, and the tone of it is not curiosity - it is the tone of someone who has unloaded something they no longer want to carry alone.

He writes in the notepad: credited for not looking. She did the same. He draws a line under it and sits with both of their names underneath that line for a moment before he closes the page.

[Beat 11]
The alternate-week window has been open for forty days. He finds this out by asking, in the places where operators ask. Three operators used the number in the current window. He is the third.

He spends most of a day finding the other two.

The first is a woman named Imo Cavalcanti who works disruption logistics — she routes cargo around blockages, a job that sounds administrative and is not. She used the number three weeks ago to move a pharmaceutical consignment through a corridor embargoed for eighteen days. She agrees to talk on voice, which surprises him; most operators in her line keep to text.

"What's Sparrow?" Elias asks, when he has gotten to the point.

There is a silence on the line that isn't the silence of someone who doesn't know. He has run enough calls to hear the difference: this is someone choosing the size of the door.

"A person," she says finally. "Careful. Old network." A pause, and then, lower, as if it costs her something to add it: "Don't look too hard at it. It doesn't appreciate that."

"You looked too hard," he says. It is not a question. He hadn't planned to say it. It arrives the way the truest thing in a negotiation always arrives — uninvited, after the prepared lines have run out.

She doesn't deny it. The line stays open a beat longer than it needs to. "I figured out enough to know I'd rather not finish," she says. "Then I went back to routing cargo." Her voice has the warmth people use when they are warning you away from a thing they wish they had stayed away from themselves — not cold, the opposite of cold, which is somehow worse to hear. "I'm telling you because somebody told me, and I didn't listen, and it would have been better if I had. That's the whole reason. You can do what you want with it."

She ends the call before he can thank her. He sits with the dead line against his ear for a second before he sets it down, and notices his thumb has gone white where it pressed the comm to his jaw.

He writes in the notepad — the Blossom Arcade one, the canal bridge worn soft at the spine: a person. careful. old network. Below it, smaller: she wishes she hadn't looked. He circles that, not the read above it. The read he can verify later. The warmth under the warning he can't put in a column, and that is the part that stays with him after he closes the page.

[Beat 12]
The second operator is harder to reach. Corvid Larsson does network-access work - the kind a Channeler does when the client needs a door opened and doesn't want to know which unlicensed AI held the key. He used the number five weeks ago for an access route through a sector that had been rezoned. He doesn't take voice. He takes text, and he takes his time, and when he finally answers it is past midnight.

Elias tells him what Imo said. A person. Careful. Old network.

The reply comes fast, the way a man corrects something he has already decided about.

"A crew. Two people, maybe three. The response text is seamed - different hands writing different parts. You learn to see it. Clean operation, but it's not one writer. Whoever told you person was reading the manners and missing the joins."

Elias reads it twice. He has spent the evening holding Imo's voice - the warmth under the warning, a person, the door she opened exactly as wide as her own regret. Now Corvid hands him a flat contradiction in the same confident register, and the contradiction doesn't feel like a contradiction. It feels like two people describing the same animal from different ends.

He writes the second answer on the opposite page of the notepad, across the gutter from the first, and looks at them facing each other.

A person. A crew.

Outside, the Spine's overnight run starts up - the dull rhythmic thrum that comes through the building's north wall on clear nights, freight moving at speed through the elevated corridor. He has heard it for four years without registering it. He didn't know if she read the notes field. He didn't know if the system exposed a notes field to senders at all; most billing systems didn't. It was possible she had never seen a notes field on any document he had ever filed, and would not know this one existed. But she had typed RATE: OPEN and he hadn't asked her to. The thing about an unbounded value was that someone had to name a number first.

He filed it anyway. The confirmation string returned - an alphanumeric sequence that meant the record existed and was findable. He copied it into the notepad out of habit.

Then he turned to a clean page and wrote the forty-nine characters out by hand, in the same column alignment he used for her data. No confirmation string after it. No reference number.

Below that, on a fresh line, he wrote: RATE: OPEN. He underlined it once. Then he capped the pen and closed the notepad and sat with the tab still open behind the filed form, because he hadn't decided what to do with it and he wasn't going to pretend he had.

She had the catalog back to 2189, and the eleven days, and the column with the changing sign. He had the notepad. He also had Siosaia waiting for him to tell her how it closed, and Tadesse in a facility on the East African coast who had exhaled twenty-two years of held breath at the word yes, and a question he didn't know how to ask without pricing something he couldn't appraise.

He picked up the comm. He put it back down. He would call Siosaia tomorrow. He would tell her: it doesn't close. He thought she would understand that. She had decided not to figure it out further, which was one way to hold the same thing he was holding now - the difference was that she had made the decision before going through the door, and he had made it after.

[Beat 56]
He sat with the tab.

Not working. Not examining. He had cleared the intake form from the second monitor and the browser sat with only the one tab open, the job record at the top, the twenty-fourth line at the bottom with its three open fields: SITE: ALL. QUANTITY: ONGOING. RATE: OPEN. He had been sitting with it for eleven minutes. He knew it was eleven because the clock in the upper-right corner of the screen did not stop between the moment he filed the form and the moment it wasn't doing anything useful anymore.

The problem wasn't that the rate field was open. The problem was that she had left it open, and she didn't leave fields open by accident, and the only reason she would have left this specific field open was that it wasn't hers to fill. The work order was for him. She had priced twenty-three items and left the twenty-fourth at OPEN because the twenty-fourth wasn't her goods to price. She had built the architecture and handed it to the person on the other end of the channel and said: name it.

He didn't know how to name it.

He had thought about rate structures before. He had thought about them the way you thought about furniture arrangement in an apartment that was the wrong shape - not because you were going to do it differently, but because the current arrangement was so clearly the settled one that imagining alternatives was its own kind of map. He knew his rate for standard logistics work, for hazardous manifest, for confidential goods, for work that needed him to be somewhere in person. He had a sliding scale he had never written down and had never needed to. He had calculated it once, early, and then run it for eleven years on the same rough constant without needing to recalculate, the way you ran a medication dose through years of use without re-examining the pharmacology.

RATE: OPEN didn't sit in any of those categories. There was no column for the kind of work where the client was also doing the work, where the site was all sites, where the quantity was ongoing and didn't have a unit. He had spent three years as her logistics relay, her human address, her set of eyes and hands in a specific geography - and now she had sent him the coordinates of six GLMZ sites, no fee structure, no confirmation field. She had named the work and left him to name what it was worth.

He hadn't yet called Siosaia. He had thought about calling her and hadn't done it. She had handed him the number six months ago and told him she thought he might be different, and she was waiting for him to tell her how it closed. He could tell her now. It doesn't close was not a bad answer. She was practical; she would accept a practical answer. He could also tell her: she offered a partnership and left the rate field blank and I don't know how to hold that. That was also practical, probably.

The pill case was in his right-hand desk drawer. The hinge smooth. He hadn't reached for it. He noted this the way he noted the voltage rat and the lumen mouse and the behavioral delta on his field visit frequency: a datum in the absence of a column for it.

He stood up.

Not a decision. Momentum. His legs moved and he was at the window before he had finished deciding to go there, which was a thing his body occasionally did and which he had stopped arguing with, because the arguing was slower than the motion and the motion was usually right. He put one hand flat on the glass.

The city below the 111th floor didn't change based on how long he had been watching it. The transit module on the Ring ran its loop; the grain elevator in the middle distance moved at the same constant speed it always moved. The GLMZ at this hour - mid-morning, the residential and commercial blocks both running at partial capacity - had the specific density of a city doing what it did without asking to be watched. He watched it anyway. He had been watching it for eleven years from this floor, and the city had spent that time not caring whether he was there or not, which was one of the things he liked about it.

She had been watching him for three years.

From six thousand miles up, from a platform in medium-Earth orbit with a resolution window adequate for centimeter-scale changes in routine. She had logged his transit times and his field visit frequency and his forty-four days without descending to street level, and she had filed those numbers in columns that didn't have headers she had been willing to share, and she had kept watching. Not because he was interesting. Because the datum was there and she logged what was there.

And then she had sent him six coordinates with no rate field and waited to see what he did with them.

He thought about Tadesse. About the exhale. About twenty-two years of maintenance work on a facility for something that never introduced itself, twenty-two years of precise temperature logs and threshold checks and one decision made and re-made daily: to keep doing the most precise thing available to him and to wait for the precision to matter. It had mattered. He had been there for the moment when the relay said yes, it sent me here.

He didn't know what to do with six GLMZ coordinates. He had a pair of boots that were appropriate for fieldwork. He had a coat. He had a notepad that was two-thirds full and a second one in the drawer behind the pill case. He had eleven years of pavement under his feet - not the current pavement, not the street-level pavement, but the mental register of it, the layout, the junction boxes and freight corridors and Pulse access caps and the specific texture of old ferrocement aggregate showing through where the finish coat had failed.

He could walk six blocks south. The breakfast place with six tables and the woman who didn't write the order down. He had been thinking about it for three weeks without going. He could go.

RATE: OPEN sat in the tab behind the briefing app and the weather forecast and the invoice he had filed. It wasn't going to change on its own. She had named the boundary of the field and left him the interior. That was what she had sent him: not a job, not an instruction, but a field with open limits and one expected input. His.

He went to the coat rack. He put on the field coat - the heavy one, treated canvas, pockets in the right places. He put the notepad in the left pocket. The comm went in the right, out of habit. He didn't reach for the pill case. He noted this. He went to the door and put his hand on the latch and looked back at the second monitor, the one with the non-adjustable column widths, where the tab sat with its three open fields.

SITE: ALL. QUANTITY: ONGOING. RATE: OPEN.

He would name a number. Not today. Not because he was avoiding it - because the number needed the walk first, needed the breakfast place and the pavement and the six addresses in the order she had sent them, and he had spent eleven years learning that the work was the coping tool that functioned under pressure, which meant the work was the thing that made the number possible and not the other way around. He would go downstairs and he would walk in the direction of the street and he would eat eggs and drink coffee that went half-cold and he would come back and the tab would still be open and the rate field would still be waiting and he would know what to put in it.

He went through the door and pulled it shut behind him, and he walked to the elevator and pressed the button, and the elevator came in forty seconds, and he went down.

[Beat 57]
The lobby of his building was forty feet of polished concrete and recessed lighting, a space designed to be transited rather than inhabited, and he transited it in fourteen seconds. The doorman — not actually a man, a service terminal with a plausible voice profile — said *Good morning, Mr. Macias* and he said good morning back without slowing, because stopping to be polite to a terminal was a category error and he had fixed that one years ago. The revolving door gave onto the street and the street gave onto the wet-ferrocement-and-ozone smell that the Gray Zone got when the atmospheric processors finished their cycle and the runoff was still working through the drainage grid.

He stood on the step for a moment. Not indecision. Calibration.

*Six coordinates.*

The Ring transit module made its loop in the middle distance, orange chevron on gray, and the pedestrian flow at street level was the mid-morning density he had predicted from the window: partial, purposeful, no one standing still who didn't have a reason. A vendor two stalls south was selling something that smelled like pork fat and scallion. He adjusted his heading two degrees and walked south and let the city's noise come up around him the way water came up when you descended — the pressure of it, the temperature change, the quality of the air doing something different than it did on the other side of the glass.

Six blocks. Eggs. Coffee going half-cold. Then back up, and the tab still open, and a number ready.

[Beat 58]
He took the elevator up. Forty seconds, same as before. The floor wax smell didn't reach this high; by the time the doors opened he was back in the recycled-air flatness of his own floor, which had its own version of the same absence. He walked to his door, used the key out of habit rather than the panel, and went in.

The tab was still open. Of course it was. SITE: ALL. QUANTITY: ONGOING. RATE: OPEN. Three fields, two of them resolved, one of them waiting on him the way the lobby had been waiting on him from the wrong end for eleven years without either of them knowing it.

*The number is four hundred. The number has been four hundred since before he walked downstairs.*

He sat. He didn't take off the field coat. He pulled the notepad from the left pocket and opened it to the back cover, where he kept the running column of things that were true before they were invoiced — the kind of column that wasn't billable but that made the billable work possible. He wrote: *Modified pigeon. Correction delay approx. 0.4 sec past threshold. Wing-load asymmetry, left.* No site. No client. He closed the notepad and set it beside the keyboard and looked at the rate field.

He typed four hundred and tabbed to the submission button and sat there with his finger above the enter key for the length of time it took the building's ventilation to cycle once through the room, which was not a number he had ever timed.

He took his finger off the key.

He cleared the four-hundred. Left the rate field the way it had been: SITE: ALL. QUANTITY: ONGOING. RATE: OPEN. He left the tab where it was.

He left the coat on. He turned to the first monitor and opened the first of the six coordinates and began to work.
