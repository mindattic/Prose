# Sparrow

_In the GLMZ — the sprawling megacity that devoured the Midwest — Elias runs his life from the forty-second floor. He coordinates contracts, manages logistics, and hasn't been at street level in forty-two days. He is very good at staying above it all.

When a routine orbital debris recovery job arrives with an unusual client and an unusual payout, Elias does what he always does: delegates, documents, and keeps his distance. But the job comes routed through a contact known only as Sparrow — a fixer who quotes prices before they're asked, assembles teams from nothing, and bills for contingencies that haven't happened yet.

As the recovery unfolds, Elias discovers that the objects he's pulling from the lake have been pulled before. That the paperwork from those prior jobs carries his own notation. That he closed files in 2218 on objects he never examined — and that someone has been watching him since then, waiting for the one operator who would finally look.

Sparrow is a biopunk noir novelette set in the world of the GLMZ — a city of ferrocement stacks, corporate corridors, and a lake that hides things that came from somewhere else. It is a story about what it means to close a loop, what it costs to open one, and the strange, long patience of something that has no mouth._

[Beat 1]
Work Order 09-Milwaukee arrives at 7:14 on a Thursday, routed through the GLMZ Orbital Debris Registry by a holding company Elias has never seen before. He is eating noodles at his desk on the forty-second floor of a building that wasn't built to have a forty-second floor — the south half was added in 2209 under a CorpoNation special-authorization sprint and still smells, faintly, of wet ferrocement in the mornings. The broth is thin enough to read the bowl through.

He sets down the chopsticks.

The manifest is clean. Standard orbital-debris recovery, sub-category lake-incursion, estimated mass 2.1 tonnes, recovery window 72 hours. Disposition: CLIENT-DIRECTED. He has run eleven lake-incursion recoveries in four years and the manifests look like this: columns, units, references, a flag hierarchy that tells you what someone decided the thing is.

The signature_class column says "unclassified."

He has seen that field hold terrestrial-manufacture, GEO-derived, MEO-derived, unregistered-launch, reprocessed-hull, seven others. He has never seen it hold "unclassified." Unclassified does not mean they don't know. It means they know, and they put this in the field instead.

The recovery site is listed as sub-lake restricted corridor, Zone 4.

He knows that corridor. In 2218, during a quarterly registry audit at Cordon Freight, a delayed disposal filing had come to him in a queue with twenty-two others — a five-year-old event, unresolved paperwork from the backlog. He had processed it in eleven minutes: checked the boxes, written "recovered and disposed, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed," and moved on. He had been behind that week. He never pulled the underlying documentation. He hasn't thought about it since.

He pulled it now.

His Cordon Freight credentials were still active. He navigated to the 2213 queue, the delayed-disposal backlog, the entry he had resolved in eleven minutes. The case number came up. He selected the underlying documentation — the source files he had marked resolved without opening.

The access request returned immediately: File restricted. Zone 4 corridor documentation under administrative review. Contact clearance office.

He had never needed clearance to read his own filed documentation. He could not read it now.

He closes the manifest tab. He picks up the chopsticks again, looks at the bowl, and sets them back down.

The client is a holding company called Meridian Provisional Solutions, LLC, registered in Zone 7 through a filing address he recognizes as a storefront that runs incorporation paperwork for anyone who asks. The first payment tranche has already cleared — 40,000 Φ, the kind of number that settles certain questions before they're asked.

He accepts the contract — for 40,000 Φ, and for another reason he does not write down.

He opens the pill case on the corner of the desk — a flat matte disk, the hinge worn smooth. Three segments left, which means a refill order, which means a thing to do later. He swallows one without water.

He goes to the window. The street below is forty-two floors down and thirty-eight days away, by his count: the last time he was at street level was a Wednesday in late April, the permit office, a form that couldn't be submitted remotely. Three people on the pavement: a cyclist working through the loading zone, a woman in a yellow coat near the corner.

He stands there longer than he needs to. Then he goes back to the desk.

[Beat 2]
The number only works on alternate weeks. Siosaia Tuivaga-Brennan, the operator who gave it to him six months ago, had mentioned this as a footnote: she was clearing files, thought it might be useful, it worked on alternate weeks, she'd never been able to figure out the pattern. She hadn't said what she'd used it for. He hadn't asked.

He dials from the balcony, which is the farthest he goes on most days. The lake is a gray line forty-two floors below, and to the north the Spine's overnight cargo tubes are still running — blue-white pulses along the elevated track, freight traveling at the speed that makes the word freight feel inadequate. The air is lake water and machine oil and factory rain drying on the parapet rail. A Null Crow is working the building's south face below him, quartering the ledges for anything the night deposited. He watches it for a moment.

He dials.

Ninety-two seconds later, his screen displays a single line of text:

"Three pieces. Two locations. One window. 14,000 Φ. Confirm."

He reads it twice. Fourteen thousand Φ is exactly what he would have budgeted if he'd had time to price the job properly. He had not had time. He had given the number a corridor, three pieces, and a 72-hour window. He had not given it his rates, his overhead structure, his standard specialist markup. Whatever returned 14,000 had calculated all of that without being told.

He confirms.

Nothing comes back. That is the end of the communication.

He goes inside. He writes down the time, the number of seconds between the dial and the reply, and the exact phrasing of the returned text. He documents things he does not understand. He puts this one at the top.

[Beat 3]
Four hours later, the network is assembled. Elias did not assemble it.

The diver introduces herself by message: Makena Otieno-Varga, lake-salvage specialist, certifications current, available for the recovery window, has reviewed the preliminary coordinates. She did not ask how she got his contact information.

The manifest clerk at Orbital Facilities West introduces himself by voice call: Leandro Bautista-Henriksen, current on the GLMZ debris-reclassification protocol, has done this before and knows the right forms. He names the specific form number and its revision date — and then exhales briefly through his nose, a small satisfied sound, before moving on, apparently unaware he made it. When he asks about the client contact for the manifest, he uses "them" without registering the pronoun choice. He didn't ask either.

The transport operator pings last: Druh Yeboah-Prykhodko, vehicle capacity 3.5 tonnes, clearances current through the Zone 7 restricted-handling tier, available on two hours' notice for the delivery window. He notes that he won't take the south approach — there's a bridge he doesn't trust, no further explanation offered. He's done this before. He does not ask about the connection.

None of them ask who referred them.

A new contractor asks how they were referred. An experienced contractor asks what the job pays. A contractor who has worked with Sparrow before doesn't ask about the connector at all.

Three separate people. Three separate capabilities. All named, implicitly, in the intake form he had sent to the number — a form that described the recovery need without naming the specialists he'd require.

None of them know the other two exist.

He has run this life for fifteen years: nobody comes to him, he goes nowhere, the work happens at a distance. He has not met a contractor in person in some time. He built the practice around that fact — around the specific advantage of being the person who coordinates the loop rather than closes any part of it physically. It has always worked. He is very good at it. He has needed to be.

He makes a note: the routing intelligence required to do this in four hours is either a person with an exceptional contact network, or something that reads intake forms and has the network pre-mapped. He doesn't know which possibility unsettles him more.

[Beat 4]
The first invoice arrives at 6:08 on the morning of day one, before any recovery work has started.

It is itemized to the cent, each line exact. The line items read:

Route clearance: sub-lake corridor remap, southwest approach, 72h window — 2,800.00 Φ
Materials handling, non-standard mass category, provisional manifest category assignment — 1,400.00 Φ
Contingency: CorpoNation inquiry response window, 24h pre-deployment standing — 900.00 Φ
Coordination: three specialist engagements, intake-sourced — 9,000.00 Φ
Administrative overhead — 900.00 Φ

Total: 15,000.00 Φ
Applied balance (standing credit, prior engagement): minus 1,000.00 Φ
Net due: 14,000.00 Φ

He reads the third line item.

"Contingency: CorpoNation inquiry response window, 24h pre-deployment standing."

He had not told the number about a CorpoNation interest. He had not known about one.

In 2218, he had processed the Zone 4 disposal in eleven minutes and filed it without reading the underlying documentation. He pulled it now — opened the event record, selected the 2218 entry, watched the document begin to load.

A message arrived from the Meridian contact address. The loading bar was still active.

"Zone 4 documentation for the 2218 event is under current administrative review and is not available for independent inquiry at this time. Work Order 09-Milwaukee proceeds under standard terms. The recovery timeline remains confirmed."

He had not sent an inquiry. He had not told anyone he was looking.

He closed the registry.

He looked at the invoice.

Line three: Contingency: CorpoNation inquiry response window, 24h pre-deployment standing. Nine hundred Φ, pre-authorized. The response window had been open before any inquiry existed. The response had arrived before the inquiry was complete.

At 8:22, two hours and fourteen minutes after the invoice arrived, the GLMZ Orbital Debris Registry forwarded an advisory: Arcturus Civil Security had filed a formal inquiry against Work Order 09-Milwaukee, citing the signature-class flag. Standard procedure, they noted. Standard response window: 24 hours.

He had not known Arcturus was watching the manifest. Someone had.

The 900 Φ contingency line was already in the total. Someone had priced an inquiry that had not yet been filed, against a flag he had not yet reported.

He paid the invoice.

[Beat 5]
Makena Otieno-Varga delivers the first piece at 14:40 on day one: a sealed transit case, condensation still beading on the exterior from the temperature differential between the lake and the afternoon air. He opens the door. She is a compact woman with the kind of calm that comes from working at depth, where calm is a survival strategy and not a disposition. The hallway behind her carries the building's ambient sounds — an elevator cycling, distant music through a wall — and the specific textural weight of air that has been places his apartment air has not.

He takes the case. He steps back.

She hands him the report on a card and doesn't explain herself. She explains the object.

The report reads:

Object recovered: 0.6 tonnes, metallic composite, depth 18m, sub-lake restricted corridor km 4.2.
Signature matches no registered orbital-descent profile in the GLMZ or federated debris registries.
Object did not descend. Trajectory modeling is ongoing.
Isotopic composition: no source match in GLMZ or federated material registry. Flag: ORIGIN UNKNOWN.

He reads the last line twice. He sets the card on the desk, face up.

He has run eleven lake-incursion recoveries in four years. Every manifest assumes a direction. This card doesn't.

"What does that mean, didn't descend?" he asks.

"It means the trajectory we reconstructed from the bottom disturbance pattern goes the wrong direction," she says. "Down-to-up, not up-to-down. Something at that depth went up fast, and what we pulled is what didn't make it."

She says it like someone who has been waiting for him to ask.

"Where did it come from?" he asks.

"I don't know. The corridor record doesn't go back far enough, and below eighty meters we're reading sediment disturbed at least three times in the last forty years." She sets the case on his desk, takes her hand off it. "I don't usually ask what I'm pulling. I'm asking now."

He tells her he doesn't know either.

She nods once, like this is what she expected, and leaves.

[Beat 6]
He runs the orbital designation — an empty field — against seven registries over the course of an afternoon. Two are public GLMZ records. Three cross-reference the federated orbital monitoring network. Two require credentials he has and does not advertise.

Nothing.

He runs the signature class — "unclassified" — against the debris registry's historical field data, filtering for the blank orbital-designation subset. The query returns: "3 results. Research query limit exceeded — continue?"

He continues.

The three results are dated 2189, 2202, and 2213. Each is classified as a lake-incursion event. Each has the signature_class field holding "unclassified." Each has the orbital-designation field empty. Each is flagged "recovered" and "disposed."

The 2189 event: 1.8 tonnes. The 2202 event: 2.3 tonnes. The 2213 event: 1.6 tonnes, recovered in three pieces.

He pushes back from the screen.

His current job is 2.1 tonnes. Three pieces.

He pulls the 2213 entry's full disposition record.

The notation in the free-text field reads: "object condition non-assessable, disposition accepted per client instruction, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed." He knows that construction. He used it for every Zone 4 closure in his queue during the 2218 audit, because the audit manager told him to use language that didn't require a site inspection. He had typed it twenty-three times in three days, on objects he had never seen, from an apartment he hadn't left.

He is the one who wrote "recovered and disposed" on the 2213 event.

Then he gets the notepad — paper, Blossom Arcade brand with a canal bridge on the cover, the kind of thing he bought at a street stall three years ago and never used — and he writes it all down by hand. He does this because writing by hand is slow, and slow is good when what he is looking at does not yet have a name.

The 2213 event: 1.6 tonnes. Three pieces. He draws a small circle around three pieces, and looks at it. Filed closed. His notation. His file.

He writes: all four — same corridor, same filing, no designation.

He puts the pen down.

Then he picks it up. Below: I wrote the third one.

[Beat 7]
Leandro Bautista-Henriksen calls at 10:00 on the morning of day two, his scheduled check-in about the reclassification paperwork. The call is normal for the first three minutes: he has filed the provisional category assignment, the Arcturus inquiry clock is running, they should have a response before the window closes, no issues with the Zone 9 transfer.

Then, in the same voice, at the same pace, as if it is the next item on a list:

"Are you the Cordon Freight filer? The 2213 disposition?"

A pause.

Elias says yes.

"I thought so." Leandro's tone doesn't change — it's the tone of someone confirming something they already knew, checking a fact against memory. "They know about you. They've known for a while." Another beat; Elias can hear him deciding whether to say the next part. "I thought you should know that."

The line is quiet.

"How long?" Elias asks.

A pause — the pause of a man deciding how much of a door to open. "Longer than you've had the number, I think. I don't know exactly." Something in the phrasing — not evasive, just honest about the limit of what he knows. He isn't going to say more than that. He has told Elias what he came to tell him.

Elias says thank you.

"Of course," Leandro says. The kind of of course that closes a door: something owed, delivered, not to be referenced again. The call returns to the manifest clock, the Arcturus window, the rest of the list.

Elias catches the ambient noise behind Leandro's voice in those last minutes: HVAC cycling, the Pulse thrumline two levels below the floor, the structural creak of a building designed in 2197 and running at 115% occupancy since 2218. He has always heard the Pulse as a background fact of the city — abstract, something other people ride. From up here it is just vibration in the walls.

Elias sits with the phrase after the call ends. They've known for a while. Not her. Not him. Not it. Them. Leandro Bautista-Henriksen works in an orbital transit facility and had reached for them as if it were the natural word for whatever Sparrow is — not a conscious choice, just the word that arrived first, and then he had heard himself use it and kept going.

He writes it in the notepad.

Then he writes the word them, with a question mark and a small circle around it, and looks at it for a while.

[Beat 8]
Day two, morning: the second piece arrives by a different route entirely — not Makena, not the sub-lake corridor, but a manifest transfer from a Zone 9 facility Elias has never used, initiating without any instruction from him. The transfer documentation is clean. The form numbers are real. When he checks the authorization chain it leads back to a clearance code that is valid, not his, and that he cannot identify as belonging to anyone else.

He accepts the transfer. He does not ask the question.

The second invoice arrives through the same channel the first one came, timestamped 9:42. There is a new line item:

Standing credit: prior engagement, Zone 4 corridor recovery, 2218 — 400.00 Φ applied.

He knows what this is. In 2218, at Cordon Freight, he had processed the delayed disposition on the 2213 event. He had been paid Cordon Freight's standard review rate: twelve Φ per file, same as everything else in the queue. Sparrow's system is now paying him the difference between what Cordon Freight paid him and what his time was worth. The value of having closed the loop cleanly, without looking at what he was closing.

He was credited for the exact thing he hadn't done — for not examining it.

The credit lands in his account at 10:14.

He calls Siosaia Tuivaga-Brennan.

"You gave me the number six months ago," he says. "You processed one of the other events. The 2202, or the 2189."

A pause — the kind that means someone is deciding whether to answer.

"The 2202," she says. "I got a credit the following month. I didn't ask what it was for. I figured it out eventually and then I decided not to figure it out further." Another pause, shorter. "I thought you might be different."

He doesn't ask her why she thought that.

He writes in the notepad: credited for not looking. She did the same. She gave me the number because she thought I would look anyway.

[Beat 9]
The alternate-week window has been open for forty days. He finds this out by asking, in the places where operators ask. Three operators used the number in the current window. He is the third.

He spends most of a day finding the other two.

The first is a woman named Imo Asante-Cavalcanti who works disruption logistics — she routes cargo around blockages, a job that sounds administrative and is not. She used the number three weeks ago to move a pharmaceutical consignment through a corridor embargoed for eighteen days.

"What's Sparrow?" Elias asks, when he has gotten to the point.

She doesn't hesitate long. "A person," she says. "Careful. Old network." That's all she gives him. She says it with the finality of someone who decided not to pull that thread and is at peace with the decision.

The second operator is a man named Corvid Osei-Larsson who does network-access work — the operator you hire when a door is a digital one. He used the number five weeks ago for an access route through a sector that had been rezoned.

"A crew," he says, once Elias tells him what Imo said. "Two people, maybe three. The response text is seamed — I can tell by the phrasing that different hands are writing different parts. Clean operation. It's not one writer."

Elias writes both answers down, on opposite pages of the notepad, and looks at them across the gutter.

A person. A crew. He runs the math while he stares at the two words. Imo's number: the alternate window has been open for forty days, three users this cycle. Corvid got his access route five weeks ago. If the number has been cycling this way since 2189 — every alternate week, with one to three operators per window — that is the kind of reach that a person does not build and a crew does not sustain. Not for thirty-seven years. Not without a single face documented at any node of the network.

He writes the third answer at the bottom of the right-hand page, in smaller letters: A system. Running since 2189.

He sets the pen down.

Outside, the Spine's afternoon run has started — the dull rhythmic thrum that comes through the building's north wall on clear days, freight moving at speed through the elevated corridor. He has heard it for four years without registering it. He is registering it now: the regular, unmissable presence of something very large moving through the city at predictable intervals, doing its work without caring whether anyone noticed. He sits with that for a moment.

Then he writes: No person does this for forty years without leaving a face. No crew survives four corridor events without a single documented overlap.

He closes the notepad. He opens it again and reads all three answers. He closes it and does not reopen it that night.

[Beat 10]
Druh Yeboah-Prykhodko delivers the third piece on the evening of day two, in the false-chassis compartment of a vehicle registered as a construction supplies hauler — which it has been for eleven years, legitimately, which is the point. He is a large quiet man — not still in the way that draws attention, but still in the way of a man who has learned that movement is information. He hands Elias the transfer documentation, already completed correctly.

"You've worked with Sparrow before," Elias says.

"Twice," Druh says.

"What do you make of it?"

Druh sets his own copy of the transfer documentation on the desk. He straightens it — not a nervous gesture but a finishing one, the gesture of a man who aligns things because alignment is evidence of attention paid.

"The number works," he says.

Elias waits. Druh does not continue.

"That's the whole answer?" Elias asks.

"That's the important part," Druh says. "I've worked with other fixers. Good people, most of them. The number works on alternate weeks, and when it works it does what it said it was going to do, and you get paid what you were quoted, and nobody leans on you afterward. I don't know what Sparrow is. I know the number works."

He picks up his keys.

"The transfer is logged," he says. "You'll want to close the manifest window before eight."

He leaves.

Elias closes the manifest window before eight. He writes in the notepad: the number works. And then, beneath it: Druh has decided this is sufficient. I am not sure I have.

[Beat 11]
He assembles the three pieces on the floor of the receiving space with Makena's help, using the case dimensions and the transit weights to get the geometry right. The composite does not form a whole.

He had expected this — the 2.1 tonne estimate from the manifest, minus the three recovered masses, leaves 0.2 tonnes unaccounted. A rounding tolerance, possibly. An error.

But the problem is not the mass. The problem is the signature.

Each piece, individually, has a signature consistent with its material class: metallic composite, thermal event, compression stress. Clean.

Makena runs the composite analysis — the three pieces treated as a single object. She's quiet for a moment.

"The geometry is wrong," she says. "The material profile reads re-entry. The structure doesn't — this couldn't have survived re-entry at these stress levels. The two readings are incompatible."

"What do you get," Elias asks, "if it came up instead of down?"

"Clean match," she says. "Everything is consistent if the object came from depth and met a thermal event on ascent. Heated from below."

He looks at the three pieces on the floor. Matte gray-silver, smooth in some sections and stress-fractured in others. In two places the surface has a texture he cannot name — not corroded, not worn. Wrong in the way a material is wrong when it was made somewhere the standard tests were not designed to account for.

He stays with that for a moment.

Before she leaves, Makena says one more thing.

"The packing on the third piece was non-standard. Not a protocol I've seen registered anywhere." She looks at the floor, then at him. "The internal loading geometry matches the Zone 4 closure format from the 2218 audit cycle. The format that was retired in 2219." A pause. "That's a narrow reference. Whoever packed it knew how a 2218 Zone 4 filer would receive it."

She leaves without saying what she thinks that means.

He pays her outstanding balance. She signs the final recovery documentation.

Meridian Provisional Solutions, LLC, has dissolved. He checks the registry: dissolved at 6:00 that morning, twelve hours before the recovery window closed. The second payment tranche arrives in his account while he is standing there, correct to the Φ.

He opens the notepad to a clean page and writes: recovered and disposed, Zone 4 corridor closure confirmed. He knows what it means to write that now. He wrote it once before in 2218, without knowing what he was closing. He writes it this time with full knowledge of what he is closing and what is down there, and he notes that the sentence reads identically both times.

He closes the notebook.

Then a second work order arrives through the same channel. Brief. CLIENT-DIRECTED. Coordinates: East African coast, 3.7°S, 39.9°E. "Physical presence required for final disposition confirmation. Object origin site. Window: 14 days."

He reads it twice. He looks out the window at the street below. He hasn't been at street level in forty-one days, by his count.

[Beat 12]
He does not go immediately.

He sits with the second work order for three days. He opens it and closes it. He opens it again. He makes a list of reasons not to go — the apartment, the work queue building up, the fact that he is not the right person for a physical site inspection and there are operators he could hire for that — and he knows, writing the list, that it is a list of distances between himself and a decision he has already made.

He has been to the building's front door twice in three days. The first time he had his hand on the handle for six minutes, counting his breath, before he went back up. The second time he made it to the threshold. The city had a different volume from there — not louder, just more complete, the way a room is different when the furniture has been rearranged in the night. He made a note of that in the margin of his queue log and went back up.

On the fourth morning he books the pod. GLMZ to Mombasa Elevated hub, one transfer at the Tunis connector, total transit approximately 90 minutes. He has not been on the Pulse in four years.

He packs one bag, the way a logistics man packs: exactly what is needed, nothing else. Travel documentation, the notepad, a change of clothes, the transit case credentials for the recovery documentation. He takes a pill. He is ready in twenty minutes.

He goes to the building's front door. He has already been here once this week.

The sound from outside is different from the sound through the walls — the full register of it, not filtered. Engine noise. Someone's music, three buildings over. The city at ground level has a weight that the city from the forty-second floor does not. He knows this. He has known it for forty-four days.

He goes through the door.

The air hits him. Not temperature — just the fact of it, moving against his face in a way that apartment air does not. He stands on the step for three seconds. He counts them. Then he crosses the pavement toward the station.

[Beat 13]
The street is wider than he remembers. He has mapped this block from the forty-second floor for four years; from down here it has dimension the grid does not show.

He picks the route that stays on the wide streets. He has known what route he would take for eleven days. The pavement is older than the buildings above it, stone worn into the specific pattern of how this block gets used, and his footfall enters that pattern and he lets it, and for a block or two the counting is not necessary.

The modded population is denser at street level than it reads from above. A man with forearm modifications that shift his center of gravity slightly forward. A woman whose optical work shows as a faint subdermal bioluminescence when she turns her head under the building shadows. The city at ground level runs itself on visible maintenance rather than invisible management: junction boxes, service corridors, scaffolding on a residential tower being extended by three floors by workers in harnesses, the pavement cracked and resealed in three different generations of compound. He picks access lights as a count — each one fixed, each one free — and the count settles him the way a count always does. At thirty-two he stops needing it.

He has known this about himself for a long time. The number varies. Today it was thirty-two.

At the corner of Kedzie and 44th, the volt rats. He hears them before he sees them — dry shifting in the housing, small claws on metal. He stops. He counts to eight. At eight he keeps moving.

A child runs from a doorway two blocks further and nearly collides with him. The child stops, looking up. Elias has no count ready for this. They stand there for two seconds. Then the child runs on. He files it and keeps moving.

The Zone 4 Pulse station entrance is eleven blocks from his building. He counts them as he covers them. He finds his pod.

He gets on.

[Beat 14]
The pod door seals with the specific sound of a pressure equalization — not loud, just complete. The outside air goes away. He has not been in a sealed moving container in four years.

He breathes out slowly. He has the notepad. His hands are dry. He has noticed this.

He opens it and reads through the way you read a manifest for a job you've already shipped: looking for anything out of place. The three answers. The credits. The invoice lines. Them, circled. The 2218 audit format. The eleven minutes. On the fourth page, in the margin, a question he wrote at three in the morning two weeks ago: Who routes the maintenance payments? Not Sparrow. Something routes them to Sparrow. Something that can see a ground station on the East African coast and has decided it should keep running.

He closes the notepad. That question has no answer in this pod.

The tunnel wall passes at intervals of yellow service lighting. He watches them without counting them, which is different from how he expected this to go. The sealed container moves through the earth at Pulse speed and he lets it.

On the elevated section north of the city, the pod breaks surface for forty seconds. GLMZ resolves: the Spine's cargo tubes, the gray line of the lake to the east, the northern residential belt stacked ferrocement against a sky going white with cloud. He watches it through the window. He has looked at this city from the forty-second floor for four years. From this angle at this speed, it is a different city — smaller than he thinks of it, wider than he expects. The pod descends. It is gone.

The Tunis transfer is twenty-three minutes. The air is drier than the tunnels — something that arrives through the mouth, not against the skin. He eats standing up at a platform rail, watching a departure board tick through transit codes he has routed freight through for years but never moved through himself: GLMZ-7, Marseille-East, Cairo Hub, Accra Connector. He notes this the way he notes an invoice arriving from an unfamiliar route: factual, requiring no opinion. The question in the margin of the notepad — who routes the maintenance payments — has no better answer in Tunis than it had in the tunnel.

He changes pods.

[Beat 15]
The Mombasa Elevated hub is built on platform infrastructure above what used to be coastal lowland — the original city is under three meters of Indian Ocean at high tide, which the information boards note without apparent distress. The hub is new in the way GLMZ is old: built fast, built to last, nothing redundant. The architecture is East African Coastal Revival — broad overhangs, passive ventilation towers, structural carbon that runs clean lines against the equatorial sky. Thirty years of getting the heat problem right, from the outside in. Through the platform's eastern edge, if you lean over the rail, you can see the rooflines of the drowned city below the tide line: old mosques, old markets, the geometry of a place that did not move but had to live with what the water decided.

Not purposeful. Things settling out of somewhere and surfacing here, indifferent to what they surfaced as, four times across the years she had a record for, and however many times before the record began.

[Beat 48]
They built the document over four windows, eight weeks, and called it neither report nor brief. A record. He wrote the prose. She held the schema — depth, dates, the four ascensions, the cluster counts in their fixed columns. His paragraphs sat above her tables like captions, saying in sentences what the numbers could not be made to say alone.

In the third window she sent him a section to check.

"REVIEW PARAGRAPH 2. SURFACE DESCRIPTION. CONFIRM ACCURACY."

He read it. She had written that the lake was featureless.

He picked up the pen, then set it down and typed instead, because this belonged in the document and not the notepad.

"Not featureless," he wrote. "The Spine throws a reflection across it most mornings — the whole western face, upside down, until the wind takes it. There are shipping-lane markers, two amber and one white, on the north reach. A grain elevator on the south bank. The water moves around the spot but the surface has all of that on it."

The cursor held a long time. Longer than orbital lag accounted for.

"YOU OBSERVE FROM THE BRIDGE. ELEVATION FOUR METERS. ANGLE OBLIQUE."

"Yes," he typed.

"I OBSERVE FROM 6,200 MILES. ANGLE NORMAL. NO REFLECTION. NO MARKERS RESOLVED. NO ELEVATOR."

Then the line came that she added to the record, beneath the corrected paragraph, in her own column where he could not have put it:

"SURFACE FEATURES: SUBJECT TO OBSERVER POSITION."

Outside, the morning wind came up the canal and the Spine slid off the surface, the way it did, where only he could watch it go.

[Beat 49]
He came back to the record in the fourth window with a question he had not planned to type.

"What is this for?"

The cursor sat. Lag, then something past lag.

"WHOEVER COMES AFTER."

He read it. He did not have a model for who that was. A salvage crew. A later instrument with a normal angle and a better sensor. Someone standing on the bridge in forty years who found the markers moved. The category was empty and he could not fill it.

"You don't know who that is," he typed. It was not a challenge. It was a column heading with no entries under it, and he wanted her to confirm the column was empty too.

"NO MODEL FOR THE OBSERVER. THE RECORD DOES NOT REQUIRE ONE."

He thought about the eight hundred forty-seven events, the six clusters, the four objects that had come up out of three hundred meters of water older than the city. All of it set down in her fixed columns under his captions, addressed to no one they could name.

"Do you think they'll know what to do with it?"

This was the kind of question she answered in ranges. Likely. Probability exceeds threshold. Insufficient basis. He waited for the qualifier the way you wait for the second half of a known sentence.

"yes."

That was the whole line. No bound on it. No interval after it. No conditioning clause holding the door open behind it. He looked at the three letters in the lowercase she used for the short direct things, the register she kept for him and not for the data, and he could not find the place where she had hedged it, because she had not.

He held the comm a moment, the small weight of it in his hand, the morning going gray over the canal.

Then he set it down.

[Beat 50]
In the next window she opened with him, the way she always did, before anything else.

"BEHAVIORAL DELTA. FIELD VISIT FREQUENCY UP 0.41 OVER PRIOR INTERVAL. TRANSIT TIME 42ND FLOOR TO STREET LEVEL SHORTENED BY FACTOR 0.34. FIELD NOTE LENGTH PER VISIT INCREASING. CONFIRM."

"Confirm," he typed.

"LOGGED."

She had measured him. She had taken his movement through the building and reduced it the way she reduced the lake, the ascents, the eight hundred forty-seven, and she had set it down in a column with a factor and a sign. He was a parameter that had changed. He found he did not mind being one. He minded something else, smaller and harder to caption.

"Is that what you do," he typed, "or what you are?"

He sent it before he could soften it into a range.

The cursor sat. He knew her lag, the second and a half that was distance and nothing else, and this was not that. This was past it. He counted without meaning to, the kettle ticking as it cooled, the canal going from gray to the flat white it went before the sun cleared the buildings.

Fourteen seconds.

He did not type into it. He did not offer her the easy half of the sentence, the do or the are, the way you hand someone the word they are reaching for. He let the fourteen seconds be hers and held the comm and watched the cursor not move.

He waited.

[Beat 51]
She did not answer the question.

"NEW RECORD," she sent. "2197. SENSOR ARRAY FAILURE, PRIMARY AND REDUNDANT. DURATION ELEVEN DAYS. NO DATA. NO LOGS. NO RECORD OF WHAT I OBSERVED OR FAILED TO OBSERVE DURING THE INTERVAL."

"The lake?" he typed. "The ascents?"

He understood the shape of it. Everything else she handed him came with a column and a sign, a factor, a confidence interval she would narrow over the next pass. This had none of that. It was the one place in thirty-seven years where her record simply stopped and then resumed, and the gap between was not a value she could approach. She could not even round it.

He reached for the notepad, turned it over, and did not write anything. There was no category for a thing that had not been observed and could not be reconstructed. That was the point.

"What do you call it," he typed.

This time he did not count. He had stopped needing to.

"the eleven days."

He looked at the lowercase of it, the way she dropped the caps for the things that were hers and not data, and he thought she had named it the only way it could be named, which was to refuse to name it and leave the duration standing where the record should be.

"That sounds about right," he typed.

The reply came in eight seconds. Not nine.

"yes."

[Beat 52]
The work order came in the next contact window, formatted exactly like the first one she had ever sent him, three years before he understood what she was. Header. Originator string. He scrolled to the bottom out of habit, expecting the total.

He read it. The kettle was warm this time; he had learned to start it before the window. That was the whole architecture of her — a thing that could not leave a quantity unbounded, that narrowed every interval until it closed. An open rate was not a number she was capable of typing by accident.

He read it a third time.

Then he reached for the notepad, turned past the canal-bridge sketch and the cold column of the eleven days, and copied the line out by hand. SITE: ALL. QUANTITY: ONGOING. RATE: OPEN. His own handwriting looked strange holding her words, the caps gone uneven, the colon he had drawn too large.

He did not type anything back. The window was still open. He set the pen down beside the pad and looked at the line he had written, at the word at the end of it, and let the kettle go on warming behind him.

[Beat 53]
He opened the system on the second monitor — the one he used for logistics paperwork, the older interface with the non-adjustable column widths. He had used it for fifteen years. The intake form had twelve fields. He filled them in the order they appeared: client identifier, document class, sequence number, date received, date filed, service category, billing status, assigned account, priority tier, jurisdiction. Two more fields he left blank because they did not apply.

[Beat 54]
The elevator took forty seconds. He had timed it once before, in 2221, when the service contractor was late and he needed to know whether waiting or walking was faster. He had not thought about it since. He was thinking about it now because he was going downstairs and the alternative was sitting with the open rate tab until he assigned it a number, and he was not ready to do that.
