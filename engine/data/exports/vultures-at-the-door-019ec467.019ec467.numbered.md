## Chapter 1

[Beat 1]
Levin was in the driver's seat with his head tipped back and a vial held over one eye. He let a single drop fall, blinked, and the eye went glassy and far away and then came back wrong. He did the other one. He capped the Tears, put the vial in the door pocket, and started the engine.

Tomas watched him do it and said nothing, because they'd had the conversation about the drops before and having it again at four in the morning was a waste of both of them. That was the arrangement: Levin dosed, Levin drove, Levin was gentler with the accounts than a sober man would be, and Tomas ran the case and pretended not to notice what was behind the glass in his partner's eyes. The route logged six pickups and a recall, on time, every shift. That was the only thing it ever asked of either of them, and they gave it.

The manifest came up behind Tomas's eyes at 4:12, the way it did every morning — a column of names and addresses and account balances scrolling down the inside of his skull while the Wagon's compressors cycled the larder up to temperature. Six pickups. One recall. The number beside each name was what the body owed, and the number was never zero.

"Morning," Tomas said.

"It's not," Levin said, and pulled them out of the yard.

The yard sat between a bio-rendering plant and a sealed distribution hub, and at four in the morning it smelled like the rendering plant, something warm and clinical that the wind pushed at you in slow pulses. Three years in, Tomas had stopped smelling it the way you stop hearing a refrigerator. It was just what the early shift smelled like.

Carrion called them Reclamation Agents. The street called them Vultures. The city made bodies the way it made everything else, too many and too fast and financed against tomorrow, and when the payments stopped, somebody drove out before dawn and took back what the city was owed.

He had a daughter. She was nine. She had a thing in her genes with a long name that he and her mother had stopped saying out loud, and the only thing that kept the long name from finishing its sentence was a course of gene therapy — twelve thousand Φ every six months, payable in full, no plans, no deferral, no mercy from the clinic that set the price. "That was a compliment." A beat. "He did feel it."

Tomas turned back to the windshield. Somewhere down in all of it a seventy-three-year-old man was asleep in a bed with a machine beside it, not knowing the day had a number on it and the number was his. The organ in his chest had been put there by a Splicer — someone's neighborhood ripperdoc with a finance arrangement and a storefront license — and now Carrion was coming to collect on the Splicer's paperwork. A return wasn't free. The route never did one, which meant the route didn't have a shape for it, which meant it would land on some auditor's desk as the one anomaly in a clean week — a logged excision walking backward up an elevator, against a settled account, on a crew's own initiative. They'd pull the file. They'd pull the whole week's files, and the week was a minefield: a Full Repo on a man whose recall he'd voided eighteen hours earlier, a body he'd logged non-viable that hadn't been, a night four blocks from a missing Stem's son. He'd survived the week by being furniture. A flagged return was a man standing up and waving. Tomas thought about all of it, and about Paz's account and the number that wouldn't cover her course, and about a heart he'd bought with both, and a woman who used to draw buildings on everything, and a courier who ran for a living, and a dealer who'd kept his end of a deal that killed him, and a whole week of subtraction with his name logged on every line.
