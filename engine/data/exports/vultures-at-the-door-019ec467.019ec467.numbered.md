# Vultures at the Door

_In the GLMZ — the megacity that swallowed the Midwest — the body is just another financed asset. Miss a payment on your cloned kidney, your cybernetic legs, the heart a splicer installed on a thirty-month financing plan, and before dawn a Carrion Reclamation Wagon rolls up to collect.

Tomas and Levin are Vultures. They do the route. They don't ask questions.

Then a routine stop puts a bullet in the wrong man — the son of the most powerful crime lord on the south side — and they have one night to fix what they broke. That means sourcing a heart that doesn't exist on any shelf, building a lie no one will question, and staying invisible while the best hunter in the city works backward down the exact same trail.

The problem isn't the clock. The problem is the heart._

## Chapter 1

[Beat 1]
Levin was in the driver's seat with his head tipped back and a vial held over one eye. He let a single drop fall, blinked, and the eye went glassy and far away and then came back wrong. He did the other one. He capped the Tears, put the vial in the door pocket, and started the engine.

Tomas watched him do it and said nothing, because they'd had the conversation about the drops before and having it again at four in the morning was a waste of both of them. That was the arrangement: Levin dosed, Levin drove, Levin was gentler with the accounts than a sober man would be, and Tomas ran the case and pretended not to notice what was behind the glass in his partner's eyes. The route logged six pickups and a recall, on time, every shift. That was the only thing it ever asked of either of them, and they gave it.

The manifest came up behind Tomas's eyes at 4:12, the way it did every morning — a column of names and addresses and account balances scrolling down the inside of his skull while the Wagon's compressors cycled the larder up to temperature. Six pickups. One recall. The number beside each name was what the body owed, and the number was never zero.

"Morning," Tomas said.

"It's not," Levin said, and pulled them out of the yard.

The yard sat between a bio-rendering plant and a sealed distribution hub, and at four in the morning it smelled like the rendering plant, something warm and clinical that the wind pushed at you in slow pulses. Three years in, Tomas had stopped smelling it the way you stop hearing a refrigerator. It was just what the early shift smelled like.

Carrion called them Reclamation Agents. The street called them Vultures. The city made bodies the way it made everything else, too many and too fast and financed against tomorrow, and when the payments stopped, somebody drove out before dawn and took back what the city was owed.

He had a daughter. She was nine. She had a thing in her genes with a long name that he and her mother had stopped saying out loud, and the only thing that kept the long name from finishing its sentence was a course of gene therapy — twelve thousand Φ every six months, payable in full, no plans, no deferral, no mercy from the clinic that set the price. Six pickups was most of a week. A week was most of a month. A month was the whole of it, and the whole of it was Paz, and that was the whole of why Tomas Alvarado could open a person up at four-thirty in the morning and keep his hands steady. He never said it out loud. It wasn't the kind of thing you said to a partner who was four drops into the morning and still somehow managing to drive.

In the back of the Wagon the ward stood empty, the single berth folded down, the kit that kept a recovered organ viable worth more than the people they took it from. Behind it the larder hummed at four degrees, cold racks waiting for the day's freight. Between the two compartments, strapped upright in its bracket, sat the case: emergency orange, hard-shelled, BIOHAZARD stenciled across it in black. Inside, packed in foam, the two units they used for the actual work. The big one for the chest. The small one for the head. Levin had named them years ago and the names had stuck the way bad names do.

"What've we got," Levin said. He drove with two fingers, the rest of him somewhere behind the glass the drops put up.

"Kidney, first stop. Series-7, financed thirty months ago, account's been dead since spring." Tomas read it off the inside of his eye. "South side, mid-strata. Old man."

"Family?"

"A daughter, lives with him."

"Wonderful," Levin said. "I love a daughter."

Tomas looked at him. "You don't have to love anything. You have to carry the cooler and not say the word *Tears* in front of the account."

"I have never," Levin said, with the dignity of a man four drops into the morning, "said that word in front of an account."

"You told a woman her father would feel a 'lovely warmth.'"

"That was a compliment." A beat. "He did feel it."

Tomas turned back to the windshield. The route ran south and the elevated let them up over the sleeping strata, the towers stacked into the dark with their windows out — and past the eastern edge, the white gap every map printed where a block had gone to a Rift and stayed gone, the air beyond it leaning in a direction that made Tomas put his eyes back on the road.

Somewhere down in all of it a seventy-three-year-old man was asleep in a bed with a machine beside it, not knowing the day had a number on it and the number was his. The organ in his chest had been put there by a Splicer — someone's neighborhood ripperdoc with a finance arrangement and a storefront license — and now Carrion was coming to collect on the Splicer's paperwork. Tomas set the address. The Wagon took the long curve down toward the south side, and the orange case rocked once in its bracket and went still.

[Beat 2]
He was already awake when the call came. The machete was on the table — handle forward, cord-wrapped, the same blade a dead man's hands had given him in a field at the end of a war that nobody in the GLMZ had ever heard of. He didn't keep it for sentiment. He kept it because it was honest about what it was, and a man should be honest about what he is.

The call came in at 3:40, audio only, the way the Lotus woman always called him — never video, because video was a courtesy and a dog did not require courtesy. She called him Casimir. He had corrected her once, early, said the name his mother gave him, and she had looked at him through the channel the way you look at a dog that has tried to explain something, a flicker of waiting for the sound to stop, and he had not corrected her again. The name cost him nothing. The name was a collar, and a man could wear a collar and still be the thing inside it.

"A boy is missing," she said. "Han Do-yun. Since yesterday evening. The house wants him found before it becomes a thing the house has to explain."

"Send me what he looks like," Ekow said. "His last route. Three names of people who saw him last."

It came through his neuretics before he finished asking, the way it always did, because the one thing the Lotus never withheld from him was information, the same way you do not withhold the scent from the hound. The photograph bloomed behind his eyes: a soft son with a soft face, the face of someone who had never had to read a room before walking into it. Ekow looked at it the way he looked at all of them, not for the man but for the habits the man would have — where a soft thing goes to feel less soft, what it buys, who it trusts.

He was already dressed. He crossed to the table and picked up the machete — seated it at his hip in one motion, cord-wrapped handle falling to hand exactly where it should. He was always already dressed. He had served four borders and two cities for the People's Republic of Ghana, and the army had built a thing into him that did not switch off when the war did. You couldn't unmake a man who had been made to track, you could only point him or let the skill go bad inside him like meat. So he kept it pointed. Violence was his trade and the GLMZ had buyers, and the work, when it came, was the only thing that kept the blade from rusting in the dark.

He went out at 3:44, the machete at his hip where it always lived, handle forward. A man born on a Tuesday, to a woman who named him for the ocean, walking now into a city of stone, toward the first of the people who had seen the soft boy last.

[Beat 3]
The building had a glass lobby gone yellow at the seams, the kind of mid-strata block that had been somebody's idea of nice forty years ago and had been managed down ever since. The company had shipped ahead of the crew, the way it always did: a medical-grade dialysis unit sat shrink-wrapped in the freight elevator. Scheduled around the family days ago. Nobody had told the family.

"They sent the machine before us," Levin said, looking at it. He had the orange case in one hand, dead weight, like a man carrying a cooler to a cookout he didn't want to attend. "Mercy by mail order."

"Beats a loaner." For a heart or a lung the case seated a stopgap and started a seventy-two-hour clock; for a kidney they left you a machine in the corner and the rest of your life tethered to it.

The daughter answered on the second knock. Fifties, a robe over day clothes, the look of someone who'd been awake since the delivery men came. Daria Holme. Tomas had her name off the manifest before he had her face.

"You're the ones," she said. Not a question.

"Carrion Reclamations, ma'am." Tomas had the folio. "There's a reclamation order on a financed Series-7. We're authorized to access the account holder and we've brought everything he'll need after. May we come in?"

She looked at the orange case, and then at their coveralls, and then she looked at Tomas's face the way someone looks for the piece of a stranger they might be able to reach. She didn't find it — he'd learned not to let her — and she stepped back from the door, because there was no version of this where she didn't.

The old man was in the back bedroom, awake, sitting up against the headboard with his hands folded on the blanket like a man waiting for a bus. He'd been told. You could always tell the ones who'd been told. He watched them come in without surprise and without accusation.

"Floor or bed?" Levin asked him, gently, because Levin behind the glass was gentler than Levin in front of it, and the drops were good for that one thing.

"Bed's fine," the old man said.

Tomas worked the case open on the chair. He lifted the big unit out of the foam with both hands — it came out heavy and segmented, a carapace the size of a turtle's shell, its jointed legs folded under it like something asleep — and he set it on the old man's chest, and it woke. The legs unfolded and found the ribs and took hold, gentle and total, so that it sat on the man like a thing that had landed on him to feed. The drug well found a vein in his arm without being told. The old man's eyes went soft and then went nowhere. Under the carapace the undercarriage opened him along the flank in a clean line, cauterized as it cut, the smell coming up hot and coppery and electrical. Renal artery clamped. Renal vein clamped. Ureter sealed. The kidney came up into the perfusion cradle, dark and slick and exact, and the unit packed it into a cassette and snapped it cold, and then it closed what it had opened and laid down a dressing. Twenty-two minutes. Almost no noise.

Tomas logged the completion. The old man's account went to zero.

"He'll sleep an hour," Tomas told Daria in the kitchen, and walked her through the machine in the corner, the cuff, the schedule, the QR that would teach her the rest.

She signed where the folio told her to. She had the careful hand of someone signing things she didn't fully understand and had learned not to fight. Then she looked up, and for a moment the not-fighting slipped, and Tomas saw what she was carrying. "Will he know?" she said. "When he wakes up. Will he feel—"

"He won't feel the absence," Tomas said. "He'll feel the machine. That's different."

She nodded, once. Looked back down. He had given her the technically true version of the answer and they both knew it and neither of them said so.

Levin was already at the door with the cooler and the orange case. In the elevator he said, "Forty minutes to the next one."

"What's the next one?"

"Recall," Levin said, and checked the vial in his door pocket with his eyes the way other men check their watch. "Couple of tickets out past the Gray Zone edge. Dealer who quit paying."

[Beat 4]
The elevator descended in the building's smell — old ceiling tile and someone's reheated food and the faint medicinal sting of the unit's cauterizer still on Tomas's clothes. He looked at the orange case sitting upright between them, its foam interior still holding the shape of the carapace, the indentation exact, like a cast of something that had been born from it.

*Forty-two reclamations in eight months. He has never once asked what happens to the cassettes.*

"Recall on what organ?" Tomas asked.

"Liver segment," Levin said. "Dealer took the augmented unit on a payment plan through a Skarr affiliate. Stopped at month three. They want the segment back or the balance, and the balance is—" he checked his neuretics with the small inward look, the micro-pause, "—not a number this man has."

The lobby glass caught the street outside: a strip of Gray Zone night, yellow-orange, the atmospheric processors already pushing tomorrow's rain toward them from wherever the Spires didn't want it. Tomas saw his own face in the glass for a second — coveralls, folio under his arm, the look of a man whose job title existed in a legal gray as soft as the zone they were about to enter — and then the door opened and the reflection broke apart into wet pavement and moving light and none of it was him anymore.

[Beat 5]
The dealer's block sat at the frayed edge of the Gray Zone, where the map got shy about who owned what and the ground underneath belonged to somebody who didn't need a map to know. Everybody who ran a route out here knew the somebody. You learned their border the way you learned weather - not from a forecast, from the ache in a joint that had been broken once already.

"Take the next left," Tomas said.

Levin took it. It put them a block off the straight line to the shop, which put them somewhere Carrion hadn't sent them. "That's the long way."

"I know."

"We're on a clock."

"We're on a clock for a recall. We're not on a clock for whether the curb we set a BIOHAZARD box down on has eyes on it." Tomas nodded at the windshield. "Roll it slow. Once around."

"You're gonna case a whole block for one busted-ass dealer who already quit fighting?"

"Yeah."

Nobody had asked him to do this. The ticket had five verbs on it - park, climb, recall, collect, leave - and a dealer who'd already decided, from the shape of his silence on the account, not to make it a fight. A sober crew did the five verbs and went to breakfast.

But the block backed onto Lotus ground, and Tomas had learned, on a job years back he still didn't tell at parties, that the trouble on any pickup was almost never the name on the ticket. It was whoever happened to be standing near the name. So he made Levin drive them past the front of the thing before they went in the back of it - slow, unhurried, a Wagon on its way to somewhere duller - and he read the street.

A noodle stall shuttered for the dead hour. A Lotus front two doors down with its blinds drawn in that particular disciplined way, closed but awake. One man in the recessed doorway, too still to be waiting for anything with a schedule. Above the storm drain a two-inch orbit of gravel turned slow in the air, a little schism months old, the neighborhood stepping around it like a puddle it had stopped resenting. A single harpy rode the thermals off the tier-edge, minding its own altitude.

Tomas took the inventory the way he took everything, quietly, against a bill.

"Anybody home?" Levin asked.

"Not for us." The still man in the doorway wasn't watching a repo truck. He was watching the Lotus door, which meant his trouble was inside his own house and had nothing to do with two garbagemen and a busted account. "Street's clean. Park it up the block."

Which was true, as far as it went. Tomas had checked every direction the danger could come from except up off a couch four floors above his head, because a man can only read the street he can see. He'd think about that later, the way you think about a door you were sure you'd locked. For now he was satisfied, which was its own small joke, the kind the universe likes to tell at a working man's expense, and he told himself the day had a handle on it, and got out to fetch the case. He had, for about ten seconds, the feeling of a man in front of his problems.

Ten seconds was about the going rate.

[Beat 6]
The shop was on four. Tomas smelled it from the stairwell, the sweetish chemical fug of a place where people came to buy the thing that took the day off them, Tears and worse. Levin smelled it too, and something behind the glass in him leaned toward it, just slightly, and Tomas pretended not to see that the way he pretended not to see most of what the drops did.

The door was unlocked. It usually was, in a shop; locked doors cost you walk-ins.

It was one room, and Tomas read it in the doorway the way you read any room you might have to leave in a hurry. A counter ran along the right wall, and behind it stood the man they'd come for. A low couch sat against the left wall, under the window, and a second man sat on it — young, overdressed, somewhere deep behind his own eyes. The space between counter and couch was maybe four strides of bare floor. Tomas came in first. Levin came in behind him with the repo pad already in his hand.

"Carrion," Tomas said. "Recall order. Two tickets, both yours, Bram Osei. Nobody has to make it a thing."

Osei's hands went up before Tomas finished the name, both of them, high and open and steady, a man who'd been on the wrong end of a doorway before and knew the choreography. "Hands are up," he said. "Hands have been up. Whatever's on the ticket, it's right here. Take it. I never saw you, I never see anybody."

"Legs first," Levin said, and came to the counter and set the grey slab flat where Osei could watch him do it. "Older debt." He keyed the lien code, five digits, with exactly as much expression as a man reading a receipt he'd already paid. "Sit down, friend. You don't want to be standing for this part."

Osei didn't sit down. They never sat down.

The pad chirped once, like a microwave finishing. Then twenty-eight months of debt called two limbs home — the financed legs running their detach sequence at the socket, hip-mounts releasing in order, and Bram Osei dropped straight down out of his own legs and hit the floor while the legs stayed standing a half-second longer than he did, empty, before they folded at the knee and toppled. Tomas caught the near one out of habit. Osei sat in a spreading nothing of his own, the receiver-ends of his stumps capped and clean where the company's hardware had sealed itself off on the way out, breathing in short careful pulls.

"You did that right," Levin told him, not unkindly, and bagged the legs. From the case he took the folding transit chair the company packed for exactly this, stood it up, locked the wheels, and between the two of them they got Osei up off the floor and into it. The chair took his weight with a soft pneumatic sigh. Carrion would bill him for it. Carrion billed everyone for everything.

"Grievance line's on the card," Tomas said. "Charges off the wall overnight."

"Okay," Osei said, from the chair, hands finding the push-rings, testing them the way you test a thing you're going to be living in. "Okay. We're — okay." He pushed one ring and not the other and the chair turned him a slow quarter-circle to face the wall, and he corrected, and over-corrected, and wheeled most of the way back around to where he'd started. Nobody said anything about it.

Tomas looked at the pad. The second ticket sat there, patient and grey. He'd half-hoped it would feel different if he didn't look at it. It didn't.

"There's the other one," he said.

Levin looked at the screen, then at Osei in the new chair, then back at the screen. "The arm."

"The arm." Tomas let a breath out through his nose. There was no clean way to say the next part to a man you'd just lowered into a wheelchair, so he said it the only way there was, flat and procedural. "Right side, financed, four months past. Mr. Osei, I'm going to need you to —"

Osei looked down at the arm like it had volunteered him for something. "You're shitting me."

And the man on the couch came up off it fast and wrong, both hands going for the waistband of his too-good pants.

[Beat 7]
"Hey," Tomas said, and then there wasn't time for the rest of it.

There was a gun in the young man's waistband. That part was real, and later it was the part Tomas held onto, on the nights he needed to hold onto something — there had been a gun, the kid had pulled a gun, any board of review would write it clean.

But the kid was so far down in the Tears and whatever he'd cut it with that the gun came out sideways and half-fumbled, the safety still on, his thumb nowhere near it, a man trying to swim in air. A sober room would have had a full half-second to see he can't even hold it, put a boot on it, and end the morning with a story to tell.

Levin moved first. Levin always moved first — it was the job, and the start of the job was his. His hand went for the sidearm on his hip the way it had a thousand times, except the drops had him a half-second behind his own arm and the holster's retention caught the way it had never once caught, and the piece hung there, snagged, while his far-off eyes tried to file what his hand had failed to do.

Which left Tomas. Tomas, who had a daughter and a therapy bill and no intention of killing anybody today. His hands were steady because his hands were always steady; he knew the geography of a heart from the outside because taking them out was the work — and his body did the clean thing, the trained thing, the thing he would have given a year of his life not to do, and put one round through the place his hands were trained to find.

The sound of it in the small room was very loud.

Then it wasn't.

The young man sat back down on the couch he'd come up off of, almost gently, as if he'd reconsidered, and the gun finished its fall and hit the floor and spun and stopped, and a stain came through the front of the too-good shirt and spread, and his blown pupils found Tomas and stayed there, surprised, getting less surprised.

"No," Tomas said. Not loud. Just the word, leaving him.

He was across the floor before he decided to be, both hands on the wound, the kid's pulse stuttering up into his palms, fast and thin and arguing. And behind his eyes IdentiScan finished the pass it had started when the kid first turned to the door — slower than the lien check because it had to reach further, out past Carrion's ledger into the public stacks and the places a clean account told you nothing but a face still did. It finished now, a beat too late to matter, the way it always finished a beat too late, in calm grey letters:

HAN DO-YUN. NO CARRION DEBT.

And under it, the line that took the floor out from under the room:

KNOWN ASSOCIATION — LOTUS SYNDICATE (FAMILIAL). MOTHER: HAN JEONG-SUK (STEM).

Tomas knelt there with both hands full of a Lotus Stem's son and a round he had never wanted to fire, and when he found his voice it came out level in the way that meant keeping it level was costing him everything.

"Your gun snagged," he said.

"It snagged," Levin agreed, from somewhere behind the glass.

"You spooked him. Your gun snagged. And I just put one through a kid I never had a ticket on." Tomas didn't look up from his hands. "Get the case. Get Mama. You're going to take care of every part of what happens next — you've done enough with your hands today."

[Beat 8]
He read the scene from the street before going in: a scene tells you most of what it knows before you touch it, and touching it first only adds your own noise to the page. The foot traffic on the near sidewalk had a bend in it that the eye almost missed — people coming up the walk drifted a half-step wide of the stairwell door and did not know they were doing it. The body has its own democracy. It votes with its feet, and it votes against the place where something has recently gone wrong, and a hundred people will route around a death they were never told about.

A window on the fourth floor was cracked, a single radial star, the kind a shoulder makes, not a round.

He went up. The shop door was unlocked, which told him the trade here had stopped. Inside, one room. He stood in it and let it speak.

The vial drawer behind the counter was empty — not tipped, not scattered, not looted. Emptied clean, drawer slid shut after. Someone had taken the whole stock as a transaction, not a theft. A purchase. People did not buy out a dealer they had just made bleed; they took. This was something quieter. This was a rescue paying its way out.

Blood on the boards, one source, low and pooled — a man sitting, or going down to sit, when the round found him. Soft.

A gun lay by the couch leg, the safety still on. He crouched over it without lifting it. A man who clears a holster and never thumbs the safety is a man who never meant the threat, or couldn't finish it. Either way: not a hit team. Amateurs with a reason.

He fieldstripped the gun, set the parts in a row on the floor, and left them. A whole gun was a thing a frightened tenant might pick up and use badly; parts were just parts.

He stood over the empty drawer a moment and let the old thing move in him — not hunger, he had never named it hunger, just the click of a made thing finding the work it was made for. In Ghana they had spent a great deal of money turning a boy from Sekondi into an instrument that could read a fled man off the dirt he left, and the boy was gone now and only the instrument remained, and the instrument did not care whose son it found or what was done to him after. He had made his peace with that a long way back. The peace was that he chose the work and the work did not choose him; a man who picks up the leash himself each morning is not, he had decided, the same as a man it was tied to. He wasn't always sure the distinction held. He held it anyway, the way you hold a rail on a stair you can't see the bottom of.

On the door handle, going out, a thread of orange synthetic snagged on the latch screw. Carrion Reclamations coverall fiber. The kind a man wore to carry the legally dead out of buildings exactly like this one, in a truck nobody looked at twice.

He opened the channel. "He's alive. He's with a Carrion crew. I'll want their manifests for the district." The manifests came back before he reached the street. He folded the day into a route and began to walk it.

[Beat 9]
Mama came out of the case heavy and segmented, a carapace the size of a turtle's shell, and Levin set it on Han Do-yun's chest and it woke and took hold, legs unfolding across the kid's ribs, the drug well finding the vein before anyone told it to. The kid went under without knowing he'd been awake.

"Talk to me," Tomas said, because he couldn't not.

"Round's through the lower chamber. He's not getting that heart back." Levin read the feed off his own neuretics, flat and fast. "It already wants to take it."

The Scarab didn't hesitate the way men did. It opened the chest along the line it had opened a thousand chests, clamped what needed clamping, and lifted Han Do-yun's ruined heart out into the cradle — a torn fist of muscle the round had ended. Then it reached into its own underbelly, into the bay where the loaners rode, and seated the stopgap: a heart that had never been alive and never would be, a perfusion engine the size and weight of the real thing, rated by the company for exactly seventy-two hours of borrowed time. It hooked the loaner to the great vessels, primed it, started it, and the kid's pulse came back up under the carapace, steady now, mechanical now.

A line stood up in the feed, calm and grey.

LOANER SEATED. 72:00:00. PERMANENT REPLACEMENT REQUIRED.

And under it, the part that turned Tomas's stomach to water:

TISSUE MATCH — RARE PHENOTYPE. NO COMPATIBLE UNIT IN REGIONAL STOCK.

"He's a rare type," Levin said, reading it twice. "Not sick — rare. The tissue. Loaner's good for three days, and there's no heart on any shelf in this region his body won't throw on the floor." He said it the way he'd have read a delivery window. Then he stood and went to the man in the new wheelchair, who had watched all of it and understood that watching it was the most dangerous thing he had ever done.

The arm ticket was still live on the pad. Levin let it go dark.

"Your arm's current," he said. "Clerical error. The recall's voided — you keep it." He crouched, which on Tears took him a moment to organize, and his voice did the one warm thing it knew how to do, the thing it did when it wanted something. "You never saw two men today. You never saw a sick kid on your couch. You never heard a sound." A beat. "And you give me one out of the drawer. The Tears. One's plenty."

Osei reached down beside the chair, found the low drawer he kept for the customers who couldn't reach high, and put a single vial in Levin's hand.

"Don't call us," Levin said, and pocketed it.

Then he did the part Tomas couldn't. He folded the carapace down over Han Do-yun and lifted the boy and the shell and the borrowed heart all together, carried him down four flights, and laid him in the ward where the life-support berth came up out of the floor and closed around him — vent waking, perfuser waking, the loaner ticking in his chest six feet from the cold racks of the larder where, if the night went the way nights like this went, he'd be riding by tomorrow. Levin wired him in, checked the feed twice, and only then went back up for the case.

He came down with it light in one hand, loaded it into the back, and in the half-second it took to swing the doors shut he capped a fresh bead of Osei's Tears into one eye, then the other, the way another man might roll his shoulders. Then he got into the driver's seat.

Tomas was already in the cab, hands flat on his knees, not looking at them.

"You're dosing," Tomas said. "On the job. We've got a man on life support in the back and you're —"

"Sorry." Levin started the Wagon. "We just killed ourselves back there. Feels like exactly the right time to take the edge off."

Tomas didn't have an answer to that, because there wasn't one. The Wagon pulled out of the block at a quarter to three in the morning with a Lotus Stem's son breathing on borrowed hardware in the ward, the clock in the back reading 71:48:09 and counting.

"Pull up what you can find on him," Tomas said, when he could. "I want to know why a Stem's son was buying off the floor on a Tuesday."

It took Levin a minute. Then he laughed, once, with nothing in it.

"He's getting married tomorrow," he said. "Dawn ceremony. Big one."

## Chapter 2

[Beat 10]
Tomas drove nowhere in particular for the first twenty minutes, because a Wagon that kept moving was a Wagon nobody looked at twice, and he needed to think.

Levin sat on his side of the cab and said nothing. He'd been on his side of the cab and saying nothing since Tomas had told him to take care of the den and walked away from him, which was its own kind of message, and Levin had enough of his native intelligence left under the glass to receive it. He didn't reach for the door pocket. He sat with his hands flat on his thighs and let Tomas drive and didn't say the wrong thing, which was the most competent thing he'd done all morning.

"Tell me what we're actually in," he said, eventually.
