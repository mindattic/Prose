# Vultures at the Door

_Thomas and Levin are Vultures — Carrion Logistics Reclamation Agents who drive before dawn to take back what people can no longer afford. Kidneys. Legs. Hearts. In the GLMZ, the body is just another financed asset, and they are the ones who collect.

When a routine stop puts a bullet in the wrong man — the son of the most powerful crime lord in the city — they have one night to fix what they broke. That means finding a heart that doesn't exist, building a lie no one will question, and staying invisible while the best hunter in the city works backward down the same trail._

## Chapter 1

[Beat 1]
The manifest came up behind Thomas's eyes at 4:12 in the morning, the way it did every morning, a column of names and addresses and account balances scrolling down the inside of his skull while the Wagon's compressors cycled the larder up to temperature. Six pickups. One recall. The number beside each name was what the body owed, and the number was never zero, because Carrion Logistics did not dispatch a crew for an account that was paid.

Levin was in the driver's seat with his head tipped back and a vial held over one eye. He let a single drop fall, blinked, and the eye went glassy and far away and then came back wrong. He did the other one. He capped the Tears, put the vial in the door pocket, and started the engine.

"Morning," Thomas said.

"It's not," Levin said, and pulled them out of the yard.

The yard sat between a bio-rendering plant and a sealed distribution hub, and at four in the morning it smelled like the rendering plant, something warm and clinical that the wind pushed at you in slow pulses. Three years in, Thomas had stopped smelling it the way you stop hearing a refrigerator. It was just what the early shift smelled like.

Carrion called them Reclamation Agents. The street called them Vultures. The city made bodies the way it made everything else, too many and too fast and financed against tomorrow, and when the payments stopped, somebody drove out before dawn and took back what the city was owed. Thomas didn't call it anything. He called Paz, on Sundays.

She was nine. She had a thing in her genes with a long name that her mother and he had stopped saying out loud, and the only thing that kept the long name from finishing its sentence was a course of gene therapy that cost twelve thousand Φ every six months, payable to a clinic that did not take payment plans from people in their tier. Six pickups was most of a week. A week was most of a month. A month was the math, and the math was Paz, and that was the whole of why Thomas could open a person up at four-thirty in the morning and keep his hands steady. He'd found the trick. You did it for someone who wasn't in the room.

In the back of the Wagon the ward stood empty, the single life-support berth folded down, vent and perfuser dark, the way it spent most of its life. Behind it the larder hummed at four degrees, cold racks waiting for the day's freight. Between the two compartments, strapped upright in its bracket, sat the case: emergency orange, hard-shelled, BIOHAZARD stenciled across it in black. Inside, packed in foam, the two units they used for the actual work. The big one for the chest. The small one for the head. Levin had named them years ago and the names had stuck the way bad names do.

"What've we got," Levin said. He drove with two fingers, the rest of him somewhere behind the glass the drops put up.

"Kidney, first stop. Series-7, financed thirty months ago, account's been dead since spring." Thomas read it off the inside of his eye. "South side, mid-strata. Old man."

"Family?"

"A daughter, lives with him."

"Wonderful," Levin said. "I love a daughter."

"You don't have to love anything. You have to carry the cooler and not say the word *Tears* in front of the account."

"I have never," Levin said, with the dignity of a man four drops into the morning, "said that word in front of an account."

The route ran south and the elevated let them up over the sleeping strata, the towers stacked into the dark with their windows out, and somewhere down in all of it a seventy-three-year-old man was asleep in a bed with a machine beside it, not knowing the day had a number on it and the number was his. Thomas set the address. The Wagon took the long curve down toward the south side, and the orange case rocked once in its bracket and went still.

[Beat 2]
He was already awake when the call came. The machete was on the table — handle forward, cord-wrapped, the same blade a dead man's hands had given him in a field at the end of a war that nobody in the GLMZ had ever heard of. He did not keep it for sentiment. He kept it because it was honest about what it was, and a man should be honest about what he is.

The call came in at 3:40, audio only, the way the Lotus woman always called him — never video, because video was a courtesy and a dog did not require courtesy. She called him Casimir. He had corrected her once, early, said the name his mother gave him, and she had looked at him through the channel the way you look at a dog that has tried to explain something, a flicker of waiting for the sound to stop, and he had not corrected her again. The name cost him nothing. The name was a collar, and a man could wear a collar and still be the thing inside it.

"A boy is missing," she said. "Han Do-yun. Since yesterday evening. The house wants him found before it becomes a thing the house has to explain."

"Send me what he looks like," Ekow said. "His last route. Three names of people who saw him last."

It came through his neuretics before he finished asking, the way it always did, because the one thing the Lotus never withheld from him was information, the same way you do not withhold the scent from the hound. The photograph bloomed behind his eyes: a soft son with a soft face, the face of someone who had never had to read a room before walking into it. Ekow looked at it the way he looked at all of them, not for the man but for the habits the man would have — where a soft thing goes to feel less soft, what it buys, who it trusts.

He was already dressed. He was always already dressed. He had served four borders and two cities for the People's Republic of Ghana, and the army had built a thing into him that did not switch off when the war did; you could not unmake a man who had been made to track, you could only point him or let the skill go bad inside him like meat. So he kept it pointed. Violence was his trade and the GLMZ had buyers, and the work, when it came, was the only thing that kept the blade from rusting in the dark.

He went out at 3:44, the machete at his hip where it always lived, handle forward. A man born on a Tuesday, to a woman who named him for the ocean, walking now into a city of stone, toward the first of the people who had seen the soft boy last.

[Beat 3]
The building had a glass lobby gone yellow at the seams, the kind of mid-strata block that had been somebody's idea of nice forty years ago and had been managed down ever since. Carrion's logistics arm had been here already. There was a medical-grade dialysis unit sitting in the freight elevator, shrink-wrapped, with a delivery tag that said the account holder's name and the words HOME HEMODIALYSIS — STARTER and a QR code that would walk a daughter through keeping her father alive after the men left. The company shipped the machine before the crew. The appointment had been scheduled around the family days ago, and nobody had told the family.

"They sent the loaner-by-mail," Levin said, looking at the wrapped machine. He had the orange case in one hand, dead weight, like a man carrying a cooler to a cookout he didn't want to attend.

"It's a dialysis rig, not a loaner."

"Same church."

It wasn't, quite, and Thomas didn't bother. A loaner was the thing the case did, the artificial organ the unit seated in you when it took the real one, rated for seventy-two hours and not one hour more, so that a man with his heart repossessed walked around for three days on borrowed time, the clock ticking down inside his own chest until he settled the account or didn't. For a kidney you didn't need the case. For a kidney there was a machine in the corner and the rest of your life tethered to it four hours a day, which the company considered mercy and billed as a service. Either way you died on the clock. The only question was how long the clock was, and the company set the clock.

The daughter answered on the second knock. Fifties, a robe over day clothes, the look of someone who'd been awake since the delivery men came. Daria Holme. Thomas had her name off the manifest before he had her face.

"You're the ones," she said. Not a question.

"Carrion Logistics, ma'am." Thomas had a folio. "There's a reclamation order on a financed Series-7. We're authorized to access the account holder and we've brought everything he'll need after. May we come in?"

She looked at the orange case, and then at their coveralls, and then she stepped back from the door, because there was no version of this where she didn't.

The old man was in the back bedroom, awake, sitting up against the headboard with his hands folded on the blanket like a man waiting for a bus. He'd been told. You could always tell the ones who'd been told. He watched them come in without surprise and without accusation.

"Floor or bed?" Levin asked him, gently, because Levin behind the glass was gentler than Levin in front of it, and the drops were good for that one thing.

"Bed's fine," the old man said.

Thomas worked the case open on the chair. He lifted the big unit out of the foam with both hands — it came out heavy and segmented, a carapace the size of a turtle's shell, its jointed legs folded under it like something asleep — and he set it on the old man's chest, and it woke. The legs unfolded and found the ribs and took hold, gentle and total, so that it sat on the man like a thing that had landed on him to feed. The drug well found a vein in his arm without being told. The old man's eyes went soft and then went nowhere. Under the carapace the undercarriage opened him along the flank in a clean line that didn't bleed the way you'd think, because the unit cauterized as it cut, and the smell came up, hot and coppery and electrical, and Thomas breathed through his mouth out of habit and watched the readout climb the inside of his eye. Renal artery clamped. Renal vein clamped. Ureter sealed. The kidney came up out of him into the perfusion cradle, dark and slick and exact, and the unit packed it into a cassette and snapped the cassette cold, and then it went back in and closed what it had opened and laid down a dressing, and the whole thing took twenty-two minutes and made almost no noise.

Thomas logged the completion. Behind his eyes the account flipped from RECLAMATION PENDING to SETTLED IN KIND, and the old man's number, the thing he'd owed since spring, went to zero the only way the company let it.

"He'll sleep an hour," Thomas told Daria in the kitchen, and walked her through the machine in the corner, the cuff, the schedule, the QR that would teach her the rest. She signed where the folio told her to. She had the careful hand of someone signing things she didn't fully understand and had learned not to fight.

Levin was already at the door with the cooler and the orange case, the kidney inside the one and the carapace folded back asleep inside the other.

In the elevator he said, "Forty minutes to the next one."

"What's the next one?"

"Recall," Levin said, and checked the vial in his door pocket with his eyes the way other men check their watch. "Pair of legs. Some courier who quit paying."

[Beat 4]
A recall was nothing like a pickup. A pickup you needed the case and an hour and a strong stomach. A recall you needed a four-digit code and the nerve to read a man's face while you typed it.

The address was a courier flop above a noodle counter in the Circuit, six men sleeping in shifts in a room built for two, bikes and board-racks stacked along one wall. Their target was awake, eating standing up at the window. Thomas's IdentiScan caught him before Thomas did. The subroutine ran behind his eyes every waking minute, querying every face and gait it could see against Carrion's lien ledger, and it threw a soft tag over the man at the window the way it threw tags over half a crowd on a bad block: a name, a balance, an authorization. IDRIS FONTAINE. CYBER LOWER-LIMB PAIR, FINANCED. 14 MONTHS PAST DUE. RECALL AUTHORIZED.

"Idris Fontaine," Thomas said, and the man turned, and you could watch him do the math on two men in Carrion coveralls and arrive at the only answer.

"No," Idris said. "No, no — I'm three weeks from current, I've got the work, I just had a slow —"

"It's not me," Thomas said, which was true and useless, the way it was always true and useless. "The order's already cut. I can give you the grievance line. It's on the card."

Levin had the repo pad out. It was a flat grey slab that did one thing. He didn't even have to be close; the handshake reached across the room. He looked at Idris with the far-off courtesy the drops gave him and said, not unkindly, "Sit down, friend. You don't want to be standing."

Idris did not sit down. They never sat down. He was a runner; standing was the whole of what he was.

Levin keyed the code. There was no blade and no blood and no struggle, just the pad chirping once like a microwave finishing, and then the soft mechanical let-go as fourteen months of debt called two limbs home — the financed legs running their detach sequence at the socket, hip-mounts releasing in order, and Idris Fontaine dropped straight down out of his own legs and hit the floor while the legs stayed standing for a half-second longer than he did, empty, before they toppled. He sat on the boards in a spreading nothing, the receiver-ends of his stumps capped and clean where the company's hardware had sealed itself off on the way out, staring at the place his shins had been.

"You can't," he said. It wasn't an argument. It was just the last thing left to say. "I run. That's — I run for a living."

"I know," Thomas said. He did know.

Levin collected the legs. They went in the cooler like anything else, two financed lower limbs, repossessed property, scuffed at the knee from a trade that asked everything of knees. He set Idris's grievance card on the floor where the man could reach it, and a second card under it, a voucher for a transit chair the company would bill him for later, because Carrion thought of everything and charged for all of it.

The other couriers had come awake along the wall and none of them moved, because there was nothing to do and they all knew the ledger had their names on it too, somewhere, on some other morning.

In the stairwell Levin said, "He'll get a mobility grant. Tier-3 has a grant for that now."

"You looked it up?"

"I look things up," Levin said, with the flat dignity he kept for exactly this, and Thomas let him have it, because the alternative to Levin looking up the grant was Levin not being able to do the job at all, and they had four more stops.

The Wagon took them out of the Circuit and back down toward the sprawl. Behind them in the larder a kidney chilled in its cassette and a pair of legs lay in the cooler, the morning's freight, and the manifest behind Thomas's eyes rolled to the next line and held there, and the next line was a recall too, an arm this time, a dealer in a residential block off the Gray Zone edge who'd stopped paying on a cyber arm and didn't know yet that today was the day.

It should have been the easiest stop of the week.

[Beat 5]
The block sat right on the Gray Zone edge, where the strata gave up pretending and the rents went to nothing and the people who landed there had usually fallen a long way to do it. The dealer's unit was on four. Thomas smelled it from the stairwell, the sweetish chemical fug of a place where people came to buy the thing that took the day off them, Tears and worse. Levin smelled it too, and something behind the glass in him leaned toward it, just slightly, and Thomas pretended not to see that the way he pretended not to see most of what the drops did.

The door was unlocked. It usually was, in a shop; locked doors cost you walk-ins.

It was one room, and Thomas read it in the doorway the way you read any room you might have to leave in a hurry. A counter ran along the right wall, and behind it stood the man they'd come for. A low couch sat against the left wall, under the window, and a second man sat on it. The space between — counter to couch — was maybe four strides of bare floor, and that floor was where the trouble would be if there was trouble. Thomas came in first. Levin came in behind him and stayed near the door, the repo pad already in his hand.

"Carrion," Thomas said. "Recall order. Nobody has to make it a thing."

The man behind the counter Thomas's IdentiScan lit up on sight — BRAM OSEI, CYBER ARM (RIGHT), FINANCED, PAST DUE, RECALL AUTHORIZED — a wiry man with a good arm bolted to a body it didn't match, and Osei's hands went up before the tag finished resolving, both of them, the financed one and the real one, high and open and steady, a man who had been on the wrong end of a doorway before and knew the choreography.

"Hands are up," Osei said. "Hands have been up. You want the arm, the arm's right here, you don't even have to come around the counter. Take the arm. I never saw you. I never see anybody."

"That's the spirit," Levin said, and started across the floor toward the counter with the pad.

The man on the couch was the problem.

IdentiScan ran across him and came back with nothing — no lien, no ledger, NO CARRION DEBT, which on this block was its own kind of strange. He was young, dressed too well for the couch, the clothes of someone who'd come down a long way for one night to buy something he couldn't buy where people knew his face. And he was lit up wrong, pupils blown, jaw working, one leg jackhammering, deep in whatever he'd come for and well past the part where it felt good. He'd been watching the door since it opened, the way a cornered thing watches the only exit.

"He's not on the order," Levin said, quiet, stopping where he was, halfway across the floor between the two of them.

"I know."

"You want me to —"

"I want you to do the arm and I want us to leave," Thomas said. "He's not ours. He's just high."

But the young man had heard Carrion, and heard order, and in the swamp he was wading through those words had turned into something with teeth, and he came up off the couch fast and wrong, both hands going for the waistband of his too-good pants.

"Hey," Thomas said, from the doorway, too far to reach him. "Hey — friend —"

Behind the counter Osei was already folding toward the floor, hands still up, wanting no part of it, saying to no one, "I'm not here, I'm not here, I'm not —"

And Levin — caught flat in the middle of the room, four drops into the morning and a half-second behind all of it — turned toward the couch with the repo pad in his off hand and his sidearm coming up in his right.

[Beat 6]
There was a gun in the young man's waistband. That part was real, and later it was the part Thomas held onto, on the nights he needed to hold onto something — there had been a gun, the kid had pulled a gun, any board of review would write it clean.

But the kid was so far down in the Tears and whatever he'd cut it with that the gun came out of his pants sideways and half-fumbled, the safety still on, his thumb nowhere near it, a man trying to swim in air. A sober hand across the room would have seen that. A sober hand would have had a full half-second to read he can't even hold it and put a boot on it and end the morning with a story to tell.

Levin's hand was not sober. Levin was a half-second behind the whole room and had been since 4:12, and into that half-second the drops poured their flat grey certainty, and he did the clean thing, the trained thing, the wrong thing — he put one round center mass before the kid's gun had finished clearing the waistband.

The sound of it in the apartment was very loud.

Then it wasn't.

The young man sat back down on the couch he'd come up off of, almost gently, as if he'd reconsidered, and the gun finished its fall and hit the floor and spun and stopped, and a stain came through the front of the too-good shirt and spread, and his blown pupils found Thomas and stayed there, surprised, getting less surprised, and his jackhammer leg went still.

"No," Thomas said.

He was across the room before he decided to be. The wound was high and center and wrong, the worst kind of wrong, the kind where the round went looking for the pump and found it. Thomas had taken enough hearts out of enough chests to know the geography of one going bad from the outside. He got pressure on it with both hands and felt the kid's pulse stuttering up into his palms, fast and thin and arguing.

Behind his eyes, IdentiScan finished the work it had started when the kid first turned to the door — the facial-recognition pass it ran on everyone, slower than the lien check because it had to reach further, out past Carrion's ledger into the public stacks and the social graph and the places a clean account didn't tell you anything but a face still did. It finished now, a beat too late to matter, the way it always finished a beat too late. The tag bloomed across Thomas's vision in calm grey letters.

HAN DO-YUN. NO CARRION DEBT.

And underneath it, the line that took the floor out from under the room:

KNOWN ASSOCIATION — LOTUS SYNDICATE (FAMILIAL). MOTHER: HAN JEONG-SUK (STEM).

"Levin," Thomas said, very quietly, with both hands full of a Lotus Stem's son. "Levin. Look at his name."

Levin's sidearm was still up. He lowered it slowly. Behind the glass something was trying to surface and couldn't, and his eyes did the thing they did when the dead started arriving early, going to a corner of the room where there was nothing, and coming back. He read the tag. Thomas watched him read it. He watched the number land — not a Φ number, the other kind, the kind that came due in blood and had no grievance line on any card.

From behind the counter, very small, Bram Osei said, "I didn't see anything." A pause. "Oh, you stupid — you shot Han Do-yun. You shot — " His voice climbed and then caught itself, because Osei's whole life was knowing when to be quiet, and he went quiet. His good arm and his financed one came back up over the edge of the counter, both palms open, both shaking now.

The kid's pulse argued in Thomas's hands. Still arguing. Not won yet.

They were the only two men in the GLMZ who could walk into a room with a dying boy in it and a case in the van that could keep him from dying. For seventy-two hours, anyway. They didn't fix people. But they could put the clock back on.

"Get the case," Thomas said. "Get Mama. Right now."

[Beat 7]
He read the scene from the street before going in: a scene tells you most of what it knows before you touch it, and touching it first only adds your own noise to the page. The foot traffic on the near sidewalk had a bend in it that the eye almost missed — people coming up the walk drifted a half-step wide of the stairwell door and did not know they were doing it. The body has its own democracy. It votes with its feet, and it votes against the place where something has recently gone wrong, and a hundred people will route around a death they were never told about.

A window on the fourth floor was cracked, a single radial star, the kind a shoulder makes, not a round.

He went up. The shop door was unlocked, which told him the trade here had stopped. Inside, one room. He stood in it and let it speak.

The vial drawer behind the counter was empty — not tipped, not scattered, not looted. Emptied clean, drawer slid shut after. Someone had taken the whole stock as a transaction, not a theft. A purchase. People did not buy out a dealer they had just made bleed; they took. This was something quieter. This was a rescue paying its way out.

Blood on the boards, one source, low and pooled — a man sitting, or going down to sit, when the round found him. Soft.

A gun lay by the couch leg, the safety still on. He crouched over it without lifting it. A man who clears a holster and never thumbs the safety is a man who never meant the threat, or could not finish it. Either way: not a hit team. Amateurs with a reason.

He fieldstripped the gun, set the parts in a row on the floor, and left them. A whole gun was a thing a frightened tenant might pick up and use badly; parts were just parts.

On the door handle, going out, a thread of orange synthetic snagged on the latch screw. Carrion Logistics coverall fiber. The kind a man wore to carry the legally dead out of buildings exactly like this one, in a truck nobody looked at twice.

He opened the channel. "He's alive. He's with a Carrion crew. I'll want their manifests for the district." The manifests came back before he reached the street. He folded the day into a route and began to walk it.

[Beat 8]
Levin came back with the orange case and his hands had stopped shaking, because there was work now, and work was the one thing the drops couldn't take from him. He cracked the case on the floor beside the couch and lifted the big unit out and Thomas took his hands off the wound only long enough for the carapace to come down and take hold, legs unfolding across the kid's ribs, the drug well already finding the vein. Han Do-yun went under without knowing he'd been awake.

"Talk to me," Levin said. He was reading the unit's feed off his own neuretics, flat and fast and professional, the addict gone, the technician back.

"Round's through the lower chamber. He's not getting that heart back." Thomas was watching the same feed. "Tell it to take it."

"It already wants to."

The Scarab did not hesitate the way men did. It opened the chest along the line it had opened a thousand chests, spread the ribs, clamped what needed clamping, and lifted Han Do-yun's ruined heart out of him into the cradle, a torn fist of muscle the round had ended. And then the unit reached into its own underbelly, into the bay where the loaners rode, and seated the stopgap — a heart that had never been alive and never would be, a perfusion engine the size and weight of the real thing, rated by the company for exactly seventy-two hours of borrowed time. It hooked the loaner to the great vessels, primed it, and started it, and the kid's pulse came back up under the carapace, steady now, mechanical now, a clock with a face you couldn't read.

A line stood up in the unit's feed, calm and grey and ruinous.

LOANER SEATED. 72:00:00. PERMANENT REPLACEMENT REQUIRED.

And under it, the part that turned Thomas's stomach to water:

TISSUE MATCH — RARE PHENOTYPE. NO COMPATIBLE UNIT IN REGIONAL STOCK.

"Read that twice," Thomas said.

Levin read it twice. "He's a rare type."

"He's a rare type, and the loaner's good for three days, and there's no heart on any shelf in this region that his body won't throw on the floor." Thomas sat back on his heels. The math did itself, the way the math always did itself, except this time every number in it was a way to die. Seventy-two hours to find a heart that didn't exist, for a Lotus Stem's son they'd put a hole in, before a loaner ran down inside his chest and finished what Levin's half-second had started.

Bram Osei was still behind the counter, watching all of this over the edge of it, having the worst morning of a life made of bad mornings.

Levin stood up. He looked at Osei for a while with the far-off thing in his eyes, and Thomas got ready to talk him out of whatever the drops were about to suggest, and then Levin surprised him.

"Your arm's current," Levin said.

Osei blinked. "What?"

"Clerical error. Account flagged wrong. The recall's voided." Levin crossed to the counter, unhurried, and set the repo pad down flat, screen dark, a gun he was choosing not to fire. "You keep the arm. You keep both arms. You never saw two men today, you never saw a sick kid on your couch, you never heard a sound." He leaned in, and his voice did the only warm thing Thomas ever heard it do, which was the thing it did when it wanted something. "And you give me everything you've got in the back. All of it. The Tears especially. All of it."

Osei looked at the dark pad, and at the kid the carapace had folded itself over, and at the math on Levin's face that he couldn't read but could price, and he made the only deal on the table.

"Take the whole drawer," he said.

They took the whole drawer. They took the kid, too — Thomas carried the front of the carapace and Levin the back, down four flights and out the rear into the Wagon, where the ward came up out of the floor and folded around Han Do-yun, vent and perfuser waking, the life-support berth doing the one thing it was built for, six feet from the cold racks of the larder where, if the next seventy-two hours went the way mornings like this usually went, he'd be riding by tomorrow night.

Levin shut the rear doors. In the quiet of the cab he capped a fresh vial of Osei's Tears into his palm and didn't open it, just held it, looking at the back of the Wagon through the partition.

"We could call it in," he said. "Live recovery. Hand him to a Lotus clinic. Walk away."

"He's got a hole in him the exact shape of your morning," Thomas said. "You want to be the two men who delivered Han Jeong-suk's son to her door with a Carrion round in his chest and a story about how it wasn't our fault?"

Levin didn't answer.

"We don't call it in," Thomas said. "We fix it. We put the heart back the way it's supposed to be, we put him back where he's supposed to be, and we are never, ever here." He started the Wagon. "Pull up everything you can find on Han Do-yun. I want to know why a Stem's son was buying off the floor on a Tuesday instead of being wherever a Stem's son is supposed to be."

Levin pulled it up. It took him a minute. Then he laughed, once, with no humor in it at all.

"Tomorrow," Levin said. "He's supposed to be getting married tomorrow. Dawn ceremony. Big one."

The clock in the back read 71:48:09, and counting.

## Chapter 2

[Beat 9]
Thomas drove nowhere in particular for the first twenty minutes, because a Wagon that kept moving was a Wagon nobody looked at twice, and he needed to think.

"Tell me what we're actually in," Levin said. He'd put the vial away. The drawer of Osei's product sat between his feet, and Thomas could see him not looking at it, which was its own kind of work.

"You know what the Lotus is."

"I know they pay on time and they don't haggle."

"That's because they're not a gang, they're a church, and the thing they worship is blood. Born in or you're nothing. East-blood, old-country, pure enough for the people who decide what pure enough means. You can pay them, you can bleed for them, you can't ever be them. They'll use a man his whole life and never once sit him at the table." Thomas took them up onto the elevated where the cameras were corporate, not Lotus. "And the kid's a Stem's son. His mother's Han Jeong-suk — she runs half the south side and keeps a ledger in her head of every favor and every wrong, and she pays both out to the decimal. Tomorrow that kid marries another pure line. You don't do a dawn wedding for love in that world. You do it to stitch two bloodlines together so the ones underneath stop sharpening knives."

"So the wedding's the thing keeping a war off."

"The wedding's the thing keeping a war off. And it doesn't happen if the groom's a no-show. It really doesn't happen if the groom turns up with a hole in him and a Carrion serial number on the round." Thomas came down off the elevated.
