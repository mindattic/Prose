# Vultures at the Door

_In the GLMZ, the body is just another financed asset. Miss a payment on your cloned kidney, your cybernetic legs, the heart a splicer installed on a thirty-month plan — and before dawn a Carrion Reclamation Wagon rolls up to collect.

Tomas and Levin are Vultures. They run the route. They do not ask questions.

Then a routine stop puts a bullet in the wrong man — the only son of the most feared crime boss on the south side — and they have one night to fix what they broke. That means sourcing a replacement heart that exists nowhere on any legal shelf, building a lie no one will interrogate, and staying invisible while the best hunter in the city works backward down the exact same trail.

The problem isn't the clock. The problem is that the heart they need belongs to someone who is still using it.

VULTURES AT THE DOOR is a story about two men who don't ask questions — and one night that makes that impossible._

[Beat 1]
Levin sat in the driver's seat, head tipped back, a vial held over one eye. He let a single drop fall. He blinked. The eye went glassy and far away, then came back wrong. He did the other one. Then he capped the Tears, dropped the vial in the door pocket, and started the engine.

[Beat 2]
Tomas watched and said nothing. They'd had the talk about the drops before, and having it again at four in the morning was a waste of both of them.

The deal was simple. Levin dosed. Levin drove. Levin was gentler with the accounts than a sober man would be. Tomas ran the case and pretended not to see what was behind the glass in his partner's eyes.

The route wanted six pickups and a recall, on time, every shift. Give it that and it asked for nothing else.

[Beat 3]
The manifest came up on his neuretics at 4:12, the way it did every morning. A column of names, addresses, account balances. Six pickups. One recall. The number beside each name was what the body owed, and it was never zero.

[Beat 4]
"Morning," Tomas said.

"It's not," Levin said, and pulled them out of the yard.

[Beat 5]
The yard sat between a bio-rendering plant and a sealed distribution hub. At four in the morning it smelled like the rendering plant — something warm and clinical the wind pushed at you in slow pulses.

Three years in, Tomas had stopped smelling it, the way you stop hearing a refrigerator. It was just what the early shift smelled like.

[Beat 6]
Carrion called them Reclamation Agents. The street called them Vultures. The city made too many bodies, too fast, all of them financed against tomorrow. When the payments stopped, somebody drove out before dawn and took back what the city was owed.

[Beat 7]
Tomas had a daughter. Paz. Nine years old. She had a thing in her genes with a long name that he and her mother had stopped saying out loud.

The only thing that kept the long name from finishing its sentence was gene therapy — Φ12,000 every six months, paid in full, no plans, no deferral. The clinic set the price and showed no mercy. Six pickups covered most of a week's pay, and a week's pay was most of what Paz's therapy cost each month. That was why Tomas Alvarado could open a person up at four-thirty in the morning and keep his hands steady. He never said it out loud. You didn't say that to a partner four drops into the morning and still somehow driving.

[Beat 8]
"What've we got," Levin asked. He drove with two fingers. The rest of him was wherever the drops had taken him.

"Kidney, first stop. Series-7, financed thirty months ago. Account's been dead since spring." Tomas read it off his neuretics. "South side, mid-strata. Old man."

"Family?" Levin asked.

"A daughter. Lives with him."

"Wonderful," Levin said. "I love a daughter."

Tomas looked at him. "You don't have to love anything. You have to carry the cooler and not say the word *Tears* in front of the account."

"I have never," Levin said, with the dignity of a man four drops into the morning, "said that word in front of an account."

"You told a woman her father would feel a 'lovely warmth.'"

"That was a compliment." A beat. "He did feel it?"

[Beat 9]
Tomas turned back to the windshield. The route ran south. The elevated road lifted them over the sleeping strata, the towers stacked into the dark with their windows out.

Past the eastern edge lay the white gap every map printed — where a block had gone to a Rift and stayed gone, the air beyond it leaning in a direction that made Tomas put his eyes back on the road.

[Beat 10]
Somewhere down in all of it, a seventy-three-year-old man was asleep in a bed with a machine beside it. He didn't know the day had a number on it, and the number was his.

The organ in his chest had been put there by a Splicer — a neighborhood ripperdoc with a finance deal and a storefront license — and now Carrion was coming to collect on the Splicer's paperwork.

Tomas set the address. The Wagon took the long curve down toward the south side. The orange case rocked once in its bracket and went still.

[Beat 11]
He pulled up the first account to confirm the recall order — chain of custody, Splicer's paper, Carrion's authorization stamped across the bottom.

He'd read a thousand of them. He read this one twice, and the second time his stomach went cold.

[Beat 12]
The stamp wasn't there. Where the authorization should have been was a flag he'd never seen fire on a live manifest: **ACCOUNT CONTESTED — LITIGATION HOLD — DO NOT RECLAIM.** And under it, in the small gray type Carrion used for the things it didn't want you to read out loud, the name of the account holder's next of kin. The daughter. She wasn't a widow or a shut-in. She was a litigator, and she'd filed against Carrion three weeks ago, and the hold had gone up nineteen days back.

[Beat 13]
Which meant somebody at dispatch had pushed a recall onto a contested body on purpose, timed it for the pre-dawn shift when nobody read the fine print, and put Tomas's name and Levin's hands on the paperwork.

If they took the kidney, they were the ones who'd crossed the hold — not dispatch, not Carrion. The two Vultures on the manifest.

[Beat 14]
"Levin," Tomas said. His voice came out wrong. "Pull over."

"We're nine minutes out."

"Pull *over*?" But Levin only smiled at the road, four drops deep and gentle, and kept them curving down toward the south side, toward the old man, toward the address Tomas had already set and couldn't now unset — the Wagon locking the route once the destination was confirmed, no override before arrival.

[Beat 15]
He was already awake when the call came.

The machete lay on the table, handle forward, cord-wrapped — the same blade a dying man's hands had given him in a field, at the end of a war nobody in the GLMZ — the sprawl everyone just called the zone — had ever heard of.

He didn't keep it for sentiment. He kept it because it worked, and he'd never needed anything fancier.

[Beat 16]
The call came at 3:40, audio only. The Lotus woman always called that way — never video. Video was a courtesy, and a dog didn't need courtesy.

She never used his name. He'd given it to her once, early — the name his mother gave him on the coast — and she'd looked at him the way you look at a dog that's learned to sit — waiting for the trick to end. He didn't offer it again. The name cost him nothing to keep.

[Beat 17]
"A boy is missing," she said. "Han Do-yun. Since yesterday evening. The house wants him found before it becomes a thing the house has to explain."

"Send me what he looks like," Ekow said. "His last route. Three names of people who saw him last."

[Beat 18]
It came through his neuretics before he finished asking. The one thing the Lotus never withheld from him was information — the way you don't withhold the scent from the hound. The photo bloomed behind his eyes. A soft son with a soft face, the face of someone who'd never had to read a room before walking into it.

Ekow read the coat himself, the way he read all of it: a Thornback, gray-zone music scene, distressed at the seams by a machine and not by any wear the boy had put on it — a rich boy's costume for a borrowed look, bought to pass in a place he'd never once had to survive. He studied the rest the same way. Not the man, but the man's habits. Where a soft thing goes to feel less soft. What it buys. Who it trusts.

[Beat 19]
He was already dressed. He crossed to the table and picked up the machete, seated it at his hip in one motion, the handle falling to hand exactly where it should. He was always already dressed. He'd served four borders and two cities for the People's Republic of Ghana, and the army had built something into him that didn't switch off when the war did. You couldn't unmake a man who'd been made to track. You could only point him, or let the skill rot in him like meat left in the sun. So he kept it pointed. Violence was his trade, and the GLMZ had buyers.

[Beat 20]
He went out at 3:44, the machete at his hip where it always lived, handle forward. The Thread hung to the south, its pods barely moving. He went to find the first of the people who'd seen the soft boy last.

[Beat 21]
The building had a glass lobby gone yellow at the seams — the kind of mid-strata block that had been somebody's idea of nice forty years ago and had been managed down ever since.

The company had shipped ahead of the crew, the way it always did. A medical-grade dialysis unit sat shrink-wrapped in the freight elevator. Scheduled around the family days ago. Nobody had told the family.

[Beat 22]
"They sent the machine before us," Levin said, looking at it. He had the orange case in one hand, dead weight, like a man carrying a cooler to a cookout he didn't want to attend. "Mercy by mail order."

"Beats a loaner." For a heart or a lung, the case seated a stopgap and started a seventy-two-hour clock. For a kidney they left you a machine in the corner and the rest of your life tied to it.

[Beat 23]
The daughter answered on the second knock. Fifties, a robe over day clothes, the look of someone who'd been awake since the delivery men came. Daria Holme. Tomas had her name off the manifest before he had her face.

"You're the ones," she said. Not a question.

"Carrion Reclamations, ma'am." Tomas had the folio ready. "There's a reclamation order on a financed Series-7. We're authorized to access the account holder, and we've brought everything he'll need after. May we come in?"

She looked at the orange case, then at their coveralls, then at Tomas's face — the way someone looks for the piece of a stranger they might reach. She didn't find it. He'd learned not to let her. She stepped back from the door, because there was no version of this where she didn't.

[Beat 24]
The old man was in the back bedroom, awake, sitting up against the headboard with his hands folded on the blanket like a man waiting for a bus. He'd been told. You could always tell the ones who'd been told. He watched them come in without surprise and without accusation.

"Floor or bed?" Levin asked him, gently. Levin behind the glass was gentler than Levin in front of it. The drops were good for that one thing.

"Bed's fine," the old man said.

[Beat 25]
Tomas worked the case open on the chair. He lifted the big unit out of the foam with both hands. Mama, Levin called it — he'd named both of them years ago, the big one for the chest and the small one for the head, and the names had stuck the way bad names do.

It came out heavy and segmented, a carapace the size of a turtle's shell, its jointed legs folded under it like something asleep. He set it on the old man's chest, and it woke. The legs unfolded, found the ribs, and took hold — gentle and total — so that it sat on the man like something that had landed to feed. The drug well found a vein in his arm without being told. The old man's eyes went soft, then went nowhere.

[Beat 26]
Under the carapace, the undercarriage opened him along the flank in a clean line, cauterizing as it cut. The smell came up hot and coppery and electrical. Renal artery clamped. Renal vein clamped. Ureter sealed.

The kidney came up into the perfusion cradle, dark and slick and exact. The unit packed it into a cassette and snapped it cold, then closed what it had opened and laid down a dressing. Twenty-two minutes. Almost no noise.

[Beat 27]
Tomas logged the completion. The old man's account went to zero.

[Beat 28]
"He'll sleep an hour," Tomas told Daria in the kitchen. He walked her through the machine in the corner, the cuff, the schedule, the QR that would teach her the rest.

[Beat 29]
She signed where the folio told her to. She had the careful hand of someone signing things she didn't fully understand and had learned not to fight. Then she looked up, and for a moment the not-fighting slipped, and Tomas saw what she was carrying.

"Will he know?" she asked. "When he wakes up. Will he feel—"

"He won't feel the absence," Tomas said. "He'll feel the machine. That's different."

She nodded, once. Looked back down.

[Beat 30]
Levin was already at the door with the cooler and the orange case. In the elevator he said, "Forty minutes to the next one."

"What's the next one?" Tomas asked.

"Recall," Levin said, and checked the vial in his door pocket with his eyes, the way other men check a watch. "Couple of tickets out past the Gray Zone edge. Dealer who quit paying."

[Beat 31]
The elevator dropped through the building's smell — old ceiling tile, someone's reheated food, the faint medicinal sting of the unit's cauterizer still on Tomas's clothes. He looked at the orange case standing upright between them.

The foam inside still held the shape of the carapace, the dent exact, like a cast of something that had been born out of it. Forty-two of these in eight months, and he'd never once asked what happened to the cassettes after he handed them off at the depot. Never asked. Not asking was how the hands stayed steady.

[Beat 32]
"Recall on what?" Tomas asked.

"Cyberware," Levin said. "Dealer financed both legs through a Skarr affiliate — one of the finance houses that carried the paper on street cyberware. Stopped paying at month three. They want the legs back or the balance, and the balance is—" he checked his neuretics, a small inward pause, "—not a number this man has."

[Beat 33]
The lobby glass caught the street outside: a strip of Gray Zone night, yellow-orange, the atmospheric processors already pushing tomorrow's rain toward them from wherever the Spires didn't want it. Tomas saw his own face in the glass for a second — coveralls, folio under his arm — and then the door opened. The reflection broke apart into wet pavement and moving light, and he walked out into it.

[Beat 34]
The dealer's block sat at the frayed edge of the Gray Zone, where the map got vague about who owned what. The ground underneath belonged to somebody who didn't need a map. Everybody who ran a route out here knew that somebody. You learned their border the way you learned weather — not from a forecast, but from the ache in a joint that had been broken once already.

[Beat 35]
"Take the next left," Tomas said.

Levin took it. It put them a block off the straight line to the shop, somewhere Carrion hadn't sent them. "That's the long way."

"I know."

"We're on a clock."

"We're on a clock for a recall. We're not on a clock for whether the curb we set a BIOHAZARD box down on has eyes on it." Tomas nodded at the windshield. "Roll it slow. Once around."

"You're gonna case a whole block for one busted-ass dealer who already quit fighting?"

"Yeah."

[Beat 36]
Nobody had asked him to do this. The ticket had five verbs on it — park, climb, recall, collect, leave — and a dealer who'd already decided, from the shape of his silence on the account, not to make it a fight.

A sober crew did the five verbs and went to breakfast.

[Beat 37]
But the block backed onto Lotus ground. Tomas had learned, on a job years back he still didn't tell at parties, that the trouble on a pickup was almost never the name on the ticket. It was whoever happened to be standing near the name. So he made Levin drive them past the front before they went in the back — slow, unhurried, a Wagon on its way to somewhere duller — and he read the street.

[Beat 38]
A noodle stall shuttered for the dead hour. A Lotus front two doors down, blinds drawn in that particular disciplined way: closed, but awake. One man in a recessed doorway, too still to be waiting for anything with a schedule. Above the storm drain, a two-inch orbit of gravel turned slowly in the air — a little schism, months old, the neighborhood stepping around it like a puddle it had stopped resenting. A single harpy rode the thermals off the tier-edge, minding its own altitude. The overlay panels above the strip ran dark, one after another down the block. No product holograms firing off the shopfronts. No Waxwing Neuromedia civic skin — the ad-overlay grid that painted storefronts with whatever a strip could afford to want — pushing foot traffic through a strip that hadn't had enough foot traffic to manage in years. That was the tell the map didn't print: follow the dead panels to find where the credential line really ran.

[Beat 39]
"Anybody home?" Levin asked.

"Not for us." The still man in the doorway wasn't watching a repo truck. He was watching the Lotus door — which meant his trouble was inside his own house and had nothing to do with two garbagemen and a busted account. "Street's clean. Park it up the block."

[Beat 40]
Which was true, as far as it went. Tomas had checked every direction the danger could come from except up off a couch four floors above his head. A man can only read the street he can see. He'd think about that later, the way you think about a door you were sure you'd locked. For now he told himself the day had a handle on it, and got out to fetch the case.

[Beat 41]
The shop was on four. Tomas smelled it from the stairwell — the sweetish chemical fug of a place where people came to buy the thing that took the day off them, Tears and worse. Levin smelled it too, and something far back in him leaned toward it, just slightly, and Tomas pretended not to see it, the way he pretended not to see most of what the drops did.

[Beat 42]
The door was unlocked. It usually was, in a shop. Locked doors cost you walk-ins.

[Beat 43]
It was one room, and Tomas read it from the doorway the way you read any room you might have to leave in a hurry. A counter ran along the right wall, and behind it stood the man they'd come for. A low couch sat against the left wall, under the window, and a second man sat on it — young, overdressed, somewhere deep behind his own eyes. The coat caught Tomas's eye in the same pass: Ironmoth cut, too clean and too expensive for a man sleeping something off on a dealer's couch. He noted it the way he noted everything he wasn't there for, and put his attention back on the floor between counter and couch — maybe four strides of it, bare.

Tomas came in first. Levin came in behind him, the repo pad already in his hand.

[Beat 44]
"Carrion," Tomas said.

"Recall order. Two tickets, both yours, Bram Osei. Nobody has to make it a thing."

Osei's hands went up before Tomas finished the name — both of them, high and open and steady, a man who'd been on the wrong end of a doorway before and knew the choreography.

"Hands are up," he said.

"Hands have been up. Whatever's on the ticket, it's right here. Take it. I never saw you, I never see anybody."

"Legs first," Levin said. He came to the counter and set the grey slab flat where Osei could watch him do it.

"Older debt." He keyed the lien code, five digits, with as much expression as a man reading a receipt he'd already paid.

"Sit down, friend. You don't want to be standing for this part."

Osei didn't sit down. They never sat down.

[Beat 45]
The pad chirped once, like a microwave finishing. Then twenty-eight months of debt called two limbs home. The financed legs ran their detach sequence at the socket, hip-mounts releasing in order. Bram Osei dropped straight down out of his own legs and hit the floor. The legs stayed standing a half-second longer than he did, empty, before they folded at the knee and toppled.

Tomas caught the near one out of habit. Osei sat in a spreading nothing of his own, the stumps capped and clean where the company's hardware had sealed itself off on the way out, breathing in short careful pulls.

[Beat 46]
"You did that right," Levin told him, not unkindly, and bagged the legs. From the case he took the folding transit chair the company packed for exactly this. He stood it up, locked the wheels, and between the two of them they got Osei up off the floor and into it.

The chair took his weight with a soft pneumatic sigh. Carrion would bill him for it. Carrion billed everyone for everything.

[Beat 47]
"Grievance line's on the card," Tomas said. "Charges off the wall overnight."

"Okay," Osei said, from the chair, hands finding the push-rings, testing them the way you test a thing you're going to be living in. "Okay. We're — okay."

He pushed one ring and not the other, and the chair turned him a slow quarter-circle to face the wall. He corrected, over-corrected, wheeled most of the way back around to where he'd started. Nobody said anything about it.

[Beat 48]
Tomas looked at the pad. The second ticket sat there, patient and grey. He'd half-hoped it would feel different if he didn't look at it. It didn't.

[Beat 49]
"There's the other one," he said.

Levin looked at the screen, then at Osei in the new chair, then back at the screen.

"The arm?"

"The arm." Tomas let a breath out through his nose. There was no clean way to say the next part to a man you'd just lowered into a wheelchair, so he said it the only way there was, flat and procedural.

"Right side, financed, four months past. Mr. Osei, I'm going to need you to—"

[Beat 50]
Osei looked down at the arm like it had volunteered him for something.

"You're shitting me."

[Beat 51]
And the man on the couch came up off it fast and wrong, both hands going for the waistband of his too-good pants.

[Beat 52]
"Hey," Tomas said, and then there wasn't time for the rest of it.

[Beat 53]
There was a gun in the young man's waistband. That part was real, and later it was the part Tomas held onto, on the nights he needed to hold onto something.

There had been a gun. The kid had pulled a gun. Any board of review would write it clean.

[Beat 54]
But the kid was so far down in the Tears, and whatever he'd cut it with, that the gun came out sideways and half-fumbled. The safety was still on. His thumb was nowhere near it. A sober room would have had a full half-second to see he couldn't even hold it, put a boot on it, and end the morning with a story to tell.

[Beat 55]
Levin moved first. Levin always moved first — it was the job, and the start of the job was his. His hand went for the sidearm on his hip the way it had a thousand times. But the drops had him a half-second behind his own arm, and the holster's retention caught the way it had never once caught, and the gun hung there, snagged, while his far-off eyes tried to make sense of what his hand had failed to do.

[Beat 56]
Which left Tomas. Tomas, who had a daughter and a therapy bill and no plan to kill anybody today. His hands were steady, because his hands were always steady. He knew where a heart sat and how to reach it, because taking them out was the work.

So his body did the clean thing, the trained thing, the thing he would have given a year of his life not to do. He put one round through the place his hands were trained to find. The recoil settled through his arms the way it always did. His breath came out.

[Beat 57]
The sound of it in the small room was very loud.

Then it wasn't.

[Beat 58]
The young man sat back down on the couch he'd come up off of, almost gently, as if he'd reconsidered. The gun finished its fall, hit the floor, spun, and stopped. A stain came through the front of the too-good shirt and spread. His blown pupils found Tomas and stayed there, surprised, getting less surprised.

[Beat 59]
"No," Tomas said. Not loud. Just the word, leaving him.

[Beat 60]
His hands found the wound ahead of thought, both pressing against the gap, the kid's pulse stuttering up into his palms — fast and thin and arguing.

And behind his eyes, IdentiScan finished the pass it had started when the kid first turned to the door. It was slower than the lien check, because it had to reach further — out past Carrion's ledger, into the public stacks, the places a clean account tells you nothing but a face still does. It finished now, a beat too late to matter, the way it always finished a beat too late, in calm grey letters:

HAN DO-YUN. NO CARRION DEBT.

And under it, the line that took the floor out from under the room:

KNOWN ASSOCIATION — LOTUS SYNDICATE (FAMILIAL). MOTHER: HAN JEONG-SUK (STEM).

[Beat 61]
Tomas knelt there with both hands full of a Lotus Stem's son and a round he had never wanted to fire. When he found his voice, it came out level in the way that meant keeping it level was costing him everything.

"Your gun snagged," he said.

"It snagged," Levin agreed, from somewhere far off.

"You spooked him. Your gun snagged. And I just put one through a kid I never had a ticket on." Tomas didn't look up from his hands. His fingers stayed pressed to the wound a half-second longer than the bleeding needed, like letting go first was its own kind of admission. Then he made them move. "Get the case. Get Mama. You're going to take care of every part of what happens next — you've done enough with your hands today."

[Beat 62]
He read the scene from the street before going in. A scene told you most of what it knew before you touched it, and touching it first only added your own noise. The foot traffic on the near sidewalk had a bend in it the eye almost missed — people coming up the walk drifted a half-step wide of the stairwell door and didn't know they were doing it.

[Beat 63]
A window on the fourth floor was cracked in a single radial star, the kind a shoulder makes, not a round.

[Beat 64]
He went up. The shop door was unlocked, which told him the trade here had stopped. Inside, one room. He stood in it and let it speak.

[Beat 65]
The vial drawer behind the counter was empty — not tipped, not scattered, not looted. Emptied clean, the drawer slid shut after. Someone had taken the whole stock as a transaction, not a theft. A purchase. People didn't buy out a dealer they'd just made bleed; they took. This was quieter. This was a rescue paying its way out.

[Beat 66]
Blood on the boards, one source, low and pooled — a man sitting, or going down to sit, when the round found him.

[Beat 67]
A gun lay by the couch leg, safety still on. He crouched over it without lifting it. A man who clears a holster and never thumbs the safety never meant the threat, or couldn't finish it. Either way: not a hit team. Amateurs with a reason.

[Beat 68]
He fieldstripped the gun, set the parts in a row on the floor, and left them. A whole gun was a thing a frightened tenant might pick up and use badly. Parts were just parts.

[Beat 69]
He stood over the empty drawer a moment and felt the old thing move in him. Not hunger — he'd never named it hunger. Just the click of a made thing finding the work it was made for. Ghana had spent a great deal of money turning a boy from Sekondi into something that could read a fled man off the dirt he left, and the something didn't care whose son it found or what was done to him after. He'd made his peace with that a long way back.

[Beat 70]
On the door handle, going out, a thread of orange synthetic snagged on the latch screw.

Carrion Reclamations coverall fiber. The kind a man wore to carry the legally dead out of buildings exactly like this one, in a truck nobody looked at twice.

[Beat 71]
He opened the channel.

"He's alive. He's with a Carrion crew. I'll want their manifests for the district."

The manifests came back before he reached the street. He folded the day into a route and began to walk it.

[Beat 72]
Mama came out of the case heavy and segmented, a carapace the size of a turtle's shell. Levin set it on Han Do-yun's chest and it woke and took hold, legs unfolding across the kid's ribs, the drug well finding the vein before anyone told it to.

The kid went under without knowing he'd been awake.

[Beat 73]
"Talk to me," Tomas said, because he couldn't not.

"Round's through the lower chamber. He's not getting that heart back." Levin read the feed off his own neuretics, flat and fast. "It already wants to take it."

[Beat 74]
The Scarab didn't hesitate the way men did. It opened the chest along the line it had opened a thousand chests, clamped what needed clamping, and lifted Han Do-yun's ruined heart out into the cradle — a torn fist of muscle the round had ended. Then it reached into its own underbelly, into the bay where the loaners rode, and seated the stopgap: a heart that had never been alive and never would be, a perfusion engine the size and weight of the real thing, rated by the company for exactly seventy-two hours of borrowed time. It hooked the loaner to the great vessels, primed it, started it. The kid's pulse came back up under the carapace, steady now, mechanical now.

[Beat 75]
A line stood up in the feed, calm and grey — the loaner seated, a seventy-two-hour clock started, permanent replacement required.

Then a second line under it, and Levin read that one twice before he trusted it enough to say out loud.

[Beat 76]
"He's a rare type." He said it the way he'd have read a delivery window — flat, and then the flat failed on him mid-word. "Not sick. Rare. The tissue. Loaner's good for three days, and there's no heart on any shelf in this region his body won't throw on the floor."

He stood and went to the man in the new wheelchair, whose hands had gone still on the push-rings the way a man goes quiet when he understands he's seen too much.

[Beat 77]
The arm ticket was still live on the pad. Levin let it go dark.

"Your arm's current," he said. "Clerical error. The recall's voided — you keep it." He crouched, which on Tears took him a moment to organize, and his voice did the one warm thing it knew how to do, the thing it did when it wanted something. "You never saw two men today. You never saw a sick kid on your couch. You never heard a sound." A beat. "And give me the drawer. The whole thing."

[Beat 78]
Osei reached down beside the chair, found the low drawer he kept for the customers who couldn't reach high, and put the whole drawer in Levin's hands.

"Don't call us," Levin said, and set the drawer just inside the door to bring down with the case.

[Beat 244]
"Yes," the man said. "That is exactly what happens." And something in the way he said it — flat, tired, a man reciting the terms of his own life like a weather report — told Tomas he'd picked the wrong edge of the thing.

So he turned it over and found the other edge.

[Beat 245]
"Unless he doesn't show," Tomas said slowly.

[Beat 246]
The man's eyes came up.

[Beat 247]

[Beat 248]
The room was very quiet. The machine breathed in the hall.

[Beat 249]
"You're telling me which way to point the knife," the man said.

[Beat 250]
The man looked at him a long time. Then he looked at the boy on the gurney — the soft son, the bloodline made flesh, the thing the whole machine existed to protect — and something moved across his face that had been a long time coming, and Tomas didn't try to name it.

[Beat 251]
"My name is Ekow Ato," the man said, to no one. "My mother gave it to me on a coast a long way from here. In ten years this house has never once used it. To them I am the dog they send when a bone goes missing, and the dog does not get a name."

He let that sit, and didn't gloss it. He stood, unhurried. For one cold instant Tomas didn't know which way it was going to go — and then he did, because the man's hands had gone easy, and a man about to kill you doesn't let his hands go easy. The machete went home at his hip, handle forward, and stayed there.

[Beat 252]
"You've staged a bedroom before," Ekow said. "But not this one. You don't know which doctor the house keeps, or what hour the staff comes, or where the cameras in this hall pretend not to be. I do. I have been inside the lives of these people for ten years, learning their soft places."

He looked at the two of them, the tall one and the unwell one, and for the first time there was something almost like the nod one disposable man gives another over a body. "You're accidents trying to fix an accident. I'm a tool that has decided, this once, what it wants to cut."

A beat. "Bring him in off the gurney. I'll show you how this house likes to be lied to."

[Beat 253]
So they built the story in the heir's bedroom one careful piece at a time, and the dog the Lotus kept for finding things showed them where the seams went.

[Beat 254]
The boy went into his own bed — alive, sedated, breathing on a real heart, the chest under his half-open shirt unmarked. No line, no dressing, nothing the Scarab's weld hadn't already swallowed. A man who looked at him saw a man sleeping, not a man who'd been opened.

Around him they laid the grammar of a bender gone over a cliff: the bottles, the spilled product from a drawer that was no longer Levin's to spill, an emptied vial tipped on its side. The wreck of a last free night thrown away by a soft rich boy with one day left before the rest of his life.

[Beat 255]
Ekow moved through the room ahead of them, quiet, correcting. The bottles were wrong — too many, too neat. A real bender left fewer and worse. He took two away and knocked the rest into the disorder of a man who'd stopped counting.

He knew which drawer the boy kept his private shame in and put the product there, because the family would look there first and needed to find it themselves; the lie landed hardest when they thought they'd uncovered it.

He glanced once at the hall, named the two cameras that watched it and the one blind stretch between them, and walked Tomas through it without being asked — the way a man shows another man a job only he knows.

[Beat 256]
"You've done this," Levin said. Not quite a question.

"I've found a hundred sons their families wanted lost," Ekow said. "This is the same work with the arrows turned around." He set the discharge bag Yuen had sent where the boy alone would find it, tucked out of sight, because the story in the room had no doctor in it and couldn't.

[Beat 257]
Then Maisy came out of the larder on the hand truck, and the room went still in a different way, and even Ekow stopped.

[Beat 258]
They laid her carefully in the bed beside the boy — not the way you lay cargo, the way you lay someone you'd carried down four flights in a building where everyone knew the truck by sight and had stopped asking what it came for.

Tomas took the arranging himself. He did every inch of it and hated every inch of it and did it anyway, because there was a daughter somewhere, and the money was going to her, and this was the shape that made the money land safely. That was the thing he held onto. The only thing.

[Beat 259]
"This part," Ekow said quietly, watching, "you should let be a little wrong. A staged thing is too careful. Leave her the way a person actually falls asleep and doesn't wake. Not the way a man arranges a point he's making."

He didn't touch her. He understood, without being told, that this wasn't a body the two of them would let a stranger's hands move.

[Beat 260]
Tomas adjusted her, once, smaller, truer. Then he set her shoes by the bed, toes out — not in the script, not part of the staging, just what you did for someone. It was the only thing in the room he'd put there for the person instead of the lie, and he did it before Ekow could think to direct it.

Ekow looked at the shoes a moment and said nothing about them, which was its own kind of saying.

[Beat 261]
When it was built, the three of them stood in the doorway and looked at the thing they'd made: a soft son ruined and breathing and not a mark on him, and beside him a woman the city would tell an ugly story about to explain him, and not one true thing in the whole tableau except that both people in that bed had been failed by everyone who was supposed to keep them.

[Beat 262]
"It'll hold," Ekow said. "The doctor they'll bring reads in the family's language. He'll see exactly what's written. He won't think to look for a scar, because there isn't one, and a man with no scar is a man who simply lay down."

He turned for the service door. "I was never in this room. Neither were you. And when the wedding burns and the house turns on itself, no one will ever trace how a dead girl's heart found its way into a rare-type boy" — he paused, the closest he came all night to something with an edge of pleasure on it — "because no one will think to look, and neither will they."

[Beat 263]
He went out the way he'd come, ahead of the dawn, and was gone before Tomas thought to wonder whether a man like that ever said thank you, or was ever told it, or had stopped expecting either a long time ago.

[Beat 264]
Tomas signed nothing. There was nothing to sign. For once in the whole long day there was no completion to log, no account to flip, no number going to zero — because this was the one piece of work Carrion would never know its crew had done.

[Beat 265]
A sedan came down the street as they loaded the empty hand truck back into the Wagon. Slow. Tomas felt Levin go still beside him, and they both stood in the dock with their hands full of nothing in particular and were furniture, were garbagemen, were invisible, while the car slowed and looked and didn't stop and went on. Breathing started again somewhere around the time its taillights turned the corner.

[Beat 266]
"That's it," Levin said. His voice had the thin shake in it now — nothing to do with the dead at the edges, everything to do with a storm grate he'd poured his medicine down. "That's the last piece. He's home. She's home, where she wanted the money to go. We're done."

"We're not done," Tomas said. "We're clear. That's different." He pulled the dock door down. "Done is when she stops looking. And the only reason she ever will is that a man we should be dead at the hands of decided he'd rather watch her house burn."

[Beat 267]
He drove them two blocks and parked where they could see the tower, and they waited for the city to wake up and find the thing they'd built.

[Beat 268]
He'd been awake for thirty hours and it sat on him lightly now, lighter than it had any right to. He'd taken the corner across from the boy's tower after he left the bedroom — not to hunt, this time, but to watch a thing he'd set in motion arrive at its hour. Ten years he had spent arriving at scenes after they were over. He wanted, once, to be early.

[Beat 269]
The Lotus men in the parked car didn't know him by sight. He'd cleaned this family's worst messes for a decade, and the soldiers at its door couldn't have picked him out of a crowd.

He stood where they wouldn't look and watched them watch a door — behind which a thing was already true that none of them had read yet.

[Beat 270]
Near seven, the house staff went up. He marked the minute.

At 6:51 a scream came down the tower. It didn't reach the street, but it reached the parked car, and the men in it stopped being parked. He watched the car become four cars.

He watched a Lotus doctor go up — the family's own, the one Ekow could have named, the one who read in the family's language and would find exactly what three men had written for him to find: a ruined son, breathing, unmarked, and a dead girl beside him with her shoes set neat by the bed. No scar to trouble him. No surgery to explain. Just a soft boy who'd thrown his last free night off a roof and not come back alone.

[Beat 271]
At 7:04 his neuretics lit with the grey letters he'd been waiting for. They didn't say what they would have said an hour ago.

*Stand down. The situation is internal. Contract suspended pending review.*

[Beat 272]
Internal. He turned the word over and found it good. Not the heir is taken. Not find who did this. Internal — the house's word for a wound it had given itself, the word that meant the hunt was over because there was no outside enemy left to hunt. Only a bride's line already drafting its withdrawal, and a Stem already turning from a missing son to a fracturing house. The stitch hadn't held. The two pure lines that had been one excuse from each other's throats had their excuse now, lying in a tower bed, and the knives that were supposed to go back in their drawers were coming out instead.

[Beat 273]
There would be a price. There was always a price, and a contract suspended pending review was a sentence with his name in it, if anyone ever thought to ask why the dog had come up empty on the one night it mattered.

He weighed that against the thing he was watching: the house that owned him, that fed him under the table and called him by a collar's name, beginning — quietly, two floors up — to eat itself.

The cars at the curb were already arguing. By nightfall the south side would be choosing sides, and a house at war with itself had no patience to spend on the man who found its lost things, and none left over to wonder hard about him either.

[Beat 274]
A Carrion Wagon sat two blocks down where it had sat since dawn, dark and patient, the two men in it watching the same windows he was.

He didn't go to them, and they didn't come to him. There was nothing left to say that the bedroom hadn't said.

[Beat 275]
The woman hadn't said thank you. She wouldn't. He didn't look for it, and for the first time in ten years the not-looking cost him nothing.

[Beat 276]
He put his hands in his pockets and walked toward the lake, into a city that was about to get very loud behind him. Unhurried. Early, for once.

[Beat 277]

[Beat 278]

[Beat 279]

[Beat 280]

[Beat 281]
