# Account Liaison

*Amara receives an unscheduled visit from an Orison account liaison. She reads him correctly. That evening, the resonance carries something she didn't send.*

[Beat 1]

The building had been a metal-fabrication warehouse, and no one had bothered to tell it otherwise. Twelve calibration stations had been installed without redesigning the bones — ceilings too high to heat evenly, a floor too wide to feel occupied, structural columns every nine meters forcing the stations into rows that bent around them. It sat in the Seam between Zone 6 industrial and the edge of the territory the Orison Clearwater arcology had spent three years quietly claiming, and neither side had taken it. Inside an arcology the air would have been managed down to its mineral content; out here, in the gap where both corps stopped running their edges, you got the actual city — machine oil and the lake and, under both, the iron smell of the fabrication that the walls had not finished giving up. Slug pulses came up through the floor from the Pulse artery a block south, one every ninety seconds, regular enough that Amara had stopped hearing them and only felt them now in the soles of her feet.

Amara knew this building's geometry the way she knew a batch report — each section by function, each anomaly by position. Reception sat at the entry end. The environmental rack in the northwest corner ran two degrees warmer than spec and always had, and she had decided years ago that correcting it would cost more than the two degrees were worth. The lumen mouse lived at the base of her station, where she kept a shallow dish of seed-gel refreshed every morning. She had never touched it. It came and went on its own schedule, which she respected, the way she respected anything that maintained itself without supervision.

She was six weeks into a batch anomaly she could not yet explain — latency variance in Orison-grade neuretic units, flagged across fourteen patients in the Zone 6 rotation. Not consistent enough to file. Specific enough to log. Her personal quality log, which lived on a local partition Cellvault's system could see but did not classify, had thirty-one entries on this batch. She was writing the thirty-second when she became aware of the man at reception.

She did not know who he was yet. She knew what he was not. He was not a technician; his hands moved wrong, with no habitual routing-check when he stopped at the desk, none of the small economy that comes from doing the same six gestures ten thousand times. He was not a patient; there was no chart pull, no biometric intake, none of the particular uncertainty that came over a body on first exposure to a floor like this one. He was not a delivery. His posture was organized around something other than cargo. He spoke to the reception tech for half a minute, and the reception tech pointed, and the line of that pointing ended at Amara's station.

He walked the floor as if he had already read it.

He didn't carry the Seams with him. That was the tell. Everyone on this floor — techs, couriers, dock workers in for calibration — wore some version of the city: the half-second the body spends correcting for an irregular surface, the way a voice pitches up to clear ambient noise that has not been filtered out of the room. His clothes had been laundered somewhere that recirculated its water at exact mineral ratios. His posture had formed in spaces designed to meet a body correctly at every surface, spaces with no edges that argued back. He had the slight interior quiet of someone raised inside acoustically managed architecture, where even silence was a product. He didn't carry outside with him because outside wasn't where he lived.

She watched him cover the distance and used the time to assemble him. The suit understood restraint rather than formality — it was cut to be forgotten, which was a more expensive instruction than cut to impress. His hair sat at the outer edge of Orison's maximum length standard. She knew the number because she had processed the Orison grooming compliance brief two years ago during a sub-contract renewal, and the figure had stuck the way specific numbers stuck: one-point-eight centimeters above the collar. His was within a millimeter of it. A man did not land that close to a published maximum by accident. He had read the same brief, and he had decided exactly how far he was willing to comply.

He stopped at her station.

"Amara Osei?"

"Yes."

"Ciro Fonseca, Orison Neuretics." He offered his hand. She shook it. "Account liaison for the Zone 6 sub-contract."

In four years at Cellvault, no one from Orison had come in person. Orison did everything remotely — diagnostics, oversight, calibration protocol updates, compliance audits, all of it routed through the platform, all of it handled without a body in the room. Physical presence was reserved for acquisitions. For significant audits. For problems of a certain category.

"I wasn't notified of a visit," she said.

"That was my doing. I wanted an unscheduled look at operations." He said it plainly, without apology. "May I sit?"

She indicated the secondary stool at the station edge. He sat, positioning himself at an angle — not across from her, not beside her. It was the position that made a conversation possible without declaring it an interview.

He asked about the batch workflow first. Not about her findings. About the work itself: intake procedure, calibration interval, how she handled variance between units drawn from different Orison production runs.

She answered. He listened instead of queuing his next question. She noticed this because it was unusual in a compliance context, where most people from the oversight levels heard enough to move to the next item on a list they had brought with them. He was letting each answer finish before he decided what the next item should be.

"How do you handle variance that falls below your flagging threshold?" he asked.

"I log it."

"Cellvault's system?"

"And my own."

He did not react the way people sometimes reacted — no minor shift in posture, no recalibration around the admission. "I saw the personal log in the system review," he said. "Unofficial quality file, it looked like." It was not a question. It was a statement of something he had already read.

"Unofficial is the right word for it," she said. "I flag things I want to understand before escalation. Some items don't meet reporting criteria but are still worth tracking."

"The space between tracking and filing," he said. "Most techs file or they don't. Working that middle ground takes judgment you can't write into a protocol."

She agreed with him, and she noted, in the same motion, what he had just done: he had handed her own methodology back to her as a virtue. It was a sophisticated way to open a door.

"The Zone 6 batch," he said. "The variance."

"Fourteen units across eleven patients. Latency delta outside the standard range, not consistent enough to meet the Orison anomaly threshold."

"What's your working hypothesis?"

"What I'm seeing could be equipment origin — production variation between runs. It could also be patient-side. I don't have enough data yet to distinguish between those."

"But you're tracking it."

"Yes."

He nodded once. The questions were good questions, and that was the thing hardest to account for. She knew what it felt like to sit across from someone who did not understand the work — the tells of a person operating from a framework that lived above the technical level, who heard answers without parsing them and nodded at the wrong places. This was not that. He understood the difference between production variance and patient-side deviation. He understood why the distinction mattered, and which of the two would be the more expensive thing for Orison to be true.

*He's on the wrong side of something,* she thought. She did not say it. She filed it.

He asked, then, about her mapping methodology. She had mentioned, in the context of patient follow-up routing, that she tracked transit patterns in the zone — not the official routes, but where people actually went.

"I needed it for scheduling," she said. "Official route data was off by enough to affect appointment compliance."

"How far off?"

"Eleven percent over a two-year sample."

He considered the number. "And you built your own dataset."

"It was faster than waiting for Orison to update the zone transit integration."

He smiled at that, and the smile read as genuine, or as a very good approximation of genuine. "That's either insubordination or initiative, depending on where you're sitting."

"The appointment compliance numbers are in the system."

"They are." He paused, looking at something past the edge of her station, then back at her. "You're thorough."

She did not answer that.

He had gotten coffee from the dispenser near the door and brought the cup back with him. She had not gotten any. He set the cup on the corner of her workstation and reached into his jacket.

What he brought out had a bone handle, folded shut. A straight razor. He opened it and drew his thumb along the underside of his jaw, a checking motion, practiced. His other hand stayed around the cup. Four seconds, and then he folded the razor closed and slid it back into the pocket, all of it done while he was still looking at her — which meant he had done it enough times that the gesture asked nothing of his eyes.

He talked through it without breaking the sentence for the blade. "There's a version of calibration work that's purely procedural," he said. "Interval maintenance, variance reporting, unit replacement. Then there's a version that understands a neuretic unit as a relationship between the tech and the patient. The second version produces better outcomes, but it's harder to train for."

"The second version requires the tech to track things that don't appear in the protocol," she said.

"Exactly."

She agreed with him. She also filed it: razor, bone handle, folded, produced at the end of a conversation and presented as a grooming check. Duration four seconds. Manner habitual. She did not read it as a threat. She read it as a man who had a precise relationship with his own surfaces and did not mind who watched him maintain them.

He stood when it was done. He reached into a different pocket and produced an Orison card — not the platform-generated digital standard but a physical card, which was itself a piece of information.

"If you find something in that data that concerns you," he said, "I'd rather hear it from you than find it in a compliance report."

He said it the way a person says a thing he has considered beforehand and still means. She took the card and understood, holding it, that she had just been handed a trap with the manners of a gift. The compliance report was the floor. He had named the floor and then offered to stand between her and it, and the cost of the offer was that everything she was tracking would now travel through him first.

He smiled. It reached his eyes, which she noted, because it was a specific and relevant detail.

"Good work," he said, and he left the way he had come, reading the floor as he went, unhurried.

She sat at her station.

He had not asked her directly about the anomaly log. He had not asked about specific findings. He had acknowledged the log's existence and framed her methodology as judgment, which was a way of telling her he was not there to discipline her for it without ever saying he was not there to discipline her for it. He had positioned himself as a resource. Not an interrogator. A resource was harder to refuse.

*What he wanted,* she thought, *was for me to tell him what I'm tracking before I file it.* He had not said this. He had built the conditions for it and left them standing in the room behind him.

She looked at the card. Standard Orison design. His name, his title, the account liaison routing, a direct-comm code.

She set it on the corner of the workstation beside her calibration log and returned to the thirty-second entry, which she had not finished. She completed it now: *Ciro Fonseca, Orison Neuretics account liaison. Unscheduled visit, Zone 6 floor. Reviewed personal quality log. Familiar with batch methodology. Asked about transit dataset. Produced bone-handled straight razor, end of conversation — duration four seconds, habitual. Card given, physical. Left direct-comm code. Said he'd rather hear it from me than find it in a compliance report.*

She read the entry back.

Then she added one more line: *He understands the work.*

She saved it to the local partition and closed the calibration log.

That evening she was still at the station — Zone 6 ran late shifts, and the batch anomaly did not keep clock time — when something moved through the ambient.

Five weeks into the exchange with Seto, she had stopped trying to describe it cleanly. It carried texture more than content: the quality of an attention, whether it was resolved or still in motion, what something felt like before it had language. What came through now was not Seto's calculation but the calculation's residue in the body. The slug pulse came up through the floor at interval, and over it, in her, arrived the state of someone who had been reading for a long time and reached a number that frightened him. Not the decision about what to do with it — just the moment before, when the figure is sitting there and the mind has accepted it and the body hasn't caught up, the particular stillness of understanding a thing you had hoped not to understand. She had sent him the initial batch documentation that afternoon. She was in a fabrication warehouse in the Zone 6 Seam at 22:00, and she was receiving someone else's fear as texture, clean and involuntary, across a channel neither of them had ever agreed to open and neither of them could close.

She sent: *Someone from Orison came to see me today.*

The ambient paused.

*Tell me what he wanted.*

She registered how fast that came back — fast enough that the batch documentation she'd sent him had already landed hard.

She looked at the card on the corner of her workstation.

*He wanted to seem helpful,* she sent.

The slug pulse moved through the floor again. The lumen mouse shifted at the base of the station, and she heard the small weight of it without looking down.
