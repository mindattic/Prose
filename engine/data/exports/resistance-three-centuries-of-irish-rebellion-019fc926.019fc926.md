# Irish Outlaws

_In 1601 an Irish army lost a battle at Kinsale and never came back. For the next three hundred and twenty years, anyone in Ireland who wanted to decide what happened on their own ground had to do it as a criminal. RESISTANCE follows eight of them. Hugh O'Neill, who nearly took Ireland off Elizabeth I and lost it in a single afternoon. Patrick Sarsfield, who rode five hundred horse around an army in the dark and blew up its guns. Edmund O'Dwyer in the Glen of Aherlow with a price on his head. Michael Dwyer, who held the Wicklow Mountains for five and a half years until the British built a road to reach him. Anne Devlin, half-hanged in a Dublin garden and silent through years in Kilmainham after, who died destitute in a rented room half a century later. Robert Emmet, whose rising lasted an evening and whose speech has lasted two centuries. Michael Davitt, one-armed, who invented the boycott. Michael Kilroy, who took a machine gun off the Black and Tans at Carrowkennedy and died in his own bed in 1962 with the President of Ireland at his funeral Mass. Almost none of them knew about the others — Anne Devlin was Michael Dwyer's cousin, and that is the only thread between any two of them. All of them arrived at the same answer regardless. This is a book about what happens after the last real army is gone: the ambush, the informer, the burned townland, the price on the head, the ballad that outlives the man. It is grounded in the documentary record throughout — bounty warrants, witness statements, memoirs, court rolls — and it does not flinch from what that record shows about either side. Nobody in these pages gets out with clean hands. They went anyway. That is the whole story, and it is worth three hundred years of telling._

Everyone in this book was, at some point, legally an outlaw. Not a metaphor -- a formal status. Hugh O'Neill was proclaimed a traitor by his own sovereign. Patrick Sarsfield's Jacobite commission was worth nothing the moment his side lost. Edmund O'Dwyer's men had a price on their heads, payable to anyone who brought one in. Michael Dwyer was hunted through the Wicklow Mountains for five and a half years by an army that eventually built a road to reach him. Robert Emmet was hanged for high treason at twenty-five, and Anne Devlin, who kept his house and carried his messages, was half-hanged in his garden in her early twenties for refusing to say where he was. Three Fenians went to a Manchester gallows for a killing none of them may have committed. Constance Markievicz was sentenced to death for the Easter Rising and had it commuted for the single stated reason that she was a woman. Tom Barry's flying column was, in the Crown's official language, a band of murderers.

They were, all of them, pursued. And the crime in every case was the same: each of them wanted to decide what happened on the ground they were standing on. Put as plainly as this book ever puts anything: the Irish must live free, and that single sentence is what each of the next eight rounds tries to make true again, in whatever way its own century allowed.

That is the whole book. Eight times across three hundred and twenty-seven years -- eight separate risings, each with its own generation of the hunted -- people who could count, who could see exactly how much larger, richer, and better-armed the other side was, picked up whatever they had and went anyway. Most of them lost. Several died badly. One of them, having endured torture and years in prison without giving up a single name, died half a century later of plain destitution in a rented room in the Coombe. Not one of them, so far as the record shows, thought the odds were good. They went anyway, and they kept going anyway, generation after generation, until a stubbornness that began as a series of local disasters had become something a country could recognise itself in.

In 1594 a Gaelic lord in Ulster went to war against the English Crown and won so completely, for so long, that Elizabeth I nearly lost Ireland outright. In 1921 a solicitor's clerk from Cork commanded a hundred farmers-turned-riflemen who fought their way out of a British encirclement in one afternoon and were home for their tea. Between those two dates: a Catholic bishop leading an army to annihilation because there was no one else left to lead it; a cavalry officer who rode five hundred horse around a besieging army in the dark and blew up its guns; a Presbyterian merchant's son from Belfast who was offered his life for a list of names and declined; a housekeeper who was half-hanged in her employer's garden and would not say a word; a young man who turned a failed uprising into the most quoted speech in Irish political history; three men hanged in an English city; and a one-armed former convict who invented, almost by accident, a new word for an old kind of pressure.

No source consulted for this book connects any two of these people to each other. Hugh O'Neill's campaign and Tom Barry's column are separated by three centuries and left no trace of one having informed the other; nothing in the record indicates Michael Dwyer, holding out in a Wicklow valley with a price on his head, drew on anything Edmund O'Dwyer's tories had done in the Glen of Aherlow a hundred and fifty years earlier. There is exactly one documented family thread running between any two of them, and it is worth flagging rather than folding into a tidier pattern: Anne Devlin, who kept house for Robert Emmet and was half-hanged in his garden for refusing to give him up, was Michael Dwyer's first cousin. One family connection, in three and a quarter centuries. Everything else is convergence.

That is the point of putting them in one book. This is not a story about one long war with brief intermissions -- it is a story about the same set of problems recurring, generation after generation, and being answered in strikingly similar ways by people separated by centuries, with no shared organization, no shared memory, and no reason at all to expect a different result than their predecessors got.

The eight rounds, in order: the Nine Years' War (1594-1603), fought by Hugh O'Neill and Hugh Roe O'Donnell against the English Crown, ending at the Battle of Kinsale and the Flight of the Earls; the Cromwellian conquest and the tory insurgency that followed it (1649-c.1660s); the Williamite War (1689-91), Patrick Sarsfield's war, ending at Aughrim and the broken Treaty of Limerick and leaving the rapparees behind it; the 1798 Rebellion, including Michael Dwyer's five-and-a-half-year guerrilla holdout in the Wicklow Mountains (1798-1803); Robert Emmet's rising of 1803 and Anne Devlin's long silence after it; the Fenian Rising of 1867; the Land War of 1879-82; and the Irish War of Independence (1919-21). Each of the chapters that follows takes one of these eight rounds in turn, in this same order, from first shot to last consequence, before moving to the next. The book saves its comparisons -- what recurs, what doesn't, and what the recurrence means -- for the two chapters after all eight stories have been told plainly, once each, in the order they actually happened.

One promise, made here and kept throughout: nothing in these pages is invented. Every fact carries a citation, every uncertainty is admitted as uncertainty, and where the record shows Irish rebels doing things that do not flatter them -- and it does -- this book says so. That is not a hedge against the story. It is the reason the story is worth telling. People who were frightened, outnumbered, sometimes wrong, and occasionally cruel, and who stood anyway, are worth more than saints would be.

This book starts in 1594, and it should say plainly that the conflict does not. English involvement in Ireland runs back to the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169 -- four and a quarter centuries before this book's first page. Between then and the Nine Years' War there was no shortage of fighting: Gaelic lords against Norman lords and against each other, the Desmond Rebellions of the 1560s and 1570s in Munster, the Tudor plantations of Laois and Offaly, Silken Thomas's revolt in 1534. Anyone who tells you Irish resistance to English rule begins with Hugh O'Neill is selling something.

So why start here? Because 1594 is the last time it was a fair fight.

Hugh O'Neill's confederacy was the final Irish force capable of fielding an army that could beat the Crown in open battle and win the war outright. Not raid it, not outlast it -- beat it. Contemporary observers thought Elizabeth I might genuinely lose her claim to Ireland while that war was live, and they were not being dramatic. Kinsale ended that, in a single afternoon in December 1601. The Flight of the Earls six years later removed the last native aristocratic structure capable of organizing anything on that scale again.

Everything after that runs downstream of one hard fact: there would be no more Irish armies capable of winning a conventional war. From 1603 onward the options were guile or nothing -- ambush, raid, terrain, endurance, the asymmetric tactics this book spends its middle chapters examining -- or an appeal to something entirely off the battlefield: a foreign army, a newspaper, a courtroom, a rent strike. The tories, Michael Dwyer, Robert Emmet, the Fenians, the Land League, and the flying columns of 1919 all made a version of that same adjustment, each of them independently, because the alternative had already been tried at Kinsale and had failed completely.

This is not a claim that force stopped mattering; several of the rounds ahead are shooting wars. It is a claim that force fielded conventionally, army against army, stopped being a winning strategy in 1601 -- and that every rebel in this book was working inside that constraint whether they ever articulated it or not. That is what makes these eight rounds one story rather than eight. It is also what made every one of these people an outlaw rather than a soldier: an army that cannot take the field cannot be an army in the eyes of the law. It can only be a criminal conspiracy, and its members can only be pursued.

Before any of the eight stories, one honest question needs an honest answer: why did London care this much? Ireland is a wet, unglamorous island with no gold and, for most of this period, no industry to speak of. The answer runs on three tracks that stayed remarkably constant from the 1590s to the 1920s.

The first is strategic. Ireland sits on England's western flank, and a hostile continental power based there could cut Atlantic shipping or open a second front. This was not paranoia -- Spain actually landed an army at Kinsale in 1601 to support the Nine Years' War, and revolutionary France actually landed a fleet in Bantry Bay in 1796 and again, with Wolfe Tone aboard, in 1798. Keeping Ireland pacified was, from London's point of view, cheaper than finding out what a rival power could do with it.

The second is land. From the earliest English colonization onward, conquest in Ireland meant confiscating land and handing it to loyal settlers -- soldiers who had been paid in land rather than cash, planters brought in specifically to hold territory the Crown didn't trust the existing population to hold for it. The Ulster Plantation that followed the Flight of the Earls in 1607, and the Cromwellian transplantation to Connacht that followed the 1650s conquest, are the same mechanism forty-five years apart: take the land from people judged disloyal, give it to people judged loyal, and you have solved the security problem and paid your army in the same transaction.

The third is religion, and it compounds the first two. After the Reformation split England from Rome, a Catholic Ireland looked to English eyes like a standing invitation for Catholic Spain or Catholic France to find an ally, or a beachhead, inside the British Isles. The plantations were explicit about this: a Protestant landholding class in Ireland was a security measure, not just an economic one.

By the time of the 1801 Act of Union, a fourth factor had joined the first three, quieter than the others but just as durable: simple inertia. Ireland was no longer a frontier to be won: it was Ulster's mills, the Anglo-Irish gentry's estates, a hundred Irish seats at Westminster, a whole apparatus of law and administration that had absorbed Ireland into Britain's own structure. Letting go of that, by the twentieth century, cost something different than winning it had -- and that cost, as much as any of the original three reasons, is what made every round in this book so hard to bring to a clean end.

A word about words, because two of them get used loosely and this book will not use them loosely. "Tory" comes from the Irish toraí -- built from tóir, "pursuit," plus the ordinary Irish agent suffix -aí, the same suffix that turns a verb into a "-er" in English. Read straight, that makes toraí an active-agent noun: a pursuer, a hunter, and by extension a raider or bandit, not a description of the man being chased. That is also how the Oxford English Dictionary glosses the word's seventeenth-century use: one of the dispossessed Irish who took to outlawry, "subsisting by plundering and killing the English settlers and soldiers." The popular gloss "a pursued man" circulates everywhere, including in respectable places, and it is not simply invented -- the men called toraí really were hunted by the state, often before they ever raided anyone. But the word itself, on its own grammar, names what they did once the state had made outlaws of them, not what was being done to them. Both senses were probably alive at once in the mouths of the people using the word in the 1650s: the Crown's administrators meant "bandit," and a dispossessed tenant sheltering one likely heard "a man being hunted for wanting to keep his own ground" in the same breath. This book uses "tory" for the 1650s-60s guerrilla fighters it studies in Chapter 3, with that double sense intended rather than resolved.

"Rapparee," from rapaire, a half-pike, is popularly tied to the Williamite War of 1689-91, and the two words are often treated as clean successors: tory first, rapparee later. This book covers both wars, so it cannot leave that tidy sequence unexamined.

The record does not support a clean split. Redmond O'Hanlon, the best-documented individual usually called a rapparee, was already dead -- shot in 1681 -- eight years before the Williamite War even began. The Dictionary of Irish Biography's own entry on him describes him as "a tóraidhe or rapparee," using both words for the same man in the same phrase [33]. O'Hanlon belongs to neither of the two rounds on either side of him: too late for the Cromwellian tories of Chapter 3, too early for Sarsfield's war, and active instead across the Restoration decades in between, when the vocabulary was plainly still in motion. That is exactly why he is useful here. The honest reading is that the terminology drifted gradually rather than switching over on a fixed date, and that a man could be called both things at once while he was still alive to be called anything.

"Flying column," the term for the mobile guerrilla units of the War of Independence, gets no comparable correction -- it is a twentieth-century coinage and this book uses it only for that war. Where this book uses a period term loosely for convenience (calling the Nine Years' War confederacy's soldiers simply "the Irish," for instance), it is because the more precise term would interrupt the sentence without adding information a reader needs.

Here is a problem this book will not pretend away: the eight rounds do not survive in the same kind of evidence, and that difference is itself part of the story.

The Nine Years' War and the Cromwellian tory insurgency survive almost entirely through the state's own paperwork -- council orders, bounty warrants, court proceedings, correspondence between commanders, the kind of record a government keeps about people it is trying to catch [1][9][18]. The rebels themselves left almost nothing in their own hand from this period; what we know of what O'Neill or a tory leader like Edmund O'Dwyer actually thought comes to us filtered through the men paid to hunt them.

The War of Independence is close to the opposite problem. Its richest single source, the Bureau of Military History's Witness Statements, is insurgent testimony almost exclusively -- 1,773 statements taken by the Irish state itself between 1947 and 1957, from participants recalling events twenty-six to thirty-six years in the past, after decades spent helping to build the state their revolution produced [2]. Tom Barry, Dan Breen, and Ernie O'Malley all wrote their own memoirs, in their own words, on their own terms [3][4][5]. This is richer in one sense and thinner in another: we hear the insurgent's voice directly, but we hear it decades later, shaped by everything that happened in between.

Between those two extremes sit the other five rounds, each with its own version of the same problem. Michael Dwyer's Wicklow campaign is reconstructed largely from British administrative records and later antiquarian research into previously unused Irish, British, and Australian archives [13][14] -- his own voice barely survives at all. Robert Emmet's entire rising is thinly documented except for the one document that outlasted everything else about it: the transcript of his speech from the dock [15]. The Fenian Rising is known mainly through the fact of its failure -- informers, bad weather, a leader who kept postponing -- and through the martyrology that followed its one dramatic moment in Manchester [16]. The Land War is unusually well served by its own architect, Michael Davitt, whose seven-hundred-and-fifty-page memoir is as close to a first-person corporate history as this book gets [17].

The honest response to this problem is not to pretend the evidence is even, but to say so, chapter by chapter, and to let the reader see exactly what kind of document is doing the talking at any given moment -- a bounty warrant, a witness statement, a speech, a ballad, a memoir. Chapter 10 returns to this question directly, once all eight rounds are on the table.

Alongside the tactics, a second pattern runs through every round that follows: the moment a costly or outright failed action turns into something that outlives it. Robert Emmet's rising collapsed within hours, but weeks later he stood in a Dublin courtroom and delivered a speech ending "let no man write my epitaph" -- a line memorized by people who could not have named a single other fact about his actual rising [15]. Three Fenians hanged in Manchester in 1867 for a killing that may not have been any of their doing became, within months, the subject of a ballad still sung today [16]. Michael Dwyer held the Wicklow Mountains for five and a half years against an army that eventually built an entire road -- Ireland's first purpose-built road -- for the specific purpose of catching him; the road is still there, still called the Military Road, long after the manhunt it was built for ended [13][14]. Tom Barry did this work himself, in his own lifetime, writing his own account of Kilmichael and Crossbarry before anyone else could write it for him [3].

This book calls this the folk-hero-genesis problem, and treats it as a real historical question with a traceable mechanism, not a vague claim that "these men became legends." Every one of the eight figures who anchor this book's chapters -- O'Neill, O'Dwyer, Sarsfield and Éamonn an Chnoic, Michael Dwyer, Emmet and Devlin, the Fenians, Davitt, Barry and the flying columns -- is an outlaw hero in that exact, traceable sense: a person or group the state legally declared criminal for insisting on a plain thing, the right to live free on their own ground, and whom the country kept naming long after the state's own paperwork on them went quiet. A speech survives because someone wrote it down and it was quotable. A ballad survives because it was singable and the cause it served was still alive. A road survives because roads are expensive to build and harder to remove. Each chapter that touches on this names the specific mechanism at work, not just the outcome.

One warning, stated once here and held to for the rest of the book: it would be easy, given a subject like this one, to let the English or British side play the villain and the Irish side play the uncomplicated hero. History was not that clean in any of these eight rounds. Every side in every one of these conflicts did things that do not fit a clean story -- Crown forces massacred garrisons at Drogheda and Wexford in 1649 and burned Cork in reprisal in 1920; insurgents on the Irish side burned prisoners to death at Scullabogue in 1798 and executed suspected informers on evidence that was sometimes thin, and worse, and the record of exactly who deserved it is not always clear even now. This book reports what the record shows on both sides, plainly, in the same register, and does not soften either one for the sake of a better story.

**Then and Now**

The thing a modern reader will find hardest to feel from the inside is what it meant to be put outside the law. Not arrested, not charged, not convicted -- simply declared, by proclamation, to be a person whom anyone might lawfully take or kill for money. No hearing preceded it. No court weighed it. A name went onto a document, a price went beside the name, and from that morning the man's neighbours, his cousins, and any stranger on the road had a financial interest in his death. That is what a bounty warrant was, and this book is largely built out of them.

That specific instrument is gone, and its absence is one of the genuinely large differences between then and now. A person accused today of the worst thing in the statute book still gets the machinery: charge, counsel, evidence, a hearing, an appeal. What has not gone anywhere is the older reflex underneath the paperwork -- the impulse of any authority under pressure to solve a problem by moving certain people outside the protections everyone else enjoys, and to write it down in language dull enough that it reads as administration rather than as what it is. The men and women in this book were not hunted by monsters. They were hunted by clerks, working from lists, and the lists are why we still know their names. Every name on every one of those lists wanted the same plain thing this book keeps returning to: to live free on the ground they were already standing on.

In 1593, the year before the confederacy in the north went to war, Grace O'Malley sailed to Greenwich to put her case to Elizabeth I in person. Born around 1530 near Westport, the daughter of the O'Malley chieftain Eoghan Dubhdara O'Máille, she had spent her life commanding ships and men along the western sea routes out of Clew Bay, County Mayo, based from Clare Island. Her clan's control of that coast had brought her into repeated conflict with Sir Richard Bingham, the Crown's governor of Connacht, who had imprisoned members of her family. She crossed to England to ask the queen directly for the release of her son Toby and her half-brother Dónal. The two women are said to have conducted the negotiation in Latin. O'Malley returned to Mayo having gotten substantially what she had come for. [26] She was still alive, into her seventies, as the war that followed played out across the rest of the country; she is thought to have died around 1603, the year it ended.

That war began in 1594, when Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone, and Hugh Roe O'Donnell led a Gaelic confederacy of Ulster lords into open rebellion against the English Crown. It was not a raid or a rising in the older mold. O'Neill had spent years building an army trained and armed to something close to continental standards, and he intended to fight a war, not stage a revolt. He and O'Donnell were fighting for the same plain thing every figure in this book would eventually be outlawed for wanting: the right of Ulster's lords, and the people who lived under them, to go on governing their own ground free of a crown that reached further into it every year.

The confederacy's method showed itself fully on 14 August 1598, at the Battle of the Yellow Ford, near Armagh. An English column was marching to relieve a besieged fort when O'Neill's men met it along a boggy corridor, fighting from trenches, earthworks, and hedgerows rather than in open formation. The column was destroyed: more than 1,200 English dead, wounded, or captured, including the death of the English commander. It stood as one of the worst defeats an English army suffered in Ireland in the entire period. [18] For the Crown, the Yellow Ford confirmed that the war in Ulster could not be won by the methods that had been used against it so far.

The response came with Lord Mountjoy, who arrived as Lord Deputy of Ireland in 1600. Mountjoy reformed the army he inherited — new discipline, new tactics, and a strategic shift away from seeking pitched battle with O'Neill's forces on their own ground. In its place he adopted a campaign of deliberate attrition against Ulster itself: the systematic destruction of crops and cattle across the confederacy's territory, aimed not at defeating O'Neill's army in the field but at starving the population that fed and sustained it. The campaign was already under way, grinding down the confederacy's resources county by county, when word reached Ulster in the autumn of 1601 that a Spanish fleet had landed in the south.

In September 1601, a Spanish force of 3,400 troops came ashore at Kinsale, on Ireland's south coast, sent to support the Gaelic confederacy. Mountjoy moved an English army south and besieged the Spanish in the town before O'Neill or O'Donnell could reach them. The Spanish commander, trapped, sent word north asking the Irish lords to come to his relief.

Hugh Roe O'Donnell set out from Ulster in late October on a forced march south, moving fast enough over difficult ground and hostile country that contemporaries remarked on the pace itself as a feat. [18] O'Neill followed about a week later. When the two Irish armies reached Kinsale, they faced a decision neither commander had had to make in seven years of war: whether to fight the English in open, conventional battle rather than from ambush and entrenchment. On 24 December 1601 they did. O'Neill's forces failed to achieve surprise, were forced onto unfavorable ground, and broke under the English attack. The battle was effectively decided before the besieged Spanish garrison could sortie out from the town to join it. [18] The defeat at Kinsale did not end the war immediately, but it ended any prospect of the confederacy winning it outright.

O'Neill withdrew north and held out in Ulster for another fifteen months after Kinsale, but he was no longer fighting the same war. Mountjoy's scorched-earth campaign, already under way before the battle, intensified in its aftermath: crops burned, cattle killed or driven off, the countryside that had fed the confederacy's fighters systematically stripped bare. It was this attrition, more than any single engagement, that broke the confederacy's capacity to continue. O'Neill surrendered in March 1603, and Mountjoy — the architect of the strategy that had starved him into it — granted him comparatively generous terms, allowing him to keep his title and much of his land. [18]

The peace did not hold as a settlement. In September 1607, O'Neill and the other Ulster earls sailed permanently into exile from Rathmullan, on Lough Swilly — the Flight of the Earls — leaving Ulster's Gaelic leadership vacant and clearing the ground for the plantation of the province that followed. [18]

Something changed in the law's own terms between 1594 and 1607, not only on the ground. Before Kinsale, O'Neill was a lord waging war: an enemy of the Crown, but one who commanded a recognized army, held a defined territory, and could surrender on negotiated terms, which he did. After Kinsale, and finally after the Flight of the Earls emptied Ulster of its Gaelic lordship, there was no Irish army left for English law to recognize as a belligerent force. Anyone in Ireland who kept fighting after 1607 was no longer a soldier of any state or lordship the Crown acknowledged. He was, in the law's own vocabulary, a criminal to be hunted.

**Then and Now**

Lord Mountjoy's Ulster campaign of 1600–1603 destroyed crops and killed livestock as a matter of strategy, with the explicit purpose of starving a civilian population until the fighters it sustained could no longer be fed. Nothing in the law or custom of war as practiced in 1601 restrained this; it was simply generalship, and it worked, succeeding where the Yellow Ford's battlefield victories for the confederacy had not. The famine it produced in Ulster in the winter of 1602–1603 was not incidental to the campaign but its intended mechanism.

That same act — the deliberate starvation of a civilian population as a method of war — is no longer a matter of strategic choice under the law that governs armed conflict today. Article 54 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions, adopted in 1977, expressly prohibits attacking, destroying, removing, or rendering useless objects indispensable to the survival of the civilian population, including foodstuffs and livestock, for the specific purpose of denying them for their sustenance value to that population, whatever the motive. Mountjoy's Ulster campaign is a textbook case of what that article was later written to forbid. O'Neill and O'Donnell had wanted nothing more complicated than to live free on their own ground; the war's last act was a strategy built on making sure no one who wanted that again would be left standing to try.

Between 1642 and 1649, two-thirds of Ireland was governed not by the English Crown but by the Confederation of Kilkenny. The Confederation formed in the aftermath of the Rebellion of 1641, in which Catholic Irish forces had killed a significant number of Protestant settlers in Ulster, an event that hardened both sides for the decade that followed. Out of that rebellion, Irish Catholic nobles, clergy, and military leaders built something more durable than an uprising: a functioning government. It had a parliament, the General Assembly; an executive, the Supreme Council; and its own army in the field. Historians describe it as the only example of sustained self-government by the Catholic Irish on a national level before 1919. For seven years it collected taxes, issued laws, negotiated with foreign courts, and fielded soldiers across most of the island. It was a state, of a kind, ruling most of Ireland, answering to no crown. Its officers held ranks, its assemblies kept minutes, its diplomats corresponded with Rome and Madrid. None of that changed what it was fighting, in the end, against: an English Parliament that had just executed its own king and now turned its army, flush with victory in the civil wars at home, toward the reconquest of Ireland. The Confederation's seven years of government were the context into which Oliver Cromwell's army arrived in August 1649 — not a stateless population to be pacified, but an organized polity to be destroyed. [19] Everything that followed after 1649, including Edmund O'Dwyer's outlawed raiders in the Glen of Aherlow, was fought by people who had briefly had a version of exactly what every figure in this book was ultimately after — a country free, of a kind, to govern its own ground — and would not simply accept that it was gone.

On 2 August 1649, before Cromwell had set foot in Ireland, the Parliamentarian commander Michael Jones broke a Royalist-Confederate siege of Dublin at the Battle of Rathmines, routing the besieging army in a single engagement. Cromwell landed roughly two weeks later, arriving to a war in which the decisive field battle had already been lost by the side he had come to finish off. He did not need to win Ireland in open battle; Jones had already done the work that mattered most. What remained was siege warfare, and Cromwell prosecuted it without pause. He took Drogheda by storm on 11 September 1649. The garrison was killed after the walls fell, a large proportion of the defenders put to the sword once resistance had collapsed. He took Wexford by storm on 11 October, and there the killing extended beyond the garrison to many of the townspeople as well. Both actions were justified at the time, and defended since, as customary siege law applied to towns that had refused terms — a legal argument that did nothing to reduce the number of dead inside either town's walls. The severity of Drogheda and Wexford did not end resistance in Ireland; it fed it. For the tory bands who would spend the following decade in the woods and bogs of Munster and Ulster, the memory of what had happened at those two towns was not abstract history. It was the reason given, again and again, for continuing to fight after the war itself was already lost. [9]

The Confederate Ulster army's ablest commander, Owen Roe O'Neill, died of illness in November 1649, weeks after Cromwell's landing, at the worst possible moment for the army he led. Command passed to Heber MacMahon, the Catholic Bishop of Clogher — a choice his own veteran officers argued against at the time, since MacMahon had no military experience comparable to the men he was now commanding. The argument proved correct. MacMahon led the Ulster army to destruction at the Battle of Scarrifhollis, on the River Swilly near Letterkenny, on 21 June 1650. He was captured shortly afterward and executed. The defeat broke the last coherent Confederate field army in the north. Kilkenny, the Confederation's capital and the seat of its General Assembly and Supreme Council, fell the following year. By 1652 both Catholic Confederate and Royalist resistance in Ireland had been crushed entirely. The transition was abrupt and total. A government that had run two-thirds of the island for seven years — with its own parliament, its own executive, its own army, its own diplomacy — simply ended. It did not evolve into something smaller, and it did not hand off its structures to whatever came next. What came after started from nothing: no parliament, no council, no treasury, no chain of command reaching above a single band in a single glen. The insurgency that followed inherited the Confederation's grievances and its dead. It inherited none of its institutions. [9][19]

What followed was the tory insurgency: scattered bands with no political structure at all, surviving on raiding and terrain alone. Edmund O'Dwyer, a minor Confederate officer before 1652, became a tory leader operating out of the Glen of Aherlow in County Tipperary once the formal war had ended, holding that ground with a remnant force that answered to no one above him. He and men like him survived by raiding convoys and cattle in the surrounding countryside, not to hold territory — they had none left to hold — but to keep themselves armed and fed once every other source of supply had dried up. A stolen herd of cattle or an intercepted convoy was not plunder in the ordinary sense; it was the difference between a band staying in the field through the winter or dissolving. Robert Dunlop's collected calendar of Commonwealth-era correspondence and court proceedings documents the state's side of exactly this problem in granular, bureaucratic detail: bounty warrants issued against named men, complaints from settlers about stolen cattle, and the steady, low-grade attrition of an occupying administration that could raise armies, levy fines, and garrison towns, but could never quite locate the men doing the raiding. The insurgency left almost no record of its own making. What survives of it survives because the government that hunted it kept careful books. [1][9]

The tories fought along wood-lined causeways and bog paths in the Glen of Aherlow that a mounted, conventionally equipped Cromwellian force struggled to move through at any speed. The government's answer, inherited from Elizabethan-era practice against earlier Irish resistance, was to cut broad passes through the woodland itself, removing the terrain advantage by removing the terrain. Charles Fleetwood, who succeeded Ireton as Commander-in-Chief in 1652 and oversaw the Act for the Settlement and the Connacht transplantation, went further, fining entire baronies where an attack had occurred, on the reasoning that a population living alongside active tories either knew who they were or was complicit through silence. The same records that document those fines also document the insurgency continuing for years afterward, unbroken by either the woodcutting or the collective punishment. Alongside both, the state relied on the bounty warrant: paperwork that named a man, priced him, and invited anyone at all — neighbor, cousin, stranger — to collect, with no trial and no soldier's status attached to him. This "discoverer" system paid for information rather than placing agents inside the tory bands, a far less reliable method than infiltration, but it is also, notably, the richest surviving source for the whole insurgency — these men are known largely because someone was being paid to catch them. Whatever tactical knowledge passed between one band and another passed by word of mouth alone, with no press, no organizer, and no way to preserve it once the people who held it died or were taken. Edmund O'Dwyer eventually chose exile over surrender to summary justice, taking his remaining men into French military service. He was killed in battle in France in 1654 — a negotiated way out of Ireland that still ended in death. [1][9]

**Then and Now**

"Tory" — tóraí, "pursuer, outlaw" — was already in use by around 1646 as a slur for dispossessed Irish Catholics turned bandit, the exact men described in this chapter's raids and bounty warrants. By 1680, during the Exclusion Crisis in England over whether a Catholic king could inherit the throne, the same word was thrown at English politicians who opposed barring him from succession — the insult likening them to predatory Irish rebels. Within a decade, the men being insulted had kept the name. "Tory" became the name of an English political party after 1689, and although "Conservative" formally superseded it around 1830, the older word never went away. It is still, today, the everyday name for the party that governs Britain. The state that cut passes through the Glen of Aherlow and priced Edmund O'Dwyer's head did not only remember how to fight men like him. It took their name for itself, and kept it. [20]

O'Dwyer and the men who rode with him wanted nothing the Confederation itself hadn't already tried to secure by treaty and parliament: the right to live free on their own ground. The word survived their defeat by three and a half centuries; the freedom it was coined to deny took nearly three centuries more to come back around.

In 1689 James II, the Catholic king deposed in England the previous year, landed in Ireland to fight for the restoration of his three kingdoms against his Protestant son-in-law, William of Orange. His Jacobite army moved north and settled into a siege of Derry, whose Protestant townspeople and refugees had shut the gates against a garrison they judged a threat and refused every summons to surrender through that spring and summer. The city held for roughly fifteen weeks, on short rations, until relief ships broke the boom laid across the Foyle to starve it out. The failed siege let William land an army of his own on Irish soil the following year. In July 1690, William's forces met and defeated the main Jacobite army at the Battle of the Boyne, on the river north of Drogheda. James did not remain in Ireland to contest the outcome further. He left for France in the defeat's immediate aftermath, and did not return. [30] Patrick Sarsfield, the war's central Irish commander, fought on after his king had already given up — for the last real chance to keep an Irish-held, Catholic-landed Ireland free of exactly the settlement that followed his eventual defeat.

The war's momentum did not end at the Boyne. Limerick, on the Shannon, became the Jacobite army's next stand, and by August 1690 a Williamite siege train — the heavy guns needed to break its walls — was moving up to join the besiegers. Patrick Sarsfield, commanding the Jacobite cavalry inside the city, took roughly five hundred horse out through the Williamite lines under cover of darkness and rode a long loop around the besieging army to intercept the train before it arrived. He found it halted for the night by a distinctive conical rock at Ballyneety. His men used the Williamites' own password to pass the sentries without raising an alarm, then turned surprise into destruction: sixteen guns disabled or wrecked, and more than a hundred and fifty wagons of powder, arms, and provisions destroyed in the resulting fires. Without its siege train, the army before Limerick could not continue effective operations against the walls. William abandoned the siege. [29]

On 12 July 1691 the two armies fought a conventional set-piece battle at Aughrim, in County Galway, and the Jacobites came close to winning it. Their line held through a full day of fighting against repeated Williamite assaults until their French commander, the Marquis de St Ruth, was killed as he prepared to lead a final push; his cavalry then left the field, and the position collapsed within the hour. Aughrim is generally reckoned the bloodiest battle ever fought on Irish soil, and it — not the Boyne, fought the year before — is what actually ended the war. [30]

Limerick held out through a second siege that autumn. On 3 October 1691, Sarsfield signed the Treaty of Limerick on behalf of the defeated garrison. Its military articles allowed his soldiers to leave for French service; its civil articles promised religious and legal protection to Jacobites who remained in Ireland and swore allegiance to William and Mary. The Williamite government did not honour the civil articles. Sarsfield and thousands of his men sailed for France rather than remain under a settlement already being narrowed against them — the first departure of the exiles remembered as the Wild Geese. A contemporary, George Story, reckoned the war had cost more than a hundred thousand lives to battle, hunger, and disease. [30]

Sarsfield did not long survive the peace he had signed. He went on to serve James II and then Louis XIV through the War of the Grand Alliance, was made maréchal de camp in March 1693, and was shot in the chest at the Battle of Landen, also called Neerwinden, on 29 July 1693. He was carried some twenty miles to the town of Huy and died there of the wound; sources differ on the exact date. No grave or burial record for him was located for centuries. A commemorative plaque stands today at St Martin's Church in Huy. [34]

The men who stayed in Ireland after Aughrim and the broken treaty, rather than take French service, became rapparees: a class with nothing left to lose and terrain they knew better than anyone sent to hunt them. The best-documented individual case, Redmond O'Hanlon, complicates any clean account of when that phenomenon began. He was active in Armagh, Tyrone, and Down from the late 1670s, running an extralegal protection watch over Anglo-Irish landlords and Ulster Scots merchants there, and repeatedly evaded official attempts to capture him until he was shot dead on 25 April 1681 — eight full years before the Williamite War began. The Dictionary of Irish Biography's own entry describes him, in a single phrase, as "a tóraidhe or rapparee," applying both words to the same man: evidence that tory and rapparee overlapped in live use through the Restoration decades rather than succeeding one another cleanly at the war's outset. No dedicated modern monograph on Restoration-era tory and rapparee activity has been identified in the sources available; the O'Hanlon record rests on the Dictionary's own summary, and that limitation is stated plainly rather than smoothed over. [33]

Éamonn an Chnoic — Ned of the Hill, born Edmund Ryan (Éamonn Ó Riain) of Knockmeall, County Tipperary, traditionally dated c. 1670–1724 — fought for James II at the Boyne and at Aughrim, and is recorded as having ridden with Sarsfield on the Ballyneety raid. He lived afterward as a rapparee, one of the men left with nothing by the land confiscations that followed both the Cromwellian and Williamite settlements. The sean-nós lament that carries his name dates from roughly 1698 to 1704 and is, by tradition, his own composition. His biography is substantially traditional rather than documentary: the dates and much of the surviving detail rest on later oral transmission, and the song that bears his name is, today, a better-attested object than the man who is said to have written it. [31]

**Then and Now**

Patrick Sarsfield left more documentary trail than almost any other figure of the Williamite War — treaty signatures, military commissions, a described wound and a named town of death — and still no grave was found for him for centuries; what marks his end today is a plaque on a church wall in Huy, standing in for a burial record that was never recovered. Éamonn an Chnoic left nearly the opposite kind of trace. Most of what is said about his life rests on tradition rather than record, yet the lament attributed to him has survived in something close to continuous performance for roughly three centuries, and can still be sung today by people who could not otherwise date the Treaty of Limerick or name the county where he was born.

Between the two men sits the shape of what a culture actually keeps. A name and a tune outlast a body far more reliably than a grave outlasts a peace treaty, and the record that survives best is not always the one that was written down. Neither man got to keep the ground he fought for free; what survived instead — a plaque, a song — is what a country keeps when it loses the fight but not the wanting.

Theobald Wolfe Tone founded the Society of United Irishmen on the proposition that Catholics, Protestants, and Presbyterians could set aside three centuries of division and unite against British rule in Ireland. He did not confine the project to pamphlets and secret oaths. He travelled to France in person, put the case to the Directory, and secured a French invasion force meant to land in Ireland and back the rising with regular troops and artillery. The fleet did not make it ashore. In 1798 it was intercepted off Lough Swilly by the Royal Navy before a single soldier could disembark. Tone was captured aboard one of the ships, tried, and sentenced to hang as a traitor to the Crown whose uniform he had briefly worn in French service. He did not wait for the rope. Before the sentence could be carried out, he died of a self-inflicted wound, the movement's chief architect gone before the rising he had spent years arranging for even reached its worst days. [12]

The invasion he had worked to bring never landed in force, and the rising he had helped design went forward without it, county by county, on whatever arms and numbers the United Irishmen could raise on their own ground. Belfast, Wexford, Antrim, and the mountains south of Dublin would each carry a piece of what followed, fought by people who had joined an organization built on the idea that a Protestant merchant's son from Belfast and a Catholic tenant farmer from Wexford belonged in the same fight. Tone's proposition was the rising's whole argument before a shot was fired: that Ireland's people, whatever church they answered to, should be free to govern the country they actually lived in. Michael Dwyer would still be making that same argument alone, with a price on his head, in the Wicklow Mountains five years after everyone else around him had stopped.

Henry Joy McCracken was born 31 August 1767 at 39 High Street, Belfast, into an Ulster-Scots Presbyterian merchant family — precisely the constituency Tone's United Irishmen had been built to draw in, proof in one household that the rebellion was never a solely Catholic affair. McCracken was a founding member of the Society, active in Belfast from its earliest meetings, and when the rising reached Antrim the local membership elected him adjutant general. The choice put a Belfast merchant's son in command of an armed column in the field.

He led that force at the Battle of Antrim on 7 June 1798. The engagement went against the rebels, and the wider Antrim rising collapsed in its wake. McCracken was taken into custody in the aftermath and brought before a court-martial. The court offered him a way to save his own life: clemency in exchange for naming his fellow United Irishmen. He refused it. Whatever the terms of the offer, informing on the men he had organized and led was not a price he would pay for his own survival.

He was hanged in Belfast on 17 July 1798, six weeks after Antrim, in the city where he had been born thirty years earlier and where the Society he helped found had first taken shape around kitchen tables and back rooms. [32]

Father John Murphy was a Wexford priest who did not begin the year 1798 as a rebel. He had urged his own parishioners against joining the rising, using what standing a parish priest carried to counsel patience over arms. That counsel held until government troops moved through the district burning homes and turning their threats on his congregation directly. Once the choice was reduced to that, Murphy led his people into the fight himself, and led them well: at Oulart Hill in May 1798 he won a real and substantial victory over Crown forces, the kind of early success that swelled the ranks of the Wexford rising.

What followed under his broader command did not stay on that register. On 5 June 1798, at Scullabogue Barn, rebels holding a large number of prisoners — most of them Protestant loyalists — set the barn alight and burned the prisoners inside to death. It is a documented atrocity, carried out by men fighting under Murphy's command structure, and it sits in the record beside Oulart Hill as the same rising's other face.

The wider Wexford rising did not survive much longer. When it collapsed, Murphy was captured. He was executed at Tullow shortly afterward, the priest who had once told his parish to stay out of the fight, hanged for having eventually led it. [12]

On 21 June 1798, an estimated 20,000 people gathered at Vinegar Hill, outside Enniscorthy, for what United Irishmen commanders had planned as a decisive stand against the Crown's converging columns. The camp was not composed only of armed rebels. A great many of the people there were women and children who had come to Vinegar Hill for shelter, driven from their own homes by Crown reprisals elsewhere in the county and gathering at the rebel camp because it was the safest ground they knew of, not because they intended to fight.

General Gerard Lake brought his army against the position and routed it. The rebel line broke under the assault, and the camp emptied in the direction of whatever escape the ground allowed. Roughly 1,500 people died at Vinegar Hill or in the retreat from it, and the majority of that number were non-combatants — people who had come there for shelter and were caught inside a battle rather than a fight they had chosen.

Vinegar Hill effectively ended the main Wexford rising as an organized force. What it left behind was a body count weighted toward the people the rebel camp had least equipped to protect, and a scattering of survivors who no longer had an army, a camp, or a plan — only the ground they knew and whatever fight was still left in them individually. Among those who walked away from the wreck of Vinegar Hill was a United Irishmen captain named Michael Dwyer. [12]

Michael Dwyer had fought at Arklow and at Vinegar Hill itself, and when the main rising ended around him, he did not lay down what was left of his arms. He withdrew into the Wicklow Mountains, into his native Glen of Imaal, and kept fighting. Not for a season, but for five and a half years, from 1798 until December 1803, in the mountains, in the weather, with a price on his head and no army left anywhere in the country to belong to. [13][14]

By any formal reckoning he was, for most of that stretch, nothing at all: no commission, no cause with a government behind it, no realistic prospect of victory, and no way to surrender that did not end at a rope. He stayed in the hills regardless, and the terrain he stayed in became, in effect, his only remaining ally. Crown patrols moving into Wicklow after him faced ground with no roads built to carry them through it, glens and mountain passes that could swallow days of dangerous, uncertain movement without producing a fight, let alone a capture. The British answer was to build one: the Military Road, cut through the Wicklow Mountains at considerable expense, Ireland's first purpose-built road, constructed substantially for the purpose of getting at Dwyer and men like him. [13][14]

He came down from the mountains in December 1803, five and a half years after Vinegar Hill, under a promise of safe passage to the United States. The promise was not honored. Instead of a ship to America, Dwyer was transported as a convict to New South Wales. [13][14]

**Then and Now**

The Military Road through the Wicklow Mountains was not built for commerce, pilgrimage, or administration. It was built to solve a specific military problem: a Crown government that could not get troops through terrain a single captain and a handful of followers were using to stay alive and armed for five and a half years. Ireland's first purpose-built road exists because Michael Dwyer's ground was, for that long, better than the army sent to take it.

The road survives, now a scenic drive through the same mountains, carrying tourists rather than patrols. Ground that cost a Crown column days of dangerous, uncertain movement in 1801–03 — glens and passes with no fixed route through them, no way to know what lay around the next rise — is now covered by a walker on marked trails in an afternoon. The infrastructure that took the state years and real expense to build for the purpose of ending one man's war now exists purely for recreation, walked for pleasure over ground that was once fought for, mile by mile, by people trying to keep it free. Dwyer never won that ground back by force. He simply refused, for five and a half years, to stop wanting it, and the road built to end that wanting is the only monument either side left behind.

Anne Devlin was born in 1780 at Cronebeg, County Wicklow, into a family already committed to the United Irish cause. Her father and several of her cousins had joined the society; none of them fought in the 1798 rising, but in its aftermath they sheltered men who had. One of these was her cousin Michael Dwyer, who by the turn of the century was holding out with a small band of survivors in the Wicklow mountains, a few miles from Cronebeg. [25]

By early 1803 that history of quiet complicity had made Devlin useful to a new conspiracy taking shape in Dublin. In April she went to work as an unpaid housekeeper at a rented house on Butterfield Lane in Rathfarnham, in the household of a young tenant calling himself Mr Ellis. Ellis was Robert Emmet, then assembling the men, arms, and depots for a rising aimed at the government in Dublin Castle. Devlin's presence in the house supplied the ordinary domestic routine — cooking, cleaning, answering the door — behind which the plot could proceed unremarked. She also carried Emmet's messages between the house and his other contacts, work that placed her at the center of the conspiracy's communications rather than at its edge. [25] Devlin's willingness to carry those messages, and Emmet's willingness to try again after 1798 had already failed once, answer to the same plain motive as every other figure in this book: neither would accept that Ireland's government was settled, finished business, as long as it wasn't free to govern itself.

Robert Emmet's plan, when it moved, moved on a single, fixed target: the direct seizure of Dublin Castle, the seat of government in Ireland. It was not conceived as a supply raid on an isolated barracks, not an ambush on a patrol, not the opening move of a sustained guerrilla campaign of the kind already being waged in the Wicklow mountains a few miles from where Devlin had grown up. It was a conventional military operation, planned as such, aimed at ending the matter in a single stroke by taking the Castle outright. [15]

It went forward on 23 July 1803, and it failed within hours. No coordinated seizure of the Castle occurred; no tactical pattern of the kind that might have sustained an effort for days or weeks had time to form, because there was no time for one to form. What began as a plan to take the government of Ireland by force ended, before the night was over, as a plan executed badly and abandoned. Nothing beyond that — no method, no repeatable shape — had a chance to emerge from it. [15]

On 26 July 1803, three days after the rising failed, the yeomanry came to the house on Butterfield Lane. They found Anne Devlin and her eight-year-old sister there and questioned both of them about the tenant they knew as Mr Ellis. Devlin told them she knew nothing of Mr Ellis's business. Unsatisfied, they took her outside and half-hanged her — hauled up by the neck toward death and cut down while still able to answer, the period's method of coercive interrogation — and put the same questions to her again once she was down. She still told them nothing. [25]

Her ordeal did not end there. After Emmet's arrest in August, Devlin and most of her family were taken to Kilmainham Gaol, and what followed was not one dramatic scene but a long, grinding process: solitary confinement, offers of money in exchange for names, repeated threats, sustained for more than two years. Her father was imprisoned alongside her through all of it. She named no one. It was public disgust at the treatment of a young woman and a child that eventually forced her release, in 1806. [25]

Emmet was arrested in August 1803. Twenty-five days after his capture, he stood in a Dublin courtroom and delivered a speech from the dock that ended with the words "let no man write my epitaph" — a line later memorized whole and recited by people who could not have named a single other fact about the rising he had actually led. He was convicted of high treason and hanged. He was twenty-five. [15]

Anne Devlin's story does not end where the songs built around Emmet usually stop. Released from Kilmainham in 1806, she went back into a life without means and worked the rest of her years as a laundress in Dublin. She died, the record states plainly, "of want" — of destitution — in a rented room at 2 Little Elbow Lane in the Coombe, on 18 September 1851. She was about seventy-one. [25]

**Then and Now**

Robert Emmet's dock speech survives whole, quoted in schoolbooks and recited from memory by people who know nothing else about the plot he ran from Butterfield Lane, because a trial for high treason produces an official transcript, and a transcript is a document built to last. Anne Devlin's silence produced no equivalent record in her own words. She gave her interrogators nothing across a half-hanging and more than two years in Kilmainham, and refusal — however sustained, however costly — does not generate a transcript the way a speech from the dock does. The gap between what survives from each of them measures nothing about what either one did; it measures only which kind of act, in the paperwork of the period, gets written down at all.

That gap is not confined to 1803. Oral history and testimony projects now exist specifically to go out and record people — witnesses, laborers, survivors, participants in events large and small — in their own words, rather than waiting for those words to turn up on their own in a courtroom, a memoir, or an official file. The work answers the same absence visible in Devlin's case: the documentary record has tended, by its own operating logic, to hold onto the statements of people already positioned to be recorded — defendants who spoke at trial, officials who filed reports, memoirists who chose to write — while people who simply endured something and said nothing left correspondingly little behind. Devlin's life is recoverable at all only because others eventually wrote about what was done to her. Her own account of it, in her own words, was never given — by design, and by choice. Emmet was remembered because he spoke; Devlin endured because she wouldn't. Both were outlawed for the same want, and only one of them left a transcript saying so.

James Stephens founded the Irish Republican Brotherhood in 1858, building a clandestine network of circles across Ireland, Britain, and the emigrant communities of the United States. He was an effective organizer, skilled at recruitment and secrecy, and a poor military leader. Twice in the mid-1860s he told his own membership that an armed rising was imminent, and twice he postponed it, first citing insufficient arms, then citing insufficient readiness. Each postponement cost him credibility with men who had risked exposure, employment, and family standing on his word. By late 1866 the Brotherhood's rank and file, particularly the American-trained officers who had crossed the Atlantic expecting to fight, had lost confidence in him. He was deposed and replaced as head of the organization by Thomas J. Kelly, a Galway-born veteran of the Union Army who had no patience for further delay. [16] Stephens and Kelly ran the Brotherhood for the same reason every organizer in this book ever did: enough people, across enough of Ireland and its emigrant cities, still would not accept that the country they came from wasn't theirs to govern free of Westminster.

Kelly inherited a movement that had spent years mobilizing for a rising it had never held, and he moved to hold one. Arms had been purchased and smuggled in small consignments; men had drilled quietly in fields and back rooms; American officers had been placed in county commands. The date set was early March 1867.

The rising went ahead in March 1867 and collapsed within days. Government informers inside the Brotherhood had given Dublin Castle advance warning of the plan, and police and military units were positioned before a shot was fired. Arms that had been promised from America and from Fenian stores in Britain did not arrive in the quantities needed, leaving columns of men assembled with pikes, shotguns, and little else. A severe snowstorm struck the south and west on the night set for mobilization, scattering parties who had gathered in the open and stranding others on roads they could not clear. Small engagements occurred — at Tallaght outside Dublin, in Cork, in Kerry — but none developed into anything resembling a sustained campaign. Men were arrested in the hundreds in the following weeks, tried under existing treason-felony statutes, and sentenced to penal terms or transportation. The rising had been broken less by defeat in the field than by the state's foreknowledge of it: informers had done what soldiers and constables were only called upon to finish. [16]

Kelly himself evaded capture in Ireland and made his way back to England, where he continued organizing among the Fenian circles of the industrial north.

Kelly was arrested in Manchester in September 1867 along with a fellow officer, Timothy Deasy, on a charge connected to the failed rising. On 18 September, as the two were being transported under police guard from a courthouse to Belle Vue Gaol, a group of armed Fenians ambushed the prison van on Hyde Road, broke it open, and freed both men. In the course of the rescue a police officer travelling inside the van, Sergeant Charles Brett, was shot and killed while looking through the van's ventilation lock to see what was happening outside.

Kelly and Deasy escaped and were never recaptured. Manchester police arrested a large number of men in the aftermath, and five were brought to trial for Brett's murder. Two were acquitted or reprieved; three — William Allen, Michael Larkin, and Michael O'Brien — were convicted and sentenced to death. Doubt existed at the time, among observers of the trial as well as later historians, about which of the men in the crowd around the van had actually fired the shot that killed Brett; the evidence identifying a single triggerman was contested even as the sentences were handed down. All three were hanged outside Salford Gaol on 23 November 1867. Within months, the ballad "God Save Ireland" was composed to commemorate them, built around Allen's reported final words on the scaffold. It entered the repertoire of Irish nationalist song and is still sung. [16]

**Then and Now**

News of the Manchester rescue, the trial, and the executions moved fast by any prior standard in Irish affairs. Telegraph lines already linked Manchester, London, and Dublin in 1867, and newspapers used them to file reports within a day of each event — the ambush, the verdict, the hangings — for publication in the next available edition. A reader in Dublin could know what had happened in Manchester the previous day; a reader in New York, dependent on transatlantic cable and the shipping schedules of the press, waited somewhat longer. The record traveled in hours and print runs, not months, but it still traveled through fixed points: a telegraph office, a compositor's frame, a morning or evening edition.

An equivalent event today reaches a global audience within minutes, carried by no telegraph office and no next-day edition, with public reaction — support, outrage, commemoration — forming before the official version of events has finished being assembled. The 1867 execution notices had to wait for the following day's paper to reach households outside the city where they occurred; today's audience typically knows within the hour, without an editor, a compositor, or a delivery cart standing between the event and the public response to it. The three men hanged in Manchester never saw Ireland free. The speed of the telegraph did not change what they had been outlawed for wanting — it only meant more people learned, faster, that they had wanted it at all.

Michael Davitt was born in Straide, County Mayo, in 1846, during the Great Famine, and before he was five years old his family was evicted from the land they held. They crossed to Haslingden, in Lancashire, in search of work, and there, at the age of nine, Davitt went into a cotton mill. He told the mill he was older than he was — over thirteen — so that he could be taken on, and he worked sixty hours a week. In May 1857, when he was eleven, his right arm was caught in the mill machinery and had to be amputated. The injury ended his work on the mill floor. He was afterward educated at a Wesleyan school in Haslingden, his schooling paid for by a local manufacturer, John Dean, who took an interest in the maimed boy and covered the cost of his education. He carried the missing arm for the rest of his life. [22] The eviction that started that whole chain, before he could even remember it, taught Davitt early what every other figure in this book eventually learned by harder means: a family without a secure claim on its own ground is at the mercy of whoever holds the lease, and the only real security is winning that ground back and holding it free.

As a young man Davitt joined the Fenians. He was caught running arms and served seven years of penal servitude before his release in 1877. [17][22]

Two years out of prison, in 1879, he organized the Land League of Mayo, and that same year, with Charles Stewart Parnell, founded the nationwide Irish National Land League. Agrarian Ireland already had a tradition of resisting eviction and rent by force: night-raiding, the maiming of cattle, and threatening letters delivered under invented names, most famously "Captain Moonlight," were an established and often effective way of striking back at landlords and their agents — real violence, not a formality, carried out by tenants who otherwise had no legal recourse against eviction. Davitt did not revive that tradition when he began organizing. He built his League instead as an open, aboveground movement, moving the fight into a form the law could not as easily reach: coordinated non-payment of rent, the mass refusal of League members to take a farm from which a neighbor had been evicted, and the systematic, total social isolation of any tenant who broke ranks. [17]

In September 1880, tenant farmers in County Mayo turned the League's tactic of social isolation on a specific man: a land agent named Charles Boycott. On instruction, they refused to work for him, refused to sell to him, and refused to speak to him. Within months his name had passed into English as the word for the thing itself — to boycott — and before long into most other European languages as well.

The tactic worked by ostracism rather than by force, aimed at an individual the community could identify precisely rather than at an anonymous target, and it achieved its object without creating a single martyr for the other side. A man who had lost his own arm to the machinery of the economy that had evicted his family found, in the Land League, a way to fight that economy which required no arms at all. Davitt set down his own account of the whole campaign later, in his 1904 memoir, The Fall of Feudalism in Ireland. [17]

**Then and Now**

The word "boycott" is the concrete artifact this round of Irish history produced: coined from a single land agent's name in the autumn of 1880, it entered English within the year and passed into French, German, and most other European languages soon after, becoming the standing term for organized, public, non-violent economic ostracism directed at an identified target. No other word has replaced it.

The method survived the word. Coordinated, public, non-violent economic pressure applied to a named target — a company, a retailer, a bus line, a regime — remains a standard instrument of labor unions, consumer campaigns, and civil-rights movements in the twenty-first century, on every continent where organized dissent is permitted. It is the same tactic the tenants of County Mayo proved against Charles Boycott, built on the same premise: that a group which cannot win by force can still win by simply, and together, declining to do business. The word outlived the war it was coined in because it did what force in this book's other seven rounds so often couldn't: it let ordinary people make their own ground ungovernable to anyone but themselves, without a single shot fired.

On 21 January 1919, Dan Breen and Seán Treacy co-led an ambush at Soloheadbeg, in Tipperary, that killed two Royal Irish Constabulary constables escorting a delivery of gelignite. It is conventionally dated as the opening action of the War of Independence. [4]

On the same day, in Dublin, the members elected under the Sinn Féin banner the previous December convened as Dáil Éireann, the self-declared parliament of an Irish republic the British government did not recognize. The Dáil moved quickly to become more than a debating chamber. Cathal Brugha ran a Ministry of Defence; Richard Mulcahy ran a General Staff; together they answered, at least on paper, to a government issuing decrees while the flying columns did the fighting in the field. Michael Collins, as Minister for Finance, raised money directly from the public through a bond issue of the Dáil's own devising, the National Loan. A network of Republican courts, meant to replace British justice at the local level, arose almost spontaneously in the summer of 1919 — before Dáil leadership had organized any scheme for them — and only afterward became something that leadership tried to bring under central control. Éamon de Valera, the Dáil's President, spent most of 1919 and 1920 in the United States, largely absent from the fighting in Ireland because his most useful work was being done somewhere else entirely: cultivating American public opinion as pressure on the British government. [7] Every institution the Dáil stood up that year — a defence ministry, a finance ministry, courts, a loan — answered the same question this book has been asking since 1594: not whether Ireland would fight, but whether it would finally get to govern its own ground free, and for good.

In April 1916, Irish Volunteers and Irish Citizen Army members had taken up fixed positions in Dublin — the General Post Office chief among them — and held those buildings against a conventional army equipped with artillery. The Rising lasted less than a week before the positions were shelled and stormed and the surviving leaders marched into captivity. The men who went on to fight the War of Independence had watched this happen, many of them at close range, and drew the lesson without needing it repeated: holding ground against a state that can bring artillery to bear on a fixed address is a losing proposition.

Constance Markievicz had held one of those addresses. As second-in-command at St Stephen's Green, she was the only woman court-martialled for the Rising; her death sentence was commuted to penal servitude for life on the stated ground of her sex, and she served fourteen months before her release in the general amnesty of June 1917. She was by then already President of Cumann na mBan, a major in the Irish Citizen Army, and Chief Scout of Fianna Éireann. In 1918, while back in prison, she was elected to the House of Commons with more than 65 percent of the vote — the first woman ever elected to that body. She declined the seat. Taking it would have meant swearing an oath of allegiance to the Crown she and her comrades were now fighting outright. [27]

The tactics that defined the war were not improvised at the point of contact; they were taught. Ernie O'Malley, an organizer for IRA General Headquarters, toured the brigades nationwide, drilling local Volunteers and flying columns directly in the tactics and doctrine GHQ wanted applied. [5] An t-Óglach, the IRA's own training and theory journal, had begun publishing on 31 August 1918 and kept to a twice-monthly schedule through the whole of the war that followed, despite repeated raids on the printing operation behind it; Michael Collins, in his capacity as Adjutant General and Director of Organisation, contributed to it regularly. [6]

Tom Barry brought a different kind of preparation. A veteran of the British Army's Mesopotamian campaign in the First World War, he joined the IRA's Cork No. 3 Brigade in 1920 and rose within months to command its flying column: a small, mobile, self-contained force built for one purpose above all others — never to be caught the way the GPO garrison had been caught in 1916, pinned in a fixed position until artillery settled the matter. Barry's column, like the others being formed on the same GHQ model across the country, lived off the ground it operated in, moved by night and back road, concentrated only long enough to fight, and dispersed again before a conventional force with more men and more firepower could bring either to bear on it. [3]

Cumann na mBan had been founded in Dublin in April 1914, alongside the Irish Volunteers, by Agnes O'Farrelly, Agnes MacNeill, Nancy O'Rahilly, Louise Gavan Duffy, Mary Colum, and Mary MacSwiney among others. By the time Barry's column was operating in West Cork, the organization's members were doing the work that made a flying column possible at all. They kept the safe houses the column slept in between actions. They rode the dispatches that told the column where to be and when. They scouted the roads ahead of a march and did the first aid after an engagement. In Bandon, Mary Walsh's branch cooked for the column, knitted its socks, and ran the collections that paid for powder, transport, and food. The republican Propaganda Director, Mabel FitzGerald, tasked Cumann na mBan members with gathering local evidence of British reprisals for publication in the Irish Bulletin, the underground newspaper that carried the republican account of the war abroad. A grocer's shop on Upper Dorset Street in Dublin and the Wallace sisters' newsagent's in Cork were among the dispatch centres through which the IRA leadership actually ran the war, day to day. Barry's column could not have concentrated its men, fed them, moved them, fought at Crossbarry, and dispersed the same day without this network operating underneath it. [28]

Michael Collins, as the IRA's Director of Intelligence and Adjutant General, built a counter-intelligence operation that went well past protecting Volunteers from informers. Using agents he had placed inside Dublin Castle's own bureaucracy, Collins identified — and in a number of cases had killed — the detectives of the Castle's G Division and the undercover agents the British administration depended on for political intelligence. It was a war fought inside the institutions of the state as much as on the roads of West Cork.

It was also, without qualification, a war that included the killing of Irish civilians suspected of passing information to that same administration, on evidence that was in some cases thin and in others nonexistent. The IRA executed people it believed were informing to the Crown, as a matter of policy rather than accident; some of those executed almost certainly were informers, and some almost certainly were not, and the record does not allow a clean sorting of the two categories even at this distance. This is stated here alongside everything Collins's intelligence apparatus achieved against the Castle, because both were the same operation, run by the same men. [7][2]

On 28 November 1920, Tom Barry's Cork No. 3 Brigade column ambushed a patrol of Auxiliary Division cadets at Kilmichael, in West Cork, and destroyed it. Barry stated directly, in his own account of the war, that the ambush's purpose was to take the patrol's rifles and equipment — not to hold the ground afterward, and not to make a political statement at that specific spot on the road. The Auxiliaries were an elite, better-paid force recruited specifically to hunt columns like Barry's, and their destruction at Kilmichael was read on both sides as proof that a flying column could meet the best troops the Crown had in Ireland and win. [3]

Three weeks later, in December 1920, the centre of Cork city burned. The fire was set as a reprisal for an ambush the previous day, carried out through a combination of official reprisal policy and unauthorized violence by Black and Tan and Auxiliary personnel who were, by that point in the war, operating with very little effective restraint. There is no clear evidence the burning of Cork reduced the insurgency it was meant to punish, and substantial evidence that it deepened local support for the IRA instead. [7]

On 19 March 1921, roughly a hundred IRA Volunteers, drawn from several West Cork battalions, fought clear of an attempted British encirclement at Crossbarry and dispersed the same day before the larger British force could regroup around them. The column achieved both the concentration and the dispersal using bicycles and motor transport for its scouts and dispatch riders, and enough advance warning — through the same intelligence network that kept Barry's men fed and housed — to gather in time. The British side answered with armored lorries and motorized patrols built to close on an ambush site faster than a man on foot or a bicycle could clear it. [3]

Not every column matched that record. On 19 May 1921, Michael Kilroy's West Mayo Brigade column attempted an ambush at Kilmeena, on the Westport-to-Newport road, and was badly beaten: six Volunteers killed, seven wounded. [23] Two weeks later, on 2 June 1921, Kilroy took thirty-three of the same brigade's men out again and ambushed a mobile RIC and Black and Tan patrol at Carrowkennedy, near Westport. The column shot down the patrol's machine-gunner and the man who tried to take his place at the gun; the patrol threw grenades back, and one of the patrol's own men was hit as he drew his arm to throw, so the grenade fell among his own side. The survivors surrendered. Accounts of the patrol's losses differ — thirteen killed and thirteen captured by one count, eight Black and Tans dead against sixteen RIC taken by another — and neither figure displaces the other. The column carried off a Lewis machine gun, rifles, and a large quantity of ammunition. [23] A local photographer caught the column afterward with its haul, in the image later known as "The Men of the West"; the Military Archives' own caption names one man in it as "R. Joyce." [10]

The war ended that summer in a negotiated Truce.

**Then and Now**

Michael Collins's intelligence war depended on placing a single trusted human being inside Dublin Castle's own walls, cultivating that relationship over months, and trusting one person's access and nerve to learn what the Castle was planning before it happened. It was slow, intimate, and catastrophic to lose — a single arrest or a single defection could undo years of positioning and cost lives immediately. That was the only way, in 1920, to see inside an adversary's institution from the outside.

A modern state or organization has other means of reaching the same end. Digital and signals intelligence — intercepting communications, tracking data, monitoring networks — can deliver comparable insight into an adversary's plans and intentions at a scale and speed no single human agent inside a building could match, without recruiting a source inside the walls at all. The problem Collins solved with one man's nerve and patience is now addressed, in large part, by systems that never sleep, never have to be turned, and never risk their cover with a careless word. Collins spent that one man's nerve, patched together month by month, on a war whose entire point — an Ireland finally free to run its own affairs — the seven rounds before his had already tried, and failed, to win by every other method available to them.

Chapter 1 named the evidence-asymmetry problem in general terms. Now that all eight rounds are on the table, it is worth stating exactly how differently each one survives, because "the record is uneven" is true but not specific enough to be useful.

The Nine Years' War survives mainly through military-technical reconstruction of engagements like the Yellow Ford and Kinsale, pieced together from English administrative and battle accounts with the Irish side's own perspective largely reconstructed rather than directly recorded [18]. The Cromwellian tory insurgency survives almost entirely through the state's own paperwork -- bounty warrants, council orders, court proceedings collected in Robert Dunlop's documentary calendar -- with the tories' own voice essentially absent [1][9]. The Williamite War splits cleanly in two: its set-piece engagements and Sarsfield's Ballyneety raid are well attested in military and administrative accounts on both sides [29][30], while the rapparees who fought on after Aughrim survive mainly in song -- Éamonn an Chnoic's dates and much of his biography rest on later traditional transmission, and the lament bearing his name is a far better-attested object than the man himself [31]. The 1798 Rebellion and Michael Dwyer's Wicklow campaign survive through a mix of British administrative records and much later antiquarian research that uncovered previously unused Irish, British, and Australian archives -- richer than the Cromwellian record, but still assembled mostly from the outside [13][14]. Robert Emmet's rising is thinly documented except for one document that outlasted everything else: the transcript of his own speech from the dock [15] -- and, alongside it, the separate and much sparer record of Anne Devlin, who is known largely through what was done to her rather than through anything she was permitted to write [25]. The Fenian Rising is known mainly through the shape of its failure and the martyrology that grew from its one dramatic moment in Manchester [16]. The Land War is unusually well served by its own architect's seven-hundred-fifty-page memoir, as close to an insider's corporate history as this book gets [17]. And the War of Independence is overwhelmingly insurgent-generated -- the Bureau of Military History's Witness Statements and the memoirs of Barry, Breen, and O'Malley -- recorded or written decades after the events they describe, by people who had spent those decades helping build the state their revolution produced [2][3][4][5].

Eight rounds, eight different kinds of evidence, and no single method for reading any of them safely against the others.

Even within the best-documented round, the record's generosity is not evenly distributed, and one small case makes this concrete better than any general statement could. The West Mayo Brigade Flying Column, under Michael Kilroy, fought the Tourmakeady, Kilmeena, and Carrowkennedy actions in the spring of 1921 and was photographed afterward in the portrait popularly known as "The Men of the West," its members named in the Military Archives' own caption [10]. One of those names is "R. Joyce" -- William "Rick" Joyce, a rank-and-file Volunteer who appears in that caption and, as far as this book's research could establish, nowhere else. No witness statement, no memoir, no individually-attested account of anything he actually did survives beyond his name in that one photograph [10].

This is not a gap this book is choosing to leave uninvestigated out of laziness; it is the honest shape of the record for most rank-and-file participants in most of these eight rounds. The war with the richest documentary apparatus in this book -- named officers, published memoirs, a state-sponsored oral history project -- still reduces most of the people who actually fought it to a line in a photograph caption. If that is true of the War of Independence, it should recalibrate how much confidence a reader places in the completeness of any of the other seven rounds, where even that much survives for only a handful of named leaders.

It should also change who the reader pictures. This book is organized around named men because named men are what the record preserves, but every one of those names stood in front of a great many who left nothing. For each Tom Barry there were dozens of Rick Joyces, and behind them the people who are not in any caption at all: the farmer who fed a column and said nothing afterward, the woman who carried a dispatch under her coat, the household that took the reprisal for sheltering someone and never appeared in a dispatch or a witness statement. The seven names in this book are not seven exceptional men who acted while a country watched. They are the seven the paperwork happened to catch.

**Then and Now**

A modern reader researching their own family's involvement in any conflict in living memory can usually find more about a single ordinary participant -- service records, pension files, digitized local newspapers, oral history projects actively seeking out survivors -- than this book could find about most of the named figures in any of its eight rounds, including men who fought in a war less than a lifetime removed from digital record-keeping. What has not changed, and probably never will: the closer a person stood to actual command, the better their own account survives, and the further down the chain of command a person served, the more likely they are to end up exactly where William "Rick" Joyce ended up -- a name, a date, and nothing else.

Lord Mountjoy, arriving as Lord Deputy in 1600, reformed the English army in Ireland -- new tactics, a scorched-earth attrition strategy against Ulster, and the discipline that eventually won at Kinsale -- and accepted Hugh O'Neill's surrender on relatively generous terms three years later [18]. That reform did not disappear once the Nine Years' War ended. The administrative and military lessons of pacifying Ireland carried forward into the Cromwellian settlement's own bounty-and-fine apparatus fifty years later [1][9], into the Restoration-era statutes used against tories and rapparees in the decades after that [33], into the Williamite settlement that followed Aughrim and the swift abandonment of the Treaty of Limerick's civil articles [30], into the suppression of the 1798 Rebellion and the specific decision to build an entire road through the Wicklow Mountains rather than accept that the terrain simply could not be controlled [13][14], and into the intelligence and reprisal doctrine of 1920-21 [7]. This same body of accumulated counterinsurgency practice later travelled outward from Ireland entirely, applied by British forces in Palestine, Malaya, and Kenya in the mid-twentieth century -- a continuity Charles Townshend's own scholarship traces directly [7].

This is the institutional half of this book's closing argument: across three hundred twenty-seven years, the counterinsurgent side kept its knowledge. Mountjoy's officers, Fleetwood's administrators, the Restoration-era magistrates, the Williamite commissioners, the Black and Tans' commanders -- each generation inherited something like an institutional memory of how the last round had been fought, refined it, and passed it forward, ultimately exporting it to entirely different colonial contexts thousands of miles from Ireland.

The insurgent side had no equivalent continuity. No record survives of Hugh O'Neill's confederacy drawing on any earlier native Irish resistance for tactical lessons. No record survives of the Cromwellian-era tories drawing on O'Neill's campaign fifty years before them either -- no journal, no organizer, nothing but local, oral, unrecorded custom, passed hand to hand and lost as soon as the hands holding it were killed or taken.

Patrick Sarsfield is the sharpest case of all, because his was not a failure that deserved forgetting. Ballyneety was as elegant a piece of work as any in this book: a night ride around an army, an enemy password turned against its owners, and a siege broken by destroying the means of besieging rather than the men conducting it. It should have become doctrine. It became a song and a place name. A hundred and eight years later, Michael Dwyer took to the Wicklow Mountains and had to work out from scratch how a small force lives off terrain against a larger one. No source connects the two men or their methods. Nothing in the surviving record indicates that Sarsfield's raid was written down anywhere Dwyer, or anyone advising him, could have read it.

Michael Dwyer's holdout, Robert Emmet's rising, the Fenians, the Land League, and the flying columns of 1919-21 each, in turn, largely reinvented their own tactical and organizational answers from first principles, occasionally studying a very recent disaster of their own (as the War of Independence generation studied 1916) but leaving no evidence of drawing on anything from the deeper span of rounds recounted in the chapters before this one. Every insurgent generation here solved the same handful of problems -- how to survive after a conventional army is destroyed, how to keep weapons flowing without a state behind you, how to move faster than the enemy chasing you, how to handle informers, how to answer collective punishment, how to build legitimacy without recognition, how to reach an audience beyond the battlefield -- largely from scratch, each time, because whatever record their own predecessors left behind was too thin, too scattered, or too dangerous to keep, to hand forward.

The inversion is this: the side that remembered its method, generation after generation, is remembered today mostly as an institution -- "the Crown," "the administration," "the Black and Tans" -- while the side that had to reinvent its method every single time is remembered, overwhelmingly, as individuals. Hugh O'Neill. Michael Dwyer. Robert Emmet, whose fifteen words from the dock are quoted more often today than any specific fact about his actual rising. William Allen, Michael Larkin, and Michael O'Brien, hanged in Manchester, whose deaths produced a ballad still sung more than a hundred fifty years later. Michael Davitt, whose one act of organized ostracism against one land agent gave the English language a permanent new word. Tom Barry, who made sure of his own memory by writing it down himself.

No one writes ballads about Lord Mountjoy's administrative reforms, or about the specific magistrate who signed a Cromwellian-era bounty warrant, or about the officer who planned the burning of Cork. The institutional record is real, well-documented, and traceable in a straight line across three centuries -- and it is, almost without exception, anonymous. What survives on the other side is scattered, thin, uneven, sometimes reduced to a single surviving document or a single line in a photograph caption -- and it is the part that people actually remember, sing about, and visit on a map.

Eight rounds' worth of people, separated by centuries, with no documented connection between them -- and what actually connects them, once the tactics and the institutions are set aside, is this. Hugh O'Neill in 1601. Edmund O'Dwyer in the Glen of Aherlow in the 1650s. Patrick Sarsfield riding through the dark toward Ballyneety in 1690. Michael Dwyer in the Wicklow Mountains. Anne Devlin in a garden at Rathfarnham with a rope around her neck and her employer's whereabouts in her head. Robert Emmet on a Dublin street. William Allen, Michael Larkin, and Michael O'Brien on a Manchester gallows. Michael Davitt with one arm and a plan. Tom Barry in a West Cork lane. Michael Kilroy at Carrowkennedy.

None of them acted because the odds favoured them. Every honest reading of the record shows the opposite. Each of them, at the moment they committed, could see plainly that the state arrayed against them had more people, more money, more institutional memory, and a much better chance of winning any contest fought on its own terms. Most of them lost the specific fight they were in. Several were hanged for it. One was shipped to the far side of the world in chains after being promised otherwise. One died of hunger in a rented room fifty years after the thing she had refused to say.

They went anyway. That fact does not get less remarkable with repetition across eight separate risings -- it gets more so, because the repetition is the point. This was not one heroic generation. It was eight risings' worth of people, spread across three centuries, with no surviving record of one learning from another, each arriving independently at the same conclusion: that the ground under their feet was theirs to answer for, and that the arithmetic of who was likely to win did not change that.

Every one of them was called an outlaw for it. That was not rhetoric or insult -- it was the formal legal position, and in a narrow sense it was even accurate. An army that cannot take the field is not an army in the eyes of the law; it is a conspiracy, and its members are criminals to be hunted rather than enemies to be fought. From Kinsale onward the Crown was almost always technically correct when it called these people outlaws. What the technicality could not touch, and what three centuries of bounty warrants and gallows and transport ships never managed to settle, is the simpler question underneath it: whether a person is a criminal for refusing to accept that somebody else decides what happens on the land they were born on.

The tactics changed across those centuries. The institutions won more often than they lost. What survived -- in a speech from the dock, in a ballad about three hanged men, in a lament for a rapparee that outlived every record of him, in a road built through a mountain range to catch one farmer's son, in a memoir written by a man determined to get his own account down before anyone else could -- was not the tactic and usually not the outcome. It was the refusal itself. That refusal is the oldest continuously running thing across these eight rounds, older than the tory and older than the flying column, and it is the reason these strangers belong in the same volume.

One story to finish with, because it contains the whole book.

On 19 May 1921, the West Mayo Brigade's flying column, under Michael Kilroy, attempted an ambush at Kilmeena, on the Westport-to-Newport road, and was badly beaten. Six Volunteers were killed and seven wounded [23]. It was the kind of defeat that ends a column, and by every reasonable expectation it should have.

Two weeks to the day later, on 2 June 1921, Kilroy took thirty-three men and set them between two cottages on the road at Carrowkennedy, near Westport, and waited for a mobile patrol of RIC and Black and Tans. When the patrol came, the column shot down the machine-gunner as he tried to bring his weapon into action, then shot down the man who tried to take his place. The patrol threw grenades; one man was hit as he drew his arm back and the grenade fell into the lorry among his own people. The survivors surrendered. Accounts differ on the exact butcher's bill -- some give thirteen of the patrol killed and thirteen surrendered, others eight Tans dead and sixteen RIC taken -- which is itself a small lesson in how even the best-documented action of the entire war can leave an arithmetic that will not sit still [23]. What no account disputes is what the column carried away: a Lewis machine gun, rifles, and a large quantity of ammunition. The same logic that ran from the Glen of Aherlow to Kilmichael, in one afternoon. They came for the guns and they got them.

It is shortly after Carrowkennedy that somebody -- a local photographer, a cousin of Kilroy's -- lined the column up and took their picture with the captured weapons. That is the photograph already met earlier in this book: "The Men of the West," the one with "R. Joyce" in the caption and nothing else known about him [10]. The most famous image of the Mayo war exists because these men had just won something worth photographing.

Then follow Kilroy forward. He opposed the Treaty and fought his own former comrades in the Civil War, commanding the anti-Treaty Western Command until Free State troops took Newport and captured him [24]. That is the part of the story with no clean side: the men who had stood together at Carrowkennedy spent the following year shooting at each other.

And then, having been in turn a hunted man, a wanted man, and a prisoner of the government his own war had produced, Michael Kilroy went into politics. He joined Fianna Fáil, sat in Dáil Éireann from 1927 to 1937, and in 1932 stood up in that chamber and proposed Éamon de Valera as President of the Executive Council. He chaired Mayo County Council for years and retired from public life in 1937. He died in December 1962, at home, on Carrickaneady Road in Newport, an old man in his own bed. More than five hundred IRA veterans came to the funeral. The President of Ireland attended the Requiem Mass [24].

Three centuries of this book's rebels were declared outlaws for wanting to decide what happened on their own ground. Hugh O'Neill died in exile in Rome. Edmund O'Dwyer died in a foreign army's service in France. Patrick Sarsfield sailed with the Wild Geese, took a bullet in the chest at Landen in 1693 fighting for the King of France, and died of it in a Belgian town twenty miles from the field [34]. Michael Dwyer died a convict in New South Wales. Robert Emmet was hanged in Thomas Street, and Allen, Larkin, and O'Brien on a Manchester scaffold. Anne Devlin, who gave the yeomanry nothing while they hanged her from a rope and nothing again through two years in Kilmainham, died of hunger in a rented room in the Coombe and was buried at Glasnevin. Michael Kilroy, who was as much an outlaw as any of them and had the ambush record to prove it, died at home, in Ireland, with the head of state at his funeral Mass.

That is not a moral. It is the difference a century makes, and the people in the earlier rounds had no way of knowing it was coming. They stood anyway.

**Then and Now**

This book does not resolve which of its eight rounds "worked" and which "failed." The same shape of disaster produced attrition and transportation for the tories, a convict ship for Michael Dwyer, a gallows for Emmet and the Manchester Martyrs, a new word in the dictionary for the Land War, and a negotiated Treaty for the War of Independence. Those outcomes are not equivalent, and ranking them against each other would be a kind of dishonesty this book has tried to avoid throughout.

What a reader inherits from all eight, whether they know the specific history or not, is the shape itself: institutions remember methods, but it is the person who chose to stand -- against odds they could see clearly -- who ends up in the song. Three centuries of Crown administrators did their work competently and are almost entirely anonymous. Eight rebels who mostly lost are named on monuments, in ballads, on road signs, and in the ordinary speech of a country that decided, at some point, that the losing was not the part worth remembering.

Michael Kilroy's funeral in 1962 was attended by people who could remember Carrowkennedy personally. That is closer to us than it sounds -- there are people alive today who were alive then, and the Wicklow road built to hunt Michael Dwyer is still open to traffic. None of this is as far away as three centuries makes it sound.

There is a temptation, looking back from a century past the last of it, to treat that stubbornness as a finished historical artifact -- something the Irish used to have, safely concluded in 1921. It is not obvious that it works that way. The pattern in this book was never really about Britain, or about any particular century's weapons; it was about what a person does when someone else claims the authority to decide their life for them and holds all the cards. That question did not close in 1921 and it is not confined to one island. What these rebels supply is not a grievance to inherit. It is a demonstration -- eight times over, across eight different centuries' worth of circumstances -- that the arithmetic being against you has never actually been the same thing as the matter being settled.

This book rests on evidence of very different kinds, and it is worth gathering them in one place, by type, rather than only encountering them scattered across eleven chapters.

Primary sources and archives: Robert Dunlop's Ireland Under the Commonwealth (1913), the two-volume documentary calendar of Cromwellian settlement administration correspondence, council orders, and bounty warrants [1]. Two secondary works lend this book its analytical vocabulary, borrowed openly rather than left implicit.

Stathis Kalyvas's The Logic of Violence in Civil War (2006) argues against treating violence in civil conflict as random or purely ideological, proposing instead that the level and kind of violence a combatant uses -- selective, targeting specific individuals, versus indiscriminate, targeting whole communities -- is a function of how much control that combatant actually holds over a given territory and how much information it can gather about who is collaborating with whom [8]. This book borrows that framework directly in Chapters 3 and 9's account of collective reprisal: the Cromwellian barony fine and the 1920s official and unofficial reprisal are both cases of an incumbent power reaching for indiscriminate collective punishment precisely because it lacked the fine-grained control and information needed to punish individuals selectively.

Charles Townshend's The British Campaign in Ireland 1919-1921 (1975) remains, decades later, the standard account of the British state's own side of that war -- how the Lloyd George government initially treated the IRA campaign as a policing problem rather than a war, and how that classification shaped the RIC, Black and Tan, and Auxiliary response [7]. This book borrows and extends backward Townshend's broader observation that British counterinsurgency practice in Ireland carried forward as an accumulating body of institutional knowledge, later applied in other British colonial contexts. Chapter 11, this book's closing argument, traces that institutional continuity all the way back to Lord Mountjoy's reforms during the Nine Years' War -- three and a half centuries before the practice Townshend documents being exported to Palestine, Malaya, and Kenya.

Neither framework is treated here as settled truth beyond challenge -- both are cited as the specific analytical lens this book adopts, borrowed from named scholars, not invented from nothing.

[5] Ernie O'Malley, On Another Man's Wound (London: Rich & Cowan, 1936; published in the United States as Army Without Banners, 1937). [6] An t-Oglach (Dublin: Irish Volunteers/Irish Republican Army General Headquarters, 1918-1933). This book's Chapter 9 uses An t-Oglach as its documentary evidence that War of Independence tactics were written down and circulated as doctrine, in direct contrast to the tory insurgency's purely oral transmission of equivalent practical knowledge. [18] James O'Neill, The Nine Years War, 1593-1603: O'Neill, Mountjoy and the Military Revolution (Dublin: Four Courts Press, 2017), developed from the author's PhD thesis at Queen's University Belfast. [19] Micheal O Siochru, Confederate Ireland, 1642-1649: A Constitutional and Political Analysis (Dublin: Four Courts Press, 1999; 2nd ed. 2008), 295 pp. Judged by Irish Historical Studies to be the standard authority on the subject. The source for this book's Chapters 3 and 9 comparison between the Confederation and Dail Eireann.

[20] Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. "Tory, n.," Oxford University Press. The source for this book's Chapter 3 closing observation that the state took the insurgents' own name for itself.

[23] On the Kilmeena ambush (19 May 1921) and the Carrowkennedy ambush (2 June 1921): Military Service Pensions Collection, "The Carrowkennedy ambush in the MSPC (2 June 1921)," Military Archives (militarypensions.wordpress.com), together with the standard reference accounts of both actions. Kilmeena cost the West Mayo column six Volunteers killed and seven wounded. At Carrowkennedy, a column of thirty-three men under Michael Kilroy ambushed an RIC and Black and Tan mobile patrol between two cottages on the Westport road, killing the machine-gunner and his replacement before the survivors surrendered; the column seized a Lewis machine gun, rifles, and a large quantity of ammunition. Casualty figures differ between accounts -- some give thirteen of the patrol killed and thirteen surrendered, others eight Black and Tans killed and sixteen RIC taken prisoner -- and this book states the discrepancy rather than selecting one figure, since no account this research could reach reconciles them.

[24] On Michael Kilroy (1884-1962): the Newport, Co. Mayo local history account "General Michael Kilroy" (mayo-ireland.ie), supplemented by standard reference biography. Commanded the West Mayo Brigade flying column through Kilmeena, Carrowkennedy, and the Tourmakeady action. Opposed the Anglo-Irish Treaty; appointed O/C of the 4th Western Division and subsequently of the Western Command in 1922, covering North Mayo, West Mayo, and West Connemara; captured by Free State forces when they took Newport during the Civil War. Afterwards joined Fianna Fail, served as a TD from 1927 to 1937, and in 1932 proposed Eamon de Valera as President of the Executive Council on the formation of the first Fianna Fail government. Chaired Mayo County Council for many years before retiring from public life in 1937. Died December 1962 at his home on Carrickaneady Road, Newport; more than five hundred IRA veterans attended the funeral, and President de Valera attended the Requiem Mass at St Patrick's Church, Newport.

[25] Dictionary of Irish Biography, s.v. "Devlin, Anne," Royal Irish Academy, https://www.dib.ie/biography/devlin-anne-a2561. Source for Anne Devlin's role as unpaid housekeeper and courier at Robert Emmet's Butterfield Lane house from April 1803; the yeomanry raid of 26 July 1803, at which she and her eight-year-old sister were interrogated and she was taken out and half-hanged without giving information; and her subsequent detention in Kilmainham Gaol after Emmet's arrest, where she withstood solitary confinement, bribes, and threats across more than two years' imprisonment without informing.

[26] Anne Chambers, Granuaile: Grace O'Malley - Ireland's Pirate Queen (Dublin: Wolfhound Press, various editions from 1979; the standard modern biography), supplemented by History Ireland, "Gráinne Mhaol, pirate queen of Connacht: behind the legend." Source for Grace O'Malley's birth c.1530 near Westport to the O'Malley chieftain Eoghan Dubhdara O'Máille; the clan's control of the western sea routes from Clare Island; her conflict with Sir Richard Bingham, Elizabeth I's governor of Connacht; and her 1593 voyage to Greenwich to petition Elizabeth in person, at which the two are said to have negotiated in Latin over the release of her son Toby and half-brother Dónal, and from which she returned with substantially what she had sought.

[27] Dictionary of Irish Biography, s.v. "Markievicz, Constance Georgine," Royal Irish Academy; with Encyclopaedia Britannica, s.v. "Constance Markievicz." Source for her simultaneous offices as President of Cumann na mBan, major in the Irish Citizen Army, and Chief Scout of Fianna Éireann; her position as second-in-command at St Stephen's Green in the 1916 Easter Rising and status as the only woman court-martialled for it; the commutation of her death sentence to penal servitude for life on the stated ground of her sex, and her release after fourteen months in the general amnesty of June 1917; and her election in 1918, while imprisoned, with over 65 percent of the vote, as the first woman elected to the House of Commons -- a seat she declined to take, refusing the oath of allegiance.

[28] On Cumann na mBan: University College Cork, "Cumann na mBan and the War of Independence" (ucc.ie/theirishrevolution), with RTÉ's "Cumann na mBan and the War of Independence." Source for the organization's founding in Dublin in April 1914 alongside the Irish Volunteers; its founders, including Agnes O'Farrelly, Agnes MacNeill, Nancy O'Rahilly, Louise Gavan Duffy, Mary Colum, and Mary MacSwiney; members' work during 1919-21 as keepers of safe houses, dispatch riders, scouts, and first-aid workers; the Bandon branch under Mary Walsh, which additionally ran fundraising and provisioned the local flying columns; the tasking of members by Propaganda Director Mabel FitzGerald to gather local evidence of British reprisals for the Irish Bulletin; and the use of member-run premises -- a grocer's in Upper Dorset Street, the Wallace sisters' newsagent's in Cork -- as dispatch centres for IRA leadership.

[29] Dictionary of Irish Biography, s.v. "Sarsfield, Patrick," Royal Irish Academy, https://www.dib.ie/biography/sarsfield-patrick-a7924; with History Ireland, "Ballyneety." Source for the raid of 11 August 1690, in which Sarsfield led roughly 500 cavalry around the Williamite army, passed its sentries using their own password, and destroyed the siege train halted near a conical rock at Ballyneety -- sixteen guns and over 150 supply wagons -- forcing William III to abandon the first siege of Limerick; described in the scholarship as the greatest cavalry raid of the war and the principal source of Sarsfield's standing with later generations. Also the source for his signing of the Treaty of Limerick on 3 October 1691 and his departure with the Wild Geese into French service.

[30] On the Williamite War in Ireland (1689-91) generally: standard reference accounts of the Siege of Derry (1689), the Battle of the Boyne (July 1690), the Battle of Aughrim (12 July 1691), and the Treaty of Limerick (3 October 1691). Aughrim, an all-day engagement in County Galway in which the Jacobite line held against repeated Williamite attacks until the death of their commander the Marquis de St Ruth and the withdrawal of their cavalry, is generally reckoned the bloodiest battle fought in Ireland and the one that actually decided the war. The Treaty of Limerick's civil articles guaranteed religious and legal protections to Irish Jacobites swearing allegiance to William and Mary; those articles were not honoured by the Williamite government. A contemporary, George Story, estimated the war's total mortality from battle, hunger, and sickness at over 100,000.

[31] On Éamonn an Chnoic ("Ned of the Hill"): standard reference accounts of Edmund Ryan (Éamonn Ó Riain) of Knockmeall, County Tipperary, traditionally dated c.1670-1724, who fought for James II at the Boyne and Aughrim and is recorded as having accompanied Sarsfield on the Ballyneety raid, subsequently living as a rapparee after the post-Cromwellian and post-Williamite land confiscations. The sean-nós lament bearing his name dates from roughly 1698-1704 and is by tradition his own composition. This book flags the biography as substantially traditional rather than documentary -- the dates and much of the detail rest on later transmission, and the song is a better-attested object than the man is.

[32] Dictionary of Irish Biography, s.v. "McCracken, Henry Joy," Royal Irish Academy; with standard reference accounts of the Battle of Antrim. Source for McCracken's birth on 31 August 1767 at 39 High Street, Belfast, into Ulster-Scots Presbyterian merchant families; his founding membership of the Society of United Irishmen; his election as adjutant general by the Antrim membership and command at the Battle of Antrim on 7 June 1798; and his court-martial, his refusal of clemency offered in exchange for information on his associates, and his hanging in Belfast on 17 July 1798.

[33] Dictionary of Irish Biography, s.v. "O'Hanlon, Redmond," Royal Irish Academy, https://www.dib.ie/biography/ohanlon-redmond-a6786. Source for O'Hanlon's activity in Armagh, Tyrone, and Down from the late 1670s, the extralegal "protection" watch he ran over Anglo-Irish landlords and Ulster Scots merchants there, his repeated evasion of official attempts to take him, and his death on 25 April 1681, eight years before the Williamite War opened. Critically for this book's Chapter 1 terminology discussion, the entry describes him in a single phrase as "a tóraidhe or rapparee," applying both words to the same man -- the evidence that the two terms overlapped in live use during the Restoration decades rather than succeeding one another cleanly at the Williamite War. Note: this book has not seeded a dedicated modern monograph on Restoration-interval (1660s-80s) tory and rapparee activity; the O'Hanlon material rests on the DIB's own summary, and that limit is stated rather than papered over.

[34] On Patrick Sarsfield's death: Encyclopaedia Britannica, s.v. "Patrick Sarsfield," with standard reference accounts of the Battle of Landen (Neerwinden), 29 July 1693. Having left Ireland under the Treaty of Limerick, Sarsfield served James II and then Louis XIV through the War of the Grand Alliance and was made marechal de camp in March 1693. He was shot in the chest at Landen, carried to Huy some twenty miles away, and died there of the wound; sources differ on the exact date of death. No grave or burial record was located for centuries, though a commemorative plaque stands at St Martin's Church in Huy.

Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone -- Leads a Gaelic confederacy against the English Crown from 1593/94, winning decisively at the Battle of the Yellow Ford (1598) through ambush and terrain preparation. Marches to Kinsale in 1601 to link with a landed Spanish force; the resulting conventional battle is a rout. Surrenders on terms in 1603, then flees Ireland permanently in the Flight of the Earls (1607), dying in exile in Rome, 1616. Cited in: [18].

Hugh Roe O'Donnell (Red Hugh) -- Lord of Tyrconnell; co-leads the Gaelic confederacy with Hugh O'Neill, making a celebrated forced march to Kinsale in October 1601. After the confederacy's defeat, sails to Spain seeking further aid; dies there in 1602. Cited in: [18].

Charles Blount, Lord Mountjoy -- English Lord Deputy from 1600; reforms the English army in Ireland and adopts a scorched-earth strategy against Ulster before winning decisively at Kinsale, December 1601. Accepts O'Neill's surrender on generous terms at the Treaty of Mellifont, 1603. Cited in: [18].

Yellow Ford -- Site near Armagh of Hugh O'Neill's decisive victory over an English relief column on 14 August 1598 -- over 1,200 English casualties, including the commander Sir Henry Bagenal. Won through trenches, earthworks, and ambush in boggy, wooded terrain. This book's earliest documented case of the ambush/terrain tactical logic that recurs throughout. Coordinates: 54.3667N, 6.55W. Cited in: [18].

Kinsale -- County Cork port where a besieged Spanish force summoned O'Neill and O'Donnell south in 1601. The resulting Battle of Kinsale, 24 December 1601, was a conventional pitched battle rather than the ambush warfare that had won every prior confederacy engagement, and it was a rout, effectively ending Gaelic military resistance. Coordinates: 51.7069N, 8.5228W. Cited in: [18].

Rathmullan -- Village on Lough Swilly, County Donegal, from which Hugh O'Neill, Rory O'Donnell, and roughly ninety-nine family members and followers sailed permanently into continental exile on 14 September 1607 -- the Flight of the Earls -- directly enabling the Plantation of Ulster. Coordinates: 55.0928N, 7.5442W. Cited in: [18].

Oliver Cromwell -- Lands at Dublin on 15 August 1649 as Lord Lieutenant and Commander-in-Chief of the Commonwealth's Irish campaign. Takes Drogheda by storm (11 September 1649) and Wexford (11 October 1649), both followed by mass killing of the garrisons and, at Wexford, many townspeople. Departs Ireland in May 1650 to campaign against the Scots, leaving the conquest's harder second phase to his subordinates Henry Ireton and Charles Fleetwood. Never returns to Ireland; dies in England in 1658. Cited in: [1], [9].

Michael Jones -- Parliamentarian commander who holds Dublin through 1649 and wins the decisive field engagement of the whole Cromwellian conquest, the Battle of Rathmines, on 2 August 1649 -- routing the besieging Royalist-Confederate army before Cromwell has even landed in Ireland. Dies of fever during the siege of Clonmel, December 1649. Cited in: [9].

Henry Ireton -- Cromwell's son-in-law; succeeds him as Commander-in-Chief of Ireland when Cromwell departs in May 1650. Reduces the last Confederate/Royalist strongholds, taking Limerick in October 1651 after a long siege, while the countryside behind his lines slides into tory insurgency. Dies of plague or fever at the Limerick siege camp, 26 November 1651, with the formal reduction of Ireland still incomplete. Cited in: [1], [9].

Charles Fleetwood -- Succeeds Ireton as Commander-in-Chief of Ireland in 1652, once the formal war is over but the tory insurgency is not. Oversees the Act for the Settlement of Ireland (1652) and the transplantation of dispossessed Catholic landowners to Connacht, along with the accompanying bounty warrants and collective-fine policy against baronies sheltering tories. Recalled to England in 1655. Cited in: [1].

Owen Roe O'Neill -- The Irish Confederation's most capable field commander, veteran of Spanish service, and the general the Confederate Ulster army's discipline depends on through the 1640s. Dies of illness (not battle) at Cloughoughter, County Cavan, in November 1649, weeks after Cromwell's landing -- leaving the Ulster army leaderless at the moment it most needs continuity of command. Cited in: [9].

Heber MacMahon -- Catholic Bishop of Clogher who assumes command of the Confederate Ulster army after Owen Roe O'Neill's death, against his own officers' advice. Leads that army to destruction at the Battle of Scarrifhollis, County Donegal, on 21 June 1650 -- one of the two battlefield disasters, with Rathmines, that end conventional Irish resistance and force the shift to tory-style guerrilla warfare. Captured and executed shortly after the battle. Cited in: [9].

Murrough O'Brien, Earl of Inchiquin -- Munster-born commander whose loyalties shifted across the 1640s: he fought first for Parliament (earning the nickname "Murrough of the Burnings" for scorched-earth campaigns against Confederate Munster) before switching to the Royalist-Confederate alliance against Cromwell. His mixed record is a concrete reminder that wartime allegiance in 1640s Ireland was far less settled than the popular two-camp picture suggests. Cited in: [9].

Edmund O'Dwyer -- Commander-in-chief of Confederate/Irish forces in Tipperary and Waterford after Cromwell's 1649 landing. After the formal war collapses, continues fighting as a tory leader from the Glen of Aherlow, one of the tory insurgency's best-documented refuge areas. Negotiates exile to continental military service with his remaining men rather than surrender to summary justice -- the "Wild Geese" pattern -- and is killed in battle in France in 1654. Cited in: [1], [9].

Redmond O'Hanlon -- Irish tory active in Ulster from the late 1670s until his death on 25 April 1681, eight years before the Williamite War began. Ran an extralegal "protection" watch over Anglo-Irish landlords and Ulster Scots merchants in Armagh, Tyrone, and Down, and defied repeated attempts to capture him. His Dictionary of Irish Biography entry describes him as "a tóraidhe or rapparee" -- direct evidence that the two terms already overlapped in his own Restoration-era lifetime, rather than succeeding one another cleanly at the Williamite War. He belongs to neither of the rounds on either side of him: too late for the Cromwellian tories, too early for Sarsfield's war, and that in-between position is exactly why this book keeps him. No dedicated modern monograph on Restoration-interval tory and rapparee activity underpins this entry; it rests on the DIB summary, and the book says so. Cited in: [33].

Tom Barry -- Returned British Army veteran of the Mesopotamian campaign who joins the IRA's Cork No. 3 Brigade and commands its flying column. Leads the Kilmichael ambush (28 November 1920), destroying a patrol of Auxiliaries, and commands the column through the Crossbarry engagement (19 March 1921), fighting clear of an attempted encirclement before dispersing the same day. Survives the war and writes the memoir Guerilla Days in Ireland (1949), in which he states explicitly that ambushes existed to take rifles, not ground. Cited in: [3].

Dan Breen -- Tipperary IRA Volunteer who, with Sean Treacy, leads the Soloheadbeg ambush on 21 January 1919 -- an attack on RIC constables escorting gelignite, conventionally dated as the opening action of the War of Independence though carried out without direct GHQ sanction. Survives the war and writes the memoir My Fight for Irish Freedom (1924). Cited in: [4].

Sean Treacy -- Tipperary IRA Volunteer who co-leads the Soloheadbeg ambush with Dan Breen on 21 January 1919. Killed in a gun battle with British forces in Talbot Street, Dublin, on 14 October 1920 -- one of this book's named combatant deaths, in contrast to the tory insurgency's almost entirely anonymous casualty record. Cited in: [4].

Ernie O'Malley -- IRA General Headquarters staff officer who tours brigades nationwide training local Volunteers and flying columns in tactics and doctrine -- the mechanism, alongside the training journal An t-Oglach, by which the War of Independence side converted practice into written, transmissible doctrine. Survives the war and writes the memoir On Another Man's Wound (1936). Cited in: [5].

Michael Collins -- IRA Director of Intelligence and Adjutant General; builds and runs the counter-intelligence network, including agents inside Dublin Castle's own administration, that systematically identifies and, in many cases, kills the detective "G Division" officers and undercover agents the British administration depends on for political intelligence. Signs the Anglo-Irish Treaty in December 1921, after this book's core window closes; killed in the Civil War at Beal na Blath, August 1922 -- noted here only as a closing fact. Cited in: [7], [2].

Richard Mulcahy -- IRA Chief of Staff at General Headquarters in Dublin throughout the War of Independence, part of the standing command structure -- GHQ, a Ministry of Defence, brigade organization -- that stands as the sharpest structural difference from the tory insurgency, which had no equivalent unified command after 1650. Cited in: [7].

Cathal Brugha -- Minister for Defence in the First Dail, the counter-state civilian authority set against the brief and quickly-collapsed Confederation of Kilkenny's prior claim to Irish sovereignty in the 1640s. The Dail's ability to hold courts, raise a national loan, and maintain a defence ministry through a guerrilla war is a structural capacity the tory insurgency never had. Cited in: [7].

Eamon de Valera -- President of the First Dail, largely absent from Ireland for much of 1919-20 on a fund-raising and diplomatic mission to the United States -- a data point for this book's argument that the IRA's campaign depended on a foreign audience (British and American opinion) a purely domestic Cromwellian-era tory insurgency never had the option of appealing to. Cited in: [7].

William "Rick" Joyce -- Rank-and-file Volunteer of the West Mayo Brigade Flying Column under Michael Kilroy, named as "R. Joyce" in the Military Archives' 1920-21 photograph and caption of the column (popularly "The Men of the West") -- the same column that fought the Tourmakeady, Kilmeena, and Carrowkennedy actions. No individually-attested record of his specific actions has been found beyond this listing. Used deliberately in this book as a corrective: even a fully photographed, captioned flying column still loses most of its rank and file to the record as nothing more than a name in a caption. Cited in: [10].

Glen of Aherlow -- A wooded valley at the base of the Galtee Mountains in County Tipperary, one of the best-documented tory refuge areas of the 1650s and 1660s. Edmund O'Dwyer commanded tory forces operating from the Glen after the formal Confederate war collapsed; the terrain -- dense woodland plus a defensible valley, close to settled land yet hard for regular troops to sweep -- is this book's Cromwellian-era counterpart to the bog causeways and back roads West Cork's flying column used 270 years later. Coordinates: 52.4167N, 8.2167W. Cited in: [1], [9].

Rathmines -- Site just south of Dublin of the decisive field battle of the Cromwellian conquest: on 2 August 1649, Michael Jones's Parliamentarian garrison routed the besieging Royalist-Confederate army before Cromwell had even landed in Ireland. Treated, alongside Scarrifhollis the following year, as the battlefield disaster that ends conventional Irish resistance and forces the shift to tory-style guerrilla warfare. Coordinates: 53.3244N, 6.2653W. Cited in: [9].

Scarrifhollis -- Site on the River Swilly near Letterkenny, County Donegal, of the Battle of Scarrifhollis (also spelled Scariffhollis) on 21 June 1650, at which the Confederate Ulster army under Bishop Heber MacMahon was destroyed. Paired with Rathmines (1649) as the two battlefield catastrophes that end conventional Irish military resistance to the Cromwellian conquest. Coordinates: 54.95N, 7.7333W. Cited in: [9].

Drogheda -- Walled garrison town north of Dublin taken by storm by Cromwell's forces on 11 September 1649, followed by the mass killing of the garrison and, by most accounts, some civilians, after the town refused an initial surrender demand. One of the two 1649 siege sites (with Wexford) whose severity becomes part of the tory insurgency's grievance memory in the following decade, and an early example of the "outside the laws of war" justification this book's counterinsurgency-repertoire chapter compares to twentieth-century official reprisal doctrine. Coordinates: 53.7189N, 6.3478W. Cited in: [9].

Wexford -- Walled port town taken by Cromwell's forces on 11 October 1649 after negotiations broke down and troops stormed the town; a substantial number of the garrison and townspeople were killed in the aftermath. Paired with Drogheda as one of the 1649 siege sites whose severity fed directly into the tory insurgency's grievance memory and into the historiographical dispute over how the Cromwellian campaign should be characterized. Coordinates: 52.3369N, 6.4633W. Cited in: [9].

Connacht -- The westernmost of Ireland's four provinces, designated under the Act for the Settlement of Ireland (1652) as the destination for the forced transplantation of dispossessed Catholic landowners from the other three provinces -- the origin of the phrase "to Hell or to Connacht." Treated as necessary background (explaining why so many dispossessed men had cause and cover to become tories) rather than as this book's causal argument. Coordinates: 53.7N, 9.0W. Cited in: [1], [9].

Soloheadbeg -- Townland in County Tipperary, site of the ambush on 21 January 1919 in which Dan Breen and Sean Treacy's IRA unit attacked RIC constables escorting a consignment of gelignite, killing both constables. Conventionally dated as the opening action of the War of Independence, though carried out without direct GHQ sanction. Coordinates: 52.5167N, 8.15W. Cited in: [4].

Kilmichael -- Townland in West Cork, site of the ambush on 28 November 1920 in which Tom Barry's Cork No. 3 Brigade flying column destroyed a patrol of Auxiliary Division cadets. This book's central War of Independence case study of ambush-as-capture-economy tactics -- the operation's stated purpose, per Barry's own account, was to seize rifles and equipment, not to hold ground. Coordinates: 51.7833N, 8.9833W. Cited in: [3].

Crossbarry -- Site in West Cork of the engagement on 19 March 1921 in which Tom Barry's flying column, roughly 100 Volunteers concentrated from several battalions, fought its way clear of an attempted British encirclement before dispersing the same day. This book's central case study of the mobility contest -- the concentration-and-dispersal capability that telegraph, rail, and motor transport made possible for a War of Independence column in a way no Cromwellian-era tory band could match. Coordinates: 51.7667N, 8.6167W. Cited in: [3].

Dublin Castle -- Seat of the British administration in Ireland throughout the War of Independence, and the institution whose detective "G Division" and political-intelligence apparatus Michael Collins's counter-intelligence network systematically identifies and dismantles. The War of Independence anchor of this book's informers-and-intelligence comparison against the Cromwellian-era bounty/"discoverer" system. Coordinates: 53.3431N, 6.2674W. Cited in: [7].

Theobald Wolfe Tone -- Dublin-born Protestant lawyer who founds the Society of United Irishmen to unite Catholics, Protestants, and Presbyterians against British rule. Travels to France to secure military support; returns in 1798 with a French invasion fleet intercepted by the Royal Navy off Lough Swilly. Captured, sentenced to hang, and dies of a self-inflicted wound before the sentence is carried out. Cited in: [12].

Father John Murphy -- Catholic priest who initially urges his Wexford parishioners against rebellion, then leads them after government troops burn homes and threaten his congregation. Leads a pikemen's victory at Oulart Hill, 27 May 1798, before the wider Wexford rising is crushed at Vinegar Hill on 21 June. Captured and executed at Tullow shortly after. Cited in: [12].

Michael Dwyer -- Born 1772 in the Glen of Imaal, west Wicklow; fights as a United Irishmen captain in 1798 at Arklow and Vinegar Hill, then withdraws into the Wicklow Mountains after the main rising collapses and sustains an independent guerrilla campaign for five and a half years -- longer than the entire Cromwellian tory suppression phase or the whole War of Independence. The British built the Military Road, Ireland's first purpose-built road, specifically to reach him. Surrenders under a safe-passage agreement in December 1803 that is not honored; transported as a convict to New South Wales instead. Cited in: [13], [14].

Robert Emmet -- Young Protestant member of the Anglo-Irish elite who organizes a rising in Dublin in July 1803, aiming to seize Dublin Castle; the attempt collapses within hours. Captured, tried for high treason, and delivers a speech from the dock on 19 September 1803 ending "let no man write my epitaph" -- a line that outlived the rising itself. Hanged in Thomas Street, Dublin, 20 September 1803. Cited in: [15].

James Stephens -- Founds the Irish Republican Brotherhood (the Fenians) and proves a formidable organizer but, by historical consensus, a fatally hesitant military leader, repeatedly raising and then postponing expectations of an armed rising until his own membership loses confidence in him. Replaced by Thomas J. Kelly in late 1866. Cited in: [16].

Thomas J. Kelly -- Replaces James Stephens as Fenian leader in late 1866; the March 1867 rising he organizes in Britain is rendered ineffective by informers, official vigilance, arms shortages, and bad weather. Later that year, while being transported for trial in Manchester, is rescued by fellow Fenians in an ambush that kills a police officer -- the incident behind the Manchester Martyrs. Escapes and is never recaptured. Cited in: [16].

The Manchester Martyrs (William Allen, Michael Larkin, Michael O'Brien) -- Three Fenians captured and hanged after the September 1867 rescue of Thomas J. Kelly, during which a police officer was killed, despite doubts even at the time about which man fired the fatal shot. Their executions produced an immediate nationalist ballad tradition ("God Save Ireland") -- a case, alongside Robert Emmet, of tactical failure converting into lasting political memory. Cited in: [16].

Michael Davitt -- Born Straide, County Mayo, 25 March 1846; evicted with his family at four during the Great Famine and emigrated to Haslingden, Lancashire, where he entered a cotton mill at nine and lost his right arm to the machinery in May 1857, aged eleven. Joins the Fenians and serves seven years of penal servitude for arms-running before release in 1877. Organizes the Land League of Mayo in 1879 and, with Charles Stewart Parnell, founds the Irish National Land League the same year, shifting Irish agrarian resistance from clandestine night-raiding ("Captain Moonlight") toward organized mass non-payment and boycotting -- a tactic that gets its name from the 1880 ostracism of land agent Charles Boycott. Writes the memoir The Fall of Feudalism in Ireland (1904). Cited in: [17], [22].

Vinegar Hill -- Hill outside Enniscorthy, County Wexford, site of the decisive battle of the 1798 rebellion on 21 June 1798. An estimated 20,000 rebels, including many women and children sheltering from reprisals elsewhere, gathered for what was meant as a final stand; General Gerard Lake's army routed them, with roughly 1,500 dead, most non-combatants. Coordinates: 52.5N, 6.5667W. Cited in: [12].

Glen of Imaal (Wicklow Mountains) -- Michael Dwyer's native valley and the base for his five-and-a-half-year guerrilla campaign, 1798-1803. The British built the Military Road, Ireland's first purpose-built road, specifically to open this terrain against him -- a direct parallel to the Glen of Aherlow's role in the Cromwellian-era chapters. Coordinates: 52.9833N, 6.6667W. Cited in: [13], [14].

Thomas Street, Dublin -- Site of Robert Emmet's 1803 rising attempt and, on 20 September 1803, his public execution by hanging. His speech from the dock the day before produced the line "let no man write my epitaph," which outlived the rising itself. Coordinates: 53.3428N, 6.2836W. Cited in: [15].

Manchester -- English city where, in September 1867, Fenians ambushed a prison van to rescue Thomas J. Kelly, killing a police officer. Three men -- William Allen, Michael Larkin, and Michael O'Brien -- were hanged for the killing despite doubts about individual guilt, becoming the Manchester Martyrs. Coordinates: 53.4808N, 2.2426W. Cited in: [16].

County Mayo (Land League origin) -- Site of the Land League of Mayo, organized by Michael Davitt on 16 August 1879 in response to tenant evictions -- the local origin point that becomes, two months later, the nationwide Irish National Land League. Coordinates: 53.85N, 9.3W. Cited in: [17].

Grace O'Malley (Granuaile) -- Born c.1530 near Westport, County Mayo, daughter of the O'Malley chieftain Eoghan Dubhdara O'Máille, whose seafaring clan controlled the western sea routes from Clare Island and levied tolls on those using them. Commanded ships and men in her own right. Her long conflict with Sir Richard Bingham, Elizabeth I's governor of Connacht, led her to sail to Greenwich in 1593 and petition Elizabeth in person; the two reportedly negotiated in Latin over the release of her son and half-brother, and she returned with substantially what she had sought. Cited in: [26].

Patrick Sarsfield, 1st Earl of Lucan -- The outstanding Irish soldier of the Williamite War (1689-91). On 11 August 1690 he led roughly 500 cavalry around the Williamite army by night, passed its sentries using their own password, and destroyed the siege train halted at Ballyneety -- sixteen guns and over 150 supply wagons -- forcing William III to abandon his first siege of Limerick. Signed the Treaty of Limerick on 3 October 1691, whose civil articles the Williamite government then broke, and left for French service with the Wild Geese. Cited in: [29], [30].

Éamonn an Chnoic ("Ned of the Hill") -- Edmund Ryan (Éamonn Ó Riain) of Knockmeall, County Tipperary, traditionally c.1670-1724. Fought for James II at the Boyne and Aughrim and rode with Sarsfield on the Ballyneety raid; lived afterwards as a rapparee following the land confiscations. The sean-nós lament bearing his name, dating from roughly 1698-1704 and by tradition his own composition, is still sung. Much of his biography is traditional rather than documentary -- the song is far better attested than the man. Cited in: [31].

Henry Joy McCracken -- Born 31 August 1767 at 39 High Street, Belfast, into Ulster-Scots Presbyterian merchant families; a founder member of the Society of United Irishmen. Elected adjutant general by the Antrim membership and commanded their forces at the Battle of Antrim, 7 June 1798. Captured and court-martialled; offered clemency in exchange for information on his associates, he refused, and was hanged in Belfast on 17 July 1798. Cited in: [32].

Anne Devlin -- Born 1780 at Cronebeg, County Wicklow, into a United Irish family and a first cousin of the guerrilla Michael Dwyer -- the only documented personal connection between any two figures in this book. From April 1803 she was unpaid housekeeper at Robert Emmet's Butterfield Lane house, providing the domestic cover for his conspiracy and acting as his courier. When the yeomanry raided on 26 July 1803 she was interrogated alongside her eight-year-old sister and then half-hanged, and gave them nothing; she was subsequently held in Kilmainham for more than two years under solitary confinement, bribes, and threats, and named no one. Released in 1806 after public outrage at her treatment. She worked as a laundress and died of 'want' on 18 September 1851 at 2 Little Elbow Lane in the Coombe, Dublin, aged about seventy-one; buried at Glasnevin. Cited in: [25].

Constance Markievicz -- (1868-1927) Anglo-Irish aristocrat who held simultaneously the presidency of Cumann na mBan, a major's rank in the Irish Citizen Army, and the post of Chief Scout of Fianna Éireann. Second-in-command at St Stephen's Green in the 1916 Easter Rising and the only woman court-martialled for it; her death sentence was commuted to penal servitude for life on the stated ground of her sex, and she was released after fourteen months in the amnesty of June 1917. Elected in 1918 while imprisoned, with over 65 percent of the vote, as the first woman ever elected to the House of Commons -- a seat she refused to take, declining the oath of allegiance. Cited in: [27].

Cumann na mBan -- Irish republican women's organization founded in Dublin in April 1914 alongside the Irish Volunteers; founders included Agnes O'Farrelly, Agnes MacNeill, Nancy O'Rahilly, Louise Gavan Duffy, Mary Colum, and Mary MacSwiney. During the War of Independence members kept safe houses, rode dispatches, scouted, and provided first aid; the Bandon branch under Mary Walsh also provisioned and funded local flying columns. Members were tasked by Propaganda Director Mabel FitzGerald with gathering local evidence of British reprisals for the Irish Bulletin, and member-run premises -- a grocer's in Upper Dorset Street, the Wallace sisters' newsagent's in Cork -- served as dispatch centres for IRA leadership. Cited in: [28].

Ballyneety -- Village in County Limerick where, on the night of 11 August 1690, Patrick Sarsfield's cavalry destroyed the Williamite siege train halted beside a conical rock still known locally as Sarsfield's Rock -- sixteen guns and over 150 wagons -- forcing William III to abandon his first siege of Limerick. Coordinates: 52.5892N, 8.5442W. Cited in: [29].

Aughrim -- Village in County Galway, site on 12 July 1691 of what is generally reckoned the bloodiest battle ever fought in Ireland. The Jacobite line held against repeated Williamite attacks through an all-day engagement until the death of their commander, the Marquis de St Ruth, and the withdrawal of their cavalry caused a collapse. Aughrim, not the Boyne, decided the Williamite War. Coordinates: 53.3006N, 8.3172W. Cited in: [30].

Clew Bay and Clare Island -- The O'Malley clan's sea territory on the County Mayo coast, controlled from their castle on Clare Island at the bay's mouth, from which they held the western sea routes and levied tolls on their users. Grace O'Malley sailed from here in 1593 to petition Elizabeth I at Greenwich. Coordinates: 53.8N, 9.85W. Cited in: [26].
