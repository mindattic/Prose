# The Way Down

_Four years after they came up together, Rampart and Cutout are opposite numbers — and neither one knows the other is standing on the far side of the same job.

Reza is corporate muscle now, up-altitude, paid to hold a floor and not ask questions. Tavi is a freelance ghost, solo by choice, hired through the same fixer who once split them up — to extract a courier neither of them has met, a Gray Zone kid who came up exactly the way they did. They don't recognize each other by face. They recognize each other by work: the wall held wrong for a corp, the system killed too clean. The thing that made them a team is the exact thing that makes them the perfect enemy.

Divided between the old bond and the contract in front of them, the wall and the ghost collide — brutal, and worse, familiar. Each of them knows the other's craft too well to be surprised by it. When the choice comes, both of them pick the bond over the job. It costs them everything anyway.

The scorched second half of the Rampart & Cutout diptych: a story about what's left of a friendship after the world finishes using it — and why that isn't nothing._

[Beat 1]
The Ashgrave Spire's forty-third floor smelled like ozone and new carpet. It was the smell of a place nobody lived in, only cleaned.

Reza stood at the corridor junction and ran a palm across the security panel. It was a reflex by now. His chrome pulled the floor plan into the corner of his eye without being asked. Forty-three was clear. It had been clear every morning for eleven days.

[Beat 2]
Sixty meters below the windows, a lake caught the light and threw it back too blue — the Volume running at full saturation up here, the overlay rendering the rich tier the way it paid to be seen. Down in the Gray Zone the same feed ran thin and gray.

[Beat 3]
Onwe found him at 0800, already three moves into a conversation Reza hadn't started.

"The asset on forty-seven needs a dedicated perimeter," Onwe said.
"You'll run it yourself. Pull your two best off their current posts."

"Forty-seven's been empty."

"Not after tonight."

Behind Onwe, a wall screen was running Clean Hands — some corporate-ethics drama complete with a boardroom confession and a swelling score, standard morning filler for the executive floors. Onwe glanced at it without really seeing it. "They make three of these a year now," he said, not slowing down. "Nobody upstairs believes them and everybody upstairs quotes them." He didn't wait for a response.

Reza looked at the floor plan in his overlay, the empty rectangle of forty-seven, and did not ask what was going into it. The arrangement was the arrangement. He picked two names off his roster — Ekwe and Saldanha, both solid, both the kind who held a floor and didn't look at what stood on it — and confirmed them with a gesture. Onwe nodded once and left.

[Beat 4]
The overlay lied about the lake's blue the way it lied about everything up here. Reza watched it a moment. Far below the Spire, below the Air Tax line, was the street he came from — a level where the feeds ran gray and a nineteen-year-old nobody had once stood in an alley at dawn with a garbage bag and a body that hadn't learned yet to be useful. His hand pressed flat against the panel a half-second too long before he pulled it back — the closest thing to a flinch he still let himself have. Then he thought about the roster, the rotation, the clean perimeter Onwe wanted by tonight.

[Beat 5]
He had not thought about Tavi in a long time — not since the eigenlift terminal by the Bloom Quarter, the morning the split had gone from a feeling to a fact. He left it there.

[Beat 6]
Auda Vane avoided meeting in person when she could. So the café was a message on its own. It sat mid-altitude, near the Bloom Quarter, the kind of place with a working Substrate seam overhead and a menu in four languages nobody in the room spoke.

She had chosen it to tell Tavi, before she said a word, that she was paying for the trouble of his attention.

[Beat 7]
He was already seated when she arrived. He'd been there long enough to learn the server's rotation and the line of sight to the door. He'd ordered nothing. Ordering left a record.

[Beat 8]
She sat. She didn't say hello. She set a credstick flat on the table, face-down, and started.

"Forty-seven. Up-altitude holding site. Corporate annex dressed up as an empty floor. The asset's a courier — soft, no combat training, held against his will. Ghost extraction. No contact, no trace, no incident report in any system after."

"Source?" Tavi asked.

"Three cutouts. I'm the third." She didn't say who was first or second. She didn't know, and that told him the money behind this was old and careful, patient enough to build a chain that long. "Payment's on the stick. Half now. Half when you deliver the asset to the handoff point I'll send you."

[Beat 9]
Tavi turned the credstick over with one finger. He looked at it for a moment. Then he picked it up.

[Beat 10]
He'd worked alone for fourteen months.

The Ankle-Biter rode the inside of his left forearm in a rig fitted to his exact frame — no slack, no tell, nothing that showed through a jacket.

On a job like this one he still caught himself leaving a half-second of space at a doorway for a second set of hands that weren't coming, then closing it.

[Beat 11]
"Handoff point and timing," he said.

[Beat 12]
Vane slid a chip across the table with two fingers, the way you push a coin into a slot, and stood.

She was already walking when she said, "Don't leave a ghost."

[Beat 13]
Tavi rode the Ferrogate Metro Bus to the Spire's base-level concourse. The bus logged nothing but a zone transit, which suited a ghost fine.

The crawl through the Gray Zone's industrial haze took forty minutes. He spent it reading the chip once, then wiping it.

The chip told him what the credstick had already hinted: floor forty-seven, a holding setup, one non-combatant, a midnight window. It also gave him the asset's name. Rafi Sarkissian.

[Beat 14]
He looked out at the refinery haze, yellow-orange against the glass, and caught himself running the kid's life without meaning to. Twenty years old. Born in the Gray Zone. Courier work — the kind that paid fast and burned you just as fast, the kind you took at nineteen because nobody told you the come-up had a ceiling until your head hit it. Somewhere in there the kid had been handed a slug he should have deleted, and hadn't.

[Beat 15]
He was still holding the wiped chip when the second contract came through. Not a message — a hard override, punching straight past his relay wall to the burner he kept for one client only. The screen resolved: same Spire, same floor, same midnight window, same name. Rafi Sarkissian.

Only the verb had changed. The first brief had said *extract*. This one said *terminate*, and it carried a payment authorization already committed, non-refusable under the terms he'd signed blind eight months ago. He read it twice, waiting for the numbers to disagree with each other. They didn't.

Somebody upstream had bought both ends of the same job, and he was holding a contract that ordered him to kill the boy the other contract ordered him to save — and no clause anywhere told him which one he was allowed to break.

[Beat 16]
Rafi Sarkissian had copied the slug four days ago. He'd done it in a relay-station bathroom in the lower Bloom Quarter, with hands steadier than they had any right to be. He was twenty, and he knew exactly what he was copying.

That was the problem. Seventeen months of AshgraveMaterials' internal logistics sat on that slug — routing patterns, synthesis-corridor schedules, quantities that matched no permit on file. He'd been paid to move it once, sealed. He opened it.

Then he copied it, because at nineteen he'd learned one thing: information was the only thing a Gray Zone kid could carry that a bigger man couldn't just take.

[Beat 17]
Now he waited on the forty-seventh floor of the Ashgrave Spire, in a room nicer than anywhere he'd ever slept, with his copy and no way out. Ashgrave wanted the copy buried. The Axiom-funded broker, three cutouts back from a mid-altitude café, wanted it as leverage. And outside the Spire, moving in from opposite directions, were two operators who had once stood exactly where Rafi stood now — one hired to open the door, one hired to hold it shut, neither of them knowing the other was anywhere in the building. They had both been twenty in a room like this once, with a bad slug and no clean way to set it down. That was four years gone, and it had made them into men who would not have recognized the kids they'd been.

[Beat 18]
The forty-sixth floor had one working camera cluster, on the east stairwell. Tavi killed it the way he killed all of them. He fed it a packet that told the cluster it was still recording, a clean four-second loop of nothing, and the cluster believed it.

He moved through the silence he'd made ahead of himself. The air up here was different. Corpo-cold, twelve degrees below the street, the kind of cold that costs Φ.

[Beat 19]
He crouched at the T-junction and listened. Three floors of coverage sat above the executive suite, and he'd crossed two of them without hitting a pattern — no sweep, no handoff, no overlapping sight-lines. What he hit instead was positioning. A man held the north stairwell door without moving. A woman at the elevator bank hadn't shifted her weight in the time it took Tavi to clear two floors.

This wasn't a patrol. Corporations patrolled ground. This was somebody standing watch over a person.

[Beat 20]
Reza had the door.

[Beat 21]
He wasn't thinking about the door. He was thinking about the node two floors down, the one on the service corridor, the one that had just gone quiet. Not dark. Quiet, which was worse. A node went dark when you burned it. It went quiet when someone asked it, very politely, to look the other way.

He'd seen a system die like that exactly once. Three years back, a contractor ran a training scenario for Bear's crew — a small man who moved like he was sorry for the space he took up. He'd put seventeen nodes to sleep in eleven minutes and never touched one of them.

[Beat 22]
Reza set his shoulder against the wall beside the door frame, not in front of it. He did not go hunting for whatever was climbing the building. The hunt was the mistake — the narrow corridor, the invitation. He held the one threshold that mattered and let the quiet come to him.

One thing stayed with him. Whoever was killing the nodes had done it before, and was doing it now for someone they badly did not want to lose.

[Beat 23]
Tavi reached the doorway on the forty-seventh floor and stopped. Not because of the man in it. The man wasn't moving, hadn't moved, and wouldn't move unless Tavi moved first. He stopped because of how the man stood. Weight back on the right heel. Left shoulder dropped three degrees. Hands at the seam of the hips — not crossed, not pocketed, just resting. He'd seen that stance before. In a basement in Pilsen, in a drill with a mop handle standing in for a baton, in a man who'd spent two hours teaching a crew how to hold a doorway without advertising it.

[Beat 24]
Tavi didn't move. The man in the doorway didn't move. Tavi noticed all the things he wasn't doing — not reaching for the spike in his jacket, not stepping back into the stairwell, not running any of the three exits he'd mapped on the way up. He knew how the man stood before he let himself know why.

[Beat 25]
Two floors down, the node on the junction corridor went quiet. Reza felt it before the panel told him — a drop in the feed. He kept his shoulder to the wall and did not go looking.

The sequence was clean. Too clean. The nodes were dropping in order, not by where they sat but by what they mattered. Reza's stomach tightened before he understood why — he'd felt this rhythm once before, in a stairwell in Pilsen.

[Beat 26]
He knew one man who called that working from the inside out.

[Beat 27]
He didn't say the name. He held the door and felt the quiet come floor by floor, and he pushed the thought back where it belonged — later, not now. That discipline had kept him whole in six buildings worse than this one. Then the node on his own corridor went dark. Not quiet. Dark. That was different. That was someone telling him they'd arrived.

[Beat 28]
Tavi's comms had been dark since the thirty-eighth floor. Up here it cost him nothing — the building's own signal scrubbers ate anything civilian. He pulled the secondary channel. Not the crew's. Bear's. They'd set it up three years ago for work that wasn't supposed to exist afterward. No log. A rotating key Bear changed every ninety days, with the same password he'd used since Pilsen.

[Beat 29]
The channel opened.

[Beat 30]
One click, and nothing else. The old signal. The one that meant I see you, hold your position.

[Beat 31]
Reza's hand tightened on the door frame before the rest of him caught up. One click, on the rotating key, through a channel that kept no log. He knew exactly one other man who carried that key. That man was supposed to be in Rotterdam. That man had taught him the click-signal in a stairwell in Pilsen, both of them bleeding, both of them working a job that had gone sideways in ways nobody had paid them for.

[Beat 32]
He didn't click back. He stood with the wall at his shoulder and the door at his left hand and the dark corridor empty in front of him, and under him the floor seemed to shift — not really, but that was the feeling of it, the floor moving out from under him faster than he could plant his feet.

The man on the other side of that corridor had a name Reza wouldn't say, not even to himself. Say the name and it became a problem he had to solve. Leave it unsaid and it stayed a door he had to hold.

[Beat 33]
A click came back through the channel, the other direction this time, not his — the only language they'd ever shared that had never needed words. It told him the one thing it could tell him. Tavi knew too.

[Beat 34]
Tavi moved through the dark on old training. No light, no sound, one hand trailing the wall at knee height where the cameras never reached.

The executive suite door was unlocked. That was wrong enough to stop him for half a second, one hand on the frame, reading the room before he stepped in.

A desk. A chair turned toward the window. The Lake spread out forty-seven floors below, wide and flat and older than the building. Someone was sitting in the chair. Small. Young — seventeen at a glance, though the brief said twenty.

[Beat 35]
The kid turned around before Tavi made a sound.

[Beat 36]
"Cutout," the kid said. Not a question. Close-cropped hair, a secondhand jacket, and the stillness Gray Zone kids learn before they learn to read — the stillness of someone who's already counted the exits. His eyes went over Tavi, quick and helpless and trying not to show it.

"You're actually Cutout? Rampart said —"

He stopped himself. Swallowed.

"Rampart said you were in Rotterdam."

[Beat 37]
Tavi crossed the room without answering and crouched in front of the chair, down at eye level, careful to make it read as anything but a threat. Rafi. The package. The reason six guards stood watch across three floors, and the reason a man he'd once split every job with was standing between him and the exit.

Behind the kid's left ear sat a secondhand Tier 1 rig, the neuretic trace still pink at the edges, freshly grown. Someone had paid for that. Someone had paid for the jacket too, and the room, and the six guards. Tavi knew what that kind of payment felt like from the inside. It felt like safety. It kept feeling like safety right up until it turned into a door that only opened one way.

[Beat 38]
"You grew up on the story," Tavi said. It came out quieter than he meant.

[Beat 39]
"Everyone in the Yards grew up on the story." Rafi's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "There was a serial — Lacuna — about a courier who slipped every net they threw at him and just walked out clean. I watched it before I could read the subtitles. I thought this would go like that."

Rafi's hands sat loose in his lap. Bear's crew had drilled that into him: don't reach, don't grip, don't give the room a reason. Twenty years old and already trained out of his own instincts.

"Rampart and Cutout. The two who made it out of Pilsen without a corpo contract, without a Lotus seat, without —"

He looked at Tavi's hands, at the jacket, at the spike in Tavi's breast pocket. Tavi hadn't bothered to hide it. Hiding it would have been a lie neither of them needed.

"You're here to take me somewhere?"

[Beat 40]
"Yeah."

[Beat 41]
"Is it better than here?"

[Beat 42]
Tavi looked at the kid's face and saw the choosing going on behind it. The same choosing he'd done at nineteen, in a different room, with a different set of bad options.

The contract had a name on it, a figure, a client Tavi had never met. The client had six guards. The client had a static watch and a fresh neuretic rig for a Gray Zone kid. None of that came free. Tavi had been doing this long enough to know a cage when it had good furniture in it.

[Beat 43]
"I don't know yet," he said.

[Beat 44]
Rafi looked at him a long moment. Then he stood up. He was twenty, he'd grown up on the story, and the story said Cutout didn't lie.

It was the worst thing Tavi had ever been handed in a room.

"Stay by the window. Door locked." Tavi walked him through the service-core stairwell on the wall schematic, slow and careful, making sure it stuck. "In case," he said, and left the sentence there. The corridor between here and clear air was guarded in a way he wasn't going to walk a Low kid through — not until he'd gone first and made it something smaller.

[Beat 45]
The Spire's service corridor ran east-west, twelve meters, one overhead strip throwing amber light the color of old nicotine. Tavi had walked it twice in his head before he walked it with his feet, and the real thing matched. Drain grates every three meters. A junction box at the midpoint. One camera above the west door with a forty-degree arc and a two-second sweep lag. He'd gotten the lag from the maintenance manifest Vane's chain had sourced three days ago. The manifest was four months old. He'd told himself that was close enough.

[Beat 46]
He didn't slow down. He kept his shoulders loose and his steps even, the walk of a man whose job is boring. He counted the grates by feel through the soles of his boots — one, two, three, junction box.

At the fourth grate he stopped and crouched. The drain cover came up hard, stuck from long sitting, and let go with a low metal groan. It lasted less than a second and still felt like a shout.

Below was a maintenance shaft, straight down, three meters to the Package's floor. The air that came up smelled of condensate and something ferric.

[Beat 47]
On the floor above him, at this same moment, Reza was thickening the north corridor.

Tavi knew it the way he knew the camera lag — flat and certain, a weight sitting in his chest. The guard rotation had changed an hour ago, on his way up, and it still nagged at him.

The north corridor was where Reza's silhouette had crossed the sight-line twice, unhurried, deliberate. It was the walk of a man deciding where not to be. The south approach was thin. The east was thinner. Tavi had mapped the corridor and found one path that kept him off every floor Reza held. A route so exact it almost looked like luck.

[Beat 48]
The shaft was cold and the rungs were cold. Tavi went down into the dark, the cover back in place above him, the smell thickening with each rung. And under the smell and the dark and the steady count of his own breathing sat a fact he'd been holding at arm's length since the job was first briefed to him.

There was one door between the shaft and the Package. The door was on Reza's floor. No amount of routing fixed that.

[Beat 49]
The door opened inward. Tavi knew that — he'd read it off the hinge placement in the manifest photo — and he used it now. He came through low and left, keeping the door's sweep between him and the middle of the corridor. Reza was already there.

[Beat 50]
Not waiting. Moving. The first round came while Tavi was still in his crouch. It punched through the door panel above his right shoulder and let in daylight from somewhere. Tavi fired back low and fast, two rounds at ankle height, because Reza's first move under pressure was always to plant his feet. Tavi had watched him plant a thousand times.

[Beat 51]
Reza didn't plant. He dropped left and the rounds caught only floor. Four years ago he would have planted. Someone had spent four years teaching that out of him, and it hadn't been Tavi.

[Beat 52]
The Ankle-Biter was a cheap gun with a cheap voice — loud, flat, no suppressor, a bark that bounced off the walls and came back wrong. Reza's fire was quieter and faster and hit harder, corporate weight behind it. Tavi felt it through the junction box he'd put between himself and the muzzle.

The box shredded. Tavi moved before it finished, right, toward the camera — the short way, the lit way, the way Reza would rule out for exactly that reason. The round that should have found his chest found his left arm instead, just below the elbow. A graze, not a hole. It burned like a hole. He got behind the camera housing. The housing was concealment, not cover. He knew the difference and moved again.

[Beat 53]
Three seconds. Maybe four.

[Beat 54]
Reza came forward instead of back. Wrong — wrong by everything they'd ever run together, every corridor drill, every argument about angles. It meant Reza had landed on the same answer Tavi had. The corridor was twelve meters, and at twelve meters the Ankle-Biter's cheapness started to show. So Reza closed the distance. His hardware was built for close. His body had always been built for close. This was the one thing Tavi had routed around for three days and could not route around now.

[Beat 55]
The Ankle-Biter ran dry. Tavi heard his own trigger click on empty, heard Reza's slide lock back half a second later. Near enough to the same instant. Now they were the same distance apart with no rounds left between them — twelve meters of corridor and whatever each man's hands knew about the other's.

[Beat 56]
Reza came in at the hip. Tavi caught the wrist with his right hand, the graze-arm folding wrong under the weight, and used the turn to drive his elbow into Reza's jaw. Glancing, not the clean hit he wanted. But Reza's head snapped back, and that was half a second, enough to reload. Except the Ankle-Biter was down at his hip and his left hand wasn't working right, and Reza came back faster than half a second should have allowed. He came back like a man who's practiced getting hit. The return was a palm strike to Tavi's sternum that put him into the wall.

[Beat 57]
The wall was concrete. It said so.

And the palm angle — heel first, weight through the hips, the strike Tavi had taught him in a Pilsen basement — was Tavi's own, handed back at full price.

[Beat 58]
Tavi hit the wall and didn't stay there. Staying meant finished. He got his feet under him and his right hand found the Ankle-Biter's grip. He reloaded by feel, blind, the magazine seating with a click that felt like the only sure thing left in the corridor.

Three meters off, Reza was doing the same. Both breathing hard. Both bleeding somewhere. The guns came up at the same moment.

[Beat 59]
The guns stayed up.

[Beat 60]
Reza's chest was moving in big visible pulls. His jaw was already swelling where the elbow had caught it. Tavi watched Reza's eyes flick left, toward the Package door, and come back, and knew Reza had counted the same things he had — two guns, two people, one door, and behind it a Package one of them was paid to deliver and one paid to stop.

The job had its ending written in already. Tavi could read which way it would fall before Reza moved at all: Reza was going to let him through.

[Beat 61]
Reza's gun arm was dropping — three degrees, then more, the aim bleeding out of it. And the drop was costing him. You could see it. His jaw stayed tight and his eyes went somewhere between forgiving and beaten and settled on neither.

[Beat 62]
Tavi's own arm didn't move. He held the sight line. Held it. Held it. Then he let the Ankle-Biter fall to his side.

His hand didn't stop shaking on the grip. It hadn't stopped since the corridor went quiet, and letting the gun down didn't fix it.

[Beat 63]
Neither of them spoke.

[Beat 64]
Reza stepped back. One step. Then his shoulder turned — not all the way, not his back offered up, just enough rotation to open the corridor. Tavi walked through it. Past the swelling jaw. Past the blood on Reza's collar. Past the gun that wasn't raised. He didn't look at Reza's face. Looking would have made it a trade, and this wasn't a trade.

[Beat 65]
The Package door was ten meters ahead. Behind him, Tavi heard Reza's breathing. Then he heard it move off — Reza walking away down the corridor, taking the direction with nothing at the end of it. The smell from the shaft was everywhere now, blood-sweet, thick enough to taste. Tavi's left arm was burning. The corridor was empty. He was alone with the door.

[Beat 66]
The slug went public at 04:17.

[Beat 67]
Rafi felt it before he saw it. A pressure change, the kind that comes ahead of rain, rippling through the Volume around him. Then every display surface on the floor flickered and started cycling the package's header data. Routing manifest. Chain of custody. Three Ashgrave shell accounts and one Axiom-adjacent broker whose name Rafi had never been told and would never need. Someone upstream had copied the slug too, long before Rafi ever carried it. They'd been waiting for a moment like this to let it loose. Axiom's own containment flag had tripped something in the nano-cloud overhead, and that was the pressure that set it off.

[Beat 68]
He moved while the displays were still cycling. The suite door stood open — the wall that had held it was gone. Rafi didn't take the elevators; the elevators were logged. He took the service core. A maintenance stairwell ran the full drop of the Spire, forty-seven floors of switchbacks and dead sensors, down past the base-level concourse to a freight dock that let out below the Air Tax line. Rafi knew the route because Tavi had walked him through it, back in the suite, slow and careful, like a man making sure it would stick.

"In case," Tavi had said, and left the sentence there.

Rafi had kept it the way Gray Zone kids keep everything useful — without ceremony, without asking why.

[Beat 69]
He came out at ground level past a chained freight gate, knees wet, palms bleeding from the ferrocrete edge.

Far above and behind him the Spire still burned bright against the false gold, its overlay cycling protocols too late to matter.

Ashgrave's contracted crew was pulling back from a fight not worth having anymore. The asset they'd come to hold was everywhere now, which made it worth nothing. The broker's leverage was gone. The slug was spent.

Rafi stood in the pre-dawn dark of a Gray Zone street the city had decided didn't exist. Twenty years old. Nothing in his hands. Somewhere above him in the haze hung the next rung of the ladder. He started walking toward it, unhurried and unsure, because the only other thing to do was stop.

[Beat 70]
Onwe's message came through at 04:31 — two lines of text on Reza's display, the timestamp exact, the way Onwe did everything:

Retainer closed. Ashgrave won't seat this.

[Beat 71]
Reza read it twice in the doorway of Ozzie's building. The pre-dawn cold came off the Lake in long flat waves. Up the tiers, the Ashgrave Spire hung lit against the false gold, the Substrate around it grinding through containment protocols fourteen minutes after it could have mattered. Past all of it, on the southern horizon, the Thread climbed out of the haze with its running lights on, going where it always went.

The second read didn't change the words. He put the display away and stood with his hands at his sides — six-foot-three of paid-for muscle with nowhere to stand. He felt the altitude drain out of him. Slow and complete.

[Beat 72]
Coeli didn't know yet. She would soon. Onwe moved clean and Ashgrave moved fast. The broker whose name Reza had never been told was already spinning the loss, and Reza's name was the one surfacing as the thing that hadn't held. Not Tavi's. Tavi was the ghost. Ghosts didn't exist on paper. It was Reza's name on the coordination layer. Reza who'd put himself between the package and the up-altitude world he'd been trying to climb into. Two weeks ago that same Reza had stood in Coeli's kitchen, drinking coffee that cost more than his monthly UBC — the Universal Basic Credit that used to be the whole of his money — and believed, briefly and completely, that he was the kind of person that kitchen was built for.

[Beat 73]
Tavi heard it from a Ringo kiosk, which was worse than hearing it from a person. The kiosk's market-feed had already indexed the routing manifest. It had already tagged the chain of custody as an unresolved circuit breach — the same catch-all the kiosks slapped on any slug big enough to make the feed and small enough that nobody claimed it. The little scrolling summary at the bottom read package status: public domain, in the same flat tone it used for weather and transit delays.

He stood in front of it on a street that smelled of burned polymer and wet ferrocrete and read his own failure boiled down to fourteen words by a machine that charged Φ0.40 for the privilege. He paid. He always paid. The Φ0.40 lodged somewhere he'd feel later.

Somewhere in his queue sat the other invoice, the one he hadn't opened since the Spire — non-refusable, signed blind, ordering him to end the boy he'd just spent a night saving. It was still out there, unclosed, and whoever had paid that much for it wasn't going to let the loss go quiet.

[Beat 74]
He thought about Reza. About the moment two hours ago when Reza had stepped back and turned his shoulder to open the corridor, and Tavi had read it the only way there was to read it — I've got the egress — and let him have it, had handed off the piece of the job that should have stayed in his own hands. Because Reza was there, and the clock was tight, and for a second the word together had felt like a wall you could lean on instead of a door that opened onto nothing.

The kiosk cycled to the next story.

Tavi turned and started walking north, into the haze, into the pre-dawn gray that made every building look half-turned to rubble. He didn't run. Running was a confession, and he hadn't decided yet what he was confessing to.

[Beat 75]
The coffee counter at the Bloom Quarter's edge was standing-room only. There were no chairs — just a poured ferrocrete ledge at chest height and a woman behind it.

Tavi arrived first. He ordered the cheaper one and put his hands around the cup. Above the Quarter's boundary the Substrate coverage was failing in real time, the nano-cloud thinning until it was almost visible, a shimmer in the air where the city's basic upkeep just stopped.

He'd been here before. He'd been here with Reza, the morning after the job that first got them booked as Rampart and Cutout, two years ago, when the world had felt like it was opening instead of closing.

He'd been here again more recently too — the day Vane cut them loose for good, the day Reza's hand had come up an inch and stopped, and "same time next week" had meant nothing for the first time. This was where the two of them always ended up, for better and for worse.

[Beat 76]
Reza came from the south. Tavi saw him at forty meters and didn't straighten or turn to face him. He kept his hands around the cup and let Reza walk the full distance under his own weight — six-foot-three of it, all that corporate chrome catching no light, his face unreadable the whole way in.

Reza stopped on the other side of the ledge. He didn't order anything.

[Beat 77]
"The egress held," Tavi said.

"I know it held."

"Rafi got out."

"I know." Reza's jaw moved once, a small shift. His right forearm rested on the ledge. Where his twin used to ride — the cheap Meridian Ankle-Biter he'd carried the first two years, then traded up before he ever went corporate — there was a heavier corporate piece now, in a proper rig. Above it, a strip of medical nano-patch, clean white, already at work.

Tavi looked at it for one second and looked away. His own twin still sat against the inside of his left forearm, the same cheap Ankle-Biter it had always been — the one thing in his kit the night hadn't touched.

[Beat 78]
"They drew on you," Tavi said.

"Onwe's crew. Yeah."

[Beat 79]
The woman behind the counter moved to the far end. The shimmer above the Quarter pulsed once — some feral swarm testing the seam — and then settled.

Reza picked up Tavi's cup, looked at it, set it back down without drinking. Tavi let him.

[Beat 80]
"You're going to Coeli," Tavi said.

"No."

Tavi looked at him then.

"She already knows," Reza said. "Onwe moves fast." He pushed off the ledge. Not leaving yet — just shifting his weight, like he was deciding whether the next thing had to be said standing up. "You should have kept the egress."

"Yeah."

"You knew I'd take it if you handed it off?"

"Yeah."

[Beat 81]
Reza nodded once, slow. It wasn't agreement. It was the nod of a man confirming a thing he already knew was true. His hand came up off the ledge an inch, old reflex, two years of habit, and stopped there in the air between them, going nowhere. Tavi didn't move to meet it. Neither of them named what hadn't happened. He turned.

Tavi watched him go, south again, back into the haze. He didn't call after him. He didn't move.

Then he unclipped the twin from the inside of his left forearm and set it on the ledge, and left it there. The cup in his hands had gone cold.

[Beat 82]
Tavi stayed at the ledge a while longer, the cold cup still in his hands, the ceramic level with the air now, past any pretending. He set it down beside the Ankle-Biter and did not pick either back up.

The woman behind the counter didn't look at him. Above the Quarter's boundary the Substrate shimmer had settled back to its usual thin edge, a faint iridescence where coverage gave out, and the sky had started to gray toward morning.

[Beat 83]
He walked north, because north was where he'd been headed before Reza came, and there was no reason to change it. There was one thing left he could still do, and it was small, and it was the north edge of the Quarter: get to the augment booth before those two kids signed anything, and put himself between them and the slip.

The Bloom Quarter thinned around him. Body-mod clinics stood dark at this hour, their signage cycling procedures into the Volume's low glow. Nano-patch dispensaries were already open. The street smelled of wet ferrocrete and last night's fry oil. Three blocks east he could see Ozzie's dead storefront, the squat's lights still on. Ozzie hadn't slept. So Ozzie already knew. By now everybody did.

[Beat 84]
The augment booth stood at the Quarter's north edge, where it had stood the whole two years Tavi had worked this corridor. A half-collapsed transit shelter, its public-feed display long since stripped for parts, a man inside at a folding table with a credstick reader and a stack of slips.

Two kids stood in front of the table. Seventeen, maybe eighteen. One of them carried the new-chrome jitter of a first augment, not yet grown into what it did to a room.

The fixer handed a slip across the table. The other one — still unaugmented, still deciding — took it with both hands, the way you take a thing you've decided to believe in.

[Beat 85]
Tavi watched the kid's face change, and kept walking, and did not look back.
