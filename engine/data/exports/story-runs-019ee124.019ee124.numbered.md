# Story Runs

*Seto and Amara decide to get the evidence to a gray-zone journalist. At the Zone 3 checkpoint, he hands over the pre-paid card to stop the officer reaching the drive. The story leaves the building.*

The pre-paid card was dead at 13:47.

He had been composing a message to the 2PM contact when the channel went offline — not an error, not a network gap, just nothing. The relay handoff that had worked for three weeks returned no acknowledgment. He tried twice. The card had been used for six transmissions total and was now burned or the relay infrastructure on the receiving end was gone.

He went to Shen crossing on foot.

She was already there, standing at the transit lane edge with her jacket collar up, the porridge stall doing its early-afternoon trade behind her. She looked like someone waiting for a connection. He had learned to read her body at distance the way he'd learned to read checkpoint behavior — not because he intended to study her but because the bleed had made it impossible not to. The way she held her left shoulder slightly forward: something was wrong.

He told her about the card. She told him about the leave — two weeks, starting Monday, arranged by Ciro without her input. She told him about the razor open on the desk, the word "loss mitigation," the way Ciro had provisioned her access before inviting her to use it and then observed exactly what she accessed. She told him about the wrong coffee.

"He was establishing presence," he said. "In case you noticed the proximity alert."

"I noticed," she said.

The Seam was doing its midday business around them — vendors arguing about stall placement, a relay tech running a junction box test, the Zone 6 transit pod cycling through on schedule. Two people having a careful conversation in a public space, which was what the Seam was designed for.

"What do we have?" she said.

He told her: the manifest, 847 names, three copies; the courier routing documentation for the six Zone 7 facilities; the monitoring node localized to his building. She told him about the infrastructure logs — 112 gaps, the query structure precise enough for someone else to reproduce. She had no saved data; the query had been intercepted. But the interruption was itself in the logs. The gap where her query had been paused proved the suppression protocol was still running and that the system was watching for someone to look at it.

"The query is the evidence," he said. "Someone who knows how to run it can verify it."

She had been looking at the Seam traffic. She looked at him directly.

"If it goes," she said, "it doesn't come back."

"No."

"The story runs without us."

"Yes."

She turned back to the transit lane. A moment in which she was weighing something he couldn't see, in a register the bleed couldn't reach — not spatial, not proprioceptive, just hers. He waited.

"Then the story runs," she said.

They went separately. He took the courier route through the Seam with the drive in the inner pocket of his courier bag.

The Zone 3/4 boundary checkpoint was staffed at elevated protocol — he registered this thirty meters out, in the spacing of the officers and the angle of the secondary scanner. He had not seen elevated protocol at this checkpoint in fourteen months. He rolled into the inspection lane and a checkpoint officer held up a hand. Secondary bag inspection.

He unclipped the outer courier bag and set it on the table. The officer ran the outer compartments through the scan. Clean. She reached for the inner pocket authorization tag.

He pulled out the pre-paid card and held it toward her. Not asking a question. Answering one before she could ask it. "Gray-zone communications device," he said. "Requires separate logging under Code 7. I can hold the compartment open while you process it."

She looked at the card. She looked at him. Gray-zone devices went into a different log than physical goods — separate form, separate system entry. She took the card.

While she logged it, he kept his hand on the inner compartment tag and did not open it. She did not ask him to. She was looking at the device ID on the card, entering it character by character into the inspection terminal.

"Clear," she said. She handed the card back.

He took it. The device was now in the checkpoint log. He would not be using it again — but the channel was already dead, and the only transmissions it carried were already received. He rode two blocks past the checkpoint and dropped the card into a seam-side waste bin. There was nothing on it worth keeping.

The relay shop in Zone 3 had a front window full of second-hand networking equipment and a back room licensed as a media archive. The owner was a man named Doru who knew Seto by the weight of his bags and asked no questions about introductions.

The journalist was not there. She had a drop protocol: the drive into a sealed case, the account written by hand on physical paper, transmitted by Doru on a channel she owned. Seto sat at the back table and wrote down everything: the batch number, the 847 names, the six facilities, the monitoring node at his building, the 112 gaps in the infrastructure logs and exactly how the query had been structured. He wrote Amara's account in her words. He wrote Ciro's actual title: loss mitigation.

Doru read the account. His thumbnail whitened where he was pressing the paper flat without seeming to notice. He set it down.

"Give me three days," he said.

He took the drive. The drive was no longer in Seto's hands.

They met outside the relay shop by arrangement — she had come separately, on foot, arriving three minutes after him. The afternoon light caught the relay beacon casings at an angle that made them look older than they were. A woman two doors down was closing a shutter by hand, the ratchet mechanism catching and releasing with a regular click.

"What happens now?" she said.

"We wait. We stay in motion." He looked at her. "You go on leave Monday."

"Yes."

"That's good," he said. "The story needs time."

She nodded. She looked at the relay shop, which was closed now, the MEDIA ARCHIVE sign switched off. "He called me by my first name," she said. "At the end. Like I was already gone."

He didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that changed what had already been set in motion.

He rode home through Zone 4. The drives were gone. The account was in Doru's hands. He thought about the 847 names he had read with full attention, zone by zone, facility by facility, each name a person with a clinic appointment and a gap in their record they would never be able to see. He thought about Facility 4's new reader and the guard's jaw tightening when she ran the packet through.

He had been building this case without knowing he was building it. Every timing anomaly, every checkpoint change — the case had assembled itself through the act of paying attention.

He thought about Amara at the Shen crossing stall, the way she had stood for a moment before she answered. *Then the story runs.* Said the same way she said everything: without performance, as if the sentence had already been decided and the speaking of it was just catching up.

He didn't write anything on his wrist. He just rode.

His relay terminal flagged a compliance update at 18:03: *Zone 3/4 courier certification suspended. Orison Regulatory Compliance: licensing review pending, active routes suspended, estimated processing five to ten business days.* The story was in Doru's hands. The routes were gone. The ordering was in his favor.
