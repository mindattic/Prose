# The Other Side of the Noise

*Seto receives Amara's signal. He doesn't know what it is. He runs a trace that returns nothing.*

He repaired the rear hub at 0545 — bearing pulling three percent on the right for two weeks, monitored long enough to be sure. Flipped the bike, pulled the wheel, pressed the new Shimano set in with his thumb and the heel of his palm, feeling for the seat. Checking the rotation until the resistance ran even all the way around. Forty-three minutes. The smell of machine oil and the cold specific quiet of a morning not started yet.

He was on the road by 0640. The message arrived at 0612, before he'd left.

He pulled over in the gap between Zone 3 and Zone 4 — past the Arcturus grid forty meters east, the Seam air came in unfiltered: frying oil from a canteen window three floors up, standing water off the drainage channel, the smell of concrete that had absorbed a decade of other people's routes. He ran the diagnostic there. The signal had landed in his primary stack, not the intercept buffer where foreign traffic waited. No flag. No quarantine.

The routing trace dissolved at the fourth hop. Not a dead end — an erasure. The sender credential was a Cellvault Level 3 neuretics maintenance registration. He logged it against his route map. The maintenance registration touched the eastern boundary of the Zone 6/7 corridor — the clearance node he checked through every time he delivered to the Orison crescent. He knew that door. He knew the checkpoint and the receiving bay beyond it, the managed air that hit your sinuses the moment you stepped off the street, the bay's flat lighting that never varied between morning and close. Three years of deliveries to that building, and the second checkpoint was as far as any courier got. The sender was past it. Nuru's clinic sat four blocks inside the same boundary.

She should not have reached him.

---

The third package of the morning was in the gray corridor between the Orison crescent and the eastern relay district. Standing contact. He brought the bicycle to a stop and held the package out.

The receiver's grip closed on the casing.

His right hand did not let go.

It tightened — the specific torque of someone pressing a bearing housing into its seat. Thumb at the lip. Heel of the palm building lateral pressure. Waiting for the give that meant the seat was true. The workbench motion from forty minutes ago, with hands that were his.

The receiver's eyes came up to his face. She held still for a moment, receipt card in her left hand, a smell of machine oil on her sleeve that had nothing to do with him. Then she initialed left-handed, tucked the receipt into her collar, and said nothing about the hand.

He walked the bicycle to the corner and pressed his palm flat against the wall. Concrete. Cold. Six seconds. He counted them.

---

She had attached six years of consortium research. Peer-reviewed, dense with frequency tables — not the kind of document that circulated informally, because the people who published it waited until the corpus was undeniable. He read it at his sixth delivery stop, bike locked to a drainpipe, a woman selling laminated prayers across the path and a dog sleeping in the exact center of the walkway.

His routes had been telling him the same thing for six months: the Zone 6/7 medical corridor going white sector by sector as corp infrastructure absorbed the independent relay, the through-routes he'd worked for three years turning managed. He'd been logging it as market drift.

The dog lifted its head and looked at him, then put it down again.

He pulled his field notes against the paper's geographic data. The boundary matched Orison's maintenance territory exactly. He scrolled to Zone 6. Nuru's clinic sat four blocks inside the eastern margin. Her shift ended at twenty-two hundred, every night, in a building that Orison's maintenance infrastructure had absorbed in October.

He opened a draft to her. *Call me when* —

A ping landed in his peripheral stack: maintenance notification from the eastern boundary node, timestamped 16:47. Routine. Automated. The same node that cleared his packages through to the receiving bay. He looked at it for four seconds.

He closed the draft without finishing the sentence.

If she was on a watched node — and if Orison's footprint extended to the comm infrastructure the way it extended to the relay — sending anything could flag her account. He didn't know what the flag looked like from inside the second checkpoint. He knew only that the ping had landed while he was composing to her, and that he could not afford to guess.

He reached out to Amara first. He'd waited long enough.

*My name is Seto. What you sent — I've been mapping this boundary from the outside for six months without understanding what I was looking at. I need to know what it looks like from in.*

She answered in twelve minutes, and what she knew was impossible. He believed her. She knew his morning window at seven-minute accuracy. She described the bearing replacement not by schedule but by the physical sensation of it — resistance, give, the moment the seat ran true. She described the kind of person Seto kept outside his professional world, not by name or address but by the specific quality of the thing being protected. She had sent the message from a Zone 6 parking structure. *I'm writing this with the engine still running,* she'd written, and the sentence was there in the middle of the data like something she hadn't meant to include.

The consortium papers had called the signal's effects involuntary. He believed that too.

She said her maintenance records looked clean in the Zone 6/7 corridor when they shouldn't. Calibration visits logged, no events recorded. She described the room she'd been in when the bleed started — managed lighting she couldn't read a time of day from, no windows. A client talking about pricing tiers for his next cycle.

He had stood in the receiving bay enough times to know that light. She was describing rooms the lobby existed to protect.

She finished with: *They were mine.*

He held the field notebook. She wasn't reporting it up the chain. She was telling him what her hands felt like after. He wrote that last sentence down separately.

*Your earliest session with that pattern — what month?*

He did not give her the map. The channel had been open since morning and had already proven it carried things neither of them had sent deliberately. He gave her a question and held the map.

---

He checked his queue at twenty-one hundred. One flagged entry: a route advisory from a Zone 5 coordinator.

Nothing from Nuru. Not the first sign-off, not the typo-correction a minute behind it.

Her shift would end at twenty-two hundred. He had been keeping that hour in his peripheral awareness for six months, the way you keep a clock you haven't looked at directly but can still read. She would send *Clear. —N.* and a minute later the corrected version with whatever she'd misspelled.

He opened the draft he'd closed that afternoon. *Call me when you finish tonight.* Four words and a time. Nothing that read as urgent to anything scanning for urgency. He read it twice.

He sent it.

He put the phone face-down on the desk and picked up the field notebook. Nuru's clinic was in the building he'd been mapping from the outside. He had been logging her boundary every day for six months without thinking of it that way, treating her life as a fixed point, a thing outside the shape. It was not outside the shape. It was inside it. He had known that since October and chosen not to know it.

At 22:17 his peripheral stack returned a single notification: *Clear. —N.*

He waited.

At 22:18 it came: *Clear. —N.* Corrected. The same habit she'd had since they were children, sending a thing and then sending the right version a minute behind it.

He wrote nothing in the notebook. He didn't need to. He already knew the question — whether the window between tonight's sign-off and tomorrow's had just gotten smaller, and whether it would narrow until the sign-offs stopped arriving, and whether he would have enough time when that happened to do anything that mattered.
