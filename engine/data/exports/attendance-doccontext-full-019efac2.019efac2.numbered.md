# Attendance DocContext FULL

_Cross-district truancy investigator Yemina Fola investigates three children who vanished from schools in the Pilsen Veil — each one preceded by a neuretics burnout flag, each disappearance logged as an unexcused absence before anyone connected the cases. The schools were under three different corp contracts; nobody had connected them because nobody else could see all three. When she sweeps the third school's bathroom with a resonance sensor, she finds two traces: an echo above the child's usual seat where the bleed's signal tuned him, and a shadow in the bathroom stall where he crossed. In her car afterward, someone else's hands complete a search query in her neuretics: 47 missing children, GLMZ-wide, 22 months. The 48th entry is her own name. The onset date is six weeks ago._

## Attendance

[Beat 1]
The towers on 63rd Street did the cicada thing they always did, that subsidized neural-relay drone the brochures called ambient comfort and everyone in Cicada Lawn called the hum, a frequency that lived in the back teeth and the sinus and the soft place behind the ears until you forgot it was a sound and started thinking it was just the texture of being alive here. Mara Okonkwo walked the length of the relay line with her collar up against the redirected rain, the storm that had been someone else's storm an hour ago over the Spire district and was hers now because the atmospheric processors had decided, on a schedule, that the Gray Zone was where weather went to be spent. Her boots took the puddles. The neon from the noodle stand on the corner — Chen's, third generation, the only door on the block that opened without asking your balance — bled red and green into the water and broke apart under her step, and the broken version was prettier than the sign.

*Forty kids on the roster. You marked twelve last week. Twelve is not a class, Mara, twelve is a waiting room.*

She climbed the exterior stair to the relay maintenance landing they'd let her use because nobody else wanted it, the one room in Cicada Lawn close enough to the towers that the hum sat on every word like a thumb, and she keyed the door and counted heads while she still had the dark to hide her face in. Six. Six small shapes on folding chairs under a worklight, six faces turned up out of hoods, and the seventh chair held a boy she didn't know, maybe nine, hands flat on his knees the way you sat when somebody had told you once to sit like that and hitting was involved if you didn't.

"You're all here early," she said, which was a lie that cost her nothing and bought them a second to settle, and she pulled the door shut against the rain and let the lock take. *Don't ask where the others are. Don't make tonight about the empty chairs.* The hum closed over the room. She unwound her scarf with hands that had spent the walk wanting to be steadier than this, and she looked at the boy she didn't know, and she did not ask his name yet — names were how the Ear filed you — she just nodded at the empty chair beside him like it had always been waiting, and started.

[Beat 2]
"Hands where I can see them," she said, and watched six pairs of hands come up off knees and onto the table, palms down, the way she'd taught them — not because there was any danger in this room but because the city outside taught their hands to hide, to fold small, to apologize for taking up space, and the first thing she ever undid was that. She set out the tiles. Not screens. Tiles, real ones, ceramic squares with letters fired into the glaze, salvaged from a school that didn't exist anymore in a district that didn't either, and they made a sound against the table the Ear couldn't parse into anything because it wasn't speech and it wasn't a transaction and it wasn't on any schedule. *That sound is the whole lesson. Let them hear it.*

The boy she didn't know watched the others reach. He didn't reach. He kept his hands on the table where she'd put them but flat and still, fingers pressed together, and his eyes went to the door and then the worklight and then her scarf where she'd dropped it, mapping exits, mapping the things in the room that could be thrown, and Mara felt the old recognition come up under her ribs like a tide that knew the shape of this particular shore.

*That's a kid who counts before he sits down. You used to count.*

"You don't have to touch anything tonight," she told him, quiet, pitched under the hum so it stayed between the two of them and not up where the towers could file it. "You can just watch. Watching's allowed." And she slid one tile across the table toward him anyway — not at him, just into the space between, the letter M face-up under the worklight — and left it there, and turned to the others like she'd already forgotten she'd done it, because the only way to give a thing to a child like that was to let him decide it was his when nobody was looking.
