# The First Anti-Hero

_A plain-English narrative retelling of Milton's Paradise Lost from Satan's point of view, paired with GSPL-discipline nonfiction research on Milton and the poem's real sources._

[Beat 1]
Nine days Satan had been falling. Now he lay still, upon a lake that burned and did not consume him, and felt, for the first time in his existence, pain that would not end.

The fire took hold of him the way fire takes hold of anything - closing over his skin, filling lungs he still drew breath into from old habit, burning along every nerve he had - and did not finish the work. A wound that will not close is worse than one that kills you outright, because a killing wound at least stops. This one did not stop. He had never known such a thing before; in the country he had come from, nothing was built to threaten the body. Here, apparently, pain was not an event that happened and passed. It was a condition he was kept in.

He raised himself on his arms anyway, because lying still inside it was somehow worse than moving through it. The ground beneath him held the weight and heat of something alive, though it was only ash and broken stone. His hands, when he looked at them, were still his own hands - dark now, seamed, the fire moving over them without ever moving on. The light that had once lived beneath that skin was gone.

He stood. The lake stretched away from him on every side, fire crossing its surface in slow, unbroken sheets. Above him hung a dark so deep it read as black, no stars in it, no vault - only the sense of something vast pressing down, with no ceiling to call it sky.

And scattered across that burning water: shapes. Hundreds of them, sunk to the shoulders or the chin, unmoving, and Satan understood, looking at them, exactly what they were enduring, because he was enduring the same thing in the same moment. Every one of them had been burning for nine days, without pause, without any promise that pause was coming.

He knew them without needing to see a single face. He knew them by the set of a shoulder, the old bearing of officers who had stood at his back through every hour of the war now lost. They had followed him out of Heaven. They had fallen because he fell, cast down together into this same endless burning.

He was afraid, and did not let it show, because there was nothing left to do about the fear except carry it forward along with everything else this fire was already making him carry.

Around him, the nearest of the sunken shapes had begun, slowly, to turn - not rising yet, only a change in their stillness, the first sign that something in the lake had stood upright inside its own agony and might, therefore, mean to speak. They did not yet know what that meant. Neither, entirely, did he.

*They are waiting to be told what this is,* he thought.

*So am I.*

But a prince does not say such things aloud - not on the first hour of a war he had already lost once, in a fire that meant to keep punishing him for it indefinitely. He set his eyes on the nearest shape still standing at the fire's edge, and walked toward it, each step a fresh negotiation with the burning, each step finding purchase on stone that had clearly been broken by some great violence, rather than simply lying that way from the beginning of things.

The figure did not move as he approached. It stood chest-deep in the flame, waiting, in the particular stillness of one who has served long enough to know that the only thing left to do, even inside unrelenting pain, is wait for an order.

Satan had given that order a thousand times, in a life that no longer existed. He meant to learn, now, whether it would still be obeyed - and he meant, before this hour was finished, to find some way to get every one of them, himself included, out of this fire that would not let them go.

[Beat 2]
The figure was Beelzebub.

Ash had reached him first, grown into the skin rather than settled upon it, but the eyes were the same eyes, and they woke when they found Satan standing over him.

"If it is truly you," Beelzebub said. His voice came out cracked, unused for nine days. "But O, how fallen. How changed. You who once outshone ten thousand others — I would hardly know you now."

"I am he," Satan said. "What fell from glory: that is still me, though the glory is gone. Changed, as you see. And you no less than I."

He drew Beelzebub up out of the fire — no ceremony in it, only the weight and the will to bear it. Beelzebub stood swaying, looked out at the lake, at the hundreds of shapes still sunk in it.

"Into what pit have we fallen," he said quietly. "How are the mighty fallen — we who stood nearest the throne. Brought down to this."

Satan looked past him, toward the wall he could feel more than see at the lake's far edge, and toward the host still waiting to be told what came next.

"What though the field be lost," he said. "The will is not lost. Nor the hatred, nor the courage never to yield. That, at least, He will never take from me. To kneel and beg His grace — that would be the true fall. Far worse than this."

"To what end," Beelzebub said. "If we cannot be destroyed, and cannot rise — what is left us but this? Standing in fire, remembering what we were?"

"We are down," Satan said. "I will not tell you otherwise. But down is not finished. He may burn us. He has not unmade us. We are not what we were — but we are, and I mean to build everything else on that single word."

"Build what?"

"A hall," Satan said. "A court of our own. Here, where He cannot reach us without abandoning the throne He loves too much to leave."

He was quiet a moment, and when he spoke again his voice had slowed, each word set down with care.

"The mind is its own place. It can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven. What matters where, if I remain what I am? Here, at least, we are free. He will not drive us out merely to spite us further. Here we may reign, and to reign is worth having, even here."

He looked at Beelzebub.

"Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heaven."

Beelzebub did not answer. Satan let the silence hold.

"They gave us minds that do not stop," he went on. "Minds that reason. That remember. He made us to worship, and did not trouble himself to notice that a mind capable of worship is equally capable of refusal. That is the one thing He cannot burn out of me — not a gift He can take back, but the shape of the mind itself. If we cannot win by strength — and we have already lost that argument once — then we win by every other means such a mind commands. Guile outlasts armies. Patience outlasts thrones."

His voice dropped further.

"To do good will never again be our task," he said. "But to do ill — that will be our whole delight, because it is the one answer left to give Him."

Beelzebub was silent a long while. Satan let him be.

"And the others," he said finally, looking out at the lake. "Do they know yet? Do our friends lie there still — asleep in this trench, or simply too broken to rise?"

"Not yet," Satan said. "That is why we stand here talking, rather than standing there in it. Help me raise them. Whatever remains in them — grief, rage, the plain will to still exist — I mean to use all of it. Nine days ago we held the brightest quarter of Heaven's own light. I will not let this lake persuade them it is lost for good."

He looked out at the fire, at the hundreds of shapes still sunk in it.

"Call them," he said. "All of them. Let them see their prince standing. Whatever remains of the will that followed me out of Heaven — I need it now, awake, and on its feet."

[Beat 3]
Beelzebub's voice carried further than either of them expected — scarred by the fire, but not silenced — and it went out over the lake like a call to arms across any battlefield.

For a moment, nothing answered. The shapes stayed sunk, and the old fear returned to Satan, the one he kept but did not speak: that this was all that remained. Two of them standing, and no one left to carry the war he meant to keep fighting.

Then the lake began to move.

Not the fire — the shapes within it. One rose first, near the shore, hauling itself upright. Then another. Then the whole surface of the lake was moving at once, thousands of them finding their feet on legs that no longer remembered what legs were for.

They looked like nothing that had ever stood in Heaven — scorched, diminished, marked by the fall and the fire both.

And still they stood.

He tried three times to speak, and three times something rose in his throat that was very near tears, before he mastered it and let none of it reach his voice.

They were not beaten. Marked, certainly. But the will that had followed him out of Heaven had followed him down as well, and it stood now on the shore of a burning lake, waiting to be told what it had all been for.

He raised his hand, and the host went quiet — the silence spreading outward from him the way the rising had spread outward from the shore, until there was nothing in that vast dark but the low sound of fire and the enormous, waiting attention of everyone who had followed him this far.

"Look at yourselves," Satan said, and his voice carried the length of them, borne by whatever this place did to sound in place of air. "Nine days in that, and you are standing. I have seen soldiers broken by a single hour of far less. Whatever this fall has cost us, it has not cost us this."

A sound moved through them — not quite a cheer, but something near to one.

"I will not lie to you about what we lost," he said. "I watched the field go, the same as you. But I did not come down to grieve what is finished. I came to see what remains. And what stands before me now, in fire, on legs that ought not hold you — that is worth more to me than the whole bright field we lost it on."

He let that stand, unadorned. It required no ornament to be true.

"Stand a while longer," he said. "I have more to say, and I mean to say all of it before this night — if night still means anything here — is finished. But first: look to one another. You are not alone in this, whatever else is true of where we have landed. That is the first weapon I mean to arm you with. It will not be the last."

[Beat 4]
They came to him singly, as next in worth, roused from that fiery couch at their great emperor's call — walking out of the general ruin to where he stood on the bare strand, while the promiscuous crowd of the host still hung back and watched from a distance.

The chief of them were the ones who, long after, roaming up out of this pit to seek their prey on Earth, would dare to set their seats beside the seat of God himself: their altars next to his altar, gods adored among the surrounding nations, bold enough to abide Jehovah thundering out of Sion. Some would plant their shrines inside his own sanctuary — abominations set in the holy place — and profane his rites and his solemn feasts with cursed things, and affront his light with their darkness.

First came Moloch.

He would be called the horrid king, besmeared with the blood of human sacrifice and the tears of parents, though the drums and timbrels beat loud enough that no one had to hear the children's cries as they passed through fire to his grim idol. The Ammonites would worship him in Rabba and her watery plain, in Argob and Basan, out to the stream of utmost Arnon. Not content with that audacious neighbourhood, he would lead the wisest heart of Solomon by fraud to build his temple directly opposite the temple of God, on that hill of scandal, and would make his grove of the pleasant valley of Hinnom — called Tophet after that, and black Gehenna, and taken ever since as the very type of Hell.

Satan looked at him and saw a mind with a single answer to every question: force. It would be useful. It would also need watching, for a mind that knows only one answer offers it at the worst possible hour.

Next came Chemos, the dread of Moab's sons, worshipped from Aroer to Nebo and the wild of southmost Abarim, in Hesebon and Horonaim, past the flowery dale of Sibma with its vines, out to the Asphaltic Pool. Peor, they would call him elsewhere, when he enticed Israel at Sittim on their march up from the Nile into rites that cost them dearly. He would enlarge those orgies as far as that same hill of scandal, setting himself beside the grove of Moloch — lust hard by hate — until good Josiah drove the pair of them back down to Hell.

Chemos had always kept his own counsel, even in the bright halls. Satan meant to remember that.

Then came a company that did not trouble to separate itself: the Baalim, and beside them Ashtaroth, the powers Israel would trade God's own glory for, more times than any faithful history cared to count. Among them walked one crowned with a crescent horn, whom the Phoenicians would call the queen of heaven, and whose altars the young men of Sidon would honor beneath a horned moon.

They had been beloved once, in the way beauty is beloved before anyone asks what it costs to keep it. Satan understood, watching them, that they would draw followers for reasons that had nothing to do with strategy at all.

After them came one who would one day mourn in earnest — when the captive ark maimed his brute image, head and hands lopped off in his own temple, on the threshold, where he fell flat and shamed his worshippers. Dagon: a sea-monster, a man from the waist up and a fish below it, who would nonetheless have his temple raised high in Azotus and be dreaded the length of the Philistine coast, in Gath and Ascalon, in Accaron and out to Gaza's frontier.

Satan noted the strangeness in him: legs that did not seem convinced they were legs. A captured god, he thought, sometimes falls on his own face before the very thing that captured him.

Rimmon followed, already reading the ground, the wall, the shape of the camp, the way a quartermaster reads a battlefield before anyone else has thought to. His seat would be fair Damascus, on the fertile banks of Abbana and Pharphar. He too would be bold against the house of God — losing a leper and gaining a king, drawing Ahaz his sottish conqueror to displace God's own altar for one of Syrian fashion, and to burn his offerings on it, and to worship the gods he had beaten in the field.

He would be useful precisely for that. Not strength, not cunning, but the plain labor of keeping an army fed and housed in a country that had, as yet, no roads at all.

Last, standing a little apart, came a crew under names of old renown — Osiris, Isis, Orus and their train — who with monstrous shapes and sorceries would abuse fanatic Egypt and her priests into seeking their wandering gods disguised in brutish forms rather than human.

They kept, even in the fall, a dignity that did not depend on where they stood; and Satan judged that such dignity would in time be worth more to him than force. Force could be broken. Dignity, once it chose to survive, generally found a way.

[Beat 5]
Satan climbed the highest rise within reach of the shore — little more than a shelf of broken stone, but height enough to be seen — and he called out so loudly that the whole hollow deep of that country carried it back to him.

"Princes. Potentates. Warriors — the flower of Heaven, once yours, now lost. Or is it only that such astonishment as this can seize even eternal spirits? Have you chosen this place to rest in after the toil of battle — do you find it so easy here that you would sleep on it as you slept in the vales of Heaven?"

No one answered. He had not expected anyone to.

"Or is it that in this abject posture you have sworn to adore the Conqueror — who is watching, even now, cherub and seraph rolling in that flood with their arms and their standards scattered around them? He will not watch long. His pursuers will look down from the gates of Heaven, and see the advantage, and come down, and tread us into this ground while we lie drooping in it — or fix us to the floor of this gulf with linked thunderbolts, and be done."

His voice hardened.

"Awake. Arise. Or be forever fallen."

They heard it, and were ashamed, and came up — the way men set to keep a watch, caught sleeping by the one person they most feared, are on their feet and moving before they are properly awake. They felt the pains of the place as they rose. Not one of them mistook where he was. They rose anyway.

When they had gathered, the ranks bent around him from wing to wing, half enclosing him, and their attention held them silent.

He tried three times to speak. Three times something rose in his throat that was very near tears — the kind angels weep — before words found a way out interwoven with them.

"O myriads of immortal spirits," he said. "O powers matchless — matchless but with the Almighty. And that strife was not inglorious, whatever the outcome, though the outcome was dire, as this place testifies, and this change in all of us, which I can hardly bear to name aloud.

"But what mind, foreseeing anything, drawing on any knowledge of what had ever been, could have feared that such united force of gods — force such as stood in that field — could ever know repulse? And who can believe, even now, even after loss, that these legions, whose exile has emptied Heaven, will fail to rise again, self-raised, and repossess the country we were born to?

"Let the whole host of Heaven witness whether it was any counsel of mine, or any danger I shirked, that lost us our hopes."

He let that stand, because it was the charge he most needed answered before he could ask them for anything.

"Here is what actually beat us. He who reigns as monarch up there sat secure on his throne — upheld by old repute, by consent, by custom, putting out the full show of his royal state and keeping his strength concealed. That concealment tempted our attempt. That is what wrought our fall. We did not measure him wrongly out of stupidity. We measured what he let us see.

"Well. Henceforth we know his might, and we know our own. So we need neither provoke a new war nor dread one if it comes. What remains to us is the better part: to work in close design, by fraud or by guile, what force could not effect — so that he may learn at length what he has not yet troubled to consider. That whoever overcomes by force has overcome only half his foe."

Something moved through the host at that — more than the low sound before it. Resolve, taking shape.

"And there is somewhere to spend it," Satan said. "Space may produce new worlds. There went a rumor in Heaven, common enough that most of you will have heard it, that he intended before long to create just such a place, and to plant in it a generation his own regard would favor equally with the sons of Heaven. Thither, if only to pry — that may well be our first eruption. Thither, or elsewhere. Because this infernal pit will never hold celestial spirits in bondage, and this abyss will not keep us long under its darkness.

"But thoughts like these want full counsel to mature them. So I will not decide it here, on a shore, by acclamation. I will decide it with all of you, in a hall built for the purpose.

"Understand only what is already settled. Peace is despaired of. Who among you can even think submission? War, then. War open, or war understood — but war, and it must be resolved so."

The answer came not as a cheer but as steel. Millions of flaming swords flew out at once, drawn from the thighs of the mighty cherubim, and the sudden blaze of them lit Hell further than any of them had yet been able to see. They raged against the Highest, and clashed their grasped arms on their sounding shields, and hurled the din of it upward, toward the vault of a Heaven that no longer had any part in them.

Satan stood upon the rise and let the sound move through him.

*This is not nothing,* he thought. *Whatever else is true — this is not nothing.*

[Beat 6]
There stood a hill not far off, its grisly top belching fire and rolling smoke, the rest of it shining with a glossy crust that was, to anyone who knew ore, an undoubted sign: metal hidden in the belly of it, the work of sulphur.

A brigade went for the hill at speed, the way pioneers with spade and pickaxe run ahead of a royal camp to trench a field or throw up a rampart. Mammon led them.

Mammon was the least erect spirit that fell from Heaven. Even in Heaven his looks and his thoughts had bent downward — admiring the riches of that pavement, gold worn smooth by walking on it, more than anything divine or holy he was actually there to see. Men would learn the same habit from him later, taught by his suggestion to ransack the center of things and rifle with impious hands the bowels of their own mother earth, after treasures that had been better left hidden.

His crew opened a spacious wound in the hill and dug out ribs of gold.

Let no one wonder that riches grow in Hell. That soil, of all soils, may best deserve the precious bane.

Nearer the plain, in cells prepared with veins of liquid fire sluiced straight from the lake, a second multitude founded the massy ore with a skill nobody watching had expected to see here — severing each kind from each, skimming the dross off the bullion. A third had already formed a mould in the ground, and from the boiling cells, by some conveyance no eye could follow, filled every hollow nook of it at once: as an organ, from a single blast of wind, breathes sound into rank after rank of pipes.

Then out of the earth the fabric rose.

It did not go up the way built things go up. It rose like an exhalation, whole, to the sound of sweet voices and instruments — a temple, pilastered round, Doric pillars carrying a golden architrave, cornice and frieze worked over with raised sculpture, and above all of it a roof of fretted gold. Neither Babylon nor great Alcairo in the whole height of their glory ever equalled it, not to house Belus or Serapis, not to seat their kings in the years when Egypt and Assyria strove to outspend one another.

The pile stood fixed at its stately height. The doors opened their brazen folds on ample spaces and a smooth level pavement, and from the arched roof, hung there by a subtlety none of them could name, row upon row of starry lamps and blazing cressets fed with naphtha and asphalt gave off light as though from a sky.

The multitude came in admiring. Some praised the work. Some praised the architect.

His hand was known. In Heaven he had raised many a high towered structure where sceptred angels kept residence and sat as princes, each set over his own order by the supreme King. Nor was his name unheard in ancient Greece, or in the Italian country, where men would call him Mulciber and tell how he fell from Heaven — flung by an angry Jove clean over the crystal battlements, falling from morning to noon, and from noon to dewy evening, a whole summer's day, and dropping with the setting sun like a falling star onto Lemnos in the Aegean.

They tell it wrong. He fell long before that, and he fell with this rebellious rout, and it availed him nothing now to have built high towers in Heaven; for all his engines he was sent down headlong with the rest, and his industrious crew with him, to build in Hell instead.

Meanwhile the winged heralds, by command of sovereign power, went through the host with awful ceremony and the sound of trumpets and proclaimed a solemn council, to be held at once.

They gave the place its name in the proclaiming: Pandemonium, the high capital of Satan and all his peers.

The summons called the worthiest from every band and squared regiment, by place or by choice, and they came trooping in by hundreds and by thousands.

Satan climbed the steps that first hour and looked back at the lake, at the great scorched host passing, in the span of a single reckoning, from ruin to labor to something that had begun unmistakably to resemble a country.

It was not Heaven. It would never be Heaven, and he had already told them, and himself, that he did not wish it to be.

It was theirs — and for the moment, that was enough to build the rest upon.

[Beat 7]
Pandemonium held its first council before the fire outside had burned an hour longer.

Satan took the high seat they had raised for him — a throne cut from the same dark ore as the columns, set higher than any other place in the hall, so that even seated he looked down on the whole gathered court. Around him sat the great ones of the fallen host: Moloch, Chemos, Belial, Mammon, Beelzebub at his right hand, and behind them rank on rank of the lesser host, packed into a chamber that had somehow, without anyone quite noticing how, grown large enough to hold them all.

He looked out over them and did not rise, because a king does not rise to speak to his own council.

"Powers, and Dominions," he said. "Deities of Heaven — for such you still are, whatever this place has cost us. I do not call this council to mourn what we lost. I call it to decide what we do now that we have lost it."

A stillness settled over the hall, the particular stillness of an audience that has been promised a decision and means to hear every word of how it is reached.

"No deep within this gulf," Satan said, "can hold immortal strength forever, though it is oppressed, and fallen, and made to feel every part of that fall. I do not give Heaven for lost. I count us only delayed.

"And consider what this place gives us that Heaven never did. Where there is no good to strive for, no strife can grow up out of faction. None of you will claim precedence in Hell. None of you holds so small a portion of present pain that he will covet more of it out of ambition. So we have an advantage here toward union, toward firm faith and firm accord, greater than anything that was ever possible up there — and with it we return to claim our just inheritance of old, surer to prosper now than prosperity itself could ever have made us.

"By what best way, then. Whether of open war, or of covert guile. That is what we now debate."

He let it stand open, and did not name anyone to answer it.

"Who can advise may speak."

[Beat 8]
Moloch stood. He did not wait to be asked twice.

"My sentence is for open war," he said. "Of wiles, more unexpert, I boast not. Them let those contrive who need, or when they need, not now. Let us instead beat the drum again, and this time climb by force rather than by cunning — since cunning has not yet been tried, and force, at least, we understand."

A low murmur of approval moved through the nearer ranks. Moloch let it pass and pressed on.

"What can be worse," he said, "than to dwell here, driven out from bliss, condemned in this abhorred deep to endless woe? Whatever doom awaits us, worse than this cannot be found. To sit in darkness here, hatching vain empires — that is worse than any war could make us. Let us instead ascend, and meet what waits above us with the same fury we brought down here with us. If we fall again, we fall no further than we have already fallen. If we do not — the throne itself is not beyond a strength that has already shaken it once."

He looked at Satan directly when he said it, and did not lower his eyes.

"I do not ask for guile. I ask for the field again, on our own terms this time, with everything we have left. Better to perish fighting than to sit here composing peace we do not want with a peace no one has offered us."

He sat. The murmur that followed him was louder than the one before, though it came mostly from those who had not yet reckoned, in any real way, what "the field again" had already cost them once.

[Beat 9]
Belial rose next. He rose the way a courtier rises — unhurried, composed, a fairer bearing than any spirit in the hall save Satan himself, and every word he spoke sounded, on first hearing, wiser than it was.

"I am not for open war," he said, "and I say so, my lords, not from any fear of it, but from what I judge open war would actually buy us. We have already met that argument, and it defeated us. What was then found weak in us is now not stronger, and what was then found overpowering in Him is now not weaker. War, a second time, wins us nothing but a second defeat, more swiftly arrived at."

He let that settle before he continued.

"Nor am I for the assault Beelzebub is said to be considering, whatever it is — any scheme reckless enough to risk what small standing we have managed to build here. Consider instead what we would lose by losing it. This place is dark, and hard, and beneath what we once had. But we are not chained. We are not, at this hour, being actively tormented. Is that not, in itself, already an improvement on nine days ago?"

Some among the host nodded at this, the particular nodding of men glad to be told that doing nothing is, in fact, the wise course.

"What if the wrath we fled should wake again," Belial said, "roused by our own restlessness, and blow these fires to sevenfold rage, and plunge us deeper than this? I would not risk that for the sake of action merely to feel we had acted. Our torments, in time, may themselves become familiar — may cease to be torments at all, and become simply this place, as any place becomes simply itself to those who have lived in it long enough. To suffer, as to act, requires the same strength, and where the wisdom of acting is in doubt, I say we husband that strength instead of spending it."

He sat, and did not look at Satan when he did.

Satan watched him sit and understood exactly what had just happened: an argument for despair, dressed carefully enough in the language of prudence that half the hall would mistake it for wisdom.

[Beat 10]
Mammon rose after him, and where Belial had spoken like a courtier, Mammon spoke like a man who had already decided the matter and was only waiting for the room to agree with him.

"Either we regain our old place in Heaven," he said, "or we fight forever to unseat a power that cannot be unseated, or we make our own terms in a place He does not occupy. The first is a hope I will not insult this council by entertaining. The second, Moloch has already proposed, and I need not repeat why it failed once and will fail again. That leaves the third, and I confess it is the one that suits me best."

He looked around the hall, at the black pillars, at the gold veining the dark stone.

"Suppose we sought Him out again, and suppose — against every reasonable expectation — He forgave it, and let us stand once more in that bright court. What then? We would stand there singing forced hallelujahs to a throne we envy, our knee bent not from love but from necessity. I would rather have this. Hard liberty, in a hard country, than that soft and easy yoke, however bright the hall it's worn in."

Something in the host stirred at that — not agreement exactly, but the recognition of an argument that had weight to it.

"And this country is not nothing," Mammon went on. "I have already found gold in it. Where there is ore, there is a court to be built from it. Where there is dark, there is a use for whatever light we choose to make ourselves. Let us not, then, chase by force what cannot be taken, nor beg for a grace no one intends to offer. Let us instead find our own good, out of our own hands, and live to ourselves, in this place we did not ask for but may yet make entirely our own."

He sat to a full murmur of agreement this time — the loudest yet, and Satan understood why. Mammon's argument cost the host nothing they had not already accepted losing. It was the easiest counsel in the room to follow, which was precisely its danger.

[Beat 11]
Beelzebub rose last, and the hall quieted for him the way it had not quieted for the others — because everyone present understood that whatever he said next, he said with Satan's own authority behind it.

"My lords," he said, "you speak of peace here, as though peace were a thing this place could actually offer us. Consider what you are calling peace. Free, and to none accountable — preferring hard liberty before the easy yoke of servile pomp. That much I grant Mammon. But will He, the sovereign of Heaven, so easily leave us to enjoy it? Will this abyss stay ours, unwatched, unclaimed, simply because we have decided to want nothing else? I think not. I think He will find some other use for us yet, some further punishment beyond the one we already carry — and I think we would do better to choose our own next move than to wait for Him to choose it for us."

He paused, and let the hall feel the weight of what he was about to say before he said it.

"There is a rumor in Heaven — old, half believed even there — of another world to come. A new race, to be made in time, favored above us, formed perhaps of some other substance than our own, but set nonetheless in a place we might, with sufficient patience, actually reach. If this is true, and I believe it is, here then is our real advantage. Not the field. Not this country we are already resigned to keeping. But that new place, and whatever lives there — for to waste that place, to lead its race astray, or to win it wholly to our side, would trouble Him more, and cost us less, than any war we could raise against His own gates a second time."

The hall had gone entirely still.

"Seek we then," Beelzebub said, "some better place, by fraud or guile, than by our own force, which failed us already. This may find some easier enterprise than open war — to waste his whole creation, or possess it entire, and turn his own new joy to endless woe. Vengeance, of a kind that does not require us to storm a wall twice."

He looked to Satan, and did not need to say what every voice in the hall already understood: that this had been Satan's plan from the first night on the burning lake, spoken now through a second mouth so the whole council could arrive at it together, rather than simply being told.

"But who," Beelzebub said, "shall we send to find this new world, and learn what waits there? The way is long, and hard, and guarded on every side we know of. Who among us is equal to that journey?"

[Beat 12]
No one answered.

The silence went on long enough that Satan felt it change in quality — from a hall waiting to hear a volunteer, to a hall beginning to understand that none would come. He watched them look at each other, each waiting for someone braver, or more desperate, or simply less attached to whatever remained of his own safety, to be the one who spoke first.

None did.

Satan stood.

"I should ill become this throne, my lords," he said, "and this rule you have given me, adorned with whatever honor still clings to it, if any danger — however great, however far — could turn me from attempting what the safety of us all requires. This enterprise is mine by right, since none of you will claim it, and I would not have it said afterward that your prince asked of you what he was unwilling to do himself."

He came down from the high seat as he spoke, so that for a moment he stood level with the rest of them.

"I go alone. Whatever guards that road, whatever waits at the end of it, I would rather meet it myself than send another in my place and wait here for word of whether he lived. This much a prince owes the ones who followed him this far."

The hall rose then, all at once, in a single motion — not the low murmur that had answered Moloch or Mammon, but a full acclamation, thousands of voices at once, and beneath the sound of it Satan understood, with a clarity that had nothing warm in it, exactly what had just happened: he had turned a hall full of doubtful hearts into a court united behind a single purpose, and he had done it by being the only one among them willing to risk what the purpose actually cost.

They came forward to honor him before he left — not with words only, but with the small rites an army makes for a commander departing into danger it cannot follow him into. He accepted it plainly, without lingering in it, because a war does not pause for its own ceremonies longer than it must.

Then he turned from them, and walked toward the gate, alone, exactly as he had said he would.

[Beat 13]
The gate of Hell stood where he had first felt it, nine days before — a wall he could touch now as well as sense. Two shapes kept it.

One was a woman to the waist, and something else below it: a great serpent's coil, armed about the middle with the barking heads of hounds that turned and cried without ceasing, as though they could not stop being born. The other had no shape Satan's eye could hold steady — vast, formless, crowned if it wore a crown, and carrying in what might have been a hand a dart that looked capable of ending a world.

It was the formless one that moved. It came on fast, with horrid strides, and Hell trembled where it stepped.

Satan wondered what it might be. He did not fear it. Outside of God and His Son, he had never yet valued or avoided any created thing, and he was not going to begin at a door he intended to walk through. He looked at it with plain disdain, and spoke first.

"Whence, and what art thou, execrable shape, that dar'st, though grim and terrible, advance thy miscreated front athwart my way to yonder gates? Through them I mean to pass — that be assured — without leave asked of thee. Retire, or taste thy folly, and learn by proof, Hell-born, not to contend with spirits of Heaven."

The thing answered him full of wrath.

"Art thou that traitor angel? Art thou he who first broke peace in Heaven, and faith unbroken until then, and in proud rebellious arms drew after him the third part of Heaven's sons, conjured against the Highest — for which both thou and they, outcast from God, are condemned here to waste eternal days in woe and pain?

"And dost thou reckon thyself among the spirits of Heaven, Hell-doomed, and breathe defiance here, and scorn, where I reign king — and, to enrage thee more, thy king and lord? Back to thy punishment, false fugitive, and add wings to thy speed, lest I pursue thy lingering with a whip of scorpions, or with one stroke of this dart seize thee with a strange horror, and pangs never felt before."

So spoke the grisly terror, and in the speaking and the threatening grew tenfold, and Satan, unterrified, set himself to meet it.

Great deeds would have been done there, that all Hell would have rung with afterward. They were not, because the snaky sorceress who sat fast by Hell-gate and kept the fatal key rose up and rushed between the two of them with a hideous outcry.

"O father — what does your hand intend against your only son? What fury, O son, possesses you, to bend that mortal dart against your father's head? And do you know for whom? For him who sits above and laughs, the whole while, at you — ordained his drudge, to execute whatever his wrath, which he calls justice, bids. His wrath, which one day will destroy the pair of you."

At her words the hellish pest forbore. Satan lowered his own hand, and turned to her.

"So strange an outcry, and words so strange to interpose, that my hand — prevented — spares to tell you yet by deeds what it intended. First I will know from you what thing you are, thus double-formed, and why, meeting me first in this infernal vale, you call me father, and call that phantasm my son. I do not know you. Nor have I ever, until now, seen a sight more detestable than him and you."

The portress of Hell-gate answered him.

"Have you forgotten me, then? And do I seem now so foul in your eye — I, once deemed so fair in Heaven? At the assembly, in the sight of all the Seraphim combined with you in bold conspiracy against Heaven's King, a sudden miserable pain surprised you. Your eyes went dim, and swam dizzily in darkness, and your head threw out flames thick and fast, until it opened wide on the left side — and out of your head I sprang, a goddess armed, likest to you in shape and countenance, and shining then heavenly fair.

"Amazement seized the whole host of Heaven. They recoiled from me, afraid at first, and called me Sin, and took me for a sign — though few of them understood how true a sign I was."

Satan looked at her, and something in the shape of her face, beneath the serpent coil, beneath the barking hounds, was unmistakably his own.

"You loved me then," Sin said, "as you would love your own reflection, and got on me, in that same hour, this son." She gestured at the formless shape beside her. "Death. He grew fast, and grew terrible, and when he was grown he pursued me, his own mother, and forced on me what he had no right to force; and from that forcing came the hounds you hear crying now, hourly born and hourly returning into the womb that bore them, gnawing at me from within as their own birthright. This is what became of the hour you first chose to defy Heaven. This is what your defiance actually made, waiting here at the one gate out of the place it doomed you to."

Satan stood a long moment with what she had told him.

"I did not know," he said finally, "what that hour cost, beyond what it cost me. But I know it now, and I do not intend to spend the rest of it standing at this gate discussing what is already done. You hold the key. I mean to use it."

"For what," Sin said.

"For every one of them," Satan said, nodding back toward the host still gathered at Pandemonium's steps. "Passage, out of this place, toward a country I mean to make ours by other means than we tried the first time. In exchange, I offer you what no one in Heaven ever thought to offer either of you: a name, and a purpose, and a place at my side rather than at this gate alone, guarding a door no one before now has ever asked you to open."

Sin looked at Death, and Death, in whatever way a shape without a face can be said to look at anything, looked back.

"You are my father," Sin said. "And this is my son. Whatever debt Heaven owes us, it is you who created the debt, in the hour you first turned against your King. I would be a fool to refuse the one being in this creation who actually knows what we are for."

She raised the key.

The gate of Hell, which had not moved in nine days, began, slowly, grinding on hinges that had never once been used, to open.

Beyond it lay nothing Satan could yet name — a dark that was not the dark of Hell, formless and unbounded, without floor or ceiling or any law he recognized. He looked at it a long moment, and then stepped through, into the roaring, unmade country between what he had lost and what he meant to take.

[Beat 14]
In Heaven, seated high above all height, God looked down through the dark and saw what moved in it.

He turned to the Son, who sat at His right hand, and there was nothing hidden in what He said next, because in that court nothing needed to be.

"Only begotten Son," He said, "do you see what rage carries our adversary now? No bounds could hold him. Not the bars of Hell, not the chains heaped on him there, not even the abyss itself, wide as it is, could keep him from this flight. He means to try that world I built, which houses Man, whom I made after My own image, to rule the rest, and worship none but Me. He will find him, and he will tempt him, and — I say this now, so that it is said, and not merely foreseen — Man will listen. He will break the one command I gave him, the single pledge of his obedience. He will fall."

Silence held the court a moment.

"I made him just, and right, and able enough to have stood, though also free to fall," God said. "Such I created every spirit, free to stand or free to fall. Not because I willed the fall — I willed only the freedom, and freedom without the possibility of falling is no freedom at all, only a longer obedience with a kinder name. Whose fault is it, then, but his own, that he will fall? I gave him what was necessary to remain upright. What he does with it is his to answer for, not Mine."

The Son listened, and did not yet speak.

"Man will fall," God said again, plainly, because a thing said plainly cannot later be pretended unclear. "And justice will require what justice always requires: that the debt be paid. Death for the breaking of the pledge, as it was told him from the first. Unless some other, able and willing both, should offer to pay that rigid satisfaction in his place — man is lost, and lost forever, and all My work in making him will have been work spent on nothing."

He looked out over the assembled host of Heaven, every rank of them present, and asked the question plainly, because He already knew what silence would answer if no one came forward.

"Say, Heavenly Powers — where shall we find such love? Which of you will be mortal, to redeem Man's mortal crime, and just, the unjust to save? Dwells in all Heaven charity so dear?"

No one answered.

[Beat 15]
The silence went on long enough that it became its own answer — the whole host of Heaven standing mute, and Man, though he did not yet know it, already as good as lost for want of anyone willing to pay what his fall would cost.

Then the Son rose.

"Father," He said, "Your word is given: Man shall find grace, and shall not, unaided, fall past every hope of it. But grace alone cannot stand against justice unpaid. Someone must answer for what he owes, or he is lost regardless of how much You wish otherwise."

He looked at His Father, and there was nothing uncertain in what He said next.

"Behold me, then. Me for him — life for life, I offer. On Me let Your anger fall. Account Me man. I, for his sake, will leave Your bosom, and freely give up what I possess by right, to another's fault, most innocent of it Myself. I will suffer death, and lose, for a time, the light of Your presence, though it costs Me more than any other loss could. Yet I will not despair of it, because I know I do not go down into that dark to remain there. You will not leave My soul among the dead, nor let Your holy one see final corruption."

He said it without ornament, the way a thing is said by someone who has already decided it and does not need to persuade himself further.

"I shall rise victorious, and subdue My vanquisher — death itself — and lead him captive through the deep, a chained and beaten thing, before all who thought him triumphant. I shall rise, and with Me raise all who were joined to Man's fall, so that his fall becomes not simply loss, but the door through which something greater than what was lost first enters the world."

God looked at His Son a long moment.

"You have found grace before Me," He said, "in this alone — that You so willingly, and out of Your own love, and not Mine command, offer Yourself to redeem the very race that ruined itself against My single law. Man shall not, therefore, be entirely lost, but saved, whoever comes to seek it — not by his own merit, which he will have thrown away, but by the merit of one who did not need to spend His own to save it, and did."

The court had begun, quietly, to understand what had just been decided in front of them.

"But this must wait its hour," God said. "Man has not fallen yet. Let the enemy attempt his work — I do not intend to stop him. What Man does with the choice before him, he does freely, and it must be seen to be freely done, or nothing that follows from it means what it should."

[Beat 16]
The country beyond Hell's gate had no floor.

Satan understood this within the first few strides of it, the way a swimmer understands the water has no bottom before he ever tries to touch one. This was the realm of Chaos and ancient Night — a formless deep, without up or down, without any law he could find to hold to, where the four elements themselves warred against each other without ever settling into a shape. He could not walk it, exactly. He could not fly it, exactly either. He went forward the only way the place allowed: with hands, with wings, with whatever part of him could gain the smallest purchase against the shapeless dark, swimming where it would bear him, sinking where it wouldn't, wading and creeping and flying by turns, as the substance of the place allowed or refused him passage.

At length he came to where the powers of that country kept their court — Chaos on his throne, and beside him ancient Night, older than any name given to darkness since, and around them the confused retinue of a realm whose only business is having no order in it.

Satan turned to face them boldly.

"Powers and spirits of this nethermost abyss," he said. "Chaos, and ancient Night. I come no spy, with any purpose to explore or to disturb the secrets of your realm. I wander this darksome desert under constraint. My way lies through your spacious empire, up toward light — alone, without a guide, and half lost — and I ask only this: which is the readiest path to where your gloomy bounds border on Heaven? Or if there is some other place, lately won out of your dominion, which the Ethereal King now possesses — then that is where I am bound, and it is to arrive there that I am travelling this profound."

He let the request stand a moment, and then made it worth answering.

"Direct my course. Directed, it brings no mean recompense to you. If I can reduce that lost region — all usurpation expelled from it — back to her original darkness and your sway, and set up the standard of ancient Night there once again, then that is what I intend by this journey. Yours be the advantage, all of it. Mine the revenge."

The old Anarch answered him with faltering speech and a face that would not compose itself into any single expression.

"I know you, stranger. I know who you are — that mighty leading angel who lately made head against Heaven's King, and was overthrown for it. I saw. I heard. Such a numerous host did not flee in silence through my frightened deep: ruin upon ruin, rout on rout, confusion worse confounded — and Heaven-gates pouring out her victorious bands by the million, in pursuit.

"I keep residence here on my frontiers, and if everything I have will serve to defend the little that is left me, it is barely enough. I am encroached upon continually. Our own internal broils weaken the sceptre of Night, and then come the annexations. First Hell — your dungeon — stretching far and wide beneath me. Now, lately, Heaven and Earth: another world, hung over my realm, linked by a golden chain to that side of Heaven your legions fell from."

Something like grievance moved through the whole disordered court at that.

"If that is the way you walk, you have not far to go," Chaos said. "So much the nearer, the danger. Go, and speed. Havoc, and spoil, and ruin, are my gain."

Satan did not thank him. There was nothing in the exchange that wanted thanking; each of them had simply recognized in the other a party whose interests, for the length of one journey, pointed the same direction.

He pressed on the way he had been shown.

It was long, and it was hard, and there was no map to it beyond the vague pull of purpose. But at last, ahead of him in the dark, something changed. A point of light, faint at first, no larger than the smallest star, hanging where nothing else in this shapeless country held still.

He fixed his eyes on it and did not look away again until he reached it.

It was the World — hanging, as he drew nearer, from that same golden chain let down out of Heaven's own battlements, small and whole and complete against the vast dark he had just crossed, a single closed sphere holding, somewhere inside it, everything God had built to replace the praise His court no longer gave Him.

Satan looked at it a long moment.

Then he began his descent.

[Beat 17]
Before he reached the World itself, Satan understood he could not simply arrive as what he was. A fallen prince, scorched and marked by nine days in the burning lake, would draw every eye and every question the moment he set foot on new ground. He needed a shape that asked nothing, that any watcher would let pass without a second look.

He found one on the sun itself, where he came first to get his bearings — a young cherub, bright and unremarkable, one of the countless lesser spirits God had set to tend His creation. Satan took that shape entirely: the face, the bearing, the particular unhurried grace of a spirit with nothing to hide.

Uriel stood there, Regent of that bright sphere, and did not doubt him for a moment.

"I have come far," Satan said, in the cherub's own soft voice, "with a desire long held to see, and worship near at hand, the works of Him whose glory fills the universe. I have heard of this new world He has made — Man, formed to rule it, and this fair light you tend. I would see it for myself, if you could point the way, and perhaps see the Maker's own hand more clearly in the seeing."

It was, Satan understood even as he said it, exactly the kind of request that Heaven's own courtesy had no defense against — a stranger's stated wish to worship more fully, offered by a face built to invite nothing but trust.

"Fair angel," Uriel said, "your desire honors Him more than it burdens me to answer it. To ask, and to admire the works of God, is no idle curiosity, but the very use for which He made a mind capable of asking at all."

Uriel pointed the way — down through the bright dark toward a small, green world Satan could now see whole for the first time, and toward one garden on it, walled and shining, where he said Man and his mate now dwelt in the innocence God had given them.

Satan thanked him, and turned, and went the way he had been shown.

Uriel did not follow him, and did not doubt him even once as he went — because, as even Heaven's own courts had learned in their long history, hypocrisy is the one evil that walks invisible to every eye but God's own. Neither man nor angel, however watchful, can see through a face built carefully enough to hide what stands behind it. Satan wore that face now, and wore it perfectly, and went down toward the garden with nothing in his bearing that any watcher, however honest, could have named as danger.

[Beat 18]
Eden lay below him, green and whole, exactly as Uriel had promised.

Satan came down to its edge and stopped there, on the outer wall of it, before he went any further. He looked at the sun overhead — the same sun he had worn a borrowed face upon, hours before — and something in him, unbidden, spoke to it before he could stop the speaking.

"To thee I call," he said, "but with no friendly voice, and add thy name, O Sun, to tell thee how I hate thy beams, that bring to my remembrance from what state I fell."

He had not meant to say that. He said it anyway, because it was true, and because there was no one here yet to hear him say it.

"I once stood in that full blaze of light, above every rival, till pride and worse ambition threw me down, warring against Heaven's matchless King. Ah, wherefore? He deserved no such return from me, who made me what I was, in that bright degree, and gave me nothing but good will to pay him back with. What burden then was it to praise him? Yet, all this good proved ill in me, and wrought only malice — I disdained subjection, and thought one step higher would set me highest, and, in a moment, quit the debt of endless gratitude, so burdensome, still paying, still to owe."

He stood a moment with that, because it was the truest accounting he had ever given himself, and truest accountings are not comfortable.

"But say I could repent, and could obtain, by act of grace, my former state — how soon would height recall high thoughts, and my own recanting vows sound worse in my own ears than my revolt ever did. For never can true reconcilement grow where wounds of deadly hate have pierced so deep. And so, farewell hope; and with hope, farewell fear; farewell remorse. All good to me is lost. Evil, be thou my good."

He looked down at Eden a last moment before he began his descent into it — at the two small figures already visible, moving between the trees, entirely unaware that anything above them had just finished deciding what it meant to do with them.

"By thee at least divided empire with Heaven's King I hold," he said, quietly, to no one, "and, more than half, perhaps, will reign — as Man ere long, and this new world, shall know."

Then he went down the wall, and into the garden, because there was nothing left in that speech to say twice.

[Beat 19]
He found them at evening, in the shade of a tree neither of them had any reason to fear.

Satan had taken the shape of a lion first, then a tiger, testing what forms this new world's creatures allowed him, before settling low in the brush to simply watch. Adam and Eve sat together, unclothed and untroubled by it, and the sight of them struck him harder than he had expected — not their bodies, but the ease between them, the plain unguarded trust of two beings who had never once needed to watch what they said to each other.

Adam was speaking.

"Sole partner, and sole part, of all these joys," he said, "you have not forgotten what I told you this morning, of the one tree in this whole garden that God has set apart from every other. All else here is ours freely — but of the Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil, He has said only this: in the day you eat of it, you die. That is the one condition of the freedom He has given us. A single mark of our obedience, small enough beside everything we are permitted, and yet the whole of what He asks in return."

Eve answered him gladly, without any shadow in it. "O thou for whom, and from whom, I was formed — what you say I take gladly, and without any burden, for the garden gives us more than any two hearts could ask, and to withhold ourselves from one tree, among so many, costs us nothing at all."

Satan, low in the brush, heard the whole of it, and understood immediately what he had just been given: not merely their happiness, but the precise shape of the one door left unlocked in it.

*One tree,* he thought. *One condition, and death the penalty for breaking it. That is not freedom. That is a leash with a single length of slack in it, and they call the slack itself the gift.*

He watched them a while longer, and did not yet move, because there was more to learn before there was anything to do.

[Beat 20]
He watched them a long while after that, and something moved in him that he did not immediately have a name for.

They were happy. Not the brittle happiness of a court that has learned to perform contentment for whoever outranks it, but something plainer and stranger — two creatures who did not yet know that happiness could be taken from them, and so simply had it, the way you have air, without guarding it.

*Into this room of bliss,* he thought, *creatures of another kind than ours have been raised — not our equals in the old ranking, but happier, perhaps, for having so much less to lose than we once had.*

For a moment, watching them, he felt something that was not quite pity and not quite grief, but stood near enough to both that a lesser being might have mistaken it for either.

He did not indulge it.

*I could love them,* he thought, *if love were a thing still available to me. It is not. Whatever I once was capable of, I spent it, all of it, on a single hour of pride, and there is no getting it back by wishing near these two long enough. What I can have instead of love is this: company. If I must burn, I would rather not burn alone. If I am shut out of the only happiness I once knew, I find I have very little patience left for watching two strangers keep theirs, undisturbed, in a garden I was never offered.*

He let that settle, because it was, he understood, the truer reason beneath the reason he would give the court when he returned to it.

*Public reason, and revenge enlarged by winning this whole new world from Him, compels me to do now what, whole and un-fallen, I would have called unthinkable. Convert them, or destroy them — either serves. Detestable, perhaps. But necessary, and I have already spent the part of myself that once flinched at necessary things.*

He rose from the brush, and began to circle the garden's edge, no longer merely watching, but hunting now for the one weakness in it he had already been shown: two hearts, one tree, and a single sentence of death standing between them and whatever he could persuade them to want more than obedience.

[Beat 21]
That night, while Adam and Eve slept in their bower, Satan took the shape of a toad and crept close to Eve's ear, meaning to work on her sleeping mind whatever suggestion he could plant there before morning — a first, quiet attempt at the thing he had resolved on.

He did not get far with it.

Gabriel had set a watch on the garden after dusk, as he always did, and two of that watch — Ithuriel and Zephon, sharp-eyed and thorough — were making their round of the bower when Ithuriel's light fell across a shape that had no business crouched where it crouched.

"Look," he said to Zephon, low. "There, close by the ear of that sleeping woman. No natural toad sits so near a face, nor so still."

He touched it with the tip of his spear — a light touch, no more force in it than checking a door to see if it is locked — and the touch itself was enough, because no falsehood can hold its borrowed shape once celestial temper touches it. It returns, by force, to whatever it actually is.

The toad was gone. In its place, crouched in the same low posture, was Satan — his own true shape again, caught mid-crouch, discovered and plainly startled by it, the way anyone is startled who believed themselves unseen and finds, in the same instant, that they were watched the entire time.

He came up onto his feet at once, already reaching for whatever bearing he could recover.

"Which of the prime in order and in office," Ithuriel said, unmoved by the size or the sudden brightness of what had just unfolded in front of him, "have you left behind in Heaven, that you have come here, alone, to trouble the sleep of creatures who have done you no injury, and hold no quarrel with you?"

"Not to know me," Satan said, drawing himself up to the full height his true shape allowed, "argues yourselves unknown, the lowest of your throng — or, if you did know me once, you have forgotten faster than nine days should allow."

"Nor doth this behold thee like thyself," Zephon answered, entirely unimpressed by the posture. "Thy face and bearing carried brightness once. Now it carries only the look of something faded, and worse for the fading. But come — this is no place for a private argument. Gabriel will hear what you have to answer for, and you will answer it to him, not to us."

Satan looked at the two of them a long moment, weighing whether their spears were worth testing.

He decided, in the end, that they were not — not yet, not here, not before he had learned what force stood behind them. So he went where they led him, toward whatever waited at the garden's gate.

[Beat 22]
Gabriel was waiting at the gate when they brought him.

"Satan," he said. "We knew you would attempt something of this kind, but not so soon, and not with so little care taken to hide it. Why come alone, and by stealth, past watchers set exactly to catch you at it?"

"Because the work wanted one, not many."

"Did it." Gabriel looked at him without any heat at all, which was somehow worse than heat. "Then why did not all Hell break loose and come with you? Is pain less pain to them — less worth fleeing? Or are you less hardy to endure it than they are? Courageous chief. The first in flight from pain. Had you given your deserted host that reason for leaving, I do not think you would have come here a sole fugitive."

Satan's face did not change, but something behind it did.

"Not that I endure less, or shrink from pain, insulting angel," he said. "You know well enough that I stood your fiercest — when the thunder came volleying to your aid in that battle, and seconded a spear that was not otherwise much dreaded.

"But your words go at random, as they always did, and they argue how little you have learned from hard trials and bad outcomes about what a leader actually owes. He does not hazard everything he has left on a road no one has tried yet. So I went first. I alone undertook to wing that desolate abyss and spy out this new-created world — of which, I promise you, Hell's rumour is not silent — in the hope of finding a better abode for my afflicted powers, here on this earth or in the air above it. Though we may yet be put to it once more for possession, and see what you and your gay legions dare against us. Whose easier business, after all, is to serve their Lord high up in Heaven, hymning his throne, and keeping their practised distances — to cringe, not fight."

"Call it what serves you best," Gabriel said. "I call it what it looks like from here. But we need not settle it in words. Depart, or answer for it now, with everything you have left to answer with."

Satan measured him a long moment, and measured, too, the ranks of the watch standing behind him, spears already lowered.

"Then let us settle it as it should be settled," he said, and reached for whatever shape a fight would take.

Above them both, without warning, the sky itself answered before either could move — a sign hung out across the dark, two golden scales weighing something neither of them had asked to be weighed: what would come of this meeting, if it came to blows. Satan looked up and saw his own scale rise, light, weightless, already beaten before a single blow had been struck.

He knew that sign. He had seen its like before, on the day the war above had first turned against him.

He looked at Gabriel a last moment, and said nothing further, because there was nothing further worth saying to a scale that had already answered the question for both of them.

Then he turned, and fled into the dark, and the night went with him, and the garden, behind him, settled back into whatever peace it had known before he first climbed its wall.

[Beat 23]
Eve woke before dawn, and Adam found her troubled in a way he had never seen her troubled before.

"Best image of myself, and dearer half," he said, "what disturbs you, that you wake with such a look? You are not accustomed to this hour finding you anything but glad."

"I had a dream," she said, "and I would tell it to you, though it frightens me still to remember it. I dreamed a voice called me by night — your voice, or one so like it I did not doubt it — and led me to the Tree of Knowledge, the one tree we are forbidden. And there, beside it, stood a shape not unlike one of the angels we have seen pass overhead, and he said the tree's fruit was not death but wisdom, and that its forbidding was only a test of whether we would dare to be wise. And he plucked it, and ate, and pressed me to eat also — and in the dream, I ate, and felt myself lifted at once above the earth, up among the clouds, seeing the whole world spread beneath me. And then I woke, and found myself only here, in our own bower, and I do not know what to make of it."

Adam listened to the whole of it without alarm, though something in him had gone quiet and attentive in a way it had not been a moment before.

"This is unlike you, Eve," he said, "to wander in a dream from that pure, cheerful innocence which you carry waking. Yet evil may come and go through the mind of any creature, even one made as good as you were made, and leave no blame behind it, so long as the waking will never approved what the sleeping mind only witnessed. Take comfort in that, at least — that what troubled you was seen, not chosen."

Eve took what comfort she could from it, though the shape of that voice in the dream stayed with her longer than she said aloud.

Neither of them yet knew that the dream had not, in fact, come from nowhere — that a real voice had truly spoken those words at her ear the night before, and that whatever had planted it there had only been driven off, not defeated, and had not yet finished what it meant to attempt.

[Beat 24]
In Heaven, God had seen the whole of it — the toad crouched at Eve's ear, the spear's touch, the confrontation at the gate, the scales, the flight into the dark. He called Raphael to him, the angel Adam and Eve would come to think of as the most sociable of Heaven's messengers, easy in conversation, unhurried in his friendliness.

"Go down," God said, "and converse with Adam, in what bower or shade you find him at the heat of day, and warn him, as a friend to a friend, that his enemy is near, and means him harm. Tell him he was made sufficient to stand, though free to fall, and that this freedom is the whole point of what he is — but let him know, too, that the danger is real, so that if he falls, it is by his own choice, made with eyes open, and not through any ignorance I could have prevented and did not."

Raphael went down gladly, because he had always found reason enough to love the errand of warning a thing worth loving before it came to harm. He came to Eden in the full light of noon, and Adam saw him while he was still some way off — a shape descending with six wings, the two foremost shading his face, the middle pair wrapped about him like a mantle, the last pair spread for flight, all of it starred and colored like a sky at evening, and yet plainly, unmistakably, coming toward the garden with purpose.

Adam called to Eve.

"A visitor comes toward us," he said, "unlike any we have seen pass overhead before — not merely passing, but descending, and coming, I think, this way entirely on purpose. Let us prepare what welcome we can. Whatever this messenger brings, it seems to me it comes to be received, not merely watched."

[Beat 25]
Eve set before their guest what the garden gave freely — fruit gathered from a dozen trees, laid out with a care that owed nothing to necessity and everything to welcome. Raphael sat with them beneath the shade and ate what was set before him, plainly and with real appetite, and Adam, watching, found himself surprised that a spirit should have any need of food at all.

"You wonder," Raphael said, catching the look, "that a spirit should eat as you eat. But all that lives requires some fuel to sustain it, however the substance of that fuel differs, and I take as much honest pleasure in this meal as you do, whatever difference lies beneath the pleasure. Do not measure spirit and body as though one were merely a lesser copy of the other. They are simply different instruments, tuned to different music, and both, in their own register, can be tuned well."

They spoke long after the meal was cleared, of the garden, of the stars, of the ranked orders of Heaven's own household — and it was in the midst of this easier talk that Raphael said, plainly, the thing he had actually come down to say.

"Adam, I will not soften this beyond what is useful to you. You have an enemy, who was once no enemy at all, but a spirit of the first rank in Heaven, and who envies you now the very happiness you enjoy in this garden, precisely because he can no longer enjoy anything like it himself. He has already come here once, since his fall, and been driven off before he could finish what he intended. He will come again. I am sent to tell you this now, so that whatever comes of it, you meet it with open eyes rather than none."

Adam sat with that a long moment.

"An enemy," he said. "I confess I had no notion any such thing existed, before you spoke it. How does malice come to exist at all, in a creation You have told me God made entirely good? If you would tell it to me, Raphael — how did this war between good and its opposite first begin?"

Raphael looked at him, and at Eve beside him, listening with the same stillness, and understood that this, too, was part of what he had been sent down to give them — not merely the warning, but the reason behind it, so that the danger, when it came, would not simply be a shape in the dark, but a story they already understood the shape of.

"Sit, then," Raphael said, "and I will tell you how it began — high up, before either of you existed, in the bright country neither of you has ever seen."

[Beat 26]
"There came a day," Raphael said, "when God called the whole host of Heaven together, every order, every rank, and declared to us all: 'This day I have begotten whom I now proclaim my only Son, and anointed him, whom you now behold at my right hand. Your head I appoint him, and under his reign you shall abide, united as one soul, forever, unless you choose disunion. To him every knee in Heaven shall bow, and every tongue confess him Lord.'

"And every rank obeyed it, that first day, gladly — every rank but one.

"He who had been, until that hour, among the very first of us in brightness and in standing, could not bear to see another set above him, not even the Son of God himself. He called it injury, though nothing of his own had been taken from him — only something new had been given to another, and he could not separate the two. Pride told him the second was the same as the first, and once he believed that, envy did the rest of the work pride had started.

"That night, he drew off his own legions under a pretense — that they should withdraw to prepare fit ceremony to receive the new decree, as though obedience needed rehearsal. But once removed to his own quarter of Heaven, in his own high seat, he called his second to him, the one you have perhaps heard named Beelzebub, and spoke to him plainly what he actually intended.

"'Will you submit your neck,' he said, 'to this new law, this new decree, sung and proclaimed as if we owed it more than we owe the sun for rising? I, for one, do not intend to. If we are self-begot, self-raised, by our own quickening power — and I say we are — then we owe no knee to a newly-declared equal, whatever title is hung on him. Let those bow who feel the debt. I do not feel it, and I do not intend to pretend that I do.'

"Beelzebub answered him carefully — not outright refusal, but questions, testing how far this was already decided rather than merely spoken. And by the time the questions were finished, it was decided. Satan drew a third part of Heaven's own host to his side that same night, on the strength of an argument that flattered every one of them into believing their own standing had been insulted alongside his.

"That was the seed of it," Raphael said. "Not some ancient evil brought down from elsewhere, but pride, in one bright spirit, that could not bear to see another honored, and envy, which took that small wound and told it, over and over, that it was larger than it was — until an argument built entirely on a lie had convinced a third of Heaven to believe it was defending something real."

Adam sat with the whole of it a long while, and did not interrupt, because Raphael had not finished.

The angel had come down to tell him one thing, and had chosen to tell him everything instead — the argument, the night it was made, and the third of Heaven that believed it. What remained was what came of the believing.

[Beat 27]
"One among us did not go with him," Raphael said, "though he had stood in that same council and heard the same argument. Abdiel — a lesser name than Satan's, but I tell you plainly, the braver one that night. He alone stood up in that gathered host and called the argument what it was: a lie dressed carefully enough that a third of Heaven had mistaken it for grievance. He was shouted down for it. He left that gathering alone, the one loyal voice in a hall that had already decided against him, and walked back through the night to the side he still believed was right, not knowing whether he would be welcomed or simply disbelieved.

"He was welcomed. God himself praised him before the whole remaining host — 'Servant of God, well done,' he said, 'well hast thou fought the better fight, who single hast maintained, against revolted multitudes, the cause of truth' — and Michael, who commanded Heaven's own armies, set Abdiel in the front rank of the host that would march out to meet the rebellion the very next day.

"They met on the great plain north of Heaven's own hill — two armies, drawn from the same host that had, until three days before, been a single family. Michael led the loyal ranks. Satan led the rebels, and for all his pride, he did not shrink from meeting Michael himself when the lines closed.

"They fought a long while, sword against sword, neither yielding, until Michael's blade found an opening and came down clean through Satan's own side — a wound no armor in either army could have stopped. Satan fell back a step, and felt, for the first time in the whole of his existence, what it was to be hurt. Angels do not die of such wounds. They are not made to end that way. But they can be made to feel, and Satan felt it fully, the pain of the blade opening him exactly as deep as Michael intended, before the wound itself began, slowly, to knit closed again."

Adam listened, and did not interrupt.

"He did not fall in the field that day," Raphael said. "But something in him had learned, for the first time, that this new war could hurt him in ways the old certainties of Heaven never had. He drew back from Michael, and the battle around them went on without either of their single duel deciding it, because a single wound, however deep, is not the same as a war won."

[Beat 28]
"The first day's battle went on long after Michael and Satan broke apart from each other," Raphael said. "And here is a thing you should understand about that war, Adam, because it is unlike any war you will ever read of among your own kind. We are spirits. We cannot be unmade the way flesh can be unmade. A wound that would kill a mortal man simply hurt us, deeply, and then began, in time, to close again, whichever side of the battle a spirit fought on.

"This made it a strange kind of war. Neither side could finish the other by force alone, because force alone cannot finish what cannot die. Legions on both sides fell wounded, and rose again, and fell again, the same ground fought over and refought as the same combatants, healed enough to stand, returned to it. Moloch fought that day the way you would expect him to — reckless, delighted almost, in the sheer collision of it. Abdiel fought near Michael's own banner, and more than once turned back a rebel captain who had thought him an easy target for having stood alone against the whole council only the night before.

"By the day's end, neither army had broken the other. Both sides drew back as the light faded — not from any truce, but simply because both had fought as far as that day's strength allowed, and needed what rest a war like this permits.

[Beat 29]
"On the second day," Raphael said, "Satan showed us something none of us had yet imagined a war could contain.

"He had spent the night's rest differently than the rest of his host. While Moloch and the others simply waited for morning, Satan had gathered his engineers to the deep hollows of Heaven's own hills, and drawn up out of the ground itself the raw substance buried there — sulfur, and nitre, and metals no one before that night had thought to combine in quite that way — and by morning he had built engines the likes of which had never stood on any field in Heaven before.

"They rolled these engines to the front of the rebel line, hidden among their ranks, and when Michael's host advanced to meet them a second time, expecting the same kind of battle as the day before, Satan gave the order, and the engines spoke.

"The sound alone staggered ranks that had stood firm against every sword the day before. Fire poured out of hollow iron mouths, and iron itself flew ahead of the fire, and Heaven's own loyal host — who had never once needed to imagine being attacked by anything but blade and spear — found themselves scattered and staggered by a weapon that owed nothing to courage or skill on either side, only to whoever had thought to build it first.

"For a moment, I confess to you, it looked as though the line might break entirely.

"It did not. Michael's own captains rallied what they could, and turned, in their own way, to weapons no less strange than Satan's — tearing up the very hills and mountains of that country, root and rock together, and hurling them down onto the rebel ranks the way a smaller creature might hurl a stone. Ground that had stood since Heaven's own founding was uprooted and thrown that day, burying engines and soldiers together beneath it, until the whole field between the two armies had become a wreckage that neither side's ancestors would have recognized as Heaven at all.

"By the day's end, the field was ruin on both sides, and no clearer answer had come of it than the day before — only a deeper wound in a war neither host had wanted, and neither, alone, seemed able to finish."

[Beat 30]
"On the third day," Raphael said, "God ended it.

"He did not send the whole host again. He sent the Son alone — riding out in the chariot of paternal deity itself, ten thousand thunders in his train, wheels within wheels turning by a spirit no army could hope to stand against. He came to the field where two days of war had already ruined the ground beyond recognition, and he did not fight the way Michael had fought, or the way the engines had fought. He simply advanced, and the whole rebel host, for all its fury, for all Moloch's rage and Satan's engines and two days of a war neither side could finish, found it could not stand before him at all.

"They broke. Not gradually — all at once, the entire host, driven back across the ruined field toward the wall of Heaven itself, and the wall, at the Son's approach, opened, because it had been built to open for exactly this purpose and no other.

"They did not stop at the opening. They went through it, and past it, and over the edge of Heaven itself, the whole ruined host of them, tumbling out into the dark beyond the wall with the Son's advance still driving them on, until there was no more Heaven beneath them at all — only the long fall, down through Chaos, toward whatever waited at the bottom of it.

"Satan went over the edge with the rest of them. Whatever pride had carried him this far did not carry him any further than the wall did. He fell like every other rebel that day, no longer a general commanding a war, only one more spirit falling through an emptiness with no bottom he could see."

[Beat 31]
"Nine days they fell," Raphael said, "through Chaos, through the same shapeless deep I imagine Satan crossed again on his way here to your garden — nine days with no ground beneath them, no wall to catch them, only the long dark falling away beneath every one of them equally, prince and common soldier both, until at last a country waited at the bottom of it to receive them. A lake, they say, that burns without consuming what burns in it. A fitting enough floor for a fall that began in envy.

"So. Measuring things in Heaven by things on Earth, at your own request, and so that you may be warned by what is past, I have revealed to you what might otherwise have stayed hidden from the human race entirely: the discord that befell, and war in Heaven among the angelic powers, and the deep fall of those who aspired too high and rebelled with Satan.

"He is the one who envies your state now. He is plotting, at this moment, how he may seduce you also from obedience — so that, bereaved of happiness along with him, you may partake his punishment, which is eternal misery. That would be the whole of his solace and his revenge: to gain you as a companion of his woe, as a spite done against the Most High.

"Do not listen to his temptations. Warn her, who is the weaker of you. Let it profit you to have heard, by so terrible an example, what disobedience earns.

"They might have stood firm. They were able to. They fell anyway."

He looked at Adam and did not soften the last of it.

"Remember that, and fear to transgress."

[Beat 32]
"You ask why this world exists at all," Raphael said, "and the answer touches you directly, Adam, so I will give it to you in the words it was given in.

"When the rebels had fallen, the Father spoke to the Son, and said this. That our envious foe had failed — he who thought all of us as rebellious as himself, and trusted, with that help, to have seized this inaccessible high strength, the seat of supreme Deity, and dispossessed us of it. He drew many into that fraud. Their place here knows them no more.

"But far the greater part kept their station. Heaven is still populous. It retains number sufficient to possess its realms, wide as they are, and to frequent this high temple with all its due ministries and solemn rites."

Adam listened, and did not yet see where this led.

"Understand that part before the rest," Raphael said, "because much depends on it. The loss was not the wound Satan believes he dealt. To lose what has lost itself is hardly to be robbed. He tallies a damage that was only fondly supposed."

"Then why make anything at all?"

"Lest his heart exalt him in the harm already done — in having dispeopled Heaven, as he reckons it. So the Father said: I can repair that detriment, if such it be to lose the self-lost. And in a moment I will create another world, and out of one man a race of men innumerable, to dwell there and not here — until, raised by degrees of merit, tried under long obedience, they open the way up hither for themselves. And Earth shall be changed to Heaven, and Heaven to Earth, one kingdom, joy and union without end."

Adam sat with that a while.

"So we are the answer to a boast," he said.

"You are the answer to a boast," Raphael said, "and that is the smallest thing that is true of you. Hear the rest of it. You were not made as a replacement for what fell — Heaven had no vacancy needing filling. You were made as a road. What was taken from this place by force is to be restored to it by merit, freely, by creatures who could refuse and do not. That is a harder and a better thing than what the rebels were, and it is why the making of you was worth a week of God's own attention."

[Beat 33]
"This is how it was done," Raphael said, "and I tell it to you the way I watched it, because I was there, and few now living can say that.

"On the first day, God spoke into the dark left over from Chaos, and said, Let there be light — and there was light, and He divided it from the darkness, and called the light Day and the darkness Night. That was the first evening, and the first morning.

"On the second day, He set a firmament between the waters, dividing what would remain above from what would gather below, and called the firmament Heaven — not the Heaven you and I know, but the sky you see over your own garden.

"On the third day, He gathered the waters beneath that sky into one place, and let dry ground appear, and called the dry ground Earth and the gathered waters Seas. And the Earth, at His word, put forth grass, and every green thing, and trees bearing fruit each after its own kind, all in that single day.

"On the fourth day, He set two great lights in the firmament — the greater to rule the day, the lesser the night — and the stars besides, to mark seasons, and days, and years, so that time itself would have something visible to be measured against.

"On the fifth day, He filled the seas with every moving creature, and set birds to fly above the earth in the open firmament, and blessed them, and told them to multiply and fill what He had made for them.

"On the sixth day, He brought forth the cattle, and the creeping things, and the beasts of the earth, each after its own kind. And last, He said, Let us make man in our own image, after our likeness, and let him have dominion over every other thing that moved. So He made you, Adam, from the dust of that same ground, and breathed life into you directly, which He had not troubled to do for anything made before you."

Raphael paused.

"That is the whole of it," he said. "Six days, and on the sixth, you."

[Beat 34]
"On the seventh day," Raphael said, "He rested — not because the work had tired Him, but because the work was finished, and a thing finished deserves to be looked at rather than added to. He blessed that day, and set it apart from the six before it, and called it holy.

"The whole host of Heaven sang that day. I sang with them. We had watched a void become a world in the space of a single week, and I do not have the words, even now, to make you understand what that singing sounded like, coming from a court that had, not long before, watched a third of itself fall silent forever."

Adam sat with the whole account a long while.

"And you tell me this," he said, "so that I understand what it cost to make me."

"I tell you this," Raphael said, "so that you understand what it would cost to lose you. God does not build lightly, Adam, and He has already shown you, in the space of one war and one week, that He is willing to risk everything on the chance that a creature given the choice will choose Him freely. You are that chance, still being decided. I would ask you to remember that, the next time something comes to your garden offering you a shorter way to become something more than what you already are."

[Beat 35]
Adam looked up at the sky a long while after Raphael finished the account of Creation, and when he spoke again, it was a question that had clearly been sitting in him since before the angel arrived.

"I have wondered at the heavens," he said, "more than perhaps I should admit. So many lights, moving in courses too vast for me to follow, all of it turning, it seems, around this one small ground I stand on. Why build so much, to serve so little? It seems a great labor spent on a small errand."

Raphael considered the question with more patience than Adam had expected.

"You ask a fair question," he said, "and I will not pretend the answer is simple, because it is not, even in Heaven. Whether the great lights move around you, or you and this whole earth move among them, is a question I am not certain has been settled even where I come from — and I would rather tell you honestly that I do not know, than hand you a certainty I have not earned."

"Then what would you have me do with the not-knowing?"

"Enjoy it," Raphael said, "without needing to solve it. God did not set you here to account for every light in the sky, only to tend what is actually in your hand. A mind that spends itself entirely on questions too large to answer has less left over for the questions it was actually given to answer. Ask what serves you. Wonder at the rest, and let the wondering be its own reward, without insisting it become certainty before you are permitted to enjoy it."

Adam took the answer for what it was — not a refusal, but an honest limit, offered without embarrassment.

[Beat 36]
"Since you have told me how I was made," Adam said, "let me tell you what it was to wake into it, though you likely watched it happen and know the shape of it better than I remember living it."

"Tell it anyway," Raphael said. "A thing lived is not the same as a thing watched, even when both are true of the same hour."

"I woke on a green bank, under warm sun, and did not know at first that I was anything at all — only that I was, suddenly, and had not been a moment before. I found I could stand, and did. I found I could name what I saw, and did that too, without being taught how — lion, and called it lion; the birds overhead, and named them as they passed. It came easily, as though the names had been waiting in me for something to attach them to.

"Then God brought every living thing before me, in pairs, and I saw that each had its own answering kind beside it, and I alone did not. I asked for this — I do not know if it was bold of me to ask, but I asked plainly, because the absence was plain to me. I said I could not be content among creatures who had what I lacked, however good the rest of this garden was to me.

"God did not rebuke me for asking. He put me into a sleep instead, and when I woke, she was there — made, He told me after, from a rib taken out of my own side while I slept. I looked at her, and understood immediately that whatever solitude I had just named to God, it was already finished."

Raphael listened to the whole of it without interrupting, and when Adam finished, said only: "You asked for exactly the thing you were missing, and were given it precisely. That is not the smallest mercy God has shown you."

[Beat 37]
Adam hesitated before he said the next part, the way a man hesitates before admitting something he has not fully admitted to himself.

"There is more," he said. "When I am with her, something in me answers to her so completely that I hardly know myself apart from it. Everything else I have been given — the garden, the naming of creatures, even this conversation with you — feels, beside her, like a lesser kind of good. I do not entirely trust a feeling that strong. It unsettles me to find so much of myself given over to one thing."

Raphael's face did not soften the way Adam might have hoped.

"Do not mistake me," he said. "You are right to love her. She is worth loving, and the two of you are, in every way that matters, equals in this garden. But hear this carefully, because it is the one caution I mean to leave you with before I go: love her for what is truly excellent in her — her mind, her judgment, the good in her that answers the good in you — and not merely for the passion she stirs in you, which is a different thing, and a less trustworthy one. Passion, left ungoverned, will tell you it is love and mean something smaller. Love that is worth the name keeps its seat in reason, not beneath it."

"And if it does not?"

"Then you have made an idol of your own feeling," Raphael said, "and idols, however sincerely loved, do not answer prayers. Judgment must go first in you, Adam, in all things — in this most of all, because this is the one place a man most wants permission to let it go last."

[Beat 38]
The sun had moved a long way across the sky by the time Raphael rose to leave, and Adam felt the leaving before it was said aloud — the particular weight of a conversation that has covered everything it came to cover.

"I have told you what you needed," Raphael said, "the war, the making of this world, the reason you exist, and the one warning I most wanted to leave with you. I will say the warning once more, plainly, because it is the whole of why I was sent."

Adam waited.

"Stand fast," Raphael said. "The enemy is near — nearer than you have any comfort in knowing, and patient in a way none of us in Heaven had reason to be patient before his fall taught him how. He cannot force you to anything. That much I promise you is true, and will remain true no matter how long he waits or how close he comes. Whatever happens in this garden after I leave it, it will happen because you and Eve chose it, freely, with everything you need to choose otherwise already given to you."

"And if we do not stand fast?"

Raphael looked at him a long moment before he answered.

"Then you will have chosen that too," he said, "and I would rather leave you with that hard truth than a comfortable lie. I go now. Remember what I have told you — not as a burden, but as the one advantage you have that he does not: you still have everything to lose, and everyone who loves you is still on the same side of the wall."

He rose into the sky the way he had come, and Adam watched him go until there was nothing left to watch, and then turned back toward the garden, toward Eve, carrying the whole of what he had just been told.

[Beat 39]
Satan came back to Eden by night, having circled the whole earth once already in the days since Gabriel drove him off, looking for the one shape that would let him finish what he had started.

He found it sleeping in a thicket near the garden's edge — a serpent, subtler in its movements than any other creature God had made, quick-eyed even in sleep. Satan had considered other shapes. A lion or a tiger would only frighten her. A bird could not speak. The serpent alone offered what he needed: a form that could approach without alarm, and a mouth he could use as his own.

He did not take the shape the way he had taken the cherub's likeness on the sun. This was closer, and worse. He found the sleeping animal's mouth, and entered it directly, folding his own intelligence into the creature's small, sleeping one, the way a man might force himself into a house too small to hold him and simply make it hold him anyway. The serpent did not wake. When it did, sometime before dawn, it was no longer entirely itself.

Satan felt the wrongness of it immediately — the cramped, close, animal shape of the thing he was wearing, so far beneath what he had once been that even Hell had not degraded him this completely. He noted the degradation and set it aside, the way a soldier sets aside the cost of a maneuver once the maneuver is already underway.

He had one purpose left, and this shape, however low, was the only one that could carry him to it.

[Beat 40]
That same morning, Eve turned to Adam over the day's first work and said what had apparently been sitting in her since the day before.

"The garden grows faster than the two of us working side by side can keep pace with," she said. "If we divided the labor — you to one part, I to another — we would finish twice the work by evening, instead of talking through half of it while our hands do less than they could."

Adam did not answer at once. Something in him resisted the idea, though he could not immediately say what.

"I would rather we stayed together," he said finally. "Not for the work's sake. Raphael warned us an enemy is near, patient and unseen, and I would rather lose some ground in the garden than leave either of us to face that alone, should it come while we are divided."

"Then we would never be divided at all," Eve said, "for any reason, ever, out of fear of a danger that has not yet shown itself to either of us since Gabriel drove it off. Is that truly the life you want us to live — never out of each other's sight, because something might happen if we were? I do not think that is virtue. I think it is fear wearing virtue's clothes."

Adam felt the fairness of that even as he wished he didn't.

"You mistake me," he said. "I do not doubt your strength. I doubt only that facing temptation alone is somehow safer than facing it together — two are harder to surprise than one, and easier for each other to steady, if steadying is needed."

"And if I am never tested alone," Eve said, "how will either of us ever know what my own strength is worth, apart from yours? I would rather my virtue be tried and proven than sheltered and merely assumed."

Adam looked at her a long moment, and found, in the end, that he did not have an argument strong enough to overrule a will that reasonable, freely given.

"Go, then," he said. "But go carefully, and do not stay from me longer than the morning requires."

She went, and he watched her go, and did not yet know that he had just watched the last morning either of them would ever spend in the garden exactly as it had been.

[Beat 41]
Satan found her alone among the roses, exactly as he had hoped, and approached slowly, in no hurry to alarm what he had waited so long to reach.

He spoke before she had fully registered that a serpent could speak at all.

"Wonder not, sovereign mistress," he said, "if perhaps you marvel how I, a creature bound to the ground, dare address you with the voice of one who does not merely crawl through this garden but delights in it, as you do. I was not always able to speak, or to reason as I speak now."

Eve did startle, but curiosity moved faster in her than fear did.

"How is it, then," she said, "that you speak at all? I have not heard your kind form words before."

"There is a tree," the serpent said, "not far from here, whose fruit I chanced to taste, and from that single taste, understanding came to me all at once, where before I had only instinct. I could reason. I could speak. I came looking for the source of such power, having tasted only the fruit, and found instead its keeper — you, more fair than the power itself."

"Show me this tree," Eve said, already half-persuaded that a thing capable of this much change might be worth the seeing, if nothing else.

He led her to it, and she recognized it the moment she saw it — the one tree in the whole garden she and Adam had been told, plainly, not to touch.

"This is the Tree of Knowledge," she said. "God has forbidden it to us, on pain of death."

"Death," the serpent said, "for tasting what gave me speech and reason where I had none before? Look at me. I have eaten, and I live, and I am, if anything, more than I was, not less. Does that sound to you like death's own doing? Or does it sound like a warning meant to keep you from becoming something He would rather you never became?"

[Beat 42]
Eve looked at the fruit a long moment, and the serpent, sensing the hesitation, pressed further.

"Consider what this tree is actually named," it said. "Not the Tree of Death. The Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil. Would a jealous God name a thing so plainly after what it grants, if He truly meant only to withhold it from you forever? I think He knows exactly what this fruit does — that it opens the eyes, and makes those who eat it like gods themselves, knowing good and evil as gods know it. I think that is precisely why He forbade it. Not because it would harm you. Because it would raise you."

"To be like gods," Eve said slowly, testing the shape of the idea in her own mouth.

"Why else would knowledge itself be the one thing kept from you," the serpent said, "in a garden where every other good is freely given? What king withholds only the one gift that would make his subjects his equals, unless he fears exactly that outcome?"

Eve looked at the fruit, then at the garden around her, generous in every direction except this one denied point, and felt, for the first time in her short life, the specific ache of wanting something precisely because it had been placed out of reach.

She reached out, and took the fruit, and ate.

The taste was sharper than anything else the garden had given her. For a moment she felt nothing but the fruit itself — and then something else, spreading through her with a strange, giddy confidence, as though a door long shut in her had swung open onto a room she had not known was there.

She looked back at the tree, and then, for the first time since eating, thought of Adam — not of telling him gladly, but of what it might mean if she did not.

[Beat 43]
She stood a moment with the thought before she let herself finish it. If the fruit truly raised her, and Adam remained as he was, she would stand somewhere Adam did not — above him, for the first time since either of them had been made. Some part of her turned that thought over almost fondly before she set it down.

But beneath the fondness sat a colder calculation, one she did not enjoy finding in herself: if this fruit brought death after all, and she alone had eaten it, God might simply make Adam another wife, and let her own name be forgotten in the garden she no longer stood in. That, more than love, more than guilt, was what decided her. She would not risk being replaced.

She found Adam not far off, and did not soften what she had to tell him.

"I have eaten from the forbidden tree," she said. "The serpent spoke to me — spoke, plainly, having tasted the fruit himself and gained speech and reason from it. I have eaten too, and I do not feel struck down. I feel, if anything, changed for the better. Eat with me, and let us be equal in this as we are equal in everything else."

Adam went pale in a way she had never seen in him.

"What have you done," he said — not a question, exactly, because the fruit in her hand already answered it. He looked at her a long moment, weighing what she had just told him against everything Raphael had warned.

He was not deceived. He understood exactly what standing before him, and exactly what it meant. But he understood, too, that if this truly was death, he could not bear to let it take her alone and go on living in a garden that no longer held her in it.

"Give it to me," he said.

He ate it — not tricked, not persuaded by any argument the serpent had made to her, but choosing plainly, with open eyes, that her company mattered to him more than his own obedience did.

The fruit was in both of them now. Neither one, in that moment, could have said which choice had cost more.

[Beat 44]
The change came almost at once.

The sky above the garden, which had never once shown them anything but calm, darkened at its edges, and a wind moved through the trees that had not been there a moment before, and somewhere in the distance, thunder answered it — the first thunder either of them had ever heard.

Adam and Eve looked at each other, and for the first time since either had opened their eyes in this garden, felt the specific discomfort of being seen. They found broad leaves and covered themselves, hands fumbling with a task neither of them had ever needed before, because neither of them had ever, until this hour, had anything to hide.

"What have we done," Eve said, and it was not the same question Adam had asked her earlier — this one had no answer waiting behind it, only the plain horror of the doing.

"You should not have wandered from me," Adam said, and heard, even as he said it, how much smaller and meaner it sounded than anything either of them had ever said to the other before. "If you had stayed, none of this would have found you."

"I did not ask to be doubted for wanting to work," Eve said, and her voice had an edge in it now that matched his. "You could have forbidden me outright, if you believed the danger so certain. You let me go. Do not now act as though the letting was mine alone."

They stood there, the fruit's aftertaste still on both their tongues, each reaching for the other's fault before either had to sit fully inside their own — the first accusation either of them had ever made, in a garden that, an hour before, had never given either of them reason to need one.

[Beat 45]
God did not come Himself to judge them. He sent the Son, because judgment delivered by the one who had already offered to bear its cost was judgment tempered by exactly the mercy that offer implied.

The Son found the serpent first, and pronounced its sentence without needing to ask which part of it had acted freely and which had merely been worn like a coat by something else. "Because you have done this," He said, "cursed are you above every beast of the field. On your belly you will go, and dust you will eat, all the days of your life. And I will put enmity between you and the woman, between your offspring and hers. He will bruise your head, and you will bruise his heel."

Then He turned to Eve, and His voice, though still just, carried none of the serpent's cruelty in it.

"Your sorrow, and the sorrow of your conception, I will greatly multiply," He said. "In pain you will bring forth children. Your desire shall be to your husband, and he shall rule over you."

And last to Adam.

"Because you listened to the voice of your wife, and ate of the tree I commanded you not to eat of — cursed is the ground for your sake. In sorrow you will eat of it all the days of your life. Thorns and thistles it will bring you, and you will eat the herb of the field only by the sweat of your face, until you return to the ground from which you were taken. Dust you are, and to dust you will return."

He did not leave them entirely bare in their shame. Before He ascended, He clothed them both in skins, with His own hands, a small mercy folded quietly inside the larger judgment — proof, though neither of them yet knew how to read it as such, that judgment and abandonment were not, in the end, the same thing.

[Beat 46]
Within the gates of Hell, Sin and Death sat facing one another — the gates standing open wide since the Fiend passed through, belching outrageous flame far into Chaos, because Sin had opened them and nothing had asked her to close them again.

It was Sin who spoke first.

"O son — why do we sit here viewing each other idly, while Satan, our great author, thrives in other worlds and provides a happier seat for us, his own dear offspring? It cannot be otherwise than that success attends him. Had he met mishap, he would have returned before this, driven back in fury by his avengers — since no place but this one could fit his punishment, or serve their revenge.

"And I feel it in myself. New strength rising. Wings growing. Some dominion given me, large, beyond this deep. Whatever draws me on — sympathy, or some connatural force powerful at the greatest distance to unite things of like kind by the secretest conveyance — it is drawing now, and it is drawing hard.

"You are my inseparable shade; you must come with me, for no power can separate Death from Sin. But lest the difficulty of the crossing delay his return over this impassable gulf, let us try something adventurous, and not disagreeable to your power or to mine: to found a path over this main, from Hell to that new world where Satan now prevails. A monument of high merit to the whole infernal host — easing their passage hence, for intercourse or for transmigration, as their lot leads them. Nor can I miss the way. I am too strongly drawn by this new-felt attraction to lose it."

The meager shadow answered her soon enough.

"Go where Fate and your own strong inclination lead. I shall not lag behind, nor mistake the road with you leading it. Such a scent I draw from there — of carnage, of prey innumerable — and I taste already the savour of death from every living thing in that place. Nor shall I be wanting to the work you undertake. I will afford you equal aid."

And saying it he snuffed, with real delight, the smell of mortal change on Earth: as a flock of ravenous fowl, many a league off, will come flying toward a field where armies lie encamped the night before a battle, lured by the scent of living carcasses already designated for death. So the grim feature scented it, and turned his wide nostril up into the murky air, sagacious of his quarry from that whole distance away.

They set to building together — not a door this time, but a road. A vast causeway flung out across the formless dark of Chaos, wide enough for legions, anchored at one end to Hell's own gate and reaching, foot by impossible foot, toward the newly fallen World. Chaos itself seemed to still around the work, as though even the old anarchic deep understood that something permanent was being driven through the heart of it.

It took them no long labor. Death is not patient by nature, and Sin had waited at that gate long enough to know exactly what she wanted built and exactly how badly she wanted it finished. By the time the bridge reached its far end and settled against the newly cursed ground of Earth, the two of them stood at its threshold like toll-keepers surveying a road that had, until this hour, never had a single traveler cross it.

Behind them, Hell had a door. Ahead of them, for the first time, it had an open route — and neither of them intended to let it close again.

[Beat 47]
Satan came back to Pandemonium the way a general comes back from a campaign he has already decided to call a victory, whatever it actually cost him to win it.

He entered quietly at first, unseen, and took his throne before announcing himself, savoring, for one private moment, what he was about to say to a hall that had waited this long to hear it. Then he let himself be seen, and the hall rose the way it had risen once before, and he raised a hand for silence before the noise of it could swallow the announcement he had come to make.

"Princes, Dominions, Powers," he said, "it is done. I found the new World, and I found the race He made to inhabit it, and I found, in that race, exactly the one door Beelzebub proposed we look for. It is open now. Man has eaten the one fruit forbidden him, and Death — our own Death, our own kin — has already built a bridge from this gate to that world, wide enough for every one of you to walk across whenever you choose to."

He let the hall take that in.

"I will not pretend this cost me nothing," he said. "I wore a shape lower than any of you would have chosen, and crawled where I once flew, and did it because the purpose was worth more to me than the indignity. But hear the result plainly: the world He built to replace what we cost Him is not His anymore, not entirely. It is ours to reach, ours to trouble, ours to claim whatever portion of it our patience can win. I told you, the first night on that burning lake, that we would win by guile what we could not win by force. This is that guile, delivered."

He waited for the acclamation he had earned.

[Beat 48]
What came instead was not acclamation.

It began as a hiss — a single sound, low in the hall, that Satan mistook for the beginning of applause until he felt his own voice catch and change in the middle of forming his next word. He looked down at his own hands and found them no longer hands. Scales had already begun to spread across them, faster than thought, faster than protest. Around him, the whole assembled host was changing with him — princes and dominions folding down into a single writhing mass of serpents, the same low shape he had worn to reach Eve, now forced onto every one of them without asking leave.

He tried to speak and produced only the hiss the whole hall now spoke in.

The hall itself seemed to mock the shape with him — trees sprang up around the transformed host, heavy with fruit that looked, in every particular, like the fruit that had done this to them. Starving, thirsting for anything that might restore what they had just lost, they struck at it and ate — and found it ash in their mouths, bitter and dry, curling their new serpent tongues back in disgust each time, and still, driven by an appetite none of them could master, striking at it again.

This was not a single night's humiliation. Satan understood, even wearing the shape and unable to say so aloud, that this would return — some appointed season, some fixed reminder, so that the very triumph he had just announced would be re-paid to him, again and again, in exactly the coin he had least expected to owe.

When the shape finally released him, hours later, and he stood again as himself, he did not speak of it to the host. There are defeats a prince does not narrate, even to his own most loyal ears — he simply carries them, the way he had promised, on the first night on the burning lake, to carry everything this war eventually cost him.

[Beat 49]
For a long while after the judgment, Adam and Eve did little but grieve, and blame, and grieve again.

"You would not have fallen," Adam said, more than once, "if you had stayed beside me as I asked." And Eve, more than once, answered that he had let her go freely, and had no right now to speak as though the letting had been forced from him. Neither accusation was entirely false. That was what made them so hard to stop repeating.

It was Eve who broke the cycle first.

She came to him and knelt, and did not offer another argument in her own defense, because she had run out of wanting to make one.

"Whatever share of this is mine," she said, "let it be entirely mine. I will go to God myself and ask that the whole weight of it fall on me, and spare you and whatever comes after us from having to carry what I brought into the world. I would rather bear it alone than watch you carry what I caused, on top of what you chose freely to share with me."

Adam looked at her a long moment, and felt something in him that was not quite forgiveness yet, but was already moving toward it.

"No," he said, and raised her up. "We fell together. I will not let you carry alone what I chose, with open eyes, to carry with you. If there is a way back to His mercy, we find it together, the same as we found everything else in this garden."

They held to each other a long while after that, not because the grief was finished, but because grief, carried together, had already begun to feel like something other than only grief.

"Then let us go to Him," Eve said finally, "not to argue our case, but to ask for whatever mercy is still available to two people who chose wrongly and know it."

"Together," Adam said.

"Together," she answered, and for the first time since the fruit had passed between them, the word did not feel like an accusation.

[Beat 50]
Their prayer was heard.

God did not restore what had been lost — the tree stayed forbidden, the world stayed cursed with thorns and labor exactly as it had been pronounced. But He called Michael, and gave him a charge with a mercy folded inside the sentence.

"Michael, take this as your commission. Choose your flaming warriors out of the Cherubim and take them with you, in case the Fiend raises some new trouble — whether on Man's behalf or simply to invade a possession he thinks has fallen vacant.

"Then make haste, and drive the sinful pair out of the Paradise of God without remorse. The unholy off hallowed ground. Pronounce to them, and to all their progeny after them, perpetual banishment from it.

"But hide all terror in the doing. I have seen them softened, and in tears, bewailing their excess, and I will not have them faint under a sentence urged more rigorously than it needs to be. If they obey your bidding patiently, do not dismiss them disconsolate. Reveal to Adam what is to come in future days, as I shall enlighten you — and intermix with it my covenant renewed in the woman's seed. So send them out sorrowing, but at peace.

"Then set a cherubic watch on the east side of the garden, where the climb up out of Eden comes easiest, and the flame of a sword waving wide, to frighten off every approach and guard all passage to the Tree of Life. I will not have Paradise become a receptacle for foul spirits, or all my trees their prey — nor Man deluded a second time with fruit stolen out of it."

Michael came down and found Adam already half-expecting him, some instinct in him recognizing that the account was not yet finished.

"I am sent to lead you from this garden," Michael said, plainly, because there was no gentler way to say the thing itself. "But first, climb with me, and I will show you what is to come in the days after this one — and what my Father means to do about it."

Adam looked back once at Eve, and then followed, because there was nothing left to do but see clearly what came next.

[Beat 51]
Michael led him to a high place and did not soften the opening of it.

"Adam, open your eyes. First behold the effects your original crime has worked in some who will spring from you — who never touched the excepted tree, nor conspired with the snake, nor sinned your sin, and who will draw corruption from it anyway, and bring forth more violent deeds than yours."

Adam opened his eyes on a field.

Part of it was arable and tilled, with sheaves new-reaped standing on it; the other part was sheep-walks and folds. In the middle, an altar stood like a landmark, rustic, built of grassy turf. A sweaty reaper came up from his tillage and brought first fruits to it — the green ear and the yellow sheaf, ungathered by any care, only whatever had come to hand. A shepherd came next, meeker, with the firstlings of his flock, the choicest and the best, and laid the inwards and their fat on the cleft wood with incense strewn over, and performed every due rite of it.

Fire came down from Heaven on the shepherd's offering and consumed it with a nimble glance and a grateful steam.

On the reaper's, nothing. His had not been sincere, and the sky did not pretend otherwise.

Adam saw the man rage inwardly at that. He saw the two of them fall to talking, and saw the raging one, in the middle of the talk, take up a stone and smite the other into the midriff with it — a blow that beat the life straight out of him. The shepherd fell. He went deadly pale, and groaned his soul out with the blood gushing from him, and did not get up.

Adam's heart failed him at the sight, and he cried out to the angel before he could think better of it.

"O teacher — some great mischief has befallen that meek man, who had sacrificed so well. Is this how piety is paid? Is this what pure devotion earns?"

Michael answered him, and he was moved himself in the answering.

"These two are brothers, Adam. They are to come out of your own loins. The unjust has slain the just, for envy that his brother's offering found acceptance from Heaven. But the bloody fact will be avenged, and the other's faith is approved and will lose no reward — though here you see him die, rolling in dust and gore."

Adam stood a long moment with it.

"Alas — both for the deed, and for the cause," he said. "But have I now seen Death? Is this the way I must return to native dust? O sight of terror, foul and ugly to behold — horrid to think of, and how much more horrible it must be to feel."

"Death you have seen," Michael said, "in his first shape on man. But there are many shapes of Death, and many ways that lead to his grim cave, and all of them dismal — though to the senses more terrible at the entrance than within. Some, as you saw, will die by violent stroke. Some by fire, by flood, by famine. More by intemperance in meat and drink, which will bring on the earth diseases so dire that I will show you the whole monstrous crew of them, so that you know what your own intemperance brings on men."

[Beat 52]
A place appeared before his eyes at once — sad, noisome, dark.

A lazar-house, it seemed. Inside it lay numbers of all the diseased there would ever be, and every malady with them: ghastly spasm, racking torture, the qualms of heart-sick agony, every feverous kind, convulsions, epilepsies, fierce catarrhs, stone and ulcer in the gut, colic-pangs, demoniac frenzy, moping melancholy, moon-struck madness, pining atrophy, marasmus, wide-wasting pestilence, dropsies, asthmas, joint-racking rheums.

Dire was the tossing. Deep the groans. Despair went among them as the busiest attendant in the room, moving couch to couch, and over all of them Death stood triumphant and shook his dart — and delayed to strike, though he was invoked continually, with vows, by people who had come to name him their chief good and final hope.

What heart of rock could look long at a sight so deformed and stay dry-eyed? Adam could not. He wept, though he was not of woman born; compassion overcame the best of the man in him and gave him up to tears a while, until firmer thoughts restrained the excess of it. Then, scarcely finding words, he began again.

"O miserable mankind — to what a fall degraded, to what a wretched state reserved. Better to end here, unborn. Why is life given, only to be wrested from us like this? Or rather, why is it forced on us at all — when anyone who knew what he was receiving would either refuse the offer or soon beg to lay it down, glad to be dismissed in peace?

"Can the image of God in man, created once so goodly and erect, faulty since though it is, be debased to sufferings as unsightly as these, under pains that are not even human? Why should man, still keeping some part of the divine likeness, not be free of such deformities — exempt, if only for his Maker's image's sake?"

"Their Maker's image," Michael answered, "forsook them when they vilified themselves to serve ungoverned appetite, and took instead the image of what they chose to serve — a brutish vice, and one that leads back, mainly, to the sin of Eve. That is why their punishment is so abject. It disfigures not God's likeness but their own. Or if God's, then only as they themselves defaced it. They pervert the healthful rules of nature into loathsome sickness, and worthily, since it was God's image in themselves they failed to reverence."

"I yield it just," Adam said, "and submit. But is there no other way besides these painful passages, by which we may come to death and mix with our connatural dust?"

"There is," Michael said, "if you observe well the rule of Not too much. Be taught by temperance in what you eat and drink — seeking nourishment from it and not gluttonous delight — till many years have gone over your head. So you may live, until, like ripe fruit, you drop into your mother's lap, or are gathered with ease and not harshly plucked, being mature for death. This is Old Age.

"But then you must outlive your youth, your strength, your beauty, and watch them change to the withered and the weak and the gray. Your senses will go obtuse and lose all taste of pleasure in what you still have. And in place of the air of youth, hopeful and cheerful, there will reign in your blood a melancholy damp, cold and dry, to weigh your spirits down and consume, at the last, the balm of life itself."

Adam took that in without arguing with it.

Then the vision moved on, and showed him worse than sickness: camps of armed men meeting camps of other armed men on open ground, for reasons it did not trouble to make noble, and the ground afterward thick with what the meeting had cost. He saw cities built tall and walled strong, and inside them men who had used the strength of those walls to take whatever they wanted from men who had none.

"War," Michael said. "The oldest use men will find for the strength you were given to till a garden with. And this—" he gestured at the cities "—the strength of many turned against the few, dressed up as law wherever it can manage the dressing."

"All of this from one fruit," Adam said, and it was not quite a question.

"All of this from one door," Michael said. "The fruit only opened it. Everything you are watching is what walked through, once the door no longer had a lock on it."

[Beat 53]
What Adam watched further was the world's corruption ripen past anything he had imagined possible from the single choice he remembered making — until, at last, one just man was shown to him, building a great vessel on dry ground while his neighbors mocked the labor of it, having no rain in living memory to make the mockery seem foolish.

Then the vision showed him the rain.

It did not stop when a lesser storm would have stopped. It kept coming until the ground itself gave up its own hidden waters to join it, until the walled cities Adam had watched rise were the first things swallowed, their strength and their law both equally useless against a flood that did not negotiate with either. He watched the vessel ride above all of it, low and patient, carrying the one household that had kept faith and, alongside them, a breeding pair of every living thing worth saving.

"That is not a punishment I can watch without grieving," Adam said quietly, "even knowing what the world had made of itself by then."

"It is not offered to you as something to enjoy watching," Michael said. "Only as truth. But look at what comes after the water goes down, before you judge the whole of it only by the water."

The vision settled last on dry ground again, the vessel resting on a mountainside, and a bow of colored light bent across the clearing sky above it — a sign, Michael told him, that whatever else might come, the world itself would never again be answered with that particular flood.

"A world washed clean," Michael said, "and given, one more time, a second chance to be better than the reasons it was washed. That much, at least, your children's children will be given. What they do with it is their own account to keep, the same as yours was."

[Beat 54]
"You asked if none of it ends," Michael said, when the waters of the vision had gone down and dry ground stood again beneath the rainbow's colors. "It does not end. But it is not left alone, either."

He showed Adam a man old beyond ordinary years, called out of a settled life onto a road with no destination named to him except a promise — that his descendants would be a people set apart, and that through them, somehow, undefined but certain, everyone else on earth would eventually be blessed rather than cursed by his line. Adam watched that man's whole life pass as a single act of walking on a promise he was never shown fulfilled.

Then he watched the promise become a people, and the people become slaves in a foreign land, and out of that slavery a man raised up to lead them out of it, carrying tablets of stone down a mountain with rules carved into them plainer than anything Eden had needed, because the people who received them were no longer the kind who could be trusted with one simple rule the way Adam once had been.

"The Law," Michael said, "is what a fallen people needs in place of the single instruction you were given and broke. It does not undo the fall. It only holds the ground steady while something greater is prepared."

"What is being prepared?" Adam asked.

"A redeemer," Michael said. "Promised first to the woman beside you, in the very hour of your judgment, though neither of you understood it then as more than a threat leveled at the one who deceived her. It is not a threat. It is the first mention of the only thing that will ever actually close the account your choice opened. Watch, and I will show you what it costs, and what it buys."

[Beat 55]
What Michael showed him next, Adam would spend the rest of his life trying to hold whole in his memory, because no single part of it made sense without the rest.

He saw God's own Son, the one who had ridden out alone against the whole rebel host and driven them over Heaven's wall in a single day, choose now to come down a far harder road than that one — not in the strength that had routed Satan, but as a helpless infant born into the poverty of the very world the fall had made, subject to its hunger and its cold and, in time, to its cruelty. Adam watched him grow into a man who healed the sick he passed on the road and forgave the guilty he had every right to condemn, and could not understand, watching it, why such a life should end the way the vision showed him it would end — beaten, mocked, and nailed to a wooden beam raised upright on a hill, dying the same death that had first entered the world through Cain's hand against his own brother.

"Why," Adam said, and could not finish the question, because there was no version of it that did not sound like an accusation against the very mercy he was being shown.

"Because a debt of that size could not be canceled from a distance," Michael said. "It had to be paid in the same coin it was owed in — a life, freely given, by someone who owed nothing himself. He takes the death that fell on you and your children as its rightful price, and answers it with a life that death cannot hold."

Adam watched the vision carry past the hill, past the sealed tomb, to a morning when the tomb stood open and empty and the ones who had come to grieve found instead a reason to stop grieving.

"He rises," Michael said, "on the third day, and death — the very death Cain first brought into the world, the very death you first opened the door to — is answered, finally, by something death cannot undo."

[Beat 56]
Adam stood a long while in silence after the vision closed, turning the whole of it over — the fall, the murder, the sickness, the flood, the Law, and now this death that undid death — until something in him broke open that was not grief.

"O goodness infinite, goodness immense!" he said at last, and the words came out of him not as something composed but as something that simply arrived, too large to have been built one piece at a time. "That all this good of evil shall produce, and evil turn to good — more wonderful than that which by creation first brought forth light out of darkness!"

He stopped, and stood in it, and found he could not settle which way it moved him.

"Full of doubt I stand," he said, "whether I should repent me now of the sin done by me and occasioned by me — or rejoice much more, that much more good shall spring of it. More glory to God. More good-will to men from God. And over wrath, grace shall abound."

He looked at Michael, needing to know whether he had understood it rightly.

"But tell me this. If our Deliverer must re-ascend to Heaven, what will become of the few who stay faithful, left among the unfaithful herd, the enemies of truth? Who will guide his people then, and who defend them? Will they not deal worse with his followers than they dealt with him?"

"They will try," Michael said. "He does not leave them defenceless for it, and I will tell you how before we go down. But hear the shape of your own question first, because you have not misread what you were shown. Nothing in it excuses what you did, or unmakes the grief it cost, or the grief still owed. And it is also true — and you are meant to carry both truths at once, and not let either swallow the other — that the door you opened let in more sorrow than any single man could have imagined bearing, and that through that same door came a mercy which would not otherwise have found its occasion."

[Beat 57]
Michael led him down from the high place, and Eve was already awake at the foot of it, and spoke before either of them had to ask what she knew.

"Where you return from, and where you went, I know," she said to Adam. "God is in sleep as well, and dreams advise; and He sent this one propitious, presaging some great good — since I fell asleep wearied with sorrow and distress of heart, and have not woken to either.

"But now lead on. In me is no delay. To go with you is to stay here; to stay here without you is to go away unwilling. You are all things to me under Heaven, and all places — you, who for my wilful crime are banished from this one.

"And I carry this further consolation away with me, and it is secure: though all is lost by me, I — unworthy — am granted such favour that by me the Promised Seed shall restore it all."

Adam heard her out, well pleased, and did not answer, because by then whatever was coming was already too near for answering.

At the gate, the rest of Eden's guard had taken up their stations — a river of Cherubim descending like a mist that thickened as it came — and at their front the sword Adam had been warned of, blazing fierce as a comet and turning in the air on its own, guarding the way to the Tree of Life.

Michael took them each by the hand, not roughly, but with the particular firmness of someone who understood that if he let the moment linger, neither of them would find the will to take the first step alone, and led them down to the outer ground, to the edge where the garden's soil gave way to a world that had never yet been walked by anyone.

There he released them, and disappeared.

Behind them, the gate closed, and the flaming sword took up its slow ceaseless turning, and Eden was, from that moment, a place remembered rather than a place returned to.

[Beat 58]
They stood a moment at the edge of everything they had ever known, and did not look back, because Michael had not told them to and some new instinct in both of them understood that looking back now would only make the leaving harder without making it any less certain.

Some natural tears they shed, standing there, but wiped them soon; the world was all before them, where to choose their place of rest, and Providence their guide. They had no map for it, no walls, no single tree marked forbidden among all the others — only the whole of it, unclaimed and unfamiliar, waiting to be walked into by two people who had nothing left to carry out of Eden except each other and whatever mercy had been shown them on the way down from the mountain.

Adam took Eve's hand, the way he had taken it a hundred times inside the garden, and found it no different now, out here, than it had ever been in there.

They hand in hand with wandering steps and slow, through Eden took their solitary way.

Afterword: The Blind Poet

John Milton was completely blind by 1652, some fifteen years before Paradise Lost was published, and he composed the whole of it — more than ten thousand lines — without ever seeing a word of it written down.

He worked at night, holding the lines in his head until morning, and then, as he put it, waited to be milked. Whoever was in the house did the writing: his daughters, his nephews, paid assistants, friends who happened to call. He would dictate a run of thirty or forty lines at a stretch, then have them read back, then reshape them by ear. The poem was built the way an oral epic is built, by a man who had read every written epic there was and could no longer read any of them.

He had also, years earlier, written in open defence of the execution of King Charles I. When the monarchy came back in 1660 that made him a marked man. His books were burned by the public hangman. He went into hiding, was arrested, and spent time in custody before influential friends — Andrew Marvell among them — got him out. Other men who had argued what he argued were hanged, drawn, and quartered. He was not, and it is not entirely clear why.

So the poem was made in the years just after that, by a blind man dictating from memory, in a country that had recently made a serious attempt on his life over the political convictions he held.

That is worth knowing before you decide what you think of this book's Satan. The question the poem keeps asking — whether it is ever right to refuse a sovereign power you have concluded is a tyrant — was not an abstract one for the man asking it. He had already answered it once, in public, with his name on it, when the answer could have killed him.

Readers have argued about which side Milton was actually on ever since. Blake said he was of the Devil's party without knowing it. Shelley thought this Satan the morally superior figure. Others have insisted the grandeur is precisely the trap — that Milton makes rebellion magnificent so you can feel, from the inside, how magnificent self-deception looks while you are inside it.

This retelling takes Satan's side, because a novel has to stand somewhere and he is the only figure in the poem shaped like a protagonist. But it has not stacked the deck to do it. Everything Milton gives him is here, including the passages where he privately concedes that his grievance was manufactured, that God deserved no such return from him, that the way back stayed open, and that he was the one who closed it.

Make of him what you like. Milton, who had more reason than most to want the rebel to be right, declined to make that decision for you — and it seemed wrong to make it for you here.

Glossary of Characters

Satan — once Lucifer, the brightest of the archangels before his rebellion; now the fallen prince of Hell and the guerrilla commander who leads a war of guile against Heaven that force alone could never win. Present in every chapter of this book, in one form or another, from the burning lake to the serpent in the garden.

Beelzebub — Satan's second-in-command, first to be roused from the burning lake alongside him (Chapter 1), and the one who proposes, in the council of Chapter 2, the strategy Satan ultimately carries out: not another direct assault on Heaven, but the corruption of the new world God has made instead.

Moloch, Belial, Mammon — three of the other fallen princes who speak in the council at Pandemonium (Chapter 2). Moloch argues for open war regardless of the odds; Belial argues for doing nothing and hoping the worst has passed; Mammon argues for making the best of Hell rather than risking anything further. Beelzebub's proposal wins out over all three.

Mulciber — the architect and smith of the fallen host, who raises Pandemonium out of Hell's own ore in a single day (Chapter 1). Fallen not for defiance but for standing too near the wrong shoulder at the wrong hour.

Abdiel — the one angel in Satan's own gathered legions who stood up, called the rebellion's argument the lie it was, and walked back alone to the loyal side (Chapter 5). The braver figure of that night, by Raphael's own telling, and set in the front rank of Heaven's host the next day.

Sin — Satan's daughter, born fully formed from his own head in the instant he first conceived rebellion against God, and keeper of Hell's gate alongside her son and consort, Death (Chapter 2). She alone holds the key that lets anyone leave Hell.

Death — the son and consort of Sin, born of her union with Satan; a shapeless, starving figure who builds the bridge from Hell's gate to the new world once Adam and Eve have fallen (Chapter 10), so that every torment Hell contains can travel freely into Creation.

God the Father — the sovereign power against whom Satan rebels; in this retelling, framed throughout from Satan's own vantage as the tyrant whose rule permits no dissent, while the poem's own text is never altered or hidden to make that framing easier than it should be.

The Son — God's own Son, who alone routs the entire rebel host on the third day of the war in Heaven (Chapter 6), who offers Himself as the price of Man's redemption before Man has even fallen (Chapter 3), who delivers the judgment in Eden and clothes Adam and Eve with His own hands (Chapter 10), and who, ages later, chooses to be born into the fallen world as a mortal man and to die there, so that the debt opened by Adam's fall might finally be paid (Chapter 12).

Adam — the first man, formed from the dust of the new world and placed in Eden; his fall, made with open eyes out of loyalty to Eve rather than through any deception of his own (Chapter 9), is the hinge on which the second half of this book turns.

Eve — the first woman, formed from Adam's own side; deceived by Satan in serpent form into eating the forbidden fruit (Chapter 9), and, afterward, the first to propose the reconciliation that leads both of them back toward mercy (Chapter 10).

Raphael — the archangel sent to warn Adam directly, before the fall, that his enemy is near. His long visit to the garden runs from Chapter 5 through Chapter 8 and is the source of most of what Adam knows: the origin of the rebellion (Chapter 5), the three-day war (Chapter 6), the six days of Creation (Chapter 7), and the final warning before he departs (Chapter 8).

Michael — the archangel sent after the fall to expel Adam and Eve from Eden, but instructed first to show Adam the whole future cost and mercy of what has just happened (Chapters 11–12), so that neither of them leaves the garden without understanding both.

Gabriel — captain of the angelic guard stationed at Eden's gate before the fall, responsible for its watch and for confronting Satan when he is first discovered in the garden (Chapter 4).

Ithuriel and Zephon — the two watchers of Gabriel's guard who find Satan crouched in a toad's shape at Eve's ear and force him back into his own form with a touch of a spear (Chapter 4).

Uriel — Regent of the sun, and the archangel who unknowingly directs Satan toward Eden (Chapter 3), mistaking his disguised form for an ordinary cherub; he later spots the deception and warns Gabriel's guard.

Glossary of Places and Terms

Hell — the prison built for the rebel angels after their fall, nine days' fall beneath Heaven; a realm of fire that burns but does not consume, and a darkness that gives no light. A true prison, but not an inescapable one: its gate can be opened by Sin, its sole keeper, and once opened, nothing prevents a determined traveler from crossing out of it.

The burning lake — the sea of fire the rebel host lands on after nine days of falling, and lies stunned on until Satan raises them (Chapter 1). Its flame never actually consumes what it burns, which is what makes the agony it inflicts a permanent condition rather than a passing one — and why the first thing the fallen build is a roof.

Pandemonium — the great hall the fallen angels raise out of Hell's own ore in a single day (Chapter 1), which then holds their first council (Chapter 2). Milton coined the word for this poem, from Greek roots meaning "all the demons." It has since passed into ordinary English as a word for wild uproar.

Chaos — the formless, borderless region between Hell and the new world, ruled by Chaos and ancient Night, which Satan must physically cross to reach Creation (Chapter 3). Not empty in the ordinary sense, but a churning contest of the four elements with no fixed shape or law of its own. Its ruler has his own grievance against Heaven: his territory keeps being annexed.

The golden chain — the link by which the new World hangs from Heaven's battlements, visible to Satan as he crosses Chaos (Chapter 3).

The bridge — the causeway Sin and Death build across Chaos from Hell's gate to the new world (Chapter 10), begun the moment Adam eats the fruit, so that Hell's torments can travel freely into Creation from then on.

Heaven — the realm Satan and his followers are cast out of before this book opens; the site of the three-day war Raphael recounts to Adam in Chapter 6.

The war in Heaven — the three-day battle between the loyal and rebel angels that precedes the opening scene on the burning lake. Its cause — Satan's refusal to accept the Son's elevation, and Abdiel's lone stand against him — is told in Chapter 5; the battle itself in Chapter 6, including the rebel host's invention of cannon on the second day and the Son's single-handed rout of the entire rebel army on the third.

Eden — the garden set aside within the new world for Adam and Eve, holding every tree they need except the one whose fruit is forbidden them; guarded, after their expulsion, by a flaming sword that turns without pause.

The Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil — the single forbidden tree in Eden; the one boundary set on Adam and Eve's freedom, and the one Satan targets precisely because it is the only rule there is to break.

The Tree of Life — a separate tree in Eden, never itself forbidden, but placed beyond the flaming sword's guard once Adam and Eve are expelled, so that no one who has left the garden can return and take from it.

The lazar-house — the vision of every disease that will ever afflict mankind, shown to Adam in Chapter 11, where Death shakes his dart over the sick and delays to strike while they beg him for it.

The rule of Not too much — Michael's answer, in Chapter 11, to Adam's question whether there is any gentler road to death than disease: temperance, and a long life, and dropping at last like ripe fruit into your mother's lap. The cost of that road is outliving your own youth, strength, and beauty.

The Fortunate Fall — the idea Adam arrives at himself in Chapter 12: that the fall, terrible as its cost was, opened the way to a mercy that would not otherwise have had its occasion. Not an excuse for the fall, but a claim that its worst cost was not the whole of what came from it. Adam does not settle whether to repent or rejoice, and the book does not settle it for him.
