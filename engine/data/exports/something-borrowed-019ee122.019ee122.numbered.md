# Something Borrowed

_Seto follows a dead route to its source. The signal pattern is intentional._

[Beat 1]
﻿The depot ran three levels under the surface, and at this hour it belonged to her. Day shift wouldn't badge in for another forty minutes. The benches stood in their rows under the standby lighting Orison kept at half power before nine, each station a rectangle of dim until a body crossed it and woke the panel overhead. Hers was awake. The rest of the room held that brown underground dark, the color of a building thinking about saving money. Somewhere a coolant line ticked as it pressurized for the day. The air came off the filtration membrane the way it always did down here, scrubbed past the point of having a smell, so that the only thing it carried was the faint mineral cold of the tile and, under that, the antiseptic the cleaners ran across the floor at four in the morning. Her first appointment was at nine. Forty minutes.

Seto had sent the map an hour ago. The second message came in while she was still squaring her two visit logs against the edge of the bench.

*Ignore the first crescent coordinate ? transposed a digit. Corrected file attached.*

Brief. They were always brief, and they always came in pairs.

She laid her own visit log beside his on the screen and began the cross-check by hand, because there was no query she could run that wouldn't leave a query in the record. Client by client. A coordinate from her log, a coordinate from his, the arc he'd drawn between them ? a crescent across four corridor zones, the shape a city makes when something has been seeded along one curve of it and not the rest. The vent above her station clicked through its cycle while she worked, the little metal complaint of a damper that had needed replacing for a year. It clicked maybe forty times before she was done. Twenty-two minutes.

Inside the crescent: forty-one of the fifty-three Batch 44-C clients.

She had made a coordinate error the first time through, three weeks ago, and so she ran it again, slower, her stylus touching each entry as she counted. Forty-one. The other twelve fell outside the arc entirely, scattered through zones the crescent never reached.

She set the stylus down.

For three weeks the file had opened under her hands and closed under them again. Cross-checks across client batches were two cert tiers above her scope. She maintained hardware. She did not draw maps of where the hardware lived.

She stood, walked to the water dispenser at the end of the row, filled a cup, came back. The motion woke two panels and let them die again behind her.

The anomaly log was where she'd left it for twenty-one days. Four clients. Type C drift ? the calibration signature wandering off its baseline in a way the manual called nonspecific and she had learned to read as deliberate. Tied to a project code, ORS-MAINT-7C, that resolved, when she chased it, to nothing: no department, no owner, no line in the public library. For three weeks that nothing had been the end of the trail. Now the crescent put a shape around it.

She opened a message to Seto and started laying it out ? the coordinates, the forty-one, the arc ? and the depot door cycled.

A panel woke down the row. Footsteps, a kit bag set on a bench two stations over. She pulled the calibration log up on her secondary screen, the day's appointments in their neat grid, the face anyone would expect a technician to be reading at this hour. The colleague badged into the adjacent station, did whatever a person does at six in the morning when they think they're alone, and badged out. Six minutes. The panel above their bench faded.

She brought the draft back. Six of her thirty-seven minutes, gone. She did not send it.

She opened the batch documentation instead. Four clients, Type C drift, a mechanism the file insisted was non-random and never named. Six months she'd had it open and no mechanism. Now she had the crescent.

A transit alert surfaced for the Zone 8 line ? thirty minutes' lead. She dismissed it.

Her nine o'clock was in thirty-seven minutes. Dani Soral. Batch 44-C. Zone 8, corridor four. One of the forty-one.

Soral always came early. Soral had a habit Amara had catalogued without ever asking about it: before a calibration, two fingers pressed flat against the access port at the base of the skull. Not checking the seam, not worrying it. Just touching it, the way you touch a doorframe on your way through.

Amara's hand went to the back of her own neck. The skin there was smooth, the way it should be, unbroken over the place where the port wasn't. She made herself stop.

Soral had surfaced in the Type C log twice. Minor drift, both times, inside tolerance. The appointment was standard. She had done this one forty times. She could have done it asleep.

She closed the appointment window. Opened it again.

She pulled ORS-MAINT-7C. Three different access levels had touched it this week. Nothing sat under any of them, and nothing sat in the public library, anywhere, that said what the project was for.

She did not know what the maintenance visits had actually done.

She sat back from the bench and put her palms flat on the surface. The depot kept the metal at the temperature of the room, which was the temperature of three floors of poured concrete, which was cold. She left her hands there a moment before she let go.

What she had was the gap. A permissions ticket she'd filed against the sub-contractor portal six months ago and never closed, a seam in the access wall she'd left open because she needed it, and through which she had been reading service records two tiers above her scope for half a year. What she didn't have was a protocol ? a clean reason any of this was hers to look at.

She and her clients ran the same hardware. She was Orison staff. She typed her own certification ID into the sub-contractor portal and waited, because the sub-contractor system always ran slow, a third-party graft on Orison's spine that answered when it felt like answering.

Three calibration appointments came back. March. October. The March before that.

March she remembered. Clinic 9, Zone 5, the anteroom that smelled of warmed plastic and someone's lunch, forty minutes in the chair. She pulled her transit log for the date and it agreed with her: down to Zone 5, back. She had the day in her body, not just the record.

She scrolled down.

October. A facility in Zone 8.

She tapped the entry.

She had no memory of being in Zone 8 in October. She had October ? the ordinary weight of it, the Tuesdays she could account for, the route she always took down to Zone 5 for groceries on the fourteenth. There was no Zone 8 in any of it.

She pushed back from the bench and stood, both hands on the edge of it. The vent finished a cycle and pushed recycled air across the back of her neck, and she held there, not moving, long enough that the motion sensor over the next station gave up and let its panel go dark. Then she sat back down.

She pulled her own transit log for that Tuesday. It ended at Zone 6. Not Zone 8. She had gone down to Zone 6 and come home from Zone 6, and the day had no Zone 8 in it anywhere.

Someone had entered the October appointment. Someone had signed the service record at the end of it. Her certification ID was on both.

She had been on the maintenance list. Not as a technician. As a client.

She went still. Her right hand moved before she decided to move it ? two fingers to the back of her neck, the way Soral touched the port. She pressed. Once, with enough pressure to feel the ridge she'd always been careful not to notice, the slight raised seam where the implant housing met bone. She caught herself doing it. Stopped.

The depot was still empty. She checked the time. Thirty-four minutes. Dani Soral, Zone 8, corridor four. One of the forty-one.

She had found the gap six months ago and left the ticket open because she needed the access, and she had never once thought about what the gap looked like from the other side of the wall ? what it was for someone to be able to read her records the way she read everyone's.

She walked to the waste receptacle and dropped the cup, which she discovered was empty, which she discovered she had been holding. Came back.

Six months of access. Logged the way everything down here was logged, every read a line in a file. She was not the only one who could read those files.

Seto's messages came in pairs. The main line, then the correction a few seconds behind it, every time, so reliably that she had started waiting for the second one before she answered the first. The last one sat at the bottom of the thread.

*Eight years in Zone 7. No Orison contract, no sub-contractor has ever touched my hardware. That was a decision I made before I had all the data for it.*

She had a name. A freelance calibration specialist in Zone 6, a referral eight months old ? from before October. She opened the contact. INACTIVE. No forwarding address.

She could cancel. Equipment malfunction, schedule conflict; she had done it twice this quarter and no one had asked. Or she could keep the appointment and go down into the facility and watch the procedure happen from the inside of it ? forty minutes of the thing itself, which was more than she had gotten out of records and transit logs and a code that resolved to nothing.

She would go in knowing what she knew.

She picked up her kit bag from beside the bench leg and set it on the surface.

She closed the batch documentation and opened a reply into Seto's archive and built it carefully. The anomaly log. The batch records. The October appointment. Zone 8, and no transit record to put her there. She wrote it the careful way ? each fact set down apart from what it meant, each date noted, nothing reached for. She attached the permissions ticket and the project code.

She stopped. Read it back. It was complete, and documented, and it was the kind of message a person writes when they want a record of what they knew to exist somewhere outside the system that holds them.

Twenty-nine minutes.

She moved to cancel the appointment. The field threw a banner: THIRD EQUIPMENT CANCELLATION IN QUARTER ? SUPERVISOR AUTHORIZATION REQUIRED. The field locked.

She stared at it. She had not thought about the counter. She had not thought about which audit queue a third cancellation generates, or about the person who reads that queue, or about whether that person would notice a technician with an open permissions gap canceling appointments on Batch 44-C clients, one corridor at a time.

The maintenance comm chimed. Call request, Zone 8 clinic, caller ID Faria ? the clinic coordinator, a new role, someone she'd spoken to twice.

She answered.

"Just confirming your nine o'clock," Faria said. "Dr. Soral's already checked in."

"Confirmed. On my way."

"Bay three's set up for you. Same setup as last time ? you remember where it is."

She didn't. She said thank you.

The call ended. The batch documentation was still open in front of her. Forty-one of fifty-three. ORS-MAINT-7C. An October appointment with no transit record under it. Bay three, Faria had said, and Amara sat looking at the words and wondered whether bay three was where October had been.

She sent the message to Seto.

She checked the bag. Calibration wand. Contact patch. Service tablet. The small wrench she'd carried for three years because the Zone 5 clinic ran a nonstandard fitting and the standard kit wouldn't seat it.

She did not cancel the appointment.

She needed to know what happened inside those visits. She had sent the documentation to Seto, so that if something went wrong with her memory of the next forty minutes ? the way something had gone wrong with October ? a record would exist outside Orison's system of what she had known before she walked through the door.

She shouldered the bag and logged out of the bench. The lights came on ahead of her down the row and went dark behind, the panels waking only where a body was and nowhere a body had been, Orison standard spec, light that left no account of where you'd come from. She went out into the corridor ? filtration membrane, tile cleaner, the cold ? toward the Zone 8 transit line.

On the first flight of stairs, climbing toward the surface, her right hand closed on the strap and she felt it in the tendons of her fingers: the resistance of a fitting that was not in her kit. A heavier head than the Zone 5 wrench, a shorter handle, and the exact calibrated tension of a tool already turned a quarter turn against something ? torqued down against hardware she had never been trained to touch. Her hand knew the tool. Her hand knew the turn.

She stopped on the stairs. Her free hand went to the wall, cold tile, the hum of the ventilation faint under her palm. She held still.

The sensation didn't leave. It stayed in her fingers, the weight and the tension and the small certainty of a turn she had no memory of making, while she stood on the stair with her palm flat to the wall and waited for her own hand to be only a hand. Then, between one breath and the next, it was gone. Her hand held the strap. The strap held the bag. The bag weighed what it had always weighed.

She stood there two seconds. Then she went up.

At the top landing the transit station opened around her ? the automated chimes, the recorded voice naming Zone 8, people moving past her toward ordinary places, and the outside air that had come down through the streets above and smelled like it.

She had a crescent that ran through Zone 8. She had an October appointment that put her in Zone 8. Now she had the Zone 8 train.

She got on.
