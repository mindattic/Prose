# Something Borrowed

*Seto maps the silence and sends the shape of it to Amara.*

The calibration appointment was at nine. Amara had forty minutes.

She was still at her bench in the Orison maintenance depot — Orison being the CorpoNation whose Zone 7 facility smelled perpetually of recycled air and sweetened nutritional supplement, the arcade-corridor smell that meant you were three levels below anything with a window. The bench ran the length of the back wall. Six stations, hers in the middle, the others dark because the overnight rotation had ended and the day shift didn't start until ten. She had the room to herself, which was why she'd come in early, and why she had Seto's archive open in the left half of her workspace instead of queued for later.

He'd sent it an hour ago. She'd been working through it since.

The map was the first thing: silence geography, he called it — white routes against the corridor grid, each one a place where information had stopped moving in a specific way. She understood what she was looking at before she finished the key. The crescent in the northeast quadrant, running out of Zone 7 into the border with 8 and 9. She recognized the orientation. She'd been inside that corridor network six times in the last quarter on standard maintenance visits.

She pulled her own visit log and laid it next to his.

The depot system wasn't built to be queried this way — laterally, across client batches, against a third-party geographic dataset. She had to assemble the comparison by hand, pulling visit records one facility at a time, checking each against the crescent's edge coordinates. It took twenty-two minutes. When she finished, the result sat there the way true things sat when you found them: inert, patient, indifferent to having been found.

Forty-one of her fifty-three Batch 44-C clients were inside the crescent.

She checked it again. She'd run the coordinates wrong once already, earlier, and caught it; this time she went slower and the number came back the same. Forty-one. The other twelve were distributed across Zone 4 and Zone 6, outside the shape entirely.

She'd been meaning to cross-check Batch 44-C against the IA's geographic data for three weeks. She hadn't, because cross-checks weren't in her certification scope, and because the anomaly log she'd been building was already the kind of documentation she would have to have a conversation about before she showed it to anyone. And because every time she opened the batch file and looked at the Type C drift pattern, the visit schedule, and the project code that didn't resolve to anything in the public protocol library, the question it pointed at was a question she needed more before she could ask.

Now she had more.

She pulled up the batch documentation — the file she'd been building incrementally, each entry dated, each delta noted against the standard degradation model. Four clients showing Type C drift. The drift characterized as non-random, which she'd written carefully, because non-random required a mechanism and she hadn't had one. She had one now. She had the crescent, and Seto's silence geography, and forty-one data points arranged inside both.

She also had a calibration appointment in thirty-seven minutes.

The client was Dani Soral. Batch 44-C, facility in Zone 8 corridor 4, one of the forty-one. Soral had come up twice in the Type C log — minor drift, within tolerance, flagged for monitoring. The appointment was standard: verification run, adjustment if needed, sign the service record. Amara had done it forty times since her certification. She could do it in her sleep.

She looked at the appointment on her schedule. Then she looked at the batch documentation. Then she looked at the project code: ORS-MAINT-7C. Still not in the public library. Still not anywhere she could find it.

The question she had not been able to ask was this: what were the maintenance visits doing, exactly, if they were not only calibrating hardware. She had the drift pattern. She had the geographic scope. She had the permissions gap in the maintenance-record system — open at least six months, still open, through which she'd been reading batch composition records and facility schedules her certification level had no business touching. She didn't have the protocol. She didn't know what happened inside the visits she had no memory of running.

She pulled her own neuretics maintenance record.

The Orison sub-contractor system showed her last three calibration appointments: March, October, and the previous March. The March appointment was the one she remembered — Clinic 9, Zone 5, standard procedure, forty minutes. She'd been there. She had the transit log.

The October appointment showed a facility in Zone 8.

She had no memory of Zone 8 in October.

She sat with her hands flat on the bench. The vent above her station pushed recycled air across the back of her neck and held the smell against the roof of her mouth. She had no memory of Zone 8 in October. She checked the transit log for that date — a Tuesday. The transit log showed a route that terminated at Zone 6 station, not Zone 8. The appointment record and the transit log described two different Tuesdays.

Someone had entered the appointment. Someone had signed the service record. The signature field showed her certification ID.

She was on the maintenance list. Not as a technician. As a client.

The depot was still empty. She checked the time: thirty-four minutes. Dani Soral. Zone 8, corridor 4. One of the forty-one.

She thought about the permissions gap. She'd found it six months ago, following a routine access-rights review that had caught a misconfigured permission tier on her account. She'd flagged it as a system error in the ticket. She hadn't closed it, because she'd needed the access to build the anomaly log, and the anomaly log was the thing she was going to have a conversation about. She had never thought about what the gap looked like from the other side — not what she could see through it, but who could see that she was looking.

Seto's last message sat at the bottom of the archive: *Freelance specialist, Zone 7. No Orison affiliation. Eight years. No sub-contractor has ever touched my hardware.*

She had the name and address of a freelance calibration specialist in Zone 6. She'd found the referral eight months ago, before the anomaly log existed, before October. She'd never made the appointment.

She could message Soral and cancel. Equipment malfunction, schedule conflict, any of the standard codes. She'd done it twice this quarter already; it wouldn't read as unusual. She could cancel, send Seto her batch documentation, and sit with what she had until she understood it well enough to do something with it.

Or she could go to the appointment.

She would see the facility. She would see whether the procedure they ran matched the protocol she had on file. She would see what Dani Soral looked like when the visit was over, whether it matched what she knew about Type C drift, whether the service record that came out the other end described the same forty minutes she had witnessed.

She would go in knowing what she knew. She wasn't sure if that was protection or exposure. She wasn't sure it mattered now.

She closed the batch documentation. She opened a reply to Seto's archive and started composing — her anomaly log, the batch records, the October appointment, the Zone 8 entry she had no transit record for. She wrote it the careful way, each fact separated from its implication, each date noted. She attached the permissions-gap ticket. She attached the project code.

Then she stopped.

She read back what she'd written. It was complete. It was documented. It was the kind of thing you sent to someone when you wanted a record to exist outside the system that held you.

Twenty-nine minutes.

She sent it. Then she picked up her kit bag and checked its contents the way she checked them every morning — calibration wand, contact patch, service tablet, the small wrench she'd carried for three years because the Zone 5 clinic's adjustment housing used a nonstandard fitting she'd never found a cleaner tool for. She zipped the bag.

She did not cancel the appointment.

She needed to know what happened inside those visits. And she had just sent the documentation to Seto, so that if she went in and something went wrong with her memory of the next forty minutes, a record would exist somewhere outside Orison's system of what she had known before she walked through the door.

She shouldered the bag and logged out of the bench. The overhead lights woke ahead of her and went dark behind her, tracking her to the exit on motion sensors, standard Orison spec — light that arrived only where a body was and nowhere it had been. She walked out into the corridor, into air that smelled like filtration membrane and tile cleaner, and started for the Zone 8 transit line.

The wrench in her kit bag had a weight she knew exactly. She had used it a hundred times. But climbing the first flight of stairs toward the surface, her right hand closed on the strap, and she felt — distinctly, in the tendons of her fingers — the resistance of a fitting that was not in her kit: a heavier head, a shorter handle, the calibrated tension of a tool turned against hardware she had never been trained to touch.

She stopped on the stairs.

The sensation was already gone. Her hand held the strap. The strap held the bag. The bag weighed what it always weighed.

She stood there two seconds, then went up. She didn't name what had happened. She filed it, the way she filed anomalies, under things she would understand once she had more data. But her hand did not fully loosen on the strap, all the way to the transit line, and she noticed that, and she let it stay.
