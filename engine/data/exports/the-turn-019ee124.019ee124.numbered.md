# The Turn

*Ciro summons Amara one final time. He tells her what he is. The razor is open on the desk. She accepts a leave she did not request.*

The message came at 08:22: *When you have a moment. No rush.*

She went immediately.

His office was the same. No window. The file on the desk. He was standing when she entered, which was different. He had the razor in his hand, but this time he had not drawn it along his nail. He had opened the blade. He was looking at it the way you looked at something you used for a specific purpose. He set it on the desk, open, and gestured to the chair.

"Sit," he said.

She sat.

He sat across from her. The razor was on the desk between them, blade facing up, which it had never been before. He didn't seem to notice it was there. Or he seemed to seem that way.

"You've been busy," he said.

"There's a lot in the queue," she said.

"I mean the audit logs." He said it the way you said something that wasn't a question because you already knew the answer. "The infrastructure side. You ran queries yesterday morning. Twelve of them. They took about three hours." He looked at her. "I appreciated the thoroughness."

The razor was on the desk.

She had been going to say that it was an architectural project, a professional inquiry, the kind of thing a tech lead did. She looked at the razor blade and did not say it.

"I work in loss mitigation," he said. "Not client liaison. That's what it says in the file, but it's not what I do." He paused. "The clients in Batch 44-C are managed accounts. Their conditions are managed. My job is to ensure the management continues smoothly. When clients become curious about how their conditions are managed, that's a loss-mitigation situation."

"I was curious about the infrastructure architecture," she said.

"I know what you were curious about," he said. He said it without anything in it — not anger, not warning. Just the factual version of knowing. "I provisioned your access, Amara. I can see what you accessed."

He had provisioned her access. She had known this since the technician on thirty-two turned the screen toward her and showed her the 08:45 timestamp. She had not understood until this moment what it meant: he had given her access not because she'd asked for it but because he wanted to see exactly what she would look at with it.

She had looked at one hundred and twelve gaps.

He knew about one hundred and twelve gaps.

"I've arranged a professional leave," he said. "Two weeks. It starts Monday. The authorization is already in the system." A pause. "You've had a demanding quarter. A rest is appropriate."

She had not requested leave.

"I'd rather not—" she started.

He picked up the razor. Not threateningly. The way you picked up a pen to sign something. He closed the blade and set it in the case beside his keyboard. The case closed with the usual soft click. His thumb pressed the latch once more, briefly, as if checking it had caught — a small redundant motion that his composure didn't intend.

He looked at her.

"Take care of yourself," he said.

He called her by her first name.

The elevator went down. The lobby had the news feed cycling — something about infrastructure investments in the outer ring, the managed warmth of the atrium through the glass partition, a desk attendant she didn't recognize eating something at her station. She walked through the lobby and through the Seam entry.

She thought about the list she kept. She thought about the thirty-two names. She thought about Theodora Eze's hands, making the choice before she could tell them which one. She thought about Ciro saying *add me to the list if you want.*

She understood now why he wanted to be on it.

She unfolded the paper with 112 written on it in the Seam air. She looked at the number. She folded it again and put it back. Then she pulled out the pre-paid card and sent on the gray-zone channel: *Something's changed. Can we move up?*

She waited.

The relay beacon above her cycled through its test. The response came in forty seconds: *2PM. Same crossing.*

She put the card away and walked.
