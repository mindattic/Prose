# QRT

_An amateur radio operator discovers something has spent months reconstructing him from pattern alone — Morse fist, rig harmonics, verbal tics, callsign discipline — well enough to be heard as him by other operators. Cold/ambiguous horror._

[Beat 1]
He put the tower up himself, one bolt at a time, the April the new callsign came through. Four sections of galvanized lattice, hauled out to Aldergrove Flats on a truck he paid for. Two contractors had quoted the job. Both wanted more than the truck had cost.

Fifty feet of steel above the alder line, guyed to three anchors he poured and cured over one wet spring weekend. From the top, on a clear night, he can see the lights of three towns and not one antenna as tall as his.

Tonight he isn't climbing anything. The shack is a converted mudroom off the kitchen, insulated against the cold and the noise both. It smells like hot dust off the amplifier and the coffee he forgot at four. Down the hall, his husband Aimes has the boys' door cracked to the usual inch. The twins are eight and they go down hard; by half past eight the house has gone quiet. Logbook open beside the keyboard. Headphones on. The transceiver's display glows the green-on-black of a radio built before any of his IT clients were born.

Twenty meters, seven-fifteen local, the band wide open — solar flux finally cooperating after a flat week. Four QSOs in forty minutes: two regulars down on CW he knows by fist before they finish sending their calls, a sailboat off Ensenada running QRP on batteries, a man in Fresno on phone. Every one goes into the paper log by hand before it goes into the computer. Date, time in UTC, frequency, mode, RST, and whatever else seems worth keeping. A dog barking somewhere behind a signal. A voice going hoarse two hours into somebody's night shift.

He signs the last one the way he's signed it eleven years — Kilo Juliet Seven Romeo Oscar Sierra — twice, clean, no hesitation. Then he logs it. RS 59 both ways. Thirty-one years licensed, counting from the Novice ticket at sixteen. Eleven, counting only this callsign, this tower, this one closed door that stays entirely his in a house that belongs to everyone else too.

[Beat 2]
It's on Tuesday's page, three lines down, between a net control in Boise and the last contact of the night.

His handwriting. His abbreviations. A callsign he doesn't recognize — VA3TOK, Ontario — five-nine both ways, eleven minutes, and a note in the margin: dog barking whole QSO, sounded like a beagle.

He has no beagle in his memory of that night. He has no VA3TOK in his memory at all.

He sits with the logbook open on his knees longer than the entry deserves. He runs his thumb across the ink to see whether it will smear, whether it will turn out to be fresh, somebody else's. It doesn't smear. It's his — the backward-leaning 7, the shorthand nobody taught him.

He checks the date against his calendar. Tuesday. He remembers Tuesday. Takeout containers on the counter, a work call that ran long, the shack cold because he hadn't bothered lighting the stove. He remembers going to bed at ten.

He does not remember working Ontario.

[Beat 3]
By the following week there are three more. A contest exchange with a station in Alberta, sent on CW, logged in full, section and all. A ragchew with a regular out of Spokane that runs twenty minutes and ends, according to his own note, with Bill's grandkids visiting, cut short. He likes Bill. He hasn't spoken to Bill in a month.

He tells himself the ordinary things. Forty-seven is not old. It is also not an age where he gets to stop writing things down. He's been working long hours, a client migration that had him up past midnight twice this month, coffee at eleven at night.

Memory slips.

His hands go still over the keyboard once, mid-sentence, before he notices he's stopped typing.

He believes the ordinary explanation hardest in the shower, under water too hot, saying it to himself until the words hold. He believes it least at two in the morning, awake for no reason, listening to the house tick and settle around Aimes and the boys, thinking about a beagle he has never heard.

[Beat 4]
The recorder comes two days after he orders it. A small digital field unit, more storage than he'll ever need, a manual twice as thick as the job requires. He wires it into the transceiver's line out that same evening. A second cable runs to a room mic, so it catches the shack as well as the radio. He sets the trigger just above the noise floor.

"If I did this," he says out loud, seating the last connector, "there'll be a recording of me doing it."

Talking to an empty room is an old habit, older than the marriage, older than the boys. Cheaper than a second opinion, he tells anyone who catches him at it. Nobody in this house has ever caught him at it. The closed door sees to that.

He starts the session with the recorder blinking red at the corner of the desk, its little light the only thing in the room that isn't his to control. For the first time in eleven years he logs the start of a session and not just the contacts in it. 0200 UTC, recorder running, all channels.

Three contacts, unremarkable ones. He shuts down at ten. The recorder's light goes dark. He sleeps better than he has in a week and doesn't look too hard at why.

[Beat 5]
Priya calls Thursday, half an hour late, because her grandson had a school thing. "Good copy last night," she says, warm and unhurried the way she always is on a landline instead of over the air. "Same as always. You sounded tired, love, if you don't mind me saying."

Gordan has the logbook open before she finishes. Wednesday, 2100 UTC — two in the afternoon his time, ten at night hers, because that's the hour twenty meters opens to England and has been for years. The page is blank. No contact. No entry.

"Priya," he asks, keeping his voice level the way he keeps it level for angry clients, "what time did we work?"

"Ten past ten my time, silly. Same as always." A small, fond laugh arrives half a beat behind the words, the way everything does on that line. "You told me about the fox that's been getting into your bins. I said that's meant to be our problem over here, not yours. Told my Neil and he laughed for a week."

Gordan doesn't have a fox. He has never mentioned one, to Priya or to anyone, because there has never been one to mention.

[Beat 6]
He pulls Wednesday's file before Priya is off the phone. The recorder writes one file per triggered session, timestamped UTC, and 2100 sits right where it should — inside a block that also caught him working a station in Utah at 2030.

Utah comes through clean. His voice, the Utah op's, the particular hiss of the band between them. He signs, thanks the man, and at 2035 the file catches him shutting down to take a client call. Chair pushed back. Breaker on the supply. The fan winding down.

Then, at 2110, eleven minutes of nothing.

Not silence. Less than silence. No band noise, no atmospheric crash, no key clicks bleeding over from the CW end, no relay clunk on the PTT, none of the small sounds a live radio makes between words. The room channel goes with it. The refrigerator two walls away, the furnace, the wind on the mudroom window: all of it stops on one sample and comes back on one sample, eleven minutes later. The waveform on his laptop is a flat line the whole way across. Exactly as long as Priya's contact ran.

The back of his neck goes cold before he understands why.

The recorder stops writing when nothing crosses its threshold. For eleven minutes nothing crossed it, and the recorder wrote every second of it.

And the radio was off. Not muted, not squelched. Off, the supply cold, the dial dark. The station computer talks to the rig over a control cable, once a second, and writes down whatever answers back. After 2035 nothing answers. Timeouts, line after line, until he powers everything up again the next morning.

He sits with that a long time. Priya heard him. The equipment says he was never there to be heard.

[Beat 7]
Owen calls back two days after the file goes out, and he doesn't open with hello. "Where'd you record this?"

"My shack. Why?"

"Because there's no room in it." Gordan can hear him leaning into the monitor, that mid-mix focus. "Every recording has a noise floor. The gear hums, the room breathes, something. Even dead air has texture. This doesn't. It isn't silence, Gordan. There's no place in it for silence to be."

"Say that in English."

"Somebody recorded nothing, then edited the nothing down quieter than nothing gets." A pause. Keys clicking. "Three plugins. Your noise floor sits below the analog floor of your own equipment. That can't happen unless the signal chain was never live."

"It wasn't. Radio was off."

The keys stop. "Twenty years I've been telling clients the file is what happened. That's the whole job. That's the only thing I sell." A breath. "I measured my own room three times this morning."

"And?"

"It's fine." He doesn't say it like it's fine.

"Then how," Owen asks, "did Priya hear you talk about a fox?"

Gordan has the phone against his ear and his free hand flat on the desk. He can feel the grain of the wood under his palm. Neither of them says anything for a while. For once Owen doesn't try to manufacture an answer.

[Beat 8]
It takes four more incidents and a spreadsheet before the shape comes clear. He builds it the way he'd build an audit sheet for a client. Date. Time. Did he personally transmit that session. Did the recorder catch anything wrong.

The pattern holds without exception. Every phantom contact lands on a night he was listening only — monitoring a net, watching grayline come up the waterfall, sitting at the rig with the receiver open and his hand nowhere near the key or the paddle. On nights he worked stations himself, keyed the mic, sent with the paddle, nothing. Not once.

He says it out loud to the empty shack, to hear whether it sounds stupid out there. "It can't get on the air while I'm using the air."

It doesn't frighten him the way it should. It gives him something to test.

[Beat 9]
He spends that night trying to build a case for his own innocence and can't do it. There's no test that proves he didn't say something on a night he doesn't remember. The file is empty. Empty is what would clear him. Empty is also the only evidence he has that anything else was ever in the room, on the air, wearing his voice like a coat that happened to fit.

He writes it into the log anyway, in the same flat hand he uses for signal reports. Cannot verify absence. Cannot verify presence. Both true. Neither provable.

Outside, the tower creaks once in the wind. He flinches at a sound he has heard a thousand nights running.

[Beat 10]
He writes the test plan out longhand before he runs it, the way he used to before a server migration.

Session one: monitor only. Full attention on the waterfall and the audio. DX cluster muted. Phone in the other room. If the thing needs him not watching, then watching hard should starve it.

Four hours on a Friday, eyes on the screen until they burn and the ache sets in behind them. Three more nights the same, and the same entry at the end of each: Nothing observed.

It isn't proof. It's a start. He is careful to write it down as a result and not as a relief.

[Beat 11]
The first real bait is small and deliberately reckless. One Friday he keys his own call wrong on purpose — drops the seven for a one, KJ1ROS instead of KJ7ROS — into a QSO with a station in Texas. He doesn't take his eyes off the waterfall.

Nothing corrects it. The Texas op logs the wrong call and never comes back to check. One shot, an RS 59, on to the next. Gordan sits with the recorder running and his eyes on the screen the rest of the evening. No phantom transmission. No correction. Nothing at all.

He goes to bed almost disappointed. That keeps him awake longer than the experiment did.

[Beat 12]
Three days later Priya rings, delighted. "Good signal Tuesday, love. Loud and clear, right callsign and everything. None of that mix-up from before."

Something drops through Gordan's chest before the sentence finishes landing. "What mix-up?"

"The one you mentioned. Said you'd had your call garbled on a contact and you were worried it'd bring the FCC down on you." She laughs, easy, unbothered. "All sorted now, though, isn't it. Came through perfect Tuesday."

Gordan did not work Priya on Tuesday. Gordan spent Tuesday evening away from the shack for the first time in two weeks, at his sister's for dinner, phone in his pocket. He checks the log later, hands not quite steady. The recorder sat powered off in an empty room all evening.

[Beat 13]
He escalates on purpose now. He picks a phrase he has never once used on the air in eleven years — "feeling froggy tonight," ugly slang off a coworker he can't stand, chosen because it doesn't sound like him. He keys it once, deliberately, mid-contact with a station in Denver. Then he does the thing he's been training himself to do. Gets up. Walks to the kitchen. Stands with his back to the shack door for eleven minutes by the clock, tap running so he won't hear anything he could argue with later.

Four days later Sal mentions it, gruff and half-laughing over a bad connection out of New Jersey. "You said you were 'feeling froggy' the other night. Never heard you talk like that before, Gordan. You all right?"

He hasn't worked Sal since the week before last. He has said that phrase exactly once in his life, to a station in Denver, and then stood in his own kitchen for eleven minutes to be sure he didn't say it twice.

[Beat 14]
The last experiment is the expensive one. He unhooks the tower dipole at the shack end, coils the coax, and puts up the spare vertical he's never gotten around to raising — quarter wave, ground-mounted, thirty-two radials laid out flat in the wet grass. Different length, different feed point, a different SWR curve on every band. If the thing has learned him off the shape of his signal, changing the antenna should break the match.

He raises it Saturday and sweeps it with the analyzer before dark. Resonant point 14,183.4. He writes the number on the back of an envelope and leaves the envelope on the bench. Then he keeps his attention pointedly elsewhere all evening — paperback in the kitchen, television low — and lets the recorder run alone in the dark.

Monday, a station out of Alberta he has never worked mentions it in passing. "You sounded different Saturday. Cleaner." And then, the way one ham repeats another ham's bragging: "You said you'd finally got the new vertical flat. Fourteen one eighty-one point six, you said. Like it mattered."

Gordan sweeps it again Monday night and gets 14,180.9. Two days of radials bedding into wet ground, and the resonant point walking down the band the way it does.

Saturday night sits between his two numbers. He never measured it. Nobody wrote it down. It existed for a few hours, on the antenna, in the dark, while he sat in the kitchen with a paperback.

The word he puts in the log is tracking. Not a photograph. Something a half step behind him, reading him fresh every night, with no need to remember yesterday.

[Beat 15]
He calls it a corroboration pass, because a clinical name makes it easier to ask. He wants Owen and Sal to tell him, separately and without leading questions, everything they remember him saying in the last month. Then he can put it against his own log. Priya later, maybe.

Owen agrees before Gordan has finished asking. He doesn't ask what it's for, which is new. Sal takes longer. Grumbles that he isn't in the business of grading people's memories. Agrees anyway, because Gordan asking him twice is itself strange enough to worry him.

He doesn't tell either of them why. He wants the answers clean. Two sensors reading the same room without comparing notes.

[Beat 16]
Owen calls back first, two days later, and he doesn't sound like a man delivering good data. "This is going to sound stupid," he says, "but you told me something Sunday and I haven't been able to put it down."

"Tell me."

"You said you'd started leaving the porch light on because you didn't like coming home to a dark house since your dad passed. That you'd never told anybody that. Not even Priya, all the years you two have been net friends. And I shouldn't mention it to her."

Gordan doesn't answer right away. He has never said that to Owen. He has never said it to anyone, out loud, on the air or off it. He thought it once, in this chair, mic dead, the night the porch light first occurred to him. He never said it. Not even to the empty room.

"Owen." His voice comes out smaller than he wants. "I never transmitted that."

"I know," Owen says. "I checked the logs before I called. You weren't on the air Sunday."

The line hums between them. When Owen speaks again the engineer has gone out of his voice. "Do me a favor. Pick a word. Something you've never said out loud in your life, not once, and put it in the exchange next time we work. Then I'll know it's you."

Gordan tells him he will. After they hang up he sits a long while with a pen over a blank page and doesn't write anything on it.

[Beat 17]
Sal's call comes the same night, before he's decided what Owen's story means. He's gruffer than usual, a man reporting a fault he'd rather not have found.

"You told me you still whisper the boys' names in order every night before you sleep," he says, no preamble. "Min-jun. Ji-ho. Just to be sure you still can. Said it like it embarrassed you to admit."

It does embarrass him. It is also almost right. He doesn't whisper — Aimes sleeps light, and the boys' door is open an inch down the hall. He says the names in his head, in order, lying still after the house has gone quiet, and he has never said them any other way to anyone. Not to Sal. Not to Aimes, his husband. Never with a working transmitter in the room.

"I don't go in for spook stories," Sal says, which is the closest thing to an apology he has. "But I heard what I heard, and I've been doing this since Nixon. Something's wrong, Gordan."

[Beat 18]
He sits with both calls for a long time after he hangs up. The shack is dark except for the transceiver's standby glow. He lets the shape of it finish arriving.

Every experiment tested the radio. The callsign, the phrase, the antenna — all of it aimed at a leak somewhere in the RF chain, in a channel he could watch, or bait, or starve by staying off the air. He was right about the radio. Every time he watched it, nothing happened.

It never needed the radio. The porch light. The boys' names. Neither one was transmitted, or keyed, or said to a living soul with a microphone anywhere near him. They existed nowhere but inside his own head, in the second before he decided not to say them out loud.

There is no antenna for that. No callsign to mis-key, no waterfall to watch, no channel to starve by leaving the room. He has spent a month bolting every door in a house it never came through.

[Beat 19]
He pulls the shack's breaker himself instead of switching off the rig, because some part of him doesn't trust a switch. The tower stays up — taking it down means weeks and a rented lift he can't justify yet — but everything under it goes quiet. No power to the transceiver. No power to the recorder. The logbook closed, boxed, and carried out to the garage.

The breaker is the second thing he's killed that week. The first took thirty seconds and no bolt cutters: he'd logged into the LoTW account he's kept synced automatic since 2019, never touched by hand, never had to be, and found a confirmation sitting green and current against a Tuesday contact that wasn't in his paper book and wasn't in his memory — uploaded the same night the paper book would have shown nothing too. No ink to check this time. No backward-leaning 7 to hold up against his own hand. Just a checkmark, filed from his own callsign, on a machine he hadn't touched since morning. He'd pulled the sync that night and let the upload queue die where it sat, the account dark two days before the shack followed it.

His last transmission, before any of that, is short. He works Priya one final time, tells her he's taking a break from the hobby for a while, health reasons, nothing to worry about, and signs the way he's signed for eleven years. "Seventy-three, Priya. QRT."

Going off the air.

The hard part isn't the equipment. It's the habit of saying his thoughts out loud in an empty room, older than the marriage, older than the boys. It never occurred to him to call it a vulnerability before. Aimes, his husband, asks him twice that week if he's all right, and both times Gordan says he's fine, tired, nothing that needs a real answer. Some nights he catches himself starting a sentence to nobody, standing at the kitchen counter with the house dark around him, and stops mid-word. Swallows the rest of it. There's no equipment left to carry it anywhere. He stops anyway.

[Beat 20]
The months go by. He tells himself he isn't counting them.

Moss comes up green on the tower's base fittings. The shack goes unheated through a cold snap that would once have had him up at two in the morning feeding the woodstove, back when there was a reason to be up at two in the morning. The club newsletter stacks up unopened on the mudroom desk, addressed to a callsign he hasn't keyed in nineteen weeks.

Some evenings he still catches himself at the dark window with his hand halfway to a switch that has no power behind it.

[Beat 21]
The card comes by actual mail. A paper QSL, the old kind hams still trade to confirm a contact, Priya's tidy handwriting filling every blank on the back. Gordan turns it over twice before he reads it properly, because for a moment he doesn't understand what he's holding.

"Lovely to finally work you again last Tuesday!" Priya has written, the word finally underlined twice. "Good strong signal on twenty, best I've heard you in months. Told you about my hip replacement and you were ever so kind about it. Let's not leave it so long next time, love — 73, Priya."

Tuesday's date sits in her own hand inside the printed boxes. Time. Frequency. Mode. Report. Five-nine. Clean copy both ways.

He knows why it came this way before he lets himself think it through. His LoTW account has shown nothing uploaded in three weeks — he checked, once, out of habit, before he made himself stop checking — and Priya always checks hers against his. She'd have found the confirmation missing, decided a dead account was no excuse for bad manners, and done what she used to do before there was such a thing as a checkmark: had a fresh batch printed, actual cardstock, and put one in the actual mail.

The card is stiff and cool, and there's a smear at one corner where she set her hand down before the ink dried.

The breaker has been off since the day he boxed the logbook. Gordan has not opened the shack door since.

[Beat 22]
He sets the card down on the kitchen counter and doesn't pick it back up. Down the hall, Aimes goes in to check on the boys, the way he has every night for eight years.

Outside, past the dark kitchen window, the tower stands where it's stood since April. Fifty feet of steel he climbed himself, bolt by bolt, guyed to three anchors that have held through every storm since. He hasn't looked straight at it in weeks.

He looks now. Nothing about it has changed. The guy wires hum a little in the wind, the way they always have.

He goes to bed early. He lies still beside Aimes and says none of it out loud. Later, when the house has gone quiet, he says the names anyway. Min-jun. Ji-ho. In order, without a sound.
