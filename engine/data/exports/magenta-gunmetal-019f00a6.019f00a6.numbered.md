# Magenta & Gunmetal

_Quintessential GLMZ run: team heist → corp escalation → True Lies / Die Hard finale. No magic. Pure tech, cyberware, and competence under pressure._

## Magenta & Gunmetal

[Beat 1]
The bar ran magenta.

Not from any design intention Rook could see — the decor was the usual Z3 pretension, recycled pale wood and low-hung brass fixtures that someone had decided communicated warmth. But the east wall held a full-panel advertisement for Axiom BioNanics, Class II medical nano, the kind of installation that pulsed on a slow three-second interval. That particular corporate pink bled across the mirror behind the bar, caught in the glassware, and laid itself over every white shirt in the room like a second skin. The bartender moved through it, oblivious. Two suits at the bar were discussing something in low voices, their faces washed in it.

Rook stood at the entrance for eleven seconds and counted exits. Three. Service corridor to the left, main entrance behind her, fire egress past the bathrooms in the back. Gault was at the corner table, which meant she had sightlines on all of them and he had one. She had told him, the first time they worked together, that she preferred corners, and he'd remembered. That was either professional courtesy or the kind of manipulation a man did without thinking about it, and she hadn't decided which.

He saw her. He didn't wave.

She crossed the room and catalogued the other tables by habit: two solo drinkers, a group of four celebrating something and paying attention to nothing, a woman near the window who had ordered food and was actually reading, which was rare enough to note. No security posture. Nothing that looked like a follow. Gault had run clean, then, or he'd run dirty somewhere she hadn't caught yet.

She sat down.

"You look like you've been here a while," she said.

"Half an hour." He had a glass of something amber in front of him, and it wasn't his first. "I like to settle in."

"I know."

That closed the small talk. Gault was fifty-two and had the face of a man who had been carefully reasonable for a very long time — not soft, just worn smooth. He dressed well, always, which was its own kind of armor. He said nothing while she picked up the menu and set it back down without reading it, while the server came and she ordered mineral water and the server left.

"Five-person job," Gault said. "I have three committed. You'd be number four, but I want you running it."

"Who are the three?"

"Agwu. Väinämöinen. Okafor."

She knew Agwu by reputation and one shared contact who spoke highly of her read speed. Väinämöinen she'd seen work once from a distance, on a job she wasn't part of, and he'd been exactly what you wanted: present when required, absent when not, and apparently incapable of panic. Okafor was newer to her, but the name came attached to clean margins on three separate jobs she knew about, which mattered more than longevity did.

"Fifth?" she said.

"Nakamura-Diallo. QCE rider, drone support. She's expensive, but she's consistent."

"What's the job?"

Gault put both hands around his glass. "Extraction. Corporate researcher. Axiom BioNanics, Research Compound 7, Z5."

Rook looked at the advertisement on the wall. The pulse cycle was at its peak, the whole room going pink for a moment before it ebbed back. She looked at Gault again.

"Client?"

"Anonymous, through the standard chain. I've used this relay four times. It's clean."

"Axiom BioNanics is Nano Triumvirate," she said. "Compound 7 won't be light security."

"No," Gault agreed. "It won't."

"Who's the researcher?"

"Dr. Nadia Vasquez-Park. She's listed internally as staff, Ohara division. That's all the client is offering at intake."

Ohara was Axiom's soft-nano line, Class II medical, the kind that went in with a prescription and a consent form and built targeted structures in your blood over three months. Which meant Vasquez-Park was working on something the client wanted, or something the client wanted stopped, or something the client wanted to be sure she hadn't finished yet. Each of those was a different job once you were inside the wire, and she had no way yet to tell them apart.

Rook filed it.

"Payment," she said.

"Forty thousand QUANTA, five-way split. Eight each."

She turned the number over. Eight thousand QUANTA for a compound extraction with five bodies against a high-security target was not generous, and it was not insultingly low. It was the number that said the client had done the research and knew exactly what the job was worth to someone deciding whether to take it — calibrated to draw competence without drawing the kind of attention that came with a richer offer. She had seen that calibration before, and it told her more about the anonymous client than the anonymity did.

"Timeline?" she said.

"Seventy-two hours to commit. After that the offer closes."

"I'll want the compound layout."

"I can get you the public filings. Anything past the perimeter is on you."

She nodded. She had already known that. The water she'd ordered arrived, and she wrapped both hands around the glass and looked at Gault for a moment without saying anything. He had the good sense not to fill the silence.

"I'll run it," she said.

He nodded slowly. "I'll tell the others to expect contact."

She stood, left forty QUANTA on the table for the water she'd barely touched, and walked back through the magenta room and out the main entrance into the street. Z3 at evening was all brass-colored ambient light and the smell of recycled air, and somewhere a block over a QCE rack hummed under load. This was the real GLMZ — gunmetal and neon and the specific cold that came off the lake when the ventilation corridors lined up wrong.

She pulled her comm and opened a blank contact thread.

The job had already begun. She was just the last one to know it.

[Beat 2]
The corpo lobby at Z3-Meridian stretched three stories of imported Baltic marble and made no apologies for it. Rook stood at the mezzanine rail with a coffee she wasn't drinking and watched Blessing Agwu run a building manager.

Agwu was deep in it. The face was different — not dramatically, just precisely. The jaw was set at a slightly different angle, the brow carried lower, the whole arrangement settling into the particular expression of someone who had been tolerating a subordinate's excuses for eleven minutes and had four more of patience left before the paperwork started. Forty-three muscles, individually addressed. Rook had read the technical writeup once and then stopped, because there were things she could know about a person and things that made her feel like she was watching surgery performed on a friend.

She waited for the pivot. It came in thirty seconds. Agwu glanced at something on her lenses, the building manager suddenly found his own answers satisfying, and Blessing Agwu walked away from the conversation with the unhurried certainty of someone closing a door she'd already measured to the millimeter.

She saw Rook at the rail. Nothing on her face changed, which was itself a kind of greeting.

They went outside. The mezzanine coffee turned out to be useful after all — Rook set it on a planter ledge and let the Z3 midday light do what it did to everything up here, which was to make even a job conversation feel like a board meeting between people who owned things.

"Agwu," Rook said.

"You interrupted something."

"It looked finished."

"It was nearly finished. There's a difference." Agwu folded her hands. "What do you need?"

Rook gave her the outline in sixty seconds. One target building. One window of access. Someone who could wear whoever the room required. She said the number last: Φ 8,000.

Agwu looked past her at the cityline for a moment. It wasn't hesitation. It was consideration, the kind that already had a shape.

"I need a Level IV fabrication suite," she said. "The Daedalic Modular. They cost Φ 22,000 and the waiting list is eight months. I am shortcutting the waiting list."

"That's not a question."

"No." Agwu looked back at her. "Send me the structural brief. No shorter than six pages. I need to understand the org before I dress it."

Boiler was in a warehouse near the Z4/Z5 seam, which was exactly where Rook had expected to find him, because that particular stretch of Bloom Quarter gray market held three decommissioned Arcturus civil logistics buildings, and Mikkeli Väinämöinen found decommissioned buildings philosophically interesting.

He was standing in front of a load-bearing wall with his hands in his pockets and his head tilted, studying it the way some people studied art.

"Rook," he said, without turning around.

"Boiler."

"This column's wrong." He pointed at a reinforced concrete pillar that looked, to Rook, identical to every other reinforced concrete pillar in the warehouse. "Supposed to be carrying the northwest corner, but somebody poured the aggregate at the wrong cure temperature. She's been holding on pride for fifteen years."

He finally turned. He was very large. He was always very large — Rook had once watched him lift a floor safe with the incurious ease of a man who found the task slightly beneath his attention. "You're not here about the column," he said.

"No."

She gave him the outline. He listened with his arms crossed and his weight back on his heels, nodding at specific points — not the money, not the target, but the moments in the plan where structural knowledge would carry the load.

"What's the ceiling height in the secondary access corridor?" he asked.

"eleven feet."

"Good." He seemed relieved on behalf of the ceiling. "Some of those Z2 retrofits go down to 2.8. I have to think about myself."

"Φ 8,000."

"Yeah, all right. My dehumidifier destroyed itself, and I refuse to buy Arclight brand again." He turned back to the wall. "Send me whatever you have on the sub-basement. I like to know what I'm standing on."

---

The noodle shop on Z2-Orlanda ran a lunchtime crowd that peaked and receded in twenty-minute waves. Between waves, the small table by the frosted partition was reliably empty, except for the person who needed it empty.

Tem Okafor was sitting at that table, staring at the wall, holding a pair of chopsticks they had not used.

Rook sat across from them. Okafor's eyes did not move toward her. Their expression had the loose, slightly inward quality of someone whose processing was mostly happening elsewhere — not absent, just distributed across more rooms than this one. The neuretics burned a thin line of warmth along their left temple, commercial Atlas-grade. You could get the surgery cheaper, but the bandwidth cost you the difference.

"Thirty seconds," Okafor said — to the wall, or to the room, or to something they were currently routing through the noodle shop's network like a courier through a mountain pass. "Choi, hold the eastern egress. Yeah. Yeah. No, the residential trunk is a choke, go around it." A beat, and then, to Rook: "Sorry."

She waited the thirty seconds. Okafor set down the chopsticks.

"What's the network profile?" they asked.

"One private Arcturus-adjacent subnet, three legacy access nodes in the east wing, one high-security isolated loop around the primary vault systems."

Okafor's eyes moved slightly, cataloging. "The isolated loop's physical?"

"Air-gapped, with a mechanical failover."

"Good. I hate arguing with physical." They picked up the chopsticks again and turned them over in their hands. "My three best agents need licensing extensions. Gray-market licensing, so not cheap. I'd rather pay for clean access time than spend the run fighting a throttle."

Rook said the number.

"Fine." Okafor looked back at the wall, already half-elsewhere. "Send me the floor schematics. I need to see the topography. And tell whoever else you're running that I'll need two hours of undisturbed prep before breach."

---

The diner at Z2-Fass was gunmetal and grease — formica counters, strip lighting with one bulb running magenta where the white had given out, the particular ambiance of a room that had stopped caring about ambiance sometime before Rook was born. It was the kind of place that left you alone, which was the only amenity it advertised.

Remi Nakamura-Diallo was at a corner table, eyes rolled back.

Four crawlers sat on the table. Two more rested on the floor near her chair, oriented outward. One held the windowsill. Her tea had gone cold in the way tea goes cold when no one has looked at it in forty minutes. She was breathing at about eight breaths a minute, completely still, except for one hand that made a small adjustment every few minutes — a finger extending, then curling back — mirroring some movement she was making in a space that wasn't here. The QCE had her optic nerves; whatever she was seeing, she was seeing it through the frames, and her body had been left behind at the table like a coat over a chair.

Rook sat down across from her, ordered tea she would also not drink, and waited.

At eleven minutes, Remi Nakamura-Diallo's eyes came back. They rolled forward and found their focus the way a lens finds it, in one smooth correction, and the body behind them reoccupied itself.

She blinked once. Looked at the crawlers on the table as if counting them. Then at Rook.

"How many?"

"Seven access points. Maybe nine, if the east wing egress is what I think it is."

Nakamura-Diallo looked at her crawlers again. "I have seven operational. Three are named. The crew names them," she said. It wasn't a complaint, and it wasn't quite resignation either. "I've stopped arguing."

"Φ 8,000."

A pause. She picked up the cold tea and drank half of it without expression, the way a person refuels.

"Send me the floor plan. I'll need sight lines for the approach corridors." She set down the cup and looked at the window. Her hand moved again, checking something in a layer of attention Rook didn't have access to. "I want to do a pre-run tomorrow night. Just me."

"Done."

Five people. Five agreements. Rook walked out of the diner into the particular quality of Z2 afternoon light, which was the same as every other kind of afternoon light in the GLMZ — refracted, filtered, carrying down to the street whatever the towers had decided not to keep for themselves.

She sent five identical messages: *Brief drops in six hours.*

Then she started thinking about the building.

[Beat 3]
The café across from Compound 7 served two things worth drinking: the drip and the view. Rook had been nursing the first since ten past six. The second cost nothing.

Axiom BioNanics Research Compound 7 occupied most of the Z5 block between Morrow and the freight rail, and it wore its brand the way the brand wanted to be worn. Magenta wall panels framed every entrance. Magenta trim ran the lengths of the transit pod bays. The logo scrolled in four-foot repeats along the upper parapet, where the building couldn't decide whether it was a research facility or a billboard for one. Through the front glass the lobby was the magenta aquarium: floor, signage, and a soft arterial wash that made the two guards behind the security desk look like people waiting to be born.

Rook kept her eyes on the east-facing entrance, her coffee at her left hand, and her phone face-down on the table. She was counting.

---

Vox's voice arrived through the bone-conduction strip behind her left ear at 6:12 PM, flat and spatial, the way they always reported.

"Outer ring is settling," Vox said. "Pink wall was thinking about us around noon. It stopped."

"Stopped or paused?" Rook asked.

"Stopped. Our agents were moving slow enough that the pattern read as noise, so the wall filed us under ambient." A pause. "It's a very confident wall."

The pink wall was Axiom's outer security AI, and Vox's gray-market agents had been walking it all day at a pace Rook had approved before breakfast — slow enough to read as background traffic, deliberate enough to map what mattered. The wall believed it knew what noise looked like. Vox's job was to keep it right about that until the moment it wasn't.

"Camera system?" Rook asked.

"Ninety-second maintenance window," Vox said. "It exists. It has always existed. The rotation drops at the seam between two subroutines that were written by different teams back in 2218. The team that would have caught it got laid off in 2221. Nobody left has authorization to touch the camera firmware, and nobody with that authorization is left." They said it the way you'd describe a door left unlocked by someone who no longer lived at the address. "The window recurs every four hours and forty-three minutes. The next one after our entry window lands at 11:17 PM."

---

Scout's body was three blocks east in the safe room they'd rented for the week, sitting upright in a chair with her hands loose in her lap and her eyes rolled to white. She could not see the chair. The QCE had severed her optic nerve at jack-in, and she was somewhere inside Compound 7's HVAC secondary loop, pushing her crawlers ahead through the ducting while the relay kept her audible to the crew.

"East corridor," Scout said. Her voice came through even and dry, the way it always did when she was riding the link and the visual cortex had nothing left to argue with. "Maintenance entrance. Two cameras, neither one on the door."

"Angle?" Rook asked.

"One covers the corridor junction. One covers the service bay. The door itself sits in the gap between them. Frame is steel, older than the build — retrofitted." A pause that meant she was easing a crawler around something. "Guard rotation through the east corridor runs forty minutes. They don't check the maintenance bay between rotations. They check the log."

"How often is the log accurate?" Rook asked.

"Maintenance crews use it to clock out early," Scout said. "So it's consistently wrong, but wrong in a predictable direction."

The lobby, she confirmed at 6:28, was everything the exterior had promised. Magenta floor panels. Magenta signage. A magenta ambient wash from ceiling panels with no visible source and no visible switch. Two guards at the desk, weapons exposed, posture slack — the kind of slack that meant the shift was nearly over and nothing had happened all day.

---

Lace had sat with her target — she described him afterward as a man with a name she'd already let go of — for forty minutes outside a coffee cart on Morrow Street, which put her sixty feet from the compound's contractor entrance. She had not asked him a single question that sounded like one.

She mentioned that her firm was bidding on a support contract for a couple of Z5 facilities and that the paperwork was going to kill her before the work did. He laughed and said he knew exactly what she meant. Somewhere in the next twenty minutes he told her that contractor inspection used the north service bay off Morrow, that the badge color for inspection access this quarter was yellow — not the visitor yellow, the contractor yellow, which was more of a gold, really — and that the facility manager was named Okonkwo. Petra Okonkwo. She'd taken the role three months ago and was already making herself felt in the scheduling system.

Lace wrote none of it down. She walked two blocks before she recited it into the crew channel, flat and in order, like reading off a list she'd had in her hand the whole time.

---

At 6:44 Rook ordered a second coffee she didn't need and watched the lobby entrance.

At 6:47 the lobby doors opened and Dr. Nadia Vasquez-Park walked out.

She walked the way someone walks when they believe they know who is watching them and have decided it doesn't matter — which was different from not caring, and different from not knowing, and Rook spent three seconds sorting the distinctions while Vasquez-Park crossed to the transit pod bay and scanned her wrist against the reader. The pod arrived in under forty seconds, which was fast. She boarded alone. She did not look at her phone while she waited, and she did not look back at the building she'd just left.

The pod pulled east toward the Z5 transfer hub. Rook noted the time and finished her coffee.

---

"Rail bridge," Boiler said over the channel, from wherever he'd been for the last two hours. He had gone out to read the compound's exterior the way he always did — walking the perimeter at the pace of a man with nowhere in particular to be, clocking load tolerances and sightlines and what the ground was doing under the pavement. "Two hundred feet north. Freight elevated. Z5 spur."

"Noted," Rook said.

"Load capacity's rated for a Mikkola Class Four hauler," Boiler said, which was forty-eight metric tons, and he offered no elaboration and asked for none, and the channel went quiet.

---

By nine o'clock Rook had it spread across the table in the apartment: the camera window, the maintenance entrance, the badge color, the manager's name, the guard rotation, Scout's corridor map, Vox's timing data. She assembled it the way she assembled everything — start with the seams, find where the pieces didn't quite meet, and decide before sleeping what she meant to do about each gap.

The seams were small. The window was real. The entrance was usable. Okonkwo was a name she'd need to carry, and now she carried it.

She left Vasquez-Park for last. She looked at what she'd written: *6:47 PM, lobby exit, pod bay, east transfer, alone, always alone, forty-second pod response, no phone.*

A forty-second pod response in Z5 at quarter to seven ran three standard deviations faster than ambient traffic. You got a response time like that only if the transit system was routing for you specifically, which required a standing priority flag on the account, which required someone to set it.

Vasquez-Park hadn't set it for herself. And in thirty hours of observation, Vasquez-Park had never once looked back at the building when she left it, never checked whether the café across the street held the same two customers it had held the day before. She moved like someone who had already been promised she was safe.

Rook filed it as a variable. Not a problem. Not yet.

She pulled the 48-hour clock up on her phone, looked at it, and laid it face-down.

[Beat 4]
The room costs Φ 18 a night, which explains the smell — recycled air pushed through ductwork that hadn't been serviced since the last government that cared about such things. A table. Five chairs, and a crate pressed into service as a sixth. A portable display panel leaned against the wall, one edge held up by a strip of adhesive tape. No windows. The kind of room you rent when you need a room and nothing else the room could offer.

Rook stood at the panel and walked them through it.

"Lace goes in first. Contract inspector, Infrastructure Safety Bureau — real bureau, and nobody at Axiom knows what their inspectors actually look like, which is the whole reason the bureau is useful to us. The badge came off the maintenance worker. It carries regulatory-compliance access for the east corridor facility cluster, so it clears the lobby desk without a secondary check. She asks for the facility manager by name."

"Petra Okonkwo," Lace said.

"Petra Okonkwo. She goes into the building. She makes a distraction. How and when is her call — once she's inside, she reads the room better than anyone here could from a diagram."

Lace was already watching the panel with the focused look she got when she was measuring the slope of authority in a space. "What does the badge rate?" she asked. "Facility access, or inspection access?"

"Inspection. ISB regulatory compliance. She can demand entry to any space covered under the 2218 Infrastructure Safety Mandate. Okonkwo can push back on procedure, but not on the threshold of a door."

Lace nodded once. She had already filed it.

"While Lace is inside," Rook went on, "Vox's agents take the camera maintenance window. Ninety seconds. The east corridor and the lobby run looped footage for the length of the gap. It's an unpatched hole left over from the 2221 layoffs — Axiom cut the network maintenance team before they closed it. The window has been opening every forty-eight hours ever since, which means it's reliable, which means we can time to it."

Vox sat with their eyes not quite resting on the room. Three of their agents had been running since yesterday, and whatever those agents were telling them was audible to no one else at the table. "We ran three simulations," they said. "It isn't the wall that beats you. It's what the wall expects you to do."

Boiler looked at them. He had a large, patient face that was good at waiting for an explanation to arrive.

"Axiom's security network expects standard intrusion vectors," Vox said. "External access, badge spoofing, signal intercept. Our agents come in underneath the maintenance cycle — not through it. The security AI is watching for the wrong kind of entry."

"And the ninety seconds is ours?" Rook asked.

"The ninety seconds is ours."

Rook moved to the eastern approach on the display. "During the blind, I walk in through the east maintenance entrance. I bring Vasquez-Park out the same door. Forty meters east is the transit corridor — Scout's crawlers have confirmed the route three times in the last twelve hours. We walk it. Nothing else."

Scout sat at the far end of the table with her crawler case open in front of her. She wasn't looking at any of them. Her fingers moved in small increments against the lip of the case, adjustments to a configuration only she could see. She did not look up.

"Scout holds the route," Rook said. "One crawler at the maintenance door, one in the east corridor, one at the transit entrance. If anything moves wrong before I have Vasquez-Park clear, I'll hear it from her before I see it."

Scout's fingers stopped.

That was the confirmation.

Boiler had been sitting with his arms crossed and his weight tipped back, the way he sat when something had already occurred to him and he was deciding whether it was worth saying. "Two hundred meters?" he asked. "On the south side?"

"Two hundred meters south. You're with the vehicle."

He nodded. His eyes went to the display for a moment — to the elevated freight corridor drawn along the southeast approach, the bridge. He looked at it. He didn't say anything about it. Rook didn't ask.

"The vehicle's there if we need it," Rook said. "The plan doesn't call for it."

"I know," Boiler said. He sounded genuinely at peace with that. "You're saying it in case something goes wrong."

"I'm saying it because it's the plan."

He smiled. It was a very warm smile for a man who had once been formally cited for a structural incident. "Right. Yes."

Rook looked at the panel. The route was clean. The timing held. The cover was solid. The gap was documented, reliable, and theirs.

She had run harder extractions than this. A pull from a Lotus-adjacent facility in Z9. A two-day operation out of a building with active security rotating on six-hour cycles. A job in the Glooms where the only way out was a service tunnel that flooded at high tide. This was a ninety-second camera window and a forty-meter walk. The contingencies she had built into it were there because she always built contingencies, not because this plan demanded them.

Three things could go wrong. She had a plan for two of them. The third was low-probability, and she had decided the cost of planning for it outweighed the cost of carrying the risk.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Vox confirms the window opens at oh-nine-forty-six. Lace enters at oh-nine-thirty. I'm at the maintenance entrance by oh-nine-forty-three."

"If the timing closes before we're in position," Rook said, "we abort. Forty-eight hours and we run it again. There is no version of a job like this where we improvise around a failed window. That's not resilience, that's how people get hurt."

Nobody argued. They had all run enough jobs to know that was not philosophy — it was the line between a plan and a wish.

Rook turned off the display.

"It is simple," she said.

She meant it. She had put in the work to make it simple, which was not the same as it having been simple — but the distinction didn't matter now. What mattered was what stood in front of them. One way in. One way out. Four people who knew their jobs. A ninety-second window that had been opening on schedule for four years.

Scout closed her crawler case with a click that carried its own specific finality. She picked it up. She was already somewhere else, at least in part — already mapping whatever she would map before she slept, probably running the east corridor one more time to confirm a measurement she had confirmed twice already.

Vox was quiet in the particular way that meant their agents were reporting in, and they were listening to all three at once.

Lace was reviewing something on her personal display — the badge specifications, or the language of the ISB regulatory mandate. She would have it memorized by morning. She always did.

Boiler stood and stretched, a movement that made the room seem briefly smaller. He looked at the south wall as though the south wall were two hundred meters of city and a bridge and a parked vehicle, all of it waiting on the far side of a job that didn't need him.

"Good plan," he said.

Rook said nothing. She was already counting forward to oh-nine-forty-three.

[Beat 5]
The east maintenance entrance opened on a magnetic catch — no biometric, no card reader, just a pressure-sensitive seal meant to keep the HVAC differential from whistling. Rook had noted that in legwork. She had noted most things; that was the job, the part of it nobody paid for and everybody assumed.

She pressed through, and the corridor received her: matte walls in a shade Axiom called Heather White, which was not white, which was an off-magenta the branding team had probably billed a quarter's salaries to name. Service lighting, no ceiling panels, the kind of hallway a building keeps for the people it doesn't photograph. The crawlers had confirmed it empty. Scout's voice in her ear had been a direction and a number, which was all Scout ever gave her, which was all she had ever needed.

Rook moved. The east corridor ran forty-six feet to the lobby junction, and she had clocked it at twenty-three seconds on a normal walk. She was not walking normally. Eighteen.

"Blind holding," Vox said — not to her, to one of the agents. Rook had learned to parse the difference. "Fifty-eight seconds remaining. The pink wall's not looking."

The corridor gave onto the lobby behind a glass partition. Rook came around the corner.

She stopped.

The lobby was full of magenta. She had seen it in the crawler feeds, had known it as a fact and a color value, but the feeds couldn't resolve what the bioluminescent wall panels did in person: they breathed. A slow, even pulse, the Axiom logo blooming and fading in the reception glass like something patient. It was light that cost more than the security system installed alongside it. It was light engineered to make a person feel safe. She noted that, and moved it to the column where she kept things designed to be felt rather than trusted.

Dr. Nadia Vasquez-Park was standing near the lobby desk.

Not stepping out of the elevator. Not emerging from a side corridor. Standing, a bag over one shoulder and a hard case at her feet. Gunmetal. The exact color — not charcoal, not slate, but the specific gray of a thing built to take a hit and not care that it had. It sat on the magenta-lit floor like a refusal.

Vasquez-Park was looking straight at her. The expression was not surprise.

Rook's mind moved the pieces before she asked it to. Package is in the lobby. Package has luggage. Package has been standing still long enough to settle. Package is not nearly afraid enough for someone who did not know we were coming. The conclusions arrived in order, each one closing a door behind it.

"I hired you first," Ohara said. "Through Gault. Three weeks ago."

Rook held the problem up and turned it over once.

Gault had two clients. Axiom had hired the crew through the same fixer, the same cutout, the same clean channel — and Gault had passed the job along with no mention of a prior contract on the same target, which meant Gault either hadn't known or hadn't said. The job they had briefed for was an extraction. The job they had actually walked into was something she hadn't finished naming yet. She set it down where she could reach it later and picked up the immediate thing.

"How long have you been standing there?" Rook asked.

"Since your comms team opened the camera blind." Ohara said it the way an engineer describes a procedure — precisely, without apology, without performance. "I've held a secondary read on transit camera node four for eleven months. Axiom never audited my neuretics past the standard interface ceiling." She touched her right temple with one finger, the way a person confirms a pocket is still buttoned. "It's in here."

Not a drive. Not a file. Not anything you could pull from her hands and leave the rest behind.

Rook opened the next column. The formula was in Ohara's head, which meant the package was not the woman and was also entirely the woman: could not be separated from her, could not be copied without her, could not be left. Three constraints, all of them pointing the same way. The math simplified itself, which was usually a sign she was missing a term.
