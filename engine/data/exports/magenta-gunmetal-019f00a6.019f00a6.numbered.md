# Magenta & Gunmetal

## Magenta & Gunmetal

[Beat 1]
The bar ran magenta.

Not from any design intention Rook could see — the decor was the usual Z3 pretension, recycled pale wood and low-hung brass someone had decided communicated warmth. But the east wall held a full-panel Axiom BioNanics ad, Class II medical nano, pulsing on a slow three-second interval, and that corporate pink bled across the mirror behind the bar, caught in the glassware, and laid itself over every white shirt in the room like a second skin. Rook used the mirror the way she used every reflective surface — to read the room at her back — and her own face came with it, incidental in the magenta wash: olive and weathered, dark hair pushed back and still rain-wet at the ends, the pale grey-green eyes already finding the exits. The thin scar high on her right cheekbone caught the light half a second and let it go. The bartender moved through the pink, oblivious. Two suits at the bar talked low, faces washed in it.

Rook stood at the entrance and counted exits. Three. Service corridor left, main behind her, fire egress past the bathrooms. She had counted exits since the day city police dissolved. She counted them differently since Wennick — the same three doors, but she found herself checking which of them she'd send someone else through, and how fast she could get to that someone if the door turned out to be wrong. That was the change. She didn't have a word for it that she liked, so she didn't use one.

The Reibo flechette pistol under her coat sat where it always sat, caseless and quiet and never yet drawn on a job she had planned right, which was the point of planning them right. Gault was at the corner table — which meant she had sightlines on all three exits and he had one. She had told him once that she preferred corners, and he had remembered. Professional courtesy, or the kind of management a man did without noticing he was doing it. She hadn't decided which.

He saw her. He didn't wave.

She crossed and read the room on the way — two solo drinkers, a four-top celebrating nothing, a woman at the window who had ordered food and was actually eating it, rare enough to note. No security posture. No follow she could see. She sat.

"You look like you've been here a while."

"Half an hour." Amber in the glass, not his first. "I like to settle in."

"I know." That closed the small talk. Gault was fifty-two with the face of a man who had been carefully reasonable for a very long time — not soft, just worn smooth — and he dressed well, which was its own armor. He waited while she set the menu down unread and ordered mineral water and the server left. Up close she caught what distance had obscured: his hands were slightly too still. Not calm. Held. The way a person holds something in when they are unsure how the room will receive it.

"Five-person job," Gault said. "I've got three committed. You'd be four, but I want you running it."

"Who are the three?"

"Agwu. Väinämöinen. Okafor."

Rook let the names settle, and what came with them wasn't assessment so much as the small physical loosening of recognition — she felt her shoulders drop a degree before she decided to let them. She had run two jobs with Blessing Agwu and trusted her read of a room more than she trusted most instruments. Väinämöinen she'd put on a crane once and never had to look back at. Okafor she'd worked twice, watched her bleed off six AI agents at machine speed and never drop one, and learned the way the kid went quiet right before she said the thing that mattered. She did not keep a rule against repeat crew, whatever she told people who asked; she kept a list, in her head, of operators she would open a door in front of, and the list was short, and three of the names on it had just come out of Gault's mouth.

"Fifth?"

"Diallo. Exo, drone support. NSB specialist. Expensive. Consistent."

The fourth name she'd have picked herself. The warmth that came with it she kept off her face, because the job didn't need it yet, and spending it early was a habit she'd trained out of herself a long time before this.

"The job."

Gault put both hands around his glass. "Extraction. Corporate researcher. Axiom BioNanics, Research Compound 7, Z5."

She looked at the ad on the wall. The pulse hit its peak, the room going pink, then ebbed. "Client?"

"Anonymous, standard chain. I've used the relay four times. Clean."

"Axiom's Nano Triumvirate. Compound 7 won't be light."

"No," Gault agreed. "It won't."

"Who's the researcher?"

"Dr. Halina Soraya. Listed internally as staff, Ohara division. That's all intake's offering."

Ohara was Axiom's soft-nano line — Class II medical, the kind that went in with a prescription and built targeted structures in your blood over three months. Which meant Soraya was working on something the client wanted, or wanted stopped, or wanted to be sure she hadn't finished. Three different jobs once you were past the wire, and no way yet to tell them apart.

"Payment."

"Forty thousand QUANTA. Five-way. Eight each."

She turned the number over. Eight thousand for a compound pull, five bodies, high-security target — not generous, not insulting. The calibrated number, the one that drew competence without drawing attention. Acquisition, not ransom.

"Timeline?"

"Seventy-two hours to commit. Then it closes."

"I'll want the compound layout."

"Public filings. Past the perimeter is on you."

She nodded; she'd known that. The water came. She wrapped both hands around the cold glass and looked at Gault a moment without saying anything, and let the silence do the thing it always did before a yes.

What she caught, in the quiet, was his hands. They had unknotted when she named the crew — loosened, flat now on the laminate, the held thing let go. As if the hardest part of this conversation had been asking her at all. Which meant Gault, for all his careful-reasonable face, had wanted a particular answer and had not been certain he'd get it. She said nothing about it. But she understood, watching the hands, that Gault wasn't only managing her tonight; he was trusting her, in the small way a man trusts the person he hopes will say yes. She hadn't decided what to do with that.

"I'll run it."

He nodded slowly, and his shoulders came down the same degree hers had. "I'll tell them to expect contact."

She stood, left forty QUANTA for the water she'd barely touched, and walked back out through the magenta into the street. Z3 at evening was all brass ambient and recycled air, and a block over a quantum rack hummed under load. This was the real GLMZ — gunmetal and neon and the specific cold that came off the lake when the ventilation corridors lined up wrong.

She pulled her comm and opened a blank contact thread. Four names to bring in from wherever they'd scattered, and a frame to build around each of them before the job did it for her.

She had said yes before the silence finished. She knew it as she walked, the way you know a thing your body did ahead of you — yes the moment the third name landed, before the count was through. Once she would have run the quiet all the way to the bottom before she committed. Tonight she hadn't needed to. She let that sit, and did not pick at the place it came from.

[Beat 2]
The corpo lobby at Z3-Meridian stretched three stories of imported Baltic marble and made no apologies for it. Rook stood at the mezzanine rail with a coffee she wasn't drinking and watched Blessing Agwu run a building manager.

Agwu was deep in it. The face was different — not dramatically, just precisely. The jaw was set at a slightly different angle, the brow carried lower, the whole arrangement settling into the particular expression of someone who had been tolerating a subordinate's excuses for eleven minutes and had four more of patience left before the paperwork started. Forty-three muscles, individually addressed. Rook had read the technical writeup once and then stopped, because there were things she could know about a person and things that made her feel like she was watching surgery performed on a friend.

She waited for the pivot. It came in thirty seconds. Agwu glanced at something on her lenses, the building manager suddenly found his own answers satisfying, and Blessing Agwu walked away from the conversation with the unhurried certainty of someone closing a door she'd already measured to the millimeter.

She saw Rook at the rail. Nothing on her face changed, which was itself a kind of greeting.

They went outside. The mezzanine coffee turned out to be useful after all — Rook set it on a planter ledge and let the Z3 midday light do what it did to everything up here, which was to make even a job conversation feel like a board meeting between people who owned things.

"Agwu," Rook said.

"You interrupted something."

"It looked finished."

"It was nearly finished. There's a difference." Agwu folded her hands. "What do you need?"

Rook gave her the outline in sixty seconds. One target building. One window of access. Someone who could wear whoever the room required. She said the number last: Φ 8,000.

Agwu looked past her at the cityline for a moment. It wasn't hesitation. It was consideration, the kind that already had a shape.

"I need a Level IV fabrication suite," she said. "The Daedalic Modular. They cost Φ 22,000 and the waiting list is eight months. I am shortcutting the waiting list."

"That's not a question."

"No." Agwu looked back at her. The directness wasn't unfriendly. It was the expression Agwu always wore when she was deciding something about a person rather than about a plan. "Send me the structural brief. No shorter than six pages. I need to understand the org before I dress it."

"Done," Rook said. Then, because she had decided on the way up here to do it, and because deciding and doing were apparently no longer the same act for her: "There's something the brief won't carry on its own, so I'll say it. The client routing is wrong. Anonymous source, relay Gault swears by, but the response times don't move like a corp paying for a routine pull. Either the client is a cutout for somebody who doesn't want to be the client, or somebody's already got a hand on this job that isn't us." She had told no one else. She had not planned to tell anyone at all. "I don't have the shape of it yet. I'm telling you so you walk in knowing the room might have a second floor."

Agwu held her gaze. The micro-expression work went still — not performing anything now, just looking at her. "You're telling me because you want me to know, not because the brief requires it."

"Yes."

A beat. Then, almost too flat to be anything: "Good." Agwu picked up the coffee Rook had set down, studied it with the air of someone who had just settled a question she hadn't said aloud, and handed it back handle-first. "Last two jobs you ran, you gave me the diagram and let me find the holes myself. This is the first time you've handed me one going in." She didn't make it a thing. She let it be exactly the size it was. "I'll wear it well."

She said it without cruelty. Rook took the coffee and said nothing, because Agwu had just told her something underneath the words — that she'd noticed the change, and approved of it, and would not embarrass either of them by naming it larger than it was.

Boiler was in a warehouse near the Z4/Z5 seam, which was exactly where Rook had expected to find him, because that particular stretch of Bloom Quarter gray market held three decommissioned Arcturus civil logistics buildings, and Mikkeli Väinämöinen found decommissioned buildings philosophically interesting.

He was standing in front of a load-bearing wall with his hands in his pockets and his head tilted, studying it the way some people studied art.

"Rook," he said, without turning around.

"Boiler."

"This column's wrong." He pointed at a reinforced concrete pillar that looked, to Rook, identical to every other reinforced concrete pillar in the warehouse. "Supposed to be carrying the northwest corner, but somebody poured the aggregate at the wrong cure temperature. She's been holding on pride for fifteen years."

He finally turned. He was very large. He was always very large — Rook had once watched him lift a floor safe with the incurious ease of a man who found the task slightly beneath his attention. "You're not here about the column," he said.

"No."

She gave him the outline. He listened with his arms crossed and his weight back on his heels, nodding at specific points — not the money, not the target, but the moments in the plan where structural knowledge would carry the load.

"What's the ceiling height in the secondary access corridor?" he asked.

"Eleven feet."

"Good." He seemed relieved on behalf of the ceiling. "Some of those Z2 retrofits go down to 2.8. I have to think about myself."

"Φ 8,000."

"Yeah, all right. My dehumidifier destroyed itself, and I refuse to buy Arclight brand again." He turned back to the wall. His hand came up and rested against the column — not pushing it, just touching it, the way you touch something you have diagnosed and accepted. "Send me whatever you have on the sub-basement. I like to know what I'm standing on."

She didn't comment on the hand. She'd worked with Väinämöinen twice and she understood it the way you understand a person after watching how they move: the touch wasn't theatrical, he genuinely found it useful to feel what was under load.

"The Z5 freight corridor," she said, instead of any of that. "There's an elevated rail bridge two hundred meters north of the compound. I didn't put it in the brief. You'll want to look at it."

Boiler turned his head. Not surprise exactly — more the expression of a man whose suspicion had just been confirmed. "Public record on that structure?"

"2024 stress assessment. Budget allocation was denied."

"Of course it was." He did not say thank you. He said, "I'll look at it tonight." And the warmth in it was the warmth of a man handed a problem worth his attention, which was the only warmth Boiler offered, and Rook took it in exactly the spirit it was given.

The noodle shop on Z2-Orlanda ran a lunchtime crowd that peaked and receded in twenty-minute waves. Between waves, the small table by the frosted partition was reliably empty, except for the person who needed it empty.

Tem Okafor was sitting at that table, staring at the wall, holding a pair of chopsticks she had not used.

Rook sat across from her. Okafor's eyes did not move toward her. Her expression had the loose, slightly inward quality of someone whose processing was mostly happening elsewhere — not absent, just distributed across more rooms than this one. The neuretics burned a thin line of warmth along her left temple, commercial Atlas-grade. You could get the surgery cheaper, but the bandwidth cost you the difference.

"Thirty seconds," Okafor said — to the wall, or to the room, or to something she was currently routing through the noodle shop's network like a courier through a mountain pass. "Choi, hold the eastern egress. Yeah. Yeah. No, the residential trunk is a choke, go around it." A beat, and then, to Rook: "Sorry."

She waited the thirty seconds. Okafor set down the chopsticks.

"What's the network profile?" she asked.

"One private Arcturus-adjacent subnet, three legacy access nodes in the east wing, one high-security isolated loop around the primary vault systems."

Okafor's eyes moved slightly, cataloging. "The isolated loop's physical?"

"Air-gapped, with a mechanical failover."

"Good. I hate arguing with physical." She picked up the chopsticks again and turned them over in her hands. Her left thumb pressed into a small discoloration on the wood, back and forth — not fidgeting, something too deliberate for that. A loaded habit. "My three best agents need licensing extensions. Gray-market licensing, so not cheap. I'd rather pay for clean access time than spend the run fighting a throttle."

Rook said the number. Then, the same way she'd done it with Agwu, before she could let the discipline win: "One more thing, off the brief. The client's a cutout, or there's a second hand on the job I haven't found. I'm having everyone walk in knowing it."

Okafor's eyes came most of the way back to the room, which from her was a hard stare. "Huh." A beat, something running behind it. "You don't usually tell us the part you haven't solved yet."

"No."

"Okay." She turned the chopstick over once. "I'll keep a channel back from the wall, then. One that's just watching us instead of them." It was the kind of thing she'd normally have done without saying. She said it. "Last time I ran a three-agent job with you, the building was still standing at the end."

"It was."

"Good standard." She was quiet a moment. "Send me the floor schematics. I need to see the topography. And tell whoever else you're running that I'll need two hours of undisturbed prep before breach."

"Done."

She was already gone again, or most of her was. Rook watched her two seconds and saw what she'd seen the last time: that Okafor's body was always a half-step behind her attention, always arriving at the conversation slightly late, and that it was the most radical act of trust she knew — to be this visibly present elsewhere and not care who saw it. Most people in the work kept the visible self controlled at all costs. Okafor didn't have the bandwidth to spare on that, and had decided, apparently, that she didn't need to.

The diner at Z2-Fass was gunmetal and grease — formica counters, strip lighting with one bulb running magenta where the white had given out, the particular ambiance of a room that had stopped caring about ambiance sometime before Rook was born. It was the kind of place that left you alone, which was the only amenity it advertised.

Remi Diallo was at a corner table, eyes rolled back.

Four crawlers sat on the table. Two more rested on the floor near her chair, oriented outward. One held the windowsill. Her tea had gone cold in the way tea goes cold when no one has looked at it in forty minutes. She was breathing at about eight breaths a minute, completely still, except for one hand that made a small adjustment every few minutes — a finger extending, then curling back — mirroring some movement she was making in a space that wasn't here. Her NSB rig had her; whatever she was seeing, she was seeing through the frames, and her body had been left behind at the table like a coat over a chair.

Rook sat down across from her, ordered tea she would also not drink, and waited.

She did not find the stillness unsettling, the way some people did. The word underneath what she felt, if she'd let it surface, was honest: Diallo was entirely somewhere else and her body said so plainly, rather than performing an alertness it didn't have. There was something almost restful about a person who didn't pretend.

At eleven minutes, Remi Diallo's eyes came back. They rolled forward and found their focus the way a lens finds it, in one smooth correction, and the body behind them reoccupied itself.

She blinked once. Looked at the crawlers on the table as if counting them. Then at Rook.

"How many?"

"Seven access points. Maybe nine, if the east wing egress is what I think it is."

Diallo looked at her crawlers again. "I have seven operational. Three are named. The crew names them," she said. It wasn't a complaint, and it wasn't quite resignation either. "I've stopped arguing."

"Φ 8,000."

A pause. She picked up the cold tea and drank half of it without expression, the way a person refuels.

"Send me the floor plan. I'll need sight lines for the approach corridors." She set down the cup and looked at the window. Her hand moved again, checking something in a layer of attention Rook didn't have access to. "I want to do a pre-run tomorrow night. Just me."

"Done. And one thing the others already have." Rook said it plainly. "The client routing is wrong. Could be a cutout, could be a second hand on the job. I don't have it yet. You're going in knowing it the same as everyone."

Diallo's eyes came back to her then — all the way back, the way they rarely did. She held the look a beat longer than her custom. "You're telling me the gap before you've closed it."

"Yes."

Something in her registered it and moved on. "Then I'll run the pre-run twice." Not a complaint. An adjustment. She looked at the window again, then back, and asked the thing she had decided to ask: "Agwu's in?"

"Yes."

Something in Diallo settled — not relief, more the cessation of a tension Rook hadn't known she was carrying. "Good," she said, and looked away.

Rook held it without naming it: Diallo trusted Agwu in the field in a way she didn't advertise and hadn't been asked about, and for a person who kept herself rigorously apart from the work's social geometry, that was worth knowing, and worth not mentioning.

Four people. Four agreements, and four times now she'd handed over the part of the job she hadn't solved — which wasn't how she ran jobs, or hadn't been. Rook walked out of the diner into the particular quality of Z2 afternoon light, which was the same as every other kind of afternoon light in the GLMZ — refracted, filtered, carrying down to the street whatever the towers had decided not to keep for themselves.

She sent four identical messages: *Brief drops in six hours.*

Then she started thinking about the building, and about who had wanted her looking at it.

[Beat 3]
The café across from Compound 7 served two things worth drinking: the drip and the view. Rook had been nursing the first since ten past six. The second cost nothing.

Axiom BioNanics Research Compound 7 occupied most of the Z5 block between Morrow and the freight rail, and it wore its brand the way the brand wanted to be worn. Magenta wall panels framed every entrance. Magenta trim ran the lengths of the transit pod bays. The logo scrolled in four-foot repeats along the upper parapet, where the building couldn't decide whether it was a research facility or a billboard for one. Through the front glass the lobby was the magenta aquarium: floor, signage, and a soft arterial wash that made the two guards behind the security desk look like people waiting to be born.

Rook kept her eyes on the east-facing entrance, her coffee at her left hand, and her neuretics on passive. She was watching the way the building breathed - when the pod bays filled, when the lobby thinned, which guard checked his comm and which one didn't.

The thing about legwork alone was that it left nothing between you and the job. No voice in the earpiece, no other body to route attention through. Just the building and your read of it. She had always told herself she preferred this - that solo observation was cleaner. Watching an Axiom logo scroll its pink circuit for the fourth time, she found the old certainty thinner than it used to be. The solo read produced the right data. It also produced it inside a room she had not let anyone else into, and she had run enough jobs to know that a closed room and a clean one weren't the same thing. Wennick had been in the room once. Then he wasn't, and she had decided it was the room's fault and built her whole working method around the decision.

She let that thought stand in the light for a moment, which she did not usually do, and then put her eyes back on the east door.

Vox's voice arrived through the bone-conduction strip behind her left ear at 6:12 PM, flat and spatial, the way she always reported.

"Outer ring is settling," Vox said. "Pink wall was thinking about us around noon. It stopped."

"Stopped or paused?" Rook asked.

"Stopped. Our agents were moving slow enough that the pattern read as noise, so the wall filed us under ambient." A pause. "It's a very confident wall."

The pink wall was Axiom's outer security AI, and Vox's gray-market agents had been walking it all day at a pace Rook had approved before breakfast - slow enough to read as background traffic, deliberate enough to map what mattered. The wall believed it knew what noise looked like. Vox's whole job today was to keep it right about that until the moment it wasn't.

"Camera system?"

"Ninety-second blind, every four hours and forty-three minutes," Vox said. "It's an unlocked door nobody knows is unlocked - two subroutines written by different teams in 2218, and the team that would've caught the seam got laid off in 2221. Nobody left has the authorization to touch the firmware." She said it the way you'd describe a key left under a mat by someone who'd since moved away. "Next window after our entry slot lands at 11:17 PM."

A pause, then, quieter and without the diagnostic flatness: "Rook. I want to run a third simulation before tomorrow. The second one showed something in the east subnet I don't fully trust yet. Nothing that kills the plan. But I'd rather know what it is."

"Run it."

"It might cost an agent. The one I'd use for the third pass is the same one I need for the entry window."

"What's the probability the subnet's a problem?"

"Twelve percent. With the sim: three."

Rook thought about Vox in the noodle shop - the thumb pressing that worn mark in the chopstick, back and forth. The habit of a person who kept her costs in a currency nobody else could read. "Run it. Buy a replacement agent before morning. I'll cover the licensing out of operational."

A long pause. "That's expensive."

"I know."

Another beat. Then the quietest thing Vox had said to her in two jobs: "Okay." And Rook understood - without being told, without anything she could have cited - that the third simulation mattered to Vox past the twelve percent, that this was Vox handing her something in the one language she kept fully private, and that she had answered it in a way that met it.

Scout's body was three blocks east in the safe room they'd rented for the week, upright in a chair with her hands loose in her lap and her eyes rolled to white. She couldn't see the chair. She'd ejected herself from her Husk and injected into the Shell - the rig cutting her optic feed the moment she went - and she was somewhere inside Compound 7's HVAC secondary loop, pushing her other crawlers through ducting while the relay kept her audible.

"East corridor," Scout said, even and dry, the way she always sounded riding the link. "Maintenance entrance. Two cameras, neither on the door."

"Angle?"

"One on the corridor junction. One on the service bay. The door sits in the gap. Steel frame, older than the build - retrofitted." A pause that meant she was easing a crawler past something. "Guard rotation runs the east corridor every forty minutes. Between rotations they don't check the bay. They check the log."

"How honest is the log?"

"Maintenance crews use it to clock out early. So it's wrong, but wrong in a direction you can trust."

That was the seam. One door, two cameras that didn't see it, a log that lied the same way every day.

Lace had sat with her target - she described him afterward as a man with a name she'd already let go of - for forty minutes outside a coffee cart on Morrow Street, sixty feet from the compound's contractor entrance. She had not asked him a single question that sounded like one.

She mentioned that her firm was bidding on a support contract for a couple of Z5 facilities and that the paperwork was going to kill her before the work did. He laughed and said he knew exactly what she meant. Somewhere in the next twenty minutes he told her that contractor inspection used the north service bay off Morrow, that the badge color for inspection access this quarter was yellow - not the visitor yellow, the contractor yellow, which was more of a gold, really - and that the facility manager was named Okonkwo. Petra Okonkwo. She'd taken the role three months ago and was already making herself felt in the scheduling system.

Lace wrote none of it down. She walked two blocks before she recited it into the crew channel, flat and in order, like reading off a list she'd had in her hand the whole time. Three authenticated recordings from Axiom's public archive: a 2224 all-hands address, a field briefing transcript with timestamps, and a 2225 Q3 threat assessment Adalemo had delivered in person — enough to map the p-clipping and the low-urgency register. Then, not in the report cadence, in her own: "The manager's the one to watch. Three months in and already rewriting other people's schedules. People like that walk the floor. They don't trust the log either."

At 6:44 Rook ordered a second coffee she didn't need and watched the lobby entrance.

At 6:47 the lobby doors opened and Dr. Halina Soraya walked out.

She walked the way someone walks when they believe they know who is watching and have decided it doesn't matter - which was different from not caring, and different from not knowing, and Rook spent three seconds sorting the distinctions while Soraya crossed to the transit pod bay and scanned her wrist against the reader. The pod arrived in under forty seconds, which was fast. She boarded alone. She did not check her neuretics while she waited, and she did not look back at the building she'd just left.

The pod pulled east toward the Z5 transfer hub. Rook noted the time and finished her coffee.

At six-fifty she paid and left. She walked back north via the freight siding - a different route than in, because repeated routes became patterns. The loading dock ran two meters above the maintenance lane. The stairs were padlocked. She looked at the drop and stepped off it, the impact spreading clean through her knees the way it did when you'd done it often enough that the joints had their own precedent. Nobody was there to see it. She walked on.

"Rail bridge," Boiler said over the channel, from wherever he'd been for the last two hours, walking the perimeter at the pace of a man with nowhere to be, clocking load tolerances and sightlines and what the ground was doing under the pavement. "Two hundred feet north. Freight elevated. Z5 spur."

"Noted," Rook said.

"Load capacity's rated for a Mikkola Class Four hauler," Boiler said, which was forty-eight metric tons, and there was something underneath it - the specific satisfaction of a man who has found a useful problem and is telling you about it without telling you about it.

"2024 stress assessment," Rook said. "Column wasn't a priority."

A pause. Then, warmly: "No, it wouldn't have been." He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. They had both just confirmed something the other had already suspected, and that confirmation was its own kind of currency between them.

By nine o'clock Rook had it spread across the table in the apartment: the camera window, the maintenance entrance, the badge color, the manager's name, the guard rotation, Scout's corridor map, Vox's timing. She built it the way she built everything - start with the seams, find where the pieces didn't quite meet, decide before sleeping what she meant to do about each gap.

The seams were small. The window was real. The entrance was usable. Okonkwo was a name she'd carry now.

One item from Lace's source sat outside the diagram: a contractor access team -- Z3 logistics outfit, three persons -- had been removed from Axiom's approved-vendor list seven weeks ago. No documented incident. No filed grievance. Just a name flag and a notation in the security queue: *access suspended, security review.* Rook had no category for it yet. She wrote *seven weeks* and put a box around it.

She left Soraya for last, and read back what she'd written: *6:47 PM, lobby exit, pod bay, east transfer, alone, always alone, forty-second pod response, no comm check.*

A forty-second pod response in Z5 at quarter to seven ran three standard deviations faster than ambient. You got a response like that only if the transit system was routing for you specifically, which meant a standing priority flag on the account, which meant someone had set it.

Soraya hadn't set it for herself. Someone else wanted her moving fast and unobstructed through Z5 - wanted to know exactly where she was and have her get there quickly. And in thirty hours of watching, Soraya had never once looked back at the building, never checked whether the café across the street held yesterday's two customers. She moved like a person who had already been promised she was safe.

Rook sat with that longer than the data needed. Either someone was protecting Soraya, or someone was keeping her on a leash she couldn't feel, and the priority flag looked identical from the outside in both cases. It folded into the thing Gault's relay had already told her: a client who wasn't only a client, a hand on the job that wasn't hers. She didn't have the shape yet. She had two facts that wanted to be the same fact.

And the part she sat with longest wasn't operational. It was that Soraya moved like someone executing a decision she was still in the middle of - the absence of backward glances that wasn't confidence but commitment. Rook knew that walk. She had walked it. She set the cup down and did not look back at the building. Then she pulled the forty-eight-hour clock up on her neuretics, looked at it, and dismissed it.

Three channels instead of six now, down from the four she'd carried into the flat. *Arm's fine,* Vox had said on the ride out, to the question Rook hadn't asked. And she thought about the night before that — a conversation that had cost them both, a dead woman in the Bloom Quarter, said aloud instead of stored, two people carrying it at the right altitude together. A partner's name said out loud for the first time in three years. Rook hadn't done any of that before. She didn't yet know what to do with the fact that it had helped, only that it had, and that the help hadn't made her worse at the job, which was the thing she'd spent three years afraid of.

*That is correct,* the note had said. A receipt. Ohara had routed her own exit before Rook was ever in the room and left two words to confirm the count came out even. *You sat down,* she'd said, in the dark van, with the easy precision of a woman noting a fact she found useful. Not a criticism. Not a consolation. A fact about Rook, seen into existence from across a cargo van and handed back without agenda. Rook hadn't known what to do with it then. She turned it over once more in the booth now, and the image it produced — herself, sitting down across from Gault because she couldn't carry the conversation standing — felt less like a failure of professional distance than she'd expected it to. It felt like the part of the job she had spent two years calling a weakness and had used, this week, four separate times, to keep five people alive.

She squared the empty coffee cup on the table. Odd, what you learned about yourself on the far side of a job that went wrong in the right direction.

It had come out even. Everybody got paid, the door was open, nobody she'd brought was in the lake. Even — except for the woman she had not brought and couldn't stop seeing, who would not balance against anything and was not supposed to. Rook let the ledger stay one short. It was honest that way. She left Φ 12 on the table for the coffee, buttoned her coat, and stood.

Through the window the lake was the same flat gunmetal it had been, and she looked at it one second longer than she had to — not because she was thinking about the storm, or the VTOL, or the railing and the water forty meters down, but because she was thinking about the next time. The list that was four names and still had room for more, if she decided to let it.

She made herself stop looking, and went out through the door onto the Spine.
