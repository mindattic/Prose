# Bushido Coda - Ch2: Provenance (CODA)

## Chapter Two - Provenance

[Beat 1]
He knocked in the morning, and Pixel opened the door already talking.

"Forearm first, then the array, then you tell me why your calibration log has a gap in it the shape of a man not wanting to explain something." She had a soldering iron in one hand and yesterday's hair and the specific alertness of someone who had been awake for a while pretending she hadn't been waiting. "Sit. Not there. There. The light's honest there."

Across the hall was four steps. He'd been making them for four years, and somewhere in there the steps had stopped being a trip and started being a destination, and neither of them had filed the paperwork on it.

She took the field strip off his forearm the way she took everything off him — without ceremony, narrating as she went so he'd know where her hands were before he felt them. "Six centimeters. To the bone. You let someone do this." Not a question. She threaded the suture. "You let him, because not letting him would have cost more, and you do that math in a quarter-second and never show your work, which is the single most annoying thing about you, and I've made a list." A tear of biotape, applied slow. "Eat the egg. It's on the counter. Don't tell me you're not hungry; your body's burning a thousand calories an hour repairing what you spent last night and you skipped dinner because Reyes paid you in coins and you didn't want to break one for noodles. Eat the egg, Kyle."

He ate the egg.

The array took longer. She put the contacts at the base of his skull and ran the calibration and went very quiet, which was how he knew she'd found the brownout before she said it. "It double-printed. The duel's last cut — you went a beat blind and it came back wrong at the edges." She scrolled. Stopped. "And there's the harmonic again. Low, steady, under everything. Older than any spec I have for your hardware — older than the hardware. I've flagged it three times. It never changes and it never goes away and I cannot source it, and I hate that, professionally, more than I can tell you."

He had felt it once or twice himself — not heard it, felt it, the way you notice a refrigerator only when it stops. He didn't tell her that. There was a version of the morning where he asked her to chase the harmonic to its root, and a quieter, older thing in him — the same thing that had kept him alive eleven years — that did not want to know what she'd find. He let the want go by like a slug on the rail.

"Some facility gear never came with specs," Kyle said. It was true. It was not an answer, and she let it not be one, the way she let a lot of his sentences be the shape of a door she'd decided not to open yet.

She unclipped the contacts. Pressed two fingers flat between his shoulder blades for half a second, the way she did before she trusted a readout — except there was no readout left to trust, so it was just the hand. Then it was gone, and she was a professional again. "You're recalibrated. You're sutured. You owe me forty for the array and you'll pay it exact because you're you, and don't think the egg was free either. It's coming out of your dignity."

The crew's morning report arrived through the slot at 08:40, five different hands on one sheet, the way it always came now.

> 06:12 — TARGET (KYLE) EXITED. LIMP, LEFT SIDE, MINOR. RODE NORTH.
> 06:55 — PIGEON ON THE RAIL AGAIN. SAME PIGEON. WE HAVE NAMED IT. ITS NAME IS PROBLEM.
> 07:30 — A MAN PHOTOGRAPHED THE BUILDING FROM THE NOODLE SIDE. NOT A COP. TOO CLEAN.
> 07:31 — DENTS SAYS TOO CLEAN MEANS CORPO. RAFF SAYS TOO CLEAN MEANS NEW. WE PUT IT TO A VOTE.
> 07:34 — IT WAS A TIE. THIS LOG IS HONEST.

Boost had signed it at the bottom the way she signed everything, a single capital B with a flourish she'd been refining for weeks, and under it: *the clean man came back twice. that's a subscription, not a look. — B.*

The kid was fourteen and she was right. A man who came back twice on a schedule wasn't watching Kyle. He was *maintaining* a file on Kyle. Which meant someone was paying to keep it current. Which meant the file existed somewhere you could buy it, and there was exactly one counter in the city where a thing like that went to be sold honestly.

"Don't," said Pixel, reading his face. "You're going to go see the dead-tech man."

"Vey keeps better records than the city does."

"Vey keeps records the city *can't reach*, which is a different and worse sentence." She pointed the soldering iron at him. "Eat something at the actual market on the way. A thing with vegetables in it. I'll know."

ANTIQUITY & STATIONARY had a brass bell on the door that still worked, which was the first lie the shop told you, because everything else in it had stopped working decades ago and was worth a fortune for exactly that reason. The second word on the sign was spelled the way Vey meant it — not the paper trade he'd started in, but the older sense. Stationary. Unmoving. Fixed. The one shop in the GLMZ where nothing on the shelves would ever change again, in a city where everything else could be edited out from under you between one night and the next.

Kyle came down the three flights into the Loop's underlevel and the temperature dropped and the air went dry and dead — Faraday mesh in the walls, no signal in or out, the quietest room in the GLMZ. The shelves held the future, retired. First-generation neural rigs with their casings open like dissected hearts. Handsets that had been the most important object in a billion pockets and were now paperweights with dead glass. A rack of pre-collapse server blades, black and finned, that had once held somebody's whole life and held nothing now and would hold nothing ever again. Every one of them had had its turn at being the cutting edge, the thing that was finally going to fix everything; every one had been wrong; and Vey had bought them all afterward, by the kilo, when the future they'd promised turned out to be this one.

"Mr. Kyle," said Casper Vey, from behind the counter, the way a man says a name he filed a long time ago. Half-glasses on a chain. Hands so clean they were a statement. "Your left side is favoring. Don't tell me about it. I don't keep injuries; they change, and a record that changes is a lie I've agreed to."

"I want my file."

"No." He said it without heat, the way he'd say it to anyone. "I have never sold a man his own file and I'm not starting with the one man who'd believe what's in it. A subject who reads his file changes; a changed subject makes the file false; I do not sell false things. It's the whole shop, Mr. Kyle. It's the only thing the shop *is*." He set out two cups and poured from a pot that had been warming on a hotplate older than the war. "What I'll sell you is the list of everyone who's been buying it. That's not your file. That's *their* paper, and their paper is mine to move. Forty Φ. And one true thing, because I don't take a man's money and his ignorance in the same transaction; that's how you end up owning people, and I deal in objects."

Kyle put forty on the counter, exact. Then he gave Vey the true thing, because it was the only currency the old man actually collected.

"I have a client I've never met. Eleven years. It pays standard rate, to the half-Φ, and it pays early, and it routes through addresses that don't exist."

Something happened in Vey's face that Kyle had never seen there before, which was the effort of keeping nothing happening in it.

He came around the counter. He went to a cabinet at the back, the only locked one, and he opened it, and inside there was a single object on a velvet tray, and the object was a small brass contract token, unmarked, the kind a courier desk stamped a job with a hundred years ago. He did not touch it. He looked at it the way a man looks at the one bill he can't pay.

"Everything in this shop has a past," Vey said. "That's what you're buying when you buy from me — not the thing, the *chain*. Where it was made, whose pocket it rode in, what it survived. I can source a server blade to the floor of the building it burned in." He nodded at the token. "That came in eleven years ago as payment for an authentication. Standard rate, exact, early. And it has no past. No maker. No prior owner. No chain. It is the only object I have ever held that simply *began*, fully formed, owing nothing to anything that came before it." He closed the cabinet. "Whatever's paying you paid me too — exact, early, in the one currency I've never learned to refuse, which is a fact I can't source." He set the cup in front of Kyle. "Mind it. It's hot."

"What is it?"

"It's the only proof it exists." Vey poured. "Which is why it lives in the back, off every wire, in a room nothing can reach, behind a man who can't be made to remember he owns it. I had the memory taken out years ago — the relevant ones. Occupational. The objects remember. I just keep them." A thin smile. "You'd be amazed what people will try to make you say, once they learn you know things. The trick is to genuinely not."

The list was four names. Three were ordinary — a debt desk, a former client doing diligence, the Lotus south arm keeping book on a freelancer the way they kept book on everyone.

The fourth had bought the full dossier two days ago, same-day rate, no haggling, through a shell that resolved to a recovery firm called Halvorsen Civic. And the requisitioning office on the header, which the buyer hadn't thought to scrub because people who work in continuity don't expect anyone to read the paper, said: CONTINUITY OFFICE — DISTRICT ACQUISITION.

Kyle read it twice, and filed the name where he kept the things that were going to cost him later.

"That one came back twice," Vey said, not looking. "On a schedule. I notice schedules. Schedules are how you tell a man from an institution: a man gets tired."

Kyle took the underlevel stairs slow, the buyers' list in one pocket and the Continuity Office header in the other, and the array did what it always did, costing the morning out in hours and calories and probability bands. It could price a man who got tired, a man who got greedy, a man who ran out of budget. It had no column for the thing that didn't get tired, and he noticed the blank the way you notice a missing key on a ring â€” not the loss, just the gap where the weight should be â€” and kept climbing.

Pixel was waiting on the platform at Dock 14 with a coffee she did not offer him and a smart-dust reader she did.

"They tagged you," she said. "Last night, the noodle side. Microgram of marker dust on your right shoulder — the clean man, has to be. It's been singing your position to a collector on a four-hour clock since 06:00." She wasn't angry. She was *delighted*, in the specific way she got when a problem turned out to be solvable and rude. "I could pull it. But pulling it tells them you found it, and then they get clever, and clever's expensive."

"Or," Kyle said.

"Or," Pixel agreed, and they looked together at the 14:17 Pulse freight slug standing on the rail, sealed and humming, forty-three minutes from Rotterdam at Mach six.

He walked past the slug close enough to brush it, and let the marker dust find a new home — a sealed pod bound under the North Atlantic, forty-three minutes to Rotterdam, where it would go on reporting his position with great confidence from the wrong continent.

Someone careful enough to plant it should have been careful enough to hide it better, and he set the thought down on the platform without finishing it and left it there for the next slug.

Pixel finally handed him the coffee. "Somebody in an office files that you're in Rotterdam. Somebody above them believes it, because it's on the system." She watched the slug go. "Everybody trusts the paper. Vey's the only one who charges for the truth."

"Vey says the truth's an object."

"Vey would." She drank. "Did you eat?"

"There were vegetables," Kyle said, which was true, and which was not an answer, and she let it not be one, and they walked back up into the city a half-step apart, the way they always did, while four hours out a sealed pod told a patient institution exactly where he wasn't.
