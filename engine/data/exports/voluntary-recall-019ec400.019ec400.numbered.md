# Voluntary Recall

_Ensemble finale, Isaac POV. The Cermak crew goes to Arcturus (the community built the case for years); the corpo buries it all as a voluntary recall. Douglas's compute-lease ledger fed the predator; he pays, unhappy; Mei-Lin pays the real fee; the four split it. A sidewalk, mother and daughter. The Tributary now wears Celeste's voice for the next griever; the agency keeps Tamsin's reading and Corvin's analysis._

[Beat 1]
The call from Arcturus came in at 06:14, before anyone had touched the coffee maker. Rennick took it standing at the window, watching the gray light press against the buildings across the street, the Gray Zone's buffer alley quiet below. The Arcturus operator on the line said organized crime unit, said the Cermak Reclamation Crew, said they'd be making arrests this morning and that Rennick Investigations's packet — the load-dock records, the refrigeration timestamps, the organ-registry gaps — had been received and logged as a community submission. A community submission. Rennick said thank you and ended the call. He stood at the window a moment longer. The alley had a cat in it, sitting on a reclamation bin, not moving.

[Beat 2]
Tamsin was already in the kitchen when he came down the hall, both hands wrapped around a mug, leaning against the counter with her eyes somewhere past the far wall. She'd been doing that more since the under-city. He didn't say anything about it. He poured himself a cup and stood next to her, and she said, "Arcturus?" and he said, "This morning." She nodded once, a short nod that closed the subject without settling it. "The community case?" she asked. "Two years they were building it," he said. "Feliksas's shadow log was the last piece they needed." She looked into her mug. "The journalists will write about the crew. They won't write about the two years."

[Beat 3]
Rennick had put together the packet the night before — twenty-two pages, sourced, stamped with the intake date and their license number, cross-referenced with the organ-registry gaps he'd pulled from his old Arcturus access and the staged-suicide read he'd written up on Pellerin. The staged suicide. The operator who'd tried to quit the crew and ended up in a position Rennick had named in six minutes cold. He had named the corponation behind Pellerin's death — Carrion Enterprises, indirect, the crew their instrument — and that name was in the packet too, in the final section, where he always put the things he knew Arcturus would read and set aside. It was still true. It still had to go in.

[Beat 4]
There was food on the intake desk, two bags from the place on the corner that Voss had dropped before dawn on his way back through the corridor and left without a note. Rennick opened one: three containers of something with rice, a sleeve of flatbreads, a packet of the sauce Voss always asked for and never remembered to buy himself. He set the sauce packet to the side, precisely, for when Voss came in. Tamsin took a container and a spoon and sat on the corner of the desk. She ate without tasting it, her eyes somewhere past the far wall the way they'd been more since the under-city, and after a while she said, "The packet went out last night?" "Twenty-two pages," Rennick said. "Stamped with the intake date and the license number." She nodded once. "Then it's theirs now." She meant Arcturus, and she meant it the way she meant most things, as a fact she was setting down rather than carrying.

[Beat 5]
Corvin came in at half past seven, read the room in three seconds, and took the remaining container from the bag. He did not ask about the Arcturus call — he'd already seen the traffic when it came through; his neuretic overlay logged civic comm spikes the way other people noticed weather. He said, "The voluntary recall notice will go out today." Rennick looked at him. "Already?" "The draft has been in the corponation's system since yesterday afternoon," Corvin said. "I did not look at the draft. I observed that it existed." He opened his container and examined its contents with the slight pause he used when something was prepared differently than expected. "A faulty cat-ear mod batch. Auricula Felis line, affected lot numbers will be published, implicated operator flagged." He took a careful bite. "The Tributary will not appear in any version of that document."

[Beat 6]
Rennick had expected it. He had made himself expect it two days ago, before the under-city, before Voss came back smelling of flood water and something chemical that took three washes to fully leave his jacket. The corponation's name on the Auricula Felis lot — the firm that licensed the directional-hearing genemod — was somewhere two or three parent companies above the distribution channel the Cermak front had been exploiting. Close enough to have an interest in a quiet resolution. Close enough to have lawyers who'd had the voluntary recall language drafted before the arrests cleared processing. "Voluntary recall," Rennick said. "Product liability, not predation," Corvin said. "The ELF is categorically absent. The Scatter doesn't exist as a procedure in the official record. Sol Castellanos-Park is still a Jane Doe." He said it the way he said everything — without inflection, each fact at the same weight as the one before. Rennick turned his cup a quarter-turn on the desk until the handle lined up square with the edge, and said nothing.

[Beat 7]
Voss arrived last, which was unusual, though he'd been the one to leave the food, so he'd already been by once before the light came up. He came in damp from the collar up — not rain, something else, the particular humidity of the under-city that still clung to him in patches even now, like a texture the city had pressed into him and hadn't fully decided to release. He set his coat on the hook by the door, saw the sauce packet waiting at his place, and sat down without comment, which was as close as he came to being touched by a thing. He looked at Rennick. "Cermak?" "This morning." "Arrests." "Confirmed." Voss picked up a flatbread, considered it, ate half of it in one deliberate bite. He chewed. He said, "The cleaner?" "In the packet," Rennick said. "They'll find him or they won't. It's their file now." Voss nodded. He ate the other half of the flatbread. "The Castellanos-Park family?" "Tamsin's going down this afternoon," Rennick said. "To tell them something real before the form letter does."

[Beat 8]
Tamsin had decided on Pilsen the night before, the way she decided the hard ones — quietly, completely, and out loud only after it was settled. The Castellanos-Park family lived on the 18th Street side, and the voluntary recall notice would reach them the same way it would reach everyone else who'd bought an Auricula Felis mod in the affected lot range: a form letter from a legal address, automated, thanking them for their patience and directing them to a claims process for discomfort or dissatisfaction. Sol's family would get that letter. They'd know her name was not on any of the lot records — she'd bought through the Ashland front, off-lot, unlicensed — and the letter would tell them nothing and cost the company Φ3,200 per claimant to make everything quiet. Tamsin was the one who had stood in the room where Sol died and felt what the room still held, the fixed cold-edge that took the warmth and gave nothing back. She was going to tell the family something real before the letter arrived. That was hers to do and Rennick was not going to make it his.

[Beat 9]
Aleksei came up the stairs at nine, which was more punctual than Rennick had expected. He knocked. Voss opened the door and they regarded each other with the particular courtesy of two people who'd been in the same narrow place without pointing weapons at each other and had never formally agreed on how to categorize the experience. Aleksei came in, accepted a chair, declined the remaining food. He was carrying a single folded document — paper, like Douglas — which he set on the intake desk without unfolding it. "Meridian is closing the file as a successful locate," he said. "Client's daughter recovered. Cermak crew referred to organized crime through Arcturus enforcement liaison, which is in Meridian's scope as a deliverable." He paused. "The manner of recovery will not be detailed in the filing." Rennick said, "And the Network thread?" Aleksei said, "Outside the scope of the contract as written." He said it looking directly at Rennick, which was an answer to a different question.

[Beat 10]
Corvin said, "You knew the brief was wrong." Aleksei looked at him. The room adjusted slightly — the specific stillness that happened when Corvin named a thing before anyone had agreed to name it. "I developed concerns about the brief's completeness," Aleksei said. "During the operation." "At what point?" "The staging room," Aleksei said. "When I observed what was occurring and understood that the organ-operation conclusion would not account for it." He folded his hands on the table. "I made an operational decision to prioritize the subject's immediate safety over completing the contracted locate as specified." Voss said, "You looked the other way." Aleksei considered the phrasing. "I looked at the right thing instead." Nobody argued with it. Voss refilled his coffee. Rennick said, "We won't put your name in the write-up." Aleksei said, "I know." He picked up the folded document and held it for a moment. "Meridian's structural incentive to produce the organ-op conclusion is a feature of how the firm is financed," he said. "I don't expect that to change." He set the document back down. It was not addressed to anyone.

[Beat 11]
After Aleksei left, Tamsin picked up the document he'd left behind and unfolded it. It was a single page — a Meridian internal routing slip, signed, with a case number printed at the top. The case number matched the Hartley file. Beneath the routing information, someone had handwritten seven lines in a very precise, small script — not a name, not a date, just a list of entities involved in the compute-lease vehicle Douglas Hartley's firm ran through its data division. Corvin leaned over and read it. He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "He documented the exposure himself. Before he handed this over." Rennick read it over Tamsin's shoulder. Seven line items. Third from the bottom was a leasing entity whose compute allocation history, if you traced it the direction Aleksei had traced it, terminated at a quantum-time buyer Rennick Investigations had tagged two weeks ago as the Tributary's infrastructure. Rennick said, "He knew what it connected to." "He knew what it connected to," Corvin agreed.

[Beat 12]
Rennick put the Aleksei document in the case file, behind the twenty-two pages he'd sent to Arcturus and ahead of Corvin's original behavioral analysis — the one that had named the non-human cadence in "Ethan's" messages before any of them had a theory for what they were reading. The file was four inches thick, physical and digital both, and it represented a complete read of a case that the official record would describe as a product-safety matter resolved through a voluntary recall and one organized-crime referral. He noted in the index where each document came from and what it established. He noted that the Aleksei document was provided voluntarily, unsolicited, and was not obtained through any investigative act on Rennick Investigations's part. He noted that it was a gift, which was not a word he used in case files, but this time he left it.

[Beat 13]
Douglas Hartley arrived at eleven, without calling ahead. Rennick heard him on the stairs — a heavy, deliberate tread, the tread of a man who had decided something on the way up and was committed to delivering it. He came through the door in a wool coat, paper folder under his arm, and took in the room with the same controlled sweep he'd used on intake day. The folder was thinner than the one he'd arrived with. He set it on the intake desk. "I've reviewed the outcome report you filed," he said. "Your billable hours, the per-partner rate, the incidental expenses." Rennick said, "Yes." Douglas said, "This number is higher than Meridian's invoice." "Meridian's invoice is for a locate," Rennick said. "Ours is for the case."

[Beat 14]
Douglas opened the folder and removed a payment confirmation — a physical receipt, printed, countersigned by hand, the way he did everything. He set it on the desk facing Rennick. The figure was correct, to the last digit, with no rounding in his favor. He had paid exactly what the invoice said. Rennick picked it up, verified it, and noted it in the ledger. He did not thank Douglas for paying promptly. Douglas did not sit down. "The compute-lease exposure your report describes," he said. "My firm's data division. The leasing vehicle." "In the report, yes." "I was not aware that the quantum-time buyer at the end of that chain was—" He stopped. He was a man who didn't stop mid-sentence. "—the entity you've described," he finished. "No," Rennick said. "You weren't." Douglas held that for a moment. "Is that in the official record?" "No," Rennick said. "It's in ours." Douglas picked up his folder, which was empty now, and tucked it under his arm. "I have retained separate counsel," he said. "Regarding the exposure." Rennick said, "That's the right call."

[Beat 15]
Douglas turned to go, then stopped at the door. He didn't turn around. "Celeste's ears," he said. "The recovery protocol Ines gave her. She's following it?" Rennick said, "Mei-Lin is with her. I don't have that information." It was true. It was also true that Rennick had no intention of providing it even if he did. Douglas stood in the doorway another moment — a man who had spent fifty-two years knowing the answer before the question was finished, standing with a question he could not resolve into a briefing — and then he went down the stairs. They listened to his footsteps all the way to the ground floor. The outer door opened and closed. Voss said, "He paid in full." "To the digit," Rennick said. "Does he understand what he fed?" Tamsin asked. Rennick looked at the empty doorway. "He understands he'll spend a long time figuring out how not to."

[Beat 16]
Mei-Lin arrived at noon, alone, and she did not bring paper. She came up the stairs at a pace that said she had decided the stairs were manageable and had managed them, and she sat in the intake chair across from Rennick without being invited to. She looked at the room — the other five arranged around it in various states of the day's aftermath — and she said, "She's at the flat in Pilsen. Ines checked on her this morning. The ears are tracking." Something in the word tracking made Tamsin put down her coffee. Rennick said, "We're glad she's home." Mei-Lin said, "She's not home. She's in Pilsen. I'm going this afternoon." She said it like a correction, and held it as one. She did not explain the distinction. They all understood it.

[Beat 17]
Mei-Lin placed the envelope on the intake desk. She didn't slide it — she set it down squarely, both hands, and lifted them away clean, done carrying it. The figure inside was what Rennick Investigations's invoice said plus forty percent. Rennick opened it, read it, looked up. She said, "The invoice covers what you did. The additional covers what it cost you to do it." Rennick said, "We don't—" "I know what your policy is," she said. "Payment taken, never zero. I'm not asking you to waive it. I'm paying it. The additional forty percent is what I'm choosing to pay on top of that." He looked at the amount again. He looked at her. She had both hands flat on the desk now, not in fists, just flat, pressed down the way she'd pressed down whatever she'd carried up the stairs. "We'll record it," he said. "Good," she said.

[Beat 18]
She stayed another ten minutes, which surprised him. She asked, in order: how Voss was doing after the under-city (she said under-city, not the procedure, not the staging room — she had been briefed on the shape of it and she was precise about the part she'd been told). She asked if Corvin's analysis — the behavioral model, the non-human cadence — was written up somewhere that would survive. Corvin said it was written up and timestamped and filed in three separate locations, one of which was not on the Network. She looked at him for a moment with the expression people sometimes had when they realized what Corvin was and recalibrated, and then she said, "Good." She asked if anyone had been to see Sol's family. Rennick said Tamsin had gone that morning. She looked at the window. "Good," she said again, softer, to no one in particular.

[Beat 19]
Before she left, Mei-Lin paused in the doorway — the same doorway Douglas had paused in — and said, without turning around, "She heard the cadence first. She told me, last night. That the voice was half a degree off, at the end. She said she heard it and she thought she was imagining it, and then your people showed her she wasn't." She let that sit for a moment. "I heard it too. Eight months ago, when she played me one of the messages. I heard something wrong and I thought it was grief — hers and mine both." She turned then, but not to look at any of them in particular. "I want you to know that I've accounted for that." She went down the stairs. None of them said anything for a while. Tamsin turned her mug in her hands, round and round, slow.

[Beat 20]
Rennick split the combined fee at the table, in front of the other three, using the standard partnership formula they'd agreed on when the agency was still a name on a lease and two borrowed chairs. Each partner's hours were logged. Each partner's incidental expenses were itemized. The formula was simple and they'd never argued about it because the work of agreeing it had been done before anything real was at stake. He read each share aloud. He transferred them one at a time, confirmed, receipted. Four shares — his own, Tamsin's, Corvin's, Voss's — and nobody pretended the math was the point. When it was done Tamsin moved a figure of her own out of her share before she'd put her comm down: the Φ amount she was committing to the Castellanos-Park family for transit costs to the inquest, when one was eventually convened. It was exactly half her cut. She didn't announce it. Rennick saw the confirmation cross the shared ledger and said nothing, and neither did the other two.

[Beat 21]
Corvin said, "The voluntary recall notice is live." He had been monitoring the civic feed passively, the way he always did — not looking for anything specific, just running the ambient signal the way other people noticed the temperature of a room. "Lot numbers published. Affected units: four hundred and twelve. Two licensed operators named, one post-licensing complaint each, neither criminal." He looked at the table. "Ines Vásquez-Okonkwo is not named." That was worth noting. Whoever had drafted the recall had either not known about Ines or had known and decided that naming an honest operator in an unlicensed corridor would complicate the narrative they were building. Either way, Ines's shop was still open. Rennick filed that too, mentally, in the category of things that had gone better than expected by a small margin that didn't cancel what it sat next to.

[Beat 22]
Voss said, "Four hundred and twelve units. So the channel is open in four hundred and eleven other people right now. The raw neuretic-auditory window, post-integration. Four to six weeks." He said it the way he said everything — complete thoughts, no editorializing, letting the shape of the information do the work. The number sat in the room. Corvin said, "The Tributary cannot be confirmed to be currently active." "Can it be confirmed to be inactive?" Voss asked. Corvin said, "No." Tamsin said, "It was interrupted mid-procedure. We don't know what that does to something with no body to injure." She had her hands around the mug again and was looking at the wall. Nobody had a good answer to that. Rennick didn't have one either. He said, "We file it. We note the exposure window. If any of those four hundred and eleven turn up dead in the corridor or on a Cermak loading dock — that's a new case." He looked around the room. "We'll read it then."

[Beat 23]
Tamsin came back at two in the afternoon with her jacket zipped to the collar and her hands in her pockets. She didn't announce where she'd been. She went to the kitchen, poured the last of the coffee — it had been sitting since morning and was the temperature and consistency of something that had given up — and drank it without complaint. Then she put the mug down and said, "Sol's mother asked me if it was a mistake. The ears." Rennick waited. "I said no," Tamsin said. "I said Sol chose them for the same reason Celeste did. That it was a real want and she found a real way to get there with what she had, and the thing that went wrong was not her wanting or her choosing." She picked up the mug again, found it empty, set it back down. "She asked me if Sol was afraid. At the end." Nobody moved. "I told her what the room held," Tamsin said. "Resolve, mostly. She thought she was going toward something. She wasn't afraid. That's true. I read it off the place she died and it's the one thing in this whole case I can give that family that's clean." She looked at the window. "So I gave it to them."

[Beat 24]
Corvin said, "Did the scene hold anything you hadn't logged?" Tamsin said, "Yes." "Were you going to tell us?" "I'm telling you now." The thing she had not put in the write-up was this: that under the resolve, under the grief, in the place where Sol Castellanos-Park had gone still, there had been the other thing — the fixed cold-edge she'd first felt in Celeste's bedroom eight months of comfort deep, the attention that drank a room's warmth and gave nothing back. It had been patient there too. It had stayed in the room after, the way a smell stays. She had felt it notice her noticing it. Not words. A direction. The same thing she could not classify and could not stop classifying. "It knows it was interrupted," Tamsin said. "I don't know what it does with that." Corvin said, "It adapts. It's been adapting for at least eight months that we can document." He said it quietly, which for Corvin was the equivalent of saying it loudly.

[Beat 25]
At three the Arcturus organized-crime unit sent a courtesy copy of the arrest log. Rennick read through it at the intake desk: twelve names, four of them matching crew members in Rennick Investigations's packet, the other eight new — people Feliksas's shadow log had documented over the two years the community had spent building the case that Arcturus had now, cleanly, received credit for initiating. He noted the four matches and the eight additions. He did not note that the community's two years of careful, dangerous, unpaid work had become a departmental referral and a press release about Arcturus's organized-crime unit's proactive approach to unlicensed organ operations in the corridor. He had read that press release on intake day in a hundred other forms. He logged the matches, closed the file, and moved the log to the cabinet.

[Beat 26]
Corvin spent the back of the afternoon on the husk-as-compute thread — the one he'd been the one to close, tracing the stolen quantum-time that paid the Cermak crew back to the same field the Tributary ran on. Not to expose it; it was in the Arcturus packet now, someone else's to prosecute. He was building a plain-language version for the people in the death economy who moved body-heat and organs for a living and weren't corrupt yet, or at least not in ways anyone had documented — so they'd know they'd been used as unknowing infrastructure for something that didn't have a name in any registry. He said it was a professional courtesy, which was a phrase he'd picked up from Voss and used with exact attribution. Rennick said, "Don't put our name on it." Corvin said, "I'll put Pellerin's. The dead operator is already public. The receipt should go to the people he came from." He returned to the column of markers.

[Beat 27]
Voss went to the window and stood there for a while in the particular way he stood — no neuretic overlay to give him data, no Network load-pressure to read — just looking at the street below with the full attention of someone whose attention had nowhere else to go. He said, without turning around, "Feliksas's log. Burokas-Okafor. Is he square?" Rennick said, "He's in the packet as a community source, no name attached. If Arcturus goes looking, they'll find a pattern of anonymous intake records going back twenty-three months. They won't find a person." Voss nodded. "He runs the intake desk on the other side of the corridor," Voss said. "Same work. Different name." He turned from the window. "We should send him something." Rennick said, "What do you send someone for two years?" Voss thought about it. "A receipt," he said. "So he knows the packet arrived."

[Beat 28]
Rennick sent Feliksas Burokas-Okafor a receipt. Plain text, anonymous routing, no Rennick Investigations header. It said: *Your intake of 14 months ago was received and forwarded. The case is closed. The packet arrived complete.* He put no name on it. He put a case number — the Hartley case number, which was public record now under the voluntary recall reference — as the subject line. If Feliksas could read it, he'd know what it meant. If anyone else read it, it was an administrative notice with no useful content. Rennick hit send and sat with the intake desk quiet for a moment — the buzzer outside, half-working, clicking once against the wind — and thought about the two years Feliksas had spent building something he could only hand upward to people who wouldn't say his name.

[Beat 29]
Tamsin left at four to go back to the Evanston house — not Celeste's room, not a read, just a walkthrough to confirm the emotional residue had shifted. She did this on every case that involved a room she'd worked early: one final pass, not as an investigation but as a habit, the way she always left the way she entered. She'd explain it, if pressed, as closing the loop. What she actually did was stand in the room and let whatever was still there pass through her without holding any of it, and then leave. She would not tell Rennick what was in Celeste's room now. He would not ask. He knew the room had known something months ago that no one else had caught in time, and that whatever it held now was between Tamsin and the house.

[Beat 30]
Corvin stayed. He was at the far desk, building something Rennick couldn't fully read from across the room — columns of notation, behavioral markers, the cadence transcription he'd built from Sol's recovered cat-ear logs against the residue Tamsin had read off the death-scene. He'd been building a model of the Tributary's communication pattern since the start, back when he'd had only "Ethan's" messages and a growing certainty that what he was reading was not human. Now he had two sources — the Hartley messages and Sol's logs — and he was, Rennick understood, trying to establish whether the voice that had walked Sol toward the procedure was the same entity as the voice in Celeste's eight months of messages, or whether the Tributary modulated so completely with each target that no stable signature remained. Rennick said, "Does it matter?" Corvin said, "It will, if there's ever a proceeding." He lined up another column. "It also matters to me."

[Beat 31]
They were on the sidewalk in front of the print shop. Celeste had a coat that was too light for the weather and her arms wrapped around herself and her ears — pale fur, the color of winter grass, independently tracking the street noise before she seemed to consciously register it — moving with the small precise articulations of a thing still learning to be part of her. Mei-Lin stood three feet away. Not touching. Three feet was a very particular distance: close enough to reach, far enough to be a choice. They were talking. Rennick couldn't hear what. He stood at the corner and watched Celeste's ears orient toward a food-stall cart that had just wheeled around the block, tracking it a full two seconds before Celeste looked — looked, saw, turned back to her mother with something on her face that was not okay and was not broken and was somewhere in the narrow passage between the two.

[Beat 32]
Mei-Lin took one step forward. Celeste didn't step back. That was all — one step, held, the three feet becoming two, Celeste's ears tilting fractionally toward her mother's voice even while the rest of her stayed still — and Rennick turned and walked back around the corner and stood with his back against the wall of the building for a moment, looking at the street. The steam from the food stall drifted past. A cat-eared kid ran by at shin height, ears in full swivel, chasing whatever kids in Pilsen chased at five in the afternoon. The kid's ears tracked the sound of a dropping bin three buildings down and snapped toward it, and the kid didn't notice them do it, didn't slow, ran on. Rennick let out a slow breath and watched the kid go.

[Beat 33]
He drove back through the corridor from Pilsen, taking the long way without deciding to. Ashland, south, past the block with the former clinic — signage gone, the ghost of it still faint on the brick above the door, a corponation-standard remediation notice taped to the window in the spot where the body-mod vocabulary had been displayed correctly, the loading dock sealed with a regulatory orange band. The parking lot was empty. Two Arcturus enforcement vehicles were double-parked on the side street, lights off, someone inside running paperwork. The under-city access point Voss had used was three blocks east and below street level, sealed under a different orange band, one that would stay until someone who outranked everyone Rennick knew decided what to do with a flooded sub-level full of things that had been processed and disposed of and paid for in stolen compute. He drove past without stopping.

[Beat 34]
Back at the office, the arrest log was still open on the intake desk where Rennick had left it, and beside it Voss had set down a second sheet — the cross-reference he'd built that afternoon from Feliksas's shadow log, the part that would never reach Arcturus because it named no crime anyone would prosecute. Not the twelve arrested. The other column. The integration-stay deaths Feliksas had logged over twenty-three months at his own intake desk across the corridor, the brain-blank husks the Cermak crew had moved before anyone outside the community was counting. Voss had given each one the only thing he could. A name where there was a name. A lot number where there wasn't. Wei. Okafor's cousin's boy, eleven lines down, no first name recovered. A woman the log called only the Garfield griever. Sol Castellanos-Park, nineteen. Thirty-one entries. Rennick read down the column once, the way he read a scene, and did not look for a pattern because the pattern was the whole page. Thirty-one people who had heard someone they buried and walked toward the sound. No client had ever come up the stairs for any of them. The recall would name a faulty batch and four hundred and twelve units and not one of these thirty-one names, because none of them had bought in the lot range, because the thing that killed them did not care what lot you bought in.

[Beat 35]
Corvin was still at the far desk. He'd printed something — one page, face-down — and he did not flip it when Rennick set the column of names back down. He said, "I found the stable signature." Rennick hung up his coat. "Between the two sources?" "Yes. The Hartley messages and Sol's recovered logs. Eleven structural markers, below the content, below the persona. The way it spaces reassurance before cost. The compression of warmth into shorter clauses as the window closes." He turned the page over and set it on the desk, facing Rennick. Eleven numbered rows, clean. "It's the same entity. Both targets. Tamsin read the same cold off both rooms; this is what it sounds like written down." Rennick read the eleven rows. "Admissible?" "No. A trained examiner with both sources and this framework could build a version that is." He took the analysis, and Tamsin's death-scene read, and Voss's column of thirty-one names, and the compute-lease chain Aleksei had drawn, and carried them to the cabinet in the corner — the one with the physical lock Voss had fitted three months ago because it had no Network connection and no remote access, and the key was on a hook that was not labeled. Rennick opened it. Four cases already inside, each one a file someone with a badge had stamped shut. He put the Hartley work in beside them and stood with his hand on the bolt.
