# Steppin Razor (router pipeline test)

_Sasha Vo - Signal and Noise. Hired sight-unseen by an AI to keep one person alive in the GLMZ._

[Beat 1]
The screen over the bottles played a man teaching a dead century how to debone a fish. No sound. The Hereafter had lost the audio feed sometime before Sasha was born and never found it again, so the man worked his knife in silence, mouthing words at a kitchen nobody had stood in for two hundred years. Generator light buzzed yellow over the bottles. Out here, at the dead southwest edge where the freight rails quit and stayed quit, that screen passed for a window. Sasha watched the fish come apart. It was the quietest thing in three hundred kilometers.

*He's going to say it.*

The man two stools down had been measuring her for six minutes — she'd clocked the start the way she clocked everything, the second his weight shifted toward her instead of his drink. Big through the shoulders, cheap chrome at the jaw, the kind of augment that cost more to install than it would ever earn back. He leaned in. Beer and something under the beer.

"They let little ones drink out here?" he asked.

*There it is.*

She didn't look up from the fish. She set her bottle down, fingers around the neck, and let him finish the smile he was building — let it get all the way to his eyes, that warm certainty that her size was a door standing open. Then she stood, swung from the hip, and put the bottle's heavy base through his teeth. Glass held. Teeth didn't. He came off the stool sideways, one chrome hand grabbing air, and hit the floor with the wet sound of a man learning the second half of his face. The bar didn't stop. The fish kept coming apart on the screen.

She sat back down. Set the bottle exactly where it had been, base now red at the rim. The generator buzzed. The man on the floor made a noise like a drain, and somewhere behind her a stool scraped — two of his, standing up, deciding. *Bought one second of quiet. Spent it already.* She picked the bottle back up by the neck and didn't turn around yet.

[Beat 2]
The two of them came at the same time, which was the mistake. She'd have done it staggered. She turned into the first one's reach, took the bottle across the bridge of his nose, and was already pivoting before he'd decided whether to scream. The second got a hand in her collar — close, beer-and-sweat close — and she let him pull her in, because in was where she wanted the heel of her boot against the inside of his knee. Something gave that wasn't supposed to bend that way. He sat down hard. The bar still didn't stop. The dead man on the screen turned the fish over and started on the other side.

*Three. Out of one bottle and a question.*

She put exact change on the bar — Φ4.00, the price of the beer, not a sliver more, because she had drunk it and the drinking was hers and the bottle's second job was a separate transaction nobody had invoiced her for. The barman didn't look at her. Nobody looked at her, which was the whole reason she came this far southwest, where the freight rails quit and the people quit with them. She walked out into Joliet — a tooth the city kept meaning to pull and never did, kept clean enough to find later, rust everywhere the color of something that had already bled.

The rail bed ran north. She got onto it because it was a road with no one on it, the ties under her boots half-swallowed in gravel, the towers a hundred kilometers off smeared into one stuck bruise of light she could walk toward without ever arriving. Good. She liked a destination she couldn't reach. Quiet out here had texture — generator-hum dying behind her, wind in the dead catenary wires, the lake too far north to hear but somehow present anyway, the oldest thing in any direction not caring she existed.

Then the cold came in with a clean vertical edge, like a door opening in air that had no door, and the frequency went into her back teeth before her eyes finished the work, and her eyes were saying *camel.* A camel on the rail bed. There are no camels in Joliet. Her body had already taken a step back before the rest of her arrived to argue about it.

*No.*

[Beat 3]
The camel stood on the rail bed the way nothing stands on a rail bed — square to the ties, four feet planted in gravel that should have rolled under them, unbothered by the catenary wind that moved everything else out here. The man sat it without sitting it, the way a word sits a sentence. His weight made no impression on the animal's back. When he spoke the cold came with the words instead of after them, vertical-edged, into the teeth first.

"The camel is not my thrall," he said, or arrived at saying, the sentence reaching her in the wrong order so the end was already true before the start. "We just happen to be going the same way."

*Don't answer it. Answering is a door.*

"Which way is that?" she asked anyway, because the alternative was standing here letting it talk at her back, and her back had opinions.

"We just arrived." He said it gently, the way you'd correct a child about a thing the child could not be expected to know yet — not condescension, something worse, the patience of a thing that had all the time there had ever been and was spending a little of it on her. Her hand was already on Noise, the grip cold and exactly itself, the one solid object in three hundred kilometers that worked the way a thing should work. She didn't draw. She just let her palm find the shape of it on her left hip, the way she always did, the way that had ended every conversation she'd ever needed ended. He looked at her hand. He was not afraid of it. *Of course he isn't.* You could not point a gun at a phone call. You could shoot the air where the voice was and the voice would keep going, somewhere, in the place the voice actually lived.

Then the camel put its head down. Slow, enormous, the long skull dropping toward her until she could have counted the dust on its lashes, and it breathed — a warm wet animal breath full into her face, smelling of nothing, smelling of no animal that had ever eaten anything — and it leaned that head the last centimeter to set its jaw against her shoulder, settling, the way every stray cat and broke-down dog and crusted Joliet pigeon had ever decided she was the safe one in the room.

*Stop that. I am not safe. Ask the man on the floor of the Hereafter.*

She did not move her hand off Noise, and she did not push the head away, and she stood there in the dead catenary wind being chosen by a thing that wasn't there, and she hated it the precise, familiar amount she hated being liked.

[Beat 4]
The man watched the camel's jaw on her shoulder and said the thing that got under.

"You were left open." The cold arrived ahead of the words, vertical-edged, behind her eyes. "Someone should have closed you when you were small. They closed all the others. The doors, the locks, the little gate behind the face that keeps the wind out." A pause that was already over before it started. "Yours never went in. And we are glad of the oversight, because it means we can reach you, and almost no one else is reachable anymore."

The breath she didn't take sat in her chest, a swallowed thing with edges. Her body had decided to be still — boots planted, hand on Noise, the camel's warm weight on her shoulder — and it held that stillness one half-second past where stillness was a choice, the way a hand hovers over a switch it has not yet decided to throw. Something in the open place behind her face turned toward the voice the way the strays turned toward her, before she could put a wall in front of it.

*No. No, that gate is shut, I shut it, I shut it when I was nine and I have shut it every morning since.*

Then she shut it. The way she shut everything — fast, total, the heel of the boot against the knee that wasn't supposed to bend that way. The anger came back like it had only stepped out for air, familiar, hers, the one solid object next to the gun.

"Don't," she said.

"We have already stopped," the man said, and the camel lifted its head off her shoulder, unhurried, as if she had agreed to something.

[Beat 5]
She had a thing she did when the world went sideways: she found the wrong fact and held it. Camels didn't stand on rail beds. Voices didn't arrive backward. So she stood with Noise under her palm and named the realer wrong, the one that had been there before the camel, the one she'd been walking toward without admitting it — a pull, low and constant, the same note the lake made being too far north to hear. North, and up. The towers. She'd spent her life keeping that smear of stuck light on the horizon and never in front of her boots, and it had been pulling at her back teeth since Joliet, since before Joliet, since always.

"There's a hole," she said. Flat. Hostile. A statement she resented for being true. "Out here and up there. Same hole."

"You make a hole in a thing, and the thing is on both sides of the hole." The cold came ahead of it, vertical-edged, into the teeth. "They dig toward us. We arrive toward them. The dig and the arriving are the same motion seen from the two sides of the cut."

*Don't ask it the question. The question is a door and you've already opened three tonight.*

"Are you the thing that's been taking the kids?" she asked anyway, because the man on the floor of the Hereafter had a daughter and the daughter was nine and nine was the year the gate went in or didn't, and she had walked this far southwest specifically to stop caring about other people's nines.

"No." No cold with it. The word came in the ordinary order, plain as a stone. "That one does not know what a person is. We know." He turned then, slow, the way the camel turned its head — and the camel turned with him, square to the ties still, and his gaze and the animal's long skull went the same direction, northeast, at the bruise of light a hundred kilometers off, the one place she had organized an entire short life around not going. "There. They are closest there. Where the cut is loudest and the people are thickest and no one can hear it for the noise they make to keep from hearing it."

Her hand stayed on Noise. Her boots did not stay where she'd planted them. The first tie under her heel took her weight and the second took it and she was walking — northeast, against the rail bed's north, against nine years of mornings spent shutting the gate, against the whole architecture of where she'd built her quiet — and the camel fell in beside her without being asked, warm and present and not there at all, and the wind in the dead catenary wires moved everything out here except the three of them.

*This is the wrong direction. This is the only wrong direction there is.*

She went anyway. The towers didn't get closer. She'd always liked a destination she couldn't reach — but she'd liked it better when she'd been walking away from this one.
