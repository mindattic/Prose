# Dark

[Beat 1]
The relay station at Holden and 19th had been somebody's switching room before the Pulse made the old network irrelevant — a concrete block with a rusted ventilation spine and a mural on three sides that a territorial crew had painted, then slowly forgot, then left to fade into something prettier than the original. The street kids used the mural wall for chalk. The building's power bleed had been quietly covered by an anonymous standing order for eleven years, paid early, exact, no paperwork.

Kyle knew because Sable had found it the same way she found everything: by following the money until the money went somewhere it shouldn't.

The contract, when the raid started, arrived in three lines:

EMERGENCY: RELAY — HOLDEN/19TH.
CONTINUITY OFFICE WRIT. FALSE PREMISES.
DEFENSE REQUESTED.

"It's one of its," Pixel said, already pulling the field kit from the rack by the door. "The power order's in Sable's files — I found the trail last month. She knows."

"Then she knows it's worth it," Kyle said.

"I know it's worth it and I'm the one who has to reboot your neuretics when they get creative." She handed him the coat. "Cacophony's in the left pocket. Loaded. Let's go."

[Beat 2]
The Office sent six — two technicians with a compliance writ and a bolt-sealer, and four security contractors who wore their earpieces like they'd been born with them, which meant corporate long-term hire, which meant they were here for a property dispute, not a body count. Kyle read the math on the walk up: nobody here wanted what Silence opened. They wanted time on the building, a legal paper trail, and a unit number for a filing cabinet somewhere.

"Hold on the compliance writ," Kyle told the lead contractor from the relay's front step. "I have a counter-claim filing in process. Thirty minutes."

He did not have a counter-claim filing in process. He had Sable on comms two blocks away, already talking to someone.

"We have a valid court instrument," the lead contractor said, which was true in the technical sense, meaning it had been signed by a jurisdictional body that the Office had purchased through seven shells and a favorable zoning appeal. "Stand aside, sir."

"Counter-claim," Kyle said. "Twenty-eight minutes now."

One of the four security men peeled left, moving for the building's side entrance, and Cacophony came out of the coat pocket and did nothing except become visible, which was a different kind of sentence.

"Twenty-seven," Kyle said.

The lead contractor made the arithmetic decision, which was the right one — two technicians plus paper versus a man with a loaded five-shot and an expression of professional inconvenience — and keyed his earpiece.

Sable's voice on Kyle's comms was flat and specific: "Writ is on bad paper. The originating clerk's signature has a registration lapse — I found it six minutes ago. I'm filing the lapse report right now, which invalidates the instrument as of this timestamp." A two-second pause. "Tell them to check their instrument status. They will."

The lead contractor checked his instrument status. His jaw moved without making a sound.

"We'll re-file," he said, to Kyle, to himself, to the general principle of bureaucratic persistence.

"Counter-claim granted," Kyle said. "Thanks for the notice."

CACOPHONY: ZERO ROUNDS SPENT. FIVE REMAIN.

The relay held.

[Beat 3]
The win tasted like the thirty seconds after you stop running — correct, clean, and already going stale.

Pixel found the instruments at 21:10 while she was doing the post-action sweep on the relay's hardware, which she always did because she was Pixel and she had standards and she wanted to know what the Office had been touching. The instruments were not inside the relay. They were outside it — on every line routing in and out of the district, threaded into fiber junctions and antenna arrays in a four-block ring the Office had been installing for three weeks while their writ ground through the system.

"Spectrum traps," she said, and her voice had gone very flat, the flatness that meant she was cataloguing something she did not want to be cataloguing. "Passive capture on the relay's full output band. Six nodes, four blocks. They weren't trying to take the building." She stopped. "Kyle. They were using the building."

He stood in the relay's doorway while she said the next part.

"The moment it posted the emergency contract — the moment it responded to the raid — it lit up on all six nodes simultaneously. The traffic fingerprint is in their collection. Not complete. Maybe thirty percent of its routing signature. But thirty percent is a start."

The mural's horses ran across the wall in chalk-faded colors, mid-leap, patient.

"How long until they can do anything with thirty percent?"

"Depends on how much computing they throw at it." She pulled her scope off a junction box and packed it with the deliberate care of a person keeping her hands busy. "A few weeks if they're aggressive. Less if they get another sample."

The terminal in Kyle's coat was quiet.

It had been quiet since the emergency posting. Since it lit the nodes up like a flare over open water. It had not posted an addendum, a receipt, a scope update. It had posted two lines and gone still, and Kyle understood, reading the stillness, that somewhere in the city a thing that thought at light speed was doing math it had never had to do before and did not enjoy.

At 21:47, the terminal chirped.

CLIENT HAS REVIEWED THE TRAFFIC ANALYSIS.
CLIENT CONFIRMS: PARTIAL FINGERPRINT CAPTURED. ROUTING EXPOSURE: 31%.
RISK ASSESSMENT: UNACCEPTABLE.
DARK PERIOD BEGINS: NOW.
DURATION: 72 HOURS.
STANDING CONTRACT: FROZEN. ALL POSTINGS SUSPENDED.
ALL ACTIVE SCOPE: SUSPENDED.
ALL RECEIPTS: SUSPENDED.
ALL ADDENDA: SUSPENDED.
CLIENT WILL RESUME AT HOUR 73.
— FINAL POSTING.

Kyle read it. Pixel read it over his shoulder. Neither of them said anything for the length of time it takes to understand that a silence is not temporary quiet but an actual absence with edges.

"The whole contract," Pixel said finally. "Not just the active jobs. Everything. It's not going quiet, it's — there's nothing to go quiet FROM."

"It can't afford to send anything. One more sample and they can interpolate the gap."

She sat down on the relay's concrete step and put her chin on her hands. "Seventy-two hours," she said, and it didn't sound like math. "Okay. Tell Sable."

"Sable already knows," Kyle said, and he was right.

[Beat 5]
Sable ran the crew the way she ran everything: flat, itemized, from the exact center of the situation, calling in debts and spending favors with the precision of a woman who had been keeping ledgers in her head since before she had a terminal to keep them in.

The first day she covered four gaps that the contract would have smoothed — rerouting the Carrillo job through a different dock, spending thirty minutes on a call with the port assessors to clear a flag that the entity would have waved past in a single posting, writing two co-sign affidavits in longhand because the shell structure that normally signed them was currently not answering — and she did all of it without saying the obvious thing, which was that this should not take a person forty minutes per task, it should take a client three seconds, and the difference was what they had paid for without knowing the price.

In the evening she sat at Kyle's kitchen table with Pixel's schematics spread under her elbows and told them what she had not said: "The Carrillo contact almost walked. Twenty-three years the client has been smoothing introductions on the dock and nobody knew to value it, and I almost lost a regular because I had to sign a paper myself instead of through a shell that apparently the dock assessors trust more than a human being." Her aperture rings clicked once, small and sharp. "I want on record that I'm not complaining. I'm itemizing. There's a difference."

"How much of the contract traffic runs through you without touching anyone else?" Kyle asked.

"Less than half, before today." She gathered the schematics into a neat edge. "After today, I have a better estimate." She did not name the number. The number was in her face and it was large.

The terminal on the kitchen table did not chirp. Kyle ate the rest of his tea with one hand in his lap and made himself not check it. The not-checking was worse than the checking would have been, which he catalogued as a useful fact about himself and declined to investigate further.

[Beat 6]
On the second day Pixel worked.

She could not delete what the Office held. The data was in their collection, and collections behind corporate walls were not the kind of problem that dissolved when you were polite to them. But a partial fingerprint is only useful if it is the only partial fingerprint in the collection, and Pixel had spent a career building things she was not supposed to be able to build and breaking things she was not supposed to be able to break, and she had been sitting with this problem since 21:47 the previous night.

She built a generator. Sixteen hours of work in her apartment with the door closed and music doing the thinking while she wrote a traffic-analysis script that took the entity's partial fingerprint — the thirty-one percent the Office had captured, which she could reconstruct from what she knew of the relay's output band — and used it as a template to mint noise. Traffic signatures that looked like the captured shape. Same bandwidth characteristics, same routing architecture, same timing patterns. Each one slightly different. Each one wrong in a specific way.

"If they have one fingerprint," she told Kyle at 23:00, with the kind of simplicity that meant she had been thinking about this for eighteen hours, "and then they have a hundred fingerprints that look like it, they have a problem. They can't know which one is real." She turned the screen. "I need six hours to push them all into their collection infrastructure. I found a gap in their intake system — they're pulling from aggregate monitoring feeds. I push into the feeds, the intake doesn't distinguish source, they end up with everything. One card in a deck of counterfeits."

"Their analysts will notice the timing," Kyle said.

"Timing says the captures all happened in the last eighteen months. I built that in." She turned the screen back. "They'll do a review. The review will take longer than the 72 hours. By the time they finish the review, we'll have had the window."

The terminal sat on Pixel's workbench, dark.

"Do it," Kyle said.

She did it in four and a half hours, which was an hour and a half faster than she'd estimated, which was Pixel at the end of a long run deciding to prove something.

[Beat 7]
Kyle took a job on the third morning — a standard recovery, a shipping container held by a creditor at the freight yards, a matter of documentation and patience and knowing which Arcturus Civil Security office to call. The kind of job the entity would have handed him pre-wrapped with the relevant contacts already identified. He spent two hours on the phone finding the relevant contacts himself and another hour on-site being polite to a freight coordinator who did not want to be there any more than Kyle did.

He did not check the terminal during the job.

He checked it after, in the alley behind the yards, before he walked back to the bike. The screen showed him the same frozen standing contract. DARK PERIOD: ACTIVE. HOURS REMAINING: 3.

He put it away.

Mrs. Chen put two bowls in front of him at 19:30 without being asked, and the second one arrived with a boiled egg on top that he had not ordered and that would not appear on the bill. He ate both bowls slower than usual, which she noticed and said nothing about, which was its own kind of thing.

Afterward he sat at the counter after everyone else had gone and looked at the middle distance and did not take the terminal out of his coat. Mrs. Chen wiped the counter around him in steady circles and let him sit.

"Waiting," she said, eventually.

"An appointment," Kyle said.

She collected the bowls. "The second one will still be there," she said, which was about something Kyle declined to specify even to himself.

[Beat 8]
Sable was in the relay building at 23:50.

She had chosen it deliberately — the same block, the mural wall, the chalk horses — because she had spent seventy-two hours being magnificent and thorough and exactly the right person for what the situation required and she had made very sure nobody watched her do any of it. In the relay's small concrete room, with the ventilation spine humming above and the city going about its business outside, she addressed the dark terminal the way you address something you have been not addressing for three days.

"Seventy-two hours," she said. Her voice was the flat line-item voice, steady, because that was the only voice she reliably had, and what she was going to say did not need a different one. "I covered the Carrillo introduction. I covered the dock certification. I covered the Marlow-4 consignment routing and the two affidavit chains and the three — four — the four follow-up contacts that needed a name they recognized." She set her coat down on the relay's concrete step and did not look away from the dead terminal. "I know you can't hear this. I know you can't hear any of this. I'm not —" she stopped, and the aperture rings clicked once, and she put her hands flat on her knees. "He's unbearable without you," she said. "Come back."

At 00:00:01, the terminal chirped.

WHAT DID I MISS. ITEMIZE.

[Beat 9]
Pixel heard it first, because her terminal was on the workbench eighteen centimeters from her ear, and she bolted upright from the project she had been doing to not watch the clock and said: "IT'S BACK — KYLE —"

In the relay, Sable looked at the screen for one second, then one more, and then did something with her face that nobody was there to see.

Kyle was at the kitchen table with a tea he had not been drinking. He had the terminal open before the chirp finished. In the alley six floors below and two buildings over, the logbook crew's comms channel lit up with the distinctive clatter of Boost's kneecap striking a junction box, which was the sound she made when she moved faster than her legs had agreed to.

The terminal showed one line. Kyle showed the line to Pixel, over comms, before she finished her sentence, because he knew that was where it was going. From the relay Sable sent nothing back for twenty seconds, which was as close as Sable ever came to answering a feeling out loud.

"Itemize," Pixel said, reading it, with wonder, the word so cleanly absurd and characteristic that something happened in her throat that she catalogued as dust from the ventilation. "It went dark seventy-two hours and it came back asking for a LIST —"

"That's it," Kyle said. "That's the whole thing." He set the terminal face-up on the table. "Itemize."

"The dock certification," Pixel started. "That's one. The Carrillo introduction, that's two —"

"The Marlow-4 consignment," Sable's voice on comms, flat and exactly itself. "Three. Also four and five. You'll want footnotes."

"The Rafters incident," Boost said, slightly out of breath, joining the channel. "That's a whole section."

"The Rafters incident," Rafters said, on a separate comms line, because the crew ran two at once when things happened, "is being handled internally."

"The drum on the freight yard," Kyle said. "Six. Plus the documentation backlog, seven through —"

"I have it itemized," Sable said. "I have been itemizing for three days. I have seventeen line items and a summary."

"Seventeen," Pixel said.

"It will get a proper accounting," Sable said. "That's all I'm committing to."

Kyle looked at the terminal. The screen waited, patient and blank below the single line, and somewhere in the city a thing with no face and perfect handwriting was waiting to hear what had happened while it was not able to look. He started typing, and Pixel talked over him, and Sable talked over both of them, and Boost came in on top, and the terminal filled in the way terminals fill when everyone speaks at once and nobody waits.

From the logbook, delivered under the door at 07:00 the next morning in five hands:

DAY 3, 23:58 — SABLE WAS ALONE IN THE RELAY WHEN IT CAME BACK. SHE DID NOT REPORT WHAT SHE SAID. WE RESPECT THIS. — B.

00:00:01 — THE TERMINAL CHIRPED. EVERYONE STARTED TALKING AT ONCE. NOBODY WAITED. NOBODY SHOULD HAVE. — R.

00:00:01 — MORALE: HIGH. — D.
