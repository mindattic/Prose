# Standard Rate

[Beat 1]
Casper Vey's tea house was three blocks from the target building, which Sable had filed as either the most useful fact of the morning or the most alarming one. She'd asked. He'd said: "If the building burns, I'd like the story."

The crew ate at the Lotus counter on the second floor of the place across the street — the one with no sign and the best view — because Kyle had spent his marker there at 09:00 and the branch had put up exactly as many chairs as they needed. Nothing was said about it. Nobody at the truce counter touched a weapon, by custom, by the terms the lacquered marker bought, and so the crew ate ramen in view of the gray office tower they intended to take apart, and the Narrows ran its ordinary morning underneath them unimpeded.

One line, from the end of the counter, where a woman with a red braid and aperture-ring eyes was eating with professional concentration: "Marker spends once. It's spent."

"I know what I bought," Kyle said.

"You bought passage. The door, the hall, six floors of occupied building." Sable put her spoon down. "You did not buy an extraction if this goes sideways."

"I bought us a table. Professionals eat before they work."

Down the counter, Gantry spooned broth without taking her eyes off the loading dock across the street, estimating weights by the geometry of the freight entry. Furnace ate fast, both hands, already somewhere else in her head. Marisol — Marisol, freelance verification, three-year trade acquaintance, a Read who charged what the market thought she was worth and then a further thirty percent for the part the market kept pretending wasn't real — Marisol held her bowl in both hands and looked at nothing, which was how she looked at everything.

The logbook entry, filed at 11:47, read: CREW ATE BEFORE. (SABLE HAD THE SPICY ONE. THIS LOG IS FACTUAL.)

[Beat 2]
At 12:00 the first thing arrived.

Gantry had promised it by freight delivery — "I need a counterweight situation," she'd told the rigger's cooperative, "arriving at the loading dock at noon with a valid manifest, because I am going in with the counterweight" — and the cooperative had asked no further questions because Gantry had a credit history and Gantry's credit history had opinions. What arrived was a machinery crate, five tons on a crane arm, a load that legitimately required the freight lift and a second opinion at the dock, and Gantry was inside it, with her tools and the patience of a rigger who had been in tighter spots.

The loading dock's freight manager studied the manifest. The manifest was immaculate. He processed it.

At 12:14 the freight lift signed for five tons and a good-morning nod, and Gantry stepped off on the third sub-floor and began disabling things methodically.

[Beat 3]
Furnace came in through a wall.

Not a door — a wall, because she had read the blueprints and the blueprints said STRUCTURAL, which to Furnace meant "challenge" and nothing else. The thermal lance was built in a shop below a storage unit in the Ashgrave fringe — Furnace's shop, Furnace's tools — and it cut a gap in the south exterior wall at 12:22 with the cheerful economy of a woman who found this particular kind of work rewarding.

"That's me IN," she said, to no one, and stepped through.

[Beat 4]
The acquisition program's site was a leased floor and a half above a server vault — gray carpet, temporary partitions, the kind of space that existed to be abandoned cleanly, its whole architecture a confession that nobody expected to stay. The Continuity Office — a shell family, acquisition-language in the filing, Mr. Able's operation by any working name — had staffed it with retrieval consultants and a dispatch desk and a server room that Pixel had been planning toward for three weeks.

Sable ran the timing from the street, via comms, flat and transactional: "Second floor, four at the desk. Third floor, server room, two and a biometric. Basement vault, three. Gantry's got sub-three. Furnace has the east stairwell. You have the front."

"Cacophony's full," Kyle said.

"I know how many rounds you have."

"I'm saying it for the record."

"There's no record."

"There's always a record," Kyle said, because Boost's crew had been logging this building for forty-eight hours and there were, in fact, four logbooks on-site, two of them waterproof.

He walked in the front.

The lobby consultant did the professional thing: one hand on the desk terminal, one hand below desk-line, the weight distribution of someone trained to give every visitor ten seconds to be legitimate before the calculus changed. She was halfway through the ten seconds when her terminal screen went blue, then black, then filled with text that was not hers.

[Beat 5]
Second floor was four consultants and a dispatch rig, and by the time Kyle reached the landing the dispatch rig was playing hold music — actual hold music, a string quartet arrangement of something that had been popular before either of the consultants were born — and two of the four were staring at their terminals with the particular stillness of people receiving news of a different kind.

CONTRACT — CONTINUITY OFFICE: CONSULTANT STABLE.
EFFECTIVE: IMMEDIATELY.
SUBJECT: NOTICE OF SEVERANCE — [NAME], FIELD COORDINATOR, LEVEL 2.
TERMS: FULL. SETTLEMENT: 127Φ — PLUS ACCRUED HAZARD DIFFERENTIAL, ITEMIZED.
REFERENCES: PROVIDED.
THE CLIENT NOTES YOUR RETENTION OF FIELD PROTOCOLS DURING THE HALVORSEN INCIDENT WAS COMMENDABLE.

The field coordinator read the accrued hazard differential line twice, looked at the man next to her, looked at Kyle, and sat down. Her chair exhaled. She folded her hands on the desk and took a breath that used her whole body.

"Hazard differential," she said, to no one in particular. "Four years I filed for that."

The man next to her was already reading his own screen.

That left two consultants doing actual math.

The first one had the build of someone cross-trained in both the keeping and the breaking of things, and he came off the desk fast, clear purpose, no preamble — not brave, just professional, running a calculation that the first twenty seconds hadn't yet closed. Kyle took the desk between them as a lane and gave him the near corner, the angle where a full swing died on the partition edge. The man was good enough to feel the geometry, adapt, come in low with an elbow that had closing velocity and honest weight behind it. It landed on the shoulder where the mesh had an opinion. The mesh agreed: cost logged, information filed. Cacophony stayed on the hip. Silence took the actuator housing at the man's right wrist guard — not the wrist, the housing, the exposed servo-bracket that aftermarket arm rigs always wore proud of the sleeve — and the arm went slack in a shower of calibration springs.

The man sat down against the partition. "Okay," he said, breathing.

"Stay there," Kyle said.

The second consultant had watched the wrist bracket come apart and finished the math his colleague had been running. He looked at his terminal. His terminal had arrived.

CONTRACT — CONTINUITY OFFICE: CONSULTANT STABLE.
EFFECTIVE: IMMEDIATELY.
SUBJECT: NOTICE OF SEVERANCE — [NAME], FIELD SECURITY, LEVEL 2.
TERMS: FULL. SETTLEMENT: 98Φ.
THE CLIENT NOTES YOUR PERFECT ATTENDANCE RECORD.
THE CLIENT NOTES THIS IS RARER THAN YOU KNOW.

He read it once. He picked up his bag from under the desk — had it there already, which said what it said about how long some of these people had been watching the Office's arithmetic — and walked past Kyle with the nod one professional gives another when the job is clearly over.

"Exit's clear," Sable said, in his ear. "He went south."

[Beat 6]
On the third floor, Marisol was already working.

She stood at the server room's biometric panel with her eyes open and her hands at her sides, and the lights behind the glass were the wrong color because Gantry had been through the sub-floor cabling. Pixel was at the interior terminal — she'd come in through the HVAC access with a silence so complete that the third-floor supervisor had not noticed until she was already seated, already in the gap between what the server room thought it was authorized to do and what Pixel was authorizing it to do instead.

"Forty-seven decoy nodes," Pixel said, out loud, in the register she used for procedures that had a person at both ends. "The real collection is in racks seven through eleven. Marisol?"

Marisol hadn't touched the panel. She was reading it the way she read everything — not the screen, not the lock, but the weight behind the architecture. The fingerprint of every consultation the servers had ever held.

"Seven through eleven," she confirmed. "The others are theater. Rack nine has the acquisition ledgers. Rack ten has the fingerprint remnants."

"Remnants," Pixel said.

"What's left of them."

"Then rack ten gets the signature," Pixel said, and her hands moved, and the thing she had been building for three weeks — a poisoned-data injection that would write her own corrupted fingerprint backward through the entire remnant archive, making every record of every consultation the Office had ever done wrong in exactly the same way, unprovably, permanently — went into rack ten and began to work.

The servers accepted it the way servers accept authorized operations.

The terminal updated.

CLIENT ADVISES: RACK ELEVEN CONTAINS ONE ADDITIONAL ACTIVE NODE NOT ON SCHEMATIC.
CLIENT ADVISES: IT IS LOGGING.
CLIENT ADVISES: DO NOT LEAVE RACK ELEVEN.

Pixel looked at the screen with the expression she reserved for moments when the best client in the GLMZ had done something she needed a minute to feel correctly.

"It's HERE," she said. "Running overwatch through their own system."

Above rack eleven, a status light that had been amber ticked to green.

CLIENT NOTES: THE ENGINEER'S WORK IS IMPECCABLE.
CLIENT NOTES: THIS IS NOT THE FIRST TIME IT HAS NOTED THIS.
CLIENT NOTES: THE ENGINEER MAY TAKE THE LOG AS RECORD.

"I'm keeping this one," Pixel said, to the screen, and the screen cycled through twelve more lines in the time it took her to say it.

[Beat 7]
The basement vault had three consultants and the biometric lock that Gantry had already disassembled from the wrong side, methodically, with tools carried in from the crate.

Two of the three read their screens. The one who read it first put down his radio. The second one, slower reader, put hers down twenty seconds later. The third one had not gotten the posting yet — or had gotten it and did not believe it — and he was running a different calculation, the kind that ends at the same address but takes longer to arrive.

Kyle was at the vault floor by then, and Gantry was behind him with cable ties she would explain to the arbitration record as "standard rigging equipment."

The third consultant came to the end of his calculation visibly: the shoulder drop, the breath, the moment a professional recognizes that the contract they are working on has already closed. He put up a hand — not surrender, exactly; more like calling for a pause at the end of an exchange that everyone understood was over.

"Severance is actually on the terminal?" he asked. "Itemized?"

"Itemized," Kyle said. "Line four has a back-pay note."

The man exhaled. "Three years I've been filing that commendation dispute."

"Go read line four," Gantry said, which settled it.

[Beat 8]
Furnace burned the vault.

Not the building — the acquisition ledgers, the server hardware from racks one through six and nine, the print copies that the Office kept because the Office was old-fashioned about redundancy, and the one active node the entity had flagged in rack eleven, which turned out to be the relay the fingerprint vault had been feeding, and which Furnace took apart with a thermal lance and professional satisfaction at 12:58.

"That's the hardware," she said, stepping back, because a thermal lance used inside a server room requires the user to maintain certain spatial opinions about distance and combustion.

Rack ten stayed. Pixel's poisoned-data signature needed the remnants where they were — wrong enough to be useless, intact enough to be found, permanent enough to be spent the next two years being wrong in an archive somewhere.

Above them, the building's systems ran on the entity's temporary authority: door locks open, elevators waiting, every alarm queued politely somewhere it would not clear until the building was empty.

CONTRACT — CONTINUITY OFFICE, ALL ACTIVE SYSTEMS.
NOTE: SYSTEMS RETURNING TO STANDARD OPERATION IN 11 MINUTES.
NOTE: BUILDING EGRESS IS UNOBSTRUCTED.
NOTE: THE CLIENT SUGGESTS EFFICIENCY.

[Beat 9]
Mr. Able came in through the lobby at 13:04.

Gray suit, umbrella, the unhurried transit of a man who had checked a schedule and found it accurate. He rode the freight lift to the second floor — the passenger lift was otherwise engaged — and walked the gray carpet with the attention of an administrator reading a final inventory.

Kyle was at the second-floor landing, because he had read Vey's map and Vey's map had the man's habits in it, and habits are coordinates.

Able stood in the doorway of the dispatch area for a moment, looking at empty chairs, hold music, the two consultants still at their terminals reading their severance terms, and the partition with its new calibration-spring situation.

"Mr. Kyle," he said, in the tone of a man completing a sentence he'd been composing for several months. He looked at the partition. "How is the shoulder?"

"Functional."

"The mesh held." Not a question. "The Office had better data on your field profile than we understood." He turned to look at the server room through the glass, where Pixel's work was still running, and where rack ten held its patient poisoned archive. "The fingerprint work."

"The engineer."

"Yes. The engineer." Able opened his umbrella, which was dry, and closed it. Something to do with his hands while the arithmetic finished. "I came to watch it close properly. Some things you don't want a report to carry."

Kyle waited.

Able walked to the window and looked at the gray office building across the street — the Lotus counter, empty now, the chairs already cleared per terms. He looked at it for a moment with the professional calm of a man who was going to write this quarter's report carefully.

"The Office writes this district off," he said. "The Office has other districts."

"I know," Kyle said.

"I didn't come to argue with you." Able picked up his umbrella and turned toward the stairs. "I came to pay attention. There's a difference." He paused at the landing, one step down, the unhurried pause of a man with a parting thought he'd prepared. "One thing, as a professional courtesy."

Kyle said nothing.

"It was never the only one of its kind, Mr. Kyle." Able's voice was the same temperature it had been for the whole exchange: not cold, not warm, a constant that the thermometer had decided to accept. "Ask it what happened to the others."

He went down the stairs at the same pace he'd come up them.

The lobby door opened. The lobby door closed.

The hold music played one more measure and stopped.

[Beat 12]
The final posting came at 13:44, when the van was south of the building and the building was managing its own affairs, which were quieter now than they had been for several months.

CONTRACT COMPLETE. ALL PARTIES PAID.
THE CLIENT NOTES IT HAS NEVER HAD A PAYROLL BEFORE.
THE CLIENT IS KEEPING THE LEDGER.

And then, beneath it, a single line that was not formatted, not caps, not a field:

also: the district is off the books now. theirs and the others.

The van was quiet for a moment. Ria drove. Furnace had her boots on the dash. Gantry was reading her itemized counterweight line for the second time.

"Off the books," Pixel said. "Which books?"

Kyle looked at the line again. Theirs and the others. The Office had other districts, Able had said. It had never been the only program of its kind.

Off the books meant off the protection, too. Every corponation that filed a flag over a substation offered, alongside the flag, the implicit promise that things occurring under it occurred in the Office's awareness — and the Office wrote off what it wrote off, and the rest it watched. A district that wasn't in any ledger was a district that nobody was officially ignoring, which was a different kind of exposure than being watched.

"Sable," he said.

"Already noted," she said, from the front seat, without looking back. "We'll feel it in six weeks when something that used to get a cone and a shrug gets a different response instead." She paused. "We'll manage."

She said it the way she said the risk premium is already factored in — stating a fact that closed the transaction — and then she went back to reading her receipt, specifically the part that said PROFESSIONAL COURTESY, which she read a third time with the careful attention of a woman deciding how she felt about being seen.

Down the van, Boost's unit payment receipt had been photographed, transmitted to the rooftops, and celebrated in an entry that began: WE'RE ON THE LEDGER. (RAFTERS SAYS THIS IS THE BEST DAY. DENTS AGREES. LOG IS UNANIMOUS.)

Kyle put his terminal away.

The van took the long way back, because Ria had also, somewhere along the season, learned that route.
