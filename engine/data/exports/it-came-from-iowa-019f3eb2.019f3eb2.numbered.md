# It Came From Iowa

[Beat 1]
The smell reached him first. Turned earth and warm metal, the smell of a field right after a deep-till pass. Wes lay still in his bed and knew, without moving, that the machine had been working in the night. He'd grown up smelling that. He knew what it meant.

Then the sound.

Low. Sustained. Not a mechanical failure sound — not a bearing going wrong or a stuck actuator, not any of the dozen sounds he'd catalogued over thirteen years of watching. What a stuck actuator sounded like was a repetition, a stuttering thing. What a bearing going wrong sounded like was a rising pitch, a shimmy in the ground. This was neither. It held a single note, low enough to feel in the chest before the ears could name it, something between a chord and a groan. No mechanical purpose he knew. No maintenance record he'd ever read described anything like it.

He dressed in the dark without turning on the light. He'd done that enough times it was automatic. He went out through the kitchen, the screen door, and across the yard in his boots, laces only half-done because there wasn't time to be fussy about it. Two Eigenlift towers stood to the south in the pre-dawn grey, thirty stories each, rising from the corn with no suburb around them the way they'd been doing since before he was born. Corn, tower, corn. He'd grown up with them the same way he'd grown up with the machine. They were just part of what Iowa looked like now.

The sound continued.

The north field ran about two hundred yards to the treeline, and beyond the treeline was the clearing where the machine spent most of its off-hours. He'd been out there hundreds of times, had climbed the lower struts on a dozen of those, had sat with his back against the secondary stabilizer in the middle of summer and eaten the lunch he'd brought. The machine had tolerated it the way it tolerated everything that wasn't a field to work — with complete indifference, going about its calibration cycles, running the drill-tine into the earth and withdrawing it, making its own sounds.

He knew all those sounds. He'd learned them like a language, without meaning to.

The sound tonight was not in that language.

He reached the treeline and pushed through the underbrush and stepped into the clearing. His feet stopped.

The dead machine was at the far edge. Collapsed onto two tines, the third one snapped at the joint so the whole carapace tilted forward like a ship taking on water. The hull was darker than the living machine's — more oxidized, the brass gone to something closer to char. It had been there at least days. Maybe longer. He hadn't come to the clearing in a week, and he hadn't known to look.

The living machine was beside it.

Not working. Not calibrating. The scythe-arm was extended, the ring still, and the arm itself was resting against the dead hull's collar joint. Nudging. Not hard — just a pressure, a contact. Then withdrawing an inch. Then pressing again. Slow and steady, the same motion over and over, like checking a door that won't open.

The wail continued. Low and sustained, the chest-note that had pulled him out of bed. It didn't rise or fall. It held.

Wes stood at the edge of the clearing with his hands at his sides. He didn't move forward. He didn't call out. He'd never called out to the machine in his life — it didn't work that way, it wasn't that kind of machine — but it wasn't that which stopped him now. What stopped him was the sense that he was somewhere private.

He stood there and watched the arm nudge the dark hull. Withdraw. Nudge again. The sensor apertures on the living machine's forward face were angled down toward the dead one. The morning was very still. Two shapes in a clearing, one warm and one cold, the living one talking to the dead one in the only voice it had.

The sky was going from black to grey at the edges. The wail held without changing.

He stood at the edge of the clearing and watched one Behemoth mourn another.

[Beat 2]
Pip showed up sometime around when the sky went from black to dark grey. Wes didn't hear him coming — Pip could be quiet on grass when he chose to be — but there he was, low at Wes's left boot, sitting square on his haunches, looking at the clearing.

He hadn't been called. He'd just followed, the way he followed most things Wes did without announcement.

He sniffed twice toward the machines. Then he looked up at Wes. Then back at the clearing.

The living machine was still nudging the dead hull. The wail hadn't stopped. Pip's ears went forward, both of them, and his head tilted maybe five degrees, and Wes could see him reading it — the way a dog read a new smell, cataloguing, deciding.

Then Pip walked forward.

Not in the way he had for squirrels and the neighbor's cat, that dart-and-lunge with no thought behind it. Not hesitant either, the way dogs got when they weren't sure of their footing. He walked. Head low, ears still forward, pushing through grass that stood taller than his shoulders, the dew soaking dark up his legs, steady, not fast, like he was going somewhere he'd already decided on.

The living machine didn't turn toward him. Its sensor apertures stayed angled down at the dead hull.

Pip walked the whole width of the clearing and stopped beside the dead machine's broken tine. He sniffed the hull at the base, a thorough sniff that took a moment. Then he sat down. Just sat there, square and still, facing the same direction the living machine was facing.

The living machine stopped nudging.

The wail changed. Wes's throat tightened before he understood what had changed — the hairs on his forearms standing up, his stomach dropping half an inch — and only after that did he work out what was different: the note had gone quieter. Not fading. Not dying out. It was the way a sound changed when the shape of the room around it changed. Like something had shifted in what the machine was doing with the air.

He stood at the treeline and knew what Pip had just said. He'd known before Pip crossed the clearing, actually. He'd known since the arm first nudged the dead hull. He didn't try to put it in different terms. He didn't think the word that would've explained it, because that word would've been either too small or too big and he wasn't sure which. He knew what he knew, and he left it where it was.

Dawn came. The sky went from dark grey to pale yellow along the horizon, and the dew on the tall grass went silver for a minute before it was just wet grass again. The dead machine's hull, in the new light, looked darker and more settled. Finished.

The living machine's manipulator arm retracted. Then the drill-tine. Then the sowing tube, still sealed, swinging back to its rest bracket. One by one, the way they retracted at the end of a work cycle, the full shutdown sequence in reverse. Then the tines shifted — a long stride-test, each leg finding its weight, the body swinging south. Not a calibration movement. A bearing.

Five miles per hour is slower than a run and faster than a walk. The machine had its direction before the first full stride was complete. Each footfall came down with a weight that registered through the ground, through the grass, through the soles of Wes's boots.

The wail cut off.

Pip stood up from beside the dead machine. He shook himself once — he always did that after sitting too long — and then he trotted after the living machine, south, into the pale morning.

He didn't look back.

Wes watched the machine pass him at the treeline, close enough to smell it — turned earth, warm metal, and something else underneath that he'd noticed before but never had a name for — and the hull passed and the tines passed and the secondary stabilizers passed and then he was looking at the machine moving away from him through the grey.

South. Straight line. Five miles per hour.

He'd stood still long enough. He ran.

[Beat 3]
He knew the machine's route.

South from the north field, through the lower acreage, past the Riordan property line and down the drainage margin to the county road. The machine had walked that corridor twice a week every growing season for as long as Wes had been paying attention, taking the same correction at the Riordan fence corner — a degree east to clear a survey marker that might not even exist anymore. He'd tracked the correction for six years without knowing he was tracking it.

He ran cross-field. Not on the road — the road added half a mile. He went through the lower field's dry margin, through the drainage ditch, through the Riordan fence at the spot where two posts were gone.

The Riordan elevator stood at the county road. A hundred feet of grain storage, concrete stave and corrugated steel, the access ladder on the south face still bolted to the wall. The Riordans had sold to AgriCore in 2219, and the elevator hadn't moved grain in seven years, and the ladder hadn't been maintained since. He'd climbed it twice as a kid. Both times on a dare, which meant both times he'd stopped short of the top.

He went all the way.

The top platform was steel grating, a rusted rail he tested and didn't trust. He put his back against the concrete housing and looked south.

The machine came down the corridor between the corn.

It moved slower than its scale said it should — a quality of very large things. The stride was long enough that each step had its own duration. The brass hull was going gold in the morning light. He watched the Crown approach. At forty yards he could see the dome detail. At twenty he could see the sensor array at the forward edge. At ten he could see that the apex was coming level — maybe six feet below where he was standing, maybe eight. Close enough that six or eight didn't feel like different numbers.

He waited for the leading edge.

He jumped.

He got both hands on the Crown lip and the impact folded his arms and he swung his legs up before the machine's forward motion could drag him off. One knee on the dome. Both. He pressed flat and gripped the Crown edge and breathed.

The hull was warm.

Not just the ambient warmth of metal on a summer morning — deep-warm, the kind of warmth that came from inside, something working in there for hours. Whatever had produced the wail was still producing heat. He could feel it through his palms, through the ceramic over metal. The machine's interior running at a temperature that had no agricultural reason for it.

The Crown was a flattened oval — five feet across, the sensor array clustered near the forward edge, the rear clear. The vibration up here was different from what he'd expected. Steadier. The machine's whole rhythm transmitting through the hull as a constant low tremor, something between a sound and a feeling, more felt than heard.

He sat up and looked.

Iowa spread out in the early light. The corn rows caught the low sun and went gold for maybe thirty seconds before the angle changed and they went back to just green. The river was a silver line to the east following the tree belt. His farm was behind him, the house and barn small now. The north field running up to the treeline where the dead machine was.

A hundred feet. The world was very flat and very big.

He looked down along the forward face.

Pip was at the secondary stabilizer. Trotting in the shadow of the tine, small and low against the scale of the leg above him, keeping pace without apparent effort. He glanced up when Wes looked down — eighty feet of morning air between them — ears forward, tail going. Wes lifted a hand, barely. Pip held it a second, then put his nose back to the ground in front of him, because there was ground that needed watching.

The machine had a bearing. South. It didn't test the edges of its direction or slow for the field boundaries or the drainage ditches or anything else. It walked south at five miles per hour and the world was flat enough that south was a long straight line.

That was all there was to know.

[Beat 4]
The Dixon outer buffer post was two rooms, a charging rack for the patrol drone fleet, and a view of the Rock River in the morning that was genuinely good if you were somebody who needed that from a posting.

CJ had stopped needing it around week four.

Three months. Halfway to Iowa or the edge of the world; she'd decided early on it didn't much matter which. She'd trained at Kansas City Division — eight months, full tactical assessment track, mobile intercept certified, top quartile on the aerial deploy exam — and the placement officer had said "Dixon" the way people said things they'd already decided couldn't be argued. First posting for juniors who needed to demonstrate they could handle extended low-activity without their decision-making degrading. He hadn't used the word boredom. He hadn't needed to.

KC was different. KC in 2226 was a ground-level city, people living at street height without apology, Eigenlift infrastructure modest compared to GLMZ core, the towers more reasonable in scale and spaced apart enough that you could look between them and still see sky. The agricultural logistics running through KC — Behemoth maintenance routing, buffer zone crop-data, zone administration — gave the place a working quality that she'd liked. She'd grown up thinking of cities as places you walked through.

Dixon, Illinois wasn't quite either thing. It was the edge.

Her alert board lit at 0614.

She read it standing. Coffee still in her left hand.

**AUTONOMOUS MASS — UNREGISTERED**
Origin bearing: Davenport IA / 280° approach
Speed: 4.9 mph sustained
Mass: 38–42 tons (estimated)
Transponder response: NONE
Kill-switch protocol: NONE
Formation class: Tripodal, agricultural footprint
ETA Zone-12 boundary: approx. 6.5 hours

She read it again.

No manufacturer tag. No registry pull — the system had queried twice and returned empty both times. Either the transponder had failed or it had never been installed. And Behemoths came from somewhere, all of them, always. The kill-switch non-response was the part that sat differently in her chest. Kill-switches were hardwired. Not registered, not remotely updatable. A non-response meant either catastrophic internal failure or something the field manual didn't have language for.

She put the coffee down.

She pulled up the aerial relay from the 88 corridor network. Low-angle feed, the morning light just arriving, but the carapace was visible: domed hull, brass-colored, ninety feet at the apex per the scale readout. Tripodal base. Moving at a bearing that hadn't deviated in the six minutes of telemetry available. Two Eigenlift towers framed it — one on each side of the corridor, thirty stories, rising from the corn with nothing around them. The machine walked between them like they were just two more tall things in a flat land.

Her channel chimed. Command priority.

"Anderson." Hauser's voice arrived flat and even. "You have the mass alert."

"Received at 0614, sir. In review."

"First interdiction wave is authorized. Three units, bearing vector intercept, standard containment formation."

"Sir, I'd like time to assess the behavioral profile before—"

"Pre-authorization on demolition assets. I need your concurrence on the tier-three escalation before I move it up."

CJ looked at the aerial feed. The machine walked between the towers and kept walking and the towers didn't do anything about it because they were towers.

"More time, sir. I'm not ready to concur on demolition."

"One hour." A pause — not uncertainty, just Hauser building the record. "Logging this at 0614. One hour from my mark before the wave deploys from my authorization alone. Mark."

"Logged."

The channel closed. Hauser wasn't harsh about it. He had a doctrine and a timeline and the doctrine said an unregistered mass of this scale on a direct bearing toward a Zone boundary required a graduated response, and the doctrine wasn't wrong in the abstract. He just wasn't where she was. He hadn't seen the feed.

CJ set the stream to continuous record and looked out the east window at the Rock River, the Illinois fields flat to the horizon. Somewhere out there past two counties of corn, something was coming south at five miles per hour with no name on it. She'd driven this road when she came in three months ago, her skimmer loaded, and she'd almost stopped twice. Once for a heron on the river. Once for an Eigenlift tower standing alone at the field's edge — thirty stories, no suburb, no transition, just corn and then the tower and then corn again. She'd idled the skimmer for a moment and looked at it.

She had one hour.

She didn't touch the demolition log.

[Beat 5]
The drones arrived from the south while the machine was crossing through Hillsdale.

Wes heard them first — a triple-toned hum cutting above the ambient noise of the walking, three distinct frequencies blended together, the design that made Eigenlift interdiction units identifiable at distance. Then he saw them: a staggered V, three units, gray and angular, hovering at hull height. They came in from the south, matched the machine's pace, and held a position forty feet out from the forward sensor cluster.

Forty feet was not very far.

Wes sat very still on the Crown. The machine didn't slow. The two-second footfall interval kept going without variation. The formation adjusted their hover to match, drifting south at 4.9 mph in their holding pattern, scanning.

The scythe-arm extended.

It came off the bracket smooth, the ring already spinning by the time the arm was half-deployed — he could tell by the pitch change, that high-frequency whine he'd grown up with, the sound of the blade coming up to harvest speed. The arm tracked left, toward the hull's proximity zone where the formation sat, and then it swept.

He felt the sweep before he saw it land — the arm's weight shifting out and back through the hull, a slow lean in the tremor under him and then the return. One drone. The arm made contact at the unit's port side and the drone went sideways and down, forty feet into the Hillsdale corn below. No explosion. The sound was polymer and aluminum meeting a ceramic edge, and then the absence of the drone where it had been.

The machine kept walking. The arm reset.

The remaining two units tried to reposition — a tight adjustment, the kind programmed for single-unit loss in a formation — but the correction brought one of them inside the sweep radius and the scythe-arm came again. Same motion. No urgency in it, no targeting calculation that Wes could see. The arm moved the way it moved through corn at harvest: steady, the minimum arc required for the obstacle to not be there anymore. The second unit went down into the rows.

The third drone held its position for approximately four seconds.

The formation protocol had an answer for one-unit loss and presumably an answer for two-unit loss and apparently that answer was hold, which turned out to mean stay inside the sweep radius.

The arm extended a third time.

The third unit went into the corn.

The machine kept walking. The scythe-ring spun down and went quiet and the arm retracted to its rest bracket in the same smooth sequence it used after a harvest pass. The pace had not changed. Not during the first drone, not during the second, not during the third. Each footfall placed at two-second intervals, south, the hull steady on the flat ground.

Wes looked down at the field. One unit was still cycling, its nav light blinking patient green in the corn — still transmitting its location, still running its systems, just not flying anymore. The other two were dark shapes in the rows. A few years of someone's budget, lying in the Hillsdale dirt at various angles.

He looked at it for a moment.

Pip was below, in the shadow of the secondary stabilizer. Trotting. He hadn't changed his pace when the drones came in. He hadn't looked up when they went down. He was just running, low and steady, at five miles per hour, because that was where Wes was.

The 88 corridor was open ahead, flat land and corn and Eigenlift towers at the horizon, rising from the fields without preamble the way they always did out here. South, and more south.

The footage, Wes thought. If anyone was watching the footage — the formation coming in clean, by the book, multi-axis coverage, programmed for this scenario — they couldn't stop seeing it. The arm fires. The unit drops. The machine keeps walking. Biplanes off the Empire State Building. By the third one, you already knew. The formation strategy had answered its own question.

He sat on the Crown of a machine that had just taken apart three interdiction units in eight seconds without altering its bearing by a degree.

Whatever was going to stop this wasn't going to come from outside. No amount of formation discipline was going to change what happened when you put something inside the sweep radius. If anything worked — and he wasn't sure anything would, and he wasn't sure anything needed to — it was going to have to come from in here.

He looked south along the bearing, into the Illinois morning, and held on.

[Beat 6]
The drainage break at Erie ran under a concrete span, maybe forty feet of shadow, and the scav camp had set up in the shadow the way scav camps did — efficient, temporary, already half struck by the time you got a good look at it. Salvage frames. A flatbed with the bed stacked with stripped copper conduit and machine-salvage brackets. Three men working, maybe more behind the frames he couldn't see from up here.

Wes saw it from the Crown about two minutes out. He knew right away.

He leaned forward over the forward edge — careful, always careful with the sensor array, never touch the array — and shouted.

The wind took it. The machine's footfall ate it. He shouted again, louder, the kind of loud that tore the throat, and one of the men below looked up. Just for a second. Then he went back to work.

A hundred feet. That was the problem. A hundred feet of morning air and four tons of footstep every two seconds, and whatever his voice did up here, it didn't reach the men down there.

He watched the man at the forward tine. A winch cable in his hands, the kind with a load-bearing clip on the end, a yellow safety vest over his shirt, and he was working the clip toward the tine's mounting joint the way you work a job you've done before. Fast. Efficient. The cable ran back to the flatbed, and the flatbed was set to take the load and drag.

He'd tried to leash it.

Wes couldn't get down. Not in time — not while the machine was walking, not from a hundred feet. He couldn't stop the man from up here. He'd already shouted twice.

He looked at the sensor array. Six inches from his right hand, the apertures clustered at the Crown's forward edge. Every maintenance record he'd ever read said the same thing about direct contact: internal response, immediate, hard-coded, nothing in the schedule could turn it off. He had never wanted to test it.

His hand moved.

The hull responded before his fingers landed — a vibration from somewhere inside, fast and wrong, nothing to do with footfall — and his hand pulled back by itself, palms flat on the Crown plating, body pressing into the dome. Not a decision. The machine had answered before the question was finished.

The machine stepped.

The tine came down.

The man was in the path of the footfall.

Wes looked away before the impact fully landed and looked back and the tine was already rising into the natural stride and the cable had gone slack and the yellow vest was on the ground near the drainage concrete and the machine was taking the next step and the next, same pace, same bearing, south.

That was the whole of it. The machine had been stepping. It did not deviate.

Wes gripped the Crown edge. He did not look back at the drainage concrete.

The threat-wail came from somewhere inside the hull.

His stomach dropped before the sound reached his ears. Not the way heights did, not the way a close call did — his body decided something before his mind had it. Hair standing up along his forearms. The inside of his throat going wrong. Then the sound arrived, two layers of it. On top, an electronic shriek, high and piercing, something built into the machinery that hadn't been used in a long time. And underneath — below any frequency he could put a number to — something else. Not a Behemoth sound. Not any manufactured sound he'd ever heard, ever read about, ever catalogued in thirteen years of listening.

Old. The wrong kind of old for a hull that came off a line.

It carried.

The scavs below scattered before the second second was up — not a decision, not even a flinch, just evacuation, the kind that happens before the thought arrives. One man left the flatbed running. One went over the drainage wall. They all went, all of them, in different directions, because that was what a body did when it heard that sound, before the man on top of the body could weigh in.

The machine kept walking.

The threat-wail held, layered and wrong. The stride interval was unchanged. The bearing was unchanged. The yellow vest was behind them now, and then the drainage concrete was behind them, and then the whole camp was behind them, and the corn came back on both sides, and the Eigenlift towers rose ahead at the horizon.

Wes held the Crown edge. Both hands.

He was a hundred feet up on a machine that was not stopping. Whatever he'd thought he could do from up here — whatever had been in his head since the clearing, since the dark — wasn't enough. He was a rider on a thing that was going somewhere, and the threat-wail was still going, and the machine was still walking south.

He held on.

[Beat 7]
The call came in at 0947. ArcSec mobile, pattern five from the 88 corridor.

"Anderson." The officer's voice had the professional flatness of a man who needed the log to reflect the precise time and manner of the report. "Mass event near Erie. Civilian down. Non-responsive. Scav camp at the drainage break, approximate coordinates—"

She wrote them. She didn't ask twice.

"Machine still mobile?"

"Bearing unchanged. Speed unchanged. We attempted an interdiction-class proximity approach and the—" A pause. The flatness cracked a little. "There's wreckage in the camp. The cable's still attached to the tine. The machine walked it out."

She thanked him and closed the channel.

Hauser came in forty seconds later.

"Anderson. I have the Erie report. Logging escalation request four. I want your concurrence on demolition authorization."

"Request more time, sir."

"Define more time."

She looked at her display. The 88 corridor spread flat from Dixon west, satellite overlay, and she could trace every telemetry point: the drone wreckage near Hillsdale, two clusters, a third further east she hadn't looked at yet. The scav camp coordinates. And the moving dot that was still moving — bearing true south, 4.9 mph, unchanged. Eigenlift towers stood along the corridor at irregular intervals, each one thirty stories out of the corn with nothing around it. Corn, tower, corn. It looked wrong and it looked exactly right, both at once — GLMZ had swallowed the whole Midwest and it hadn't replaced any of it, just planted its infrastructure in the fields and let the fields keep being fields.

"Three hours before it reaches the zone boundary," she said. "I'd like to use the time to assess the behavioral profile. The machine hasn't deviated from its bearing in nine hours of telemetry. It hasn't altered pace. It isn't reactive — it answers proximity, but it doesn't change course for it."

"Request four is in the log. I'm moving it up the chain on my authorization."

"Sir—"

"Civilian down, Anderson. I'm moving it up."

Her thumb found the channel key. The concurrence was one keystroke — Hauser had the doctrine and the civilian down and the log already written, and she was standing in Dixon with her coffee gone cold and a story she hadn't filed yet. The doctrine was not wrong in the abstract. Her thumb was on the key.

The broadcast spike registered on the lower display.

Same pattern as after Hillsdale — same amplitude jump, same narrow timing window. The machine had done something, then broadcast, and the agitation markers had ticked down. The spike was live, a bright column in the nine-hour graph, still resolving.

She looked at the column. Her thumb came off the key.

"I'm not concurring on demolition," she said. "Logging my non-concurrence at—" she checked the timestamp "—0949."

Hauser was quiet for two seconds. That was about as much pause as he allowed himself. "Logged. This doesn't stop the escalation."

"Understood."

He closed the channel. He always closed when the conversation was finished, not when the frustration peaked — she'd clocked that in three months, and she didn't dislike it. He had a doctrine and a timeline, and the doctrine wasn't wrong in the abstract. He just wasn't where she was.

She pulled up the acoustic telemetry.

Six hours of data. The broadcast was steady — not a distress call, not a navigation ping, not anything in the ArcSec catalog of known Behemoth maintenance signals. It repeated on an interval of about eleven seconds. Same pattern. Every repetition identical to the last, which meant it wasn't degrading, wasn't shifting with mechanical state. The same signal at mile one as at mile forty.

She'd flagged it for the Behemoth registry cross-reference two hours ago. No match.

She set two streams side by side: the broadcast, and the behavioral signatures — stride variance, speed variance, heading variance, anything that moved off baseline.

When the drones arrived the first time, one of the behavioral signals had spiked. Not direction. Not speed. Something in the hull resonance, something the Hillsdale relay had caught and she'd almost scrolled past. The spike had lasted about eight seconds.

After the drones went down, the machine had broadcast.

She highlighted the interval. Looked at the pattern. Looked at the timing.

Three hours.

She still didn't have the match.

[Beat 8]
The second wave came in near Prophetstown.

Five units this time, not three. Better spread — two at hull height, two angled down from above, the fifth hanging back a hundred yards. Wes figured the fifth for a relay or a reserve. Smart, if it was. He didn't know enough about ArcSec drone doctrine to say.

The scythe-arm extended.

Three times. Methodical. The first unit at hull height went the way the Hillsdale drones had gone — the arm tracked to the sweep radius, the ring caught the frame, it dropped into the corn without ceremony. The second hull-height unit went next, the arm redirecting without fully retracting between passes, cleaner than the first time. The third was one of the angled-down units, higher, so the arm swung wider, and it did. No hurry in any of it. The same arc it used on corn.

Two units pulled back out of the sweep radius. The reserve at a hundred yards held.

The machine let them go.

Then it broadcast.

Not the regular signal — not the eleven-second pulse. Something louder. The hull vibration changed under Wes's palms and under the soles of his boots, a deeper resonance, and the signal went out at a new amplitude, a held note laid over the steady pulse for five seconds, maybe six.

Then the regular interval came back.

In Dixon, CJ registered the spike on the telemetry display. The broadcast amplitude, already flagged anomalous, had jumped. She marked the timestamp. Something had just happened.

The two survivors held at a hundred yards for a while, hovering, then began moving south — parallel to the machine, wide, outside the sweep radius. Tracking, not interdicting. Wes didn't know whether that was a decision someone had made on the other end or just what the formation did when it ran out of moves, but either way it was fine.

The threat-wail hadn't come back after Erie. The machine had settled into its rhythm again: five miles per hour, footfall every two seconds, the hull a drum. The broadcast ran from inside, patient, the eleven-second interval under the stride. Step. Step. Step. The morning was gone and the sun was past overhead and starting to pull its shadow long.

He'd been awake since before four.

The hull under him was warm — not uncomfortable, just warm the way a seat goes warm from sitting in it, except the warmth came from inside. Bioreactor heat, deep in the hull, constant. He'd read about that once. He understood it now, in his legs and his back, a body-warmth he was leaning into without meaning to.

The broadcast repeated.

He was already asleep.

He woke when the light had changed — lower, the kind of afternoon shadow that told you hours had gone by and the sun had committed to somewhere west of overhead. His neck was stiff. His right cheek was hot and tight where it had been turned to the sun. He gripped the Crown edge and held until the world steadied, then leaned over.

Pip was below, in the shadow of the secondary stabilizer. Trotting. Raw pads on the road gravel and still doing five miles per hour, that low, focused run, not looking up until Wes leaned over the edge — then a flick of the ear, and back to the ground.

Forty-some miles. The road and the corn and the machine's shadow shifting with the sun.

He'd fallen asleep on the back of the thing he'd watched kill a man. He hadn't decided to. He'd just gone under, the way you go under when your body has decided it's safe and doesn't consult you about it.

He held the edge and watched Pip run.

[Beat 9]
CJ went west on the 88 corridor at 0645, the skimmer two feet off the asphalt, the hum in good register, the morning air cold enough to want the layer she'd forgotten to grab off the rack.

The first drone wreckage showed from the road: a unit down in the corn about thirty yards north of the shoulder, nav light still cycling green, body canted at an angle that meant it had come down hard and fast. She marked it on the display without stopping. The second cluster was a half-mile on — two units, dark, no lights, lying in the rows at angles. She noted the positions. The camera had caught what it needed to catch.

The scav camp at Erie she reached near Hillsdale. She slowed the skimmer. Yellow vest on the ground by the drainage break. The signal had been in his chest for so long he'd stopped hearing it as separate from the hull.
