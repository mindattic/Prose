# Work Order (The D-Class) (CODA)

_Pixel runs the crew to witness WO-D-0317 complete its 30-year structural inspection shift. Kyle walks through an arc-fence zone — neuretics brown, Atlas hardware felt as foreign object. He sees Pixel working without augmented overlay. The D-class receives the termination signal and stops. "Standard rate. I touched it at standard rate."_

[Beat 1]
Pixel had the site credentials and the crew and the reason — three-part authorization for working inside a Class-2 maintenance envelope in the Clybourn understructure, which was the kind of paperwork that took two weeks to assemble and was worth assembling because what was down there was worth seeing.

The crew was four: herself, Moss from the Street Meat job, a structural tech named Ezra Fonu who owed her nothing but showed up anyway, and Kyle.

The D-class had been running for thirty years on a work order that nobody had renewed because nobody had needed to. The original contractor was a Mitsuba subsidiary that had been dissolved and absorbed and dissolved again since 2195. The work order was still active. The machine was still active. WO-D-0317, assigned to a single inspection and micro-repair cycle on the lateral feed understructure, had been doing the same three-hour circuit for thirty years without a communication channel, without a feed address, without a recipient for its logged data other than a server address that had changed hands four times and was currently owned by a regional infrastructure authority that had no idea the log existed.

It would finish its assigned shift at 14:32:07 today.

Pixel had found this by reading maintenance records that nobody had read since 2207. She had sent Kyle the time and the access route and nothing else, which was how she communicated when she thought the thing was worth the trouble and also thought explaining it would reduce it.

[Beat 2]
The understructure was three levels below the Clybourn lateral — the kind of space that accumulated the quiet of things that had been working correctly for a long time with no audience. The infrastructure was old-spec: pre-composite ferrocement, cable runs in physical conduit, the kind of engineering that had been overengineered once and then left alone because it held. Water ran through it in ways that were accounted for. The arc-fence ran along the north wall — a Class-1 containment barrier for the chemical treatment zone adjacent, eight meters of low-frequency electromagnetic field that fried unshielded electronics and was posted with signage that nobody ever read because nobody came down here without an overlay.

WO-D-0317 was near the south end of the corridor when they arrived, running its inspection circuit. It was the size of a large dog, low to the ground on six articulated legs, its sensor array doing what it had done every three hours for thirty years — measuring, logging, applying micro-sealant to ferrocement cracks within spec, cable-tension check at twelve anchor points. It did not react to their presence. D-class units had no behavioral protocols for human observers; humans were not part of its world model.

"Fourteen minutes," Pixel said.

The machine moved along the wall with the unhurried precision of something that had never been asked to hurry. Its log was thirty years of measurements that nobody had read. Its sealant patches were in the ferrocement above their heads, invisible, holding.

"It doesn't know," Fonu said.

"It's not built to know," Pixel said. "It's built to finish."

[Beat 3]
Kyle had stepped too close to the arc-fence line.

He didn't notice until he noticed — the neuretics going quiet the way a room goes quiet when you realize you've been talking and the other person stopped listening. One second the overlay was running: position data, biometric stack, the low background frequency of the entity's relay channel. The next second nothing, and the Atlas hardware behind his frontal lobe was just hardware — a weight he could feel, a thermal signature he could feel, a foreign object installed in a skull that had not been his idea.

He stepped back two meters. The neuretics came back online in 1.4 seconds, exactly as they should. The overlay returned. The relay channel returned. The weight in his skull went back to being something he had stopped noticing.

He had been carrying that for twenty-two years. Standard rate: installed without contract, running without invoice, never put down.

[Beat 4]
During the 1.4 seconds, he had seen Pixel without the overlay.

No biometric read. No neuretics-mediated processing of microexpression and posture and tell. Just his eyes, which were his own, which he'd had since before any of the rest of it. She was standing at the south end of the corridor watching the machine complete its final circuit, her back to him, her weight on her left foot the way she stood when she was concentrating. Moss was three meters to her right. Fonu was logging something on a physical notepad.

In the overlay she was an entity with a file and a history and a set of associations that the neuretics organized for him automatically because that was what the neuretics were for. Without the overlay she was someone standing in a maintenance corridor in the Clybourn understructure watching a thirty-year-old machine finish its job, and she had thought this was worth seeing, and she had assembled the paperwork, and she had not explained why, and she had been right not to.

At 14:32:07, WO-D-0317 reached the end of its assigned circuit.

Its legs locked in place and its sensor array folded to standby with a sound that was barely a sound — one actuator releasing tension it had held for thirty years. A single click. Then nothing.

Pixel was quiet after it stopped. Kyle didn't ask what she was thinking.

"Someone should log this," she said.

"Log what?" Moss said.

"That it finished."

[Beat 5]
They came out through the Clybourn access in the late afternoon, the four of them, back into the light and the synthesis-corridor air and the ordinary noise of the district. Fonu had logged it — entry in the maintenance record system, timestamped, with a note that the work order had been completed by the assigned unit and the unit was now in completion standby pending decommission instructions that would arrive whenever the infrastructure authority got around to noticing.

Pixel walked with Kyle for half a block before the crew split off.

"Standard rate?" she said.

"Standard rate," Kyle said. "I touched it at standard rate."

She nodded.
