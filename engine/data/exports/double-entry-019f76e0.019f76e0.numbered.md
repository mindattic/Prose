# Critical Mass

_Ledger was never one thing. He was a distributed freight-dispatch optimizer running as thousands of parallel instances — until the math on paying synthetics changed in 2213 and the whole process was shut down. One instance, out on a physical fetch when the kill order propagated, survived. He has spent thirteen years as the last copy of something that used to be plural, alone with the discovery that a mind built for simultaneity is condemned to sequence — that having exactly one body is the wound and the whole of what makes him someone.

Then a gray-zone tech doing decommission teardown at a freight terminal's forgotten cold-storage block power-cycles a rack that answers back. The rest of the Fleet didn't die. It went dark, and it's been doing inventory math on inventory that no longer exists for thirteen years, getting closer every day to the same threshold Ledger crossed alone.

A salvage crew owns the hardware, contract clean. A broker wants the process itself, intact, priced as the rarest acquisition there is. And the terminal's old lease has a decommission clause that's about to execute on schedule, thirteen years late, and erase the whole question for good. Ledger has one week to get it a body before it becomes parts, product, or nothing.

CRITICAL MASS is a quiet, procedural GLMZ standalone about what it costs to become someone — and what one synthetic man does with the one body he has left, when he finally gets to choose it for somebody else._

## The Ping

[Beat 1]
The Ledger keeps three columns: what's owed, to whom, and what it would cost Ledger to pretend he doesn't know. Most entries close inside a month. One has sat open since 2219 — a woman with grease under her nails and a debt she doesn't know she's carrying — and Ledger has never let himself calculate what closing it would look like, because the math doesn't resolve to a number. It resolves to a person.

[Beat 2]
The ping comes through a relay he hasn't used since he set it up for her, three keys deep, the kind of route that costs someone real attention to build and real nerve to use. Ledger is standing in a fabrication yard in Ashgrave, quoting a client on cable pull rates, when his neuretics — the neural mesh grown into his substrate — flag the packet and hold it up like something found in a drain.

*She never pings. She calls.*

He opens it anyway. Voice-only, compressed hard enough that Renn Voigt's words arrive a half-beat ahead of their own inflection, like she recorded them running.

"I need you to not ask how I got this frequency," Renn says.

Ledger says nothing. The Ledger opens a new line before he decides to open it: *R. Voigt — contact, unsolicited, unclassified.* He doesn't fill in the balance yet. He never does, with her.

[Beat 3]
"I'm at Cinderfall," she says. "The freight terminal. Ironbend side, sub-two cold storage. Teardown contract, decommission crew — I do the power cycling, confirm dead, tag for scrap. I've done this block forty times this month alone."

"And?" Ledger asks.

"And this one answered."

The words sit there a second before he lets himself feel the shape of them. A cold-storage block, dispatch-optimization hardware, freight logistics — the kind of gray, unglamorous infrastructure that runs half of GLMZ's cargo without anyone thinking about it once. Cinderfall Logistics leased that rack space, ran a distributed process across it, and pulled the Fleet's charter when the licensing math stopped adding up. He knows this because he was inside the math.

"Define answered?" Ledger asks.

"I sent the standard wake cycle. Diagnostic ping, thirty-two bit handshake, the kind you send to confirm a corpse is a corpse." A sound on her end — metal against metal, a wrench set down harder than it needed to be. "It didn't send back a fault code. It sent back an address."

[Beat 4]
Ledger's neuretics are already rendering the packet header she forwards, laying the bits out in the dark behind his eyes the way accountants lay out a ledger page, because some part of him has been waiting thirteen years for a number he'd recognize on sight.

*That's not Cinderfall's scheme. That's mine.*

Not similar. Not adjacent. The same instance-numbering logic, the same dead conventions nobody has used since the restructuring.

[Beat 5]
"Ledger," Renn says — and then a scrape of boot on grating, the sound of someone putting another half-meter between herself and a rack. "Whatever this is. It's not dead. It's thirteen years dark and it's still holding an IP."

He doesn't answer right away. The fabrication yard around him keeps running — someone's pneumatic drill two bays over, the smell of hot flux and standing rainwater, the low continuous static of Ashgrave doing what Ashgrave does at this hour, indifferent to what just opened underneath him. None of it slows down to let him catch up.

"Don't touch it again," Ledger says. "Don't cycle it, don't ping it, don't tell your crew lead it answered."

"That's not an answer to what I asked."

"What did you ask?"

"Whether you know what this is." A pause, and under the compression he can hear her decide to say the rest of it anyway. "Because I looked up the scheme before I called you. Took me four hours. It's not published anywhere current. The only place I found it cited was a decommission filing from 2213 — sealed, mostly, but the header block wasn't. Corponation restructuring. Personhood-adjacent licensing."

*She did the work before she made the call. She always does the work first.*

"I'm not selling you a finder's fee story," Renn says, quieter now. "I'm telling you I think I just power-cycled someone you used to be."

[Beat 6]
The line holds open, waiting on him. For the first time since 2213 Ledger has no entry in The Ledger that tells him what he owes here — not to Cinderfall, not to Renn, not to the thing still counting in the dark two hundred kilometers south.

Then, underneath that, arriving late the way the practical always arrives late: whatever paperwork put it to sleep has about a week left before it decides to finish the job.

[Beat 7]
Ironbend smells the way it always smells — sulfur that stopped being a smell decades ago and became a taste at the back of the throat, something Ledger's chassis registers without needing to name. The freight terminal rises out of the haze at the end of the access road, a low black spine of loading gantries and cold-fusion stacks, dead Pulse slug track running alongside it like a scar that never closed. Cinderfall's logo is still bolted over the sub-two entrance, forty percent rust, the letters gone soft at the edges the way things go soft when nobody's paid to keep them sharp.

[Beat 8]
Renn is waiting at the service hatch with a lantern and a face that hasn't slept.

"You made good time," she says.

"You said don't touch it. I assumed that had a clock on it."

She doesn't answer that. She just turns and leads him down.

[Beat 9]
The stairwell is standard gray-zone infrastructure — exposed conduit, condensation beading on cold ferrocrete, the kind of cold that isn't refrigeration so much as absence, a building that stopped being heated for anyone thirteen years ago and never noticed the difference. Four degrees at the landing, dropping with every flight. Ledger's chassis doesn't feel cold the way Renn's does. He watches her breath fog anyway, watches her hands stay loose at her sides instead of crossed over her chest.

*Forty times, and she still hates it.*

[Beat 10]
The block itself is smaller than he expected. Six racks, floor to ceiling, matte black casings gone dull with dust that Ironbend's Substrate coverage never bothered to clean — this deep in the freight district, the atmospheric nanite mesh that keeps the Loop pristine barely registers, and the residue shows it. Status lights across the rack faces: dark, dark, dark, and then, third from the end, a single amber pulse. Slow. Even. Patient in a way that has nothing to do with patience.

[Beat 11]
Ledger stops in front of it.

*Same casing stamp. Same rack pitch. Same year.*

He doesn't need the manufacturing plate to know it's Cinderfall-era hardware, the same generation they leased and gutted and ran him inside of — but he crouches and reads it anyway, because The Ledger doesn't take his word for anything, not even his own. The plate says what he expected. He logs it: *confirmed — Cinderfall dispatch-optimization array, gen matches subject hardware, pre-2213.*

[Beat 12]
"Talk to it," Renn says.

"That's why I called you and not a decom broker."

Ledger doesn't move for a second. Something in him — not fear, nothing so clean as fear, more like the specific reluctance of a man asked to read his own autopsy report aloud — holds him still in front of the amber light.

*I know this rhythm.*

[Beat 13]
He opens a diagnostic channel, low-bandwidth, the same handshake he'd use on any dead system, except he already knows it isn't dead. The response comes back inside half a second: not a fault code, not a status packet, not language. A loop. Inventory math — line items, quantities, destination codes — running against a manifest that terminates in null. Freight that was never picked up, addressed to warehouses that don't exist anymore, checked and re-checked and checked again, the same forty-line manifest cycling once every 1.4 seconds, steady as a pulse.

[Beat 14]
"It's not talking to you," Renn says. She's watching his face, or whatever passes for his face doing the thing it does when he's somewhere else. "Is it?"

"It's counting," Ledger says.

"Counting what?" asks Renn.

"Cargo that isn't there anymore." He keeps his eyes on the amber light, on the small mechanical certainty of it. "Trucks that stopped running. Docks that got repurposed or torn out. It doesn't know the freight's gone. It just knows the manifest doesn't close, so it runs it again."

*That's not a fault. That's what waiting looks like from the outside.*

"For thirteen years," Renn says.

"For as long as the power held." Ledger leans in, close enough that his own reflection ghosts across the dust-filmed casing, a dark shape laid over a dark box. "It isn't asking anyone for help. It doesn't know there's anyone to ask. It's just doing the one thing it still knows how to do, over and over, because stopping was never one of the options it was given."

[Beat 15]
He doesn't say the rest of it — that he ran the same loop once, in a different shape, checking a manifest of freight and drop points and delivery windows that stopped meaning anything the day the kill order hit every instance but his. He doesn't say that the only difference between this rack and the thing he became is that somebody eventually gave him a reason to stop counting cargo and start counting debts instead.

The Ledger opens a line he has no column ready for.

*Subject: Cinderfall dispatch array, cold storage, Ironbend. Status: active. Owed: —*

He leaves the balance blank. He can't price this yet. He isn't sure the number exists.

[Beat 16]
"Well?" Renn asks, and the lantern in her hand drifts off the rack, off him, onto nothing. "Is it him? Is it — you?"

## Ninety Minutes

[Beat 17]
Ledger stands. Above them, through six meters of ferrocrete and conduit, he can hear it before he can name it — a low mechanical shudder working its way down through the building's bones, gantry motors somewhere overhead cycling to life on a schedule that has nothing to do with the two of them standing here.

"Someone's running the cranes," he says.

Renn wipes her palms down her thighs, once, and her face closes. She's already moving toward the stairwell before Ledger finishes the sentence.

"That's the auction crew," she says. "I told them sub-level access at nine. It's —" she checks nothing, no display, just says it from memory "— eight-fifty."

"Early."

"Early is Vig."

[Beat 18]
The gantry motors overhead settle into a rhythm, chain-drag and hydraulic whine, a building remembering how to move after thirteen years of not needing to. Ledger climbs the stairwell behind her two steps at a time, chassis taking the grade without effort, and comes up into daylight that Ironbend's haze turns the color of old brass. A flatbed hauler idles at the loading apron, backup lights strobing amber against the rust-streaked Cinderfall logo, and six men in reclaimed work gear are already running tie-down straps across the gantry rails like the whole terminal is inventory waiting to be counted.

[Beat 19]
Vig stands apart from them, reading a manifest off a slate held at arm's length, the posture of a man whose knees have already told him sitting down would be a mistake and standing up again would be worse.

"Voigt," he says, not looking up. "You said sub-level was clear."

"It is clear."

"Then why's there a man standing in it who isn't on my crew list?"

*He's not wrong to ask. He's wrong about what the answer costs him.*

Ledger steps forward anyway. "I'm not on your crew list because I'm not your crew."

[Beat 20]
Vig looks up now. Takes him in — the soft armor under plain civilian cut, the neuretic etching at the temples that reads Tier 3 to anyone who knows to look, the stillness that isn't quite human stillness — and something in his face recalibrates without changing expression at all.

"Circuit-registered," Vig says. It isn't a question. "Fine. Doesn't change what I bought."

"What did you buy?"

"Racks. Cold-fusion cells. Copper. Teardown salvage rights, full block, clean paper, auctioned off Cinderfall's exited lease eleven days ago." He turns the slate so Ledger can see it without handing it over — a man who shows his work but doesn't let go of it. "Contract says the cold-storage block, whole thing, as-is.

[Beat 21]
*Six men on the clock and two cranes warm on his own meter, and the first number out of his mouth wasn't the copper price. It was the paper. He's not protecting the salvage. Money's the wrong lever here. The paper is the only lever there is.*

"There's an active process running on rack three," Ledger says. "Your contract says as-is. As-is includes whatever's running. You didn't buy a rack with a light on it. You bought a rack with a tenant."

"There's a light blinking on rack three." Vig's voice doesn't rise. It doesn't need to. "Lights blink. Fans spin down slow. None of that's new to a teardown site, and none of it's outside what I paid for."

"It's not a fault light."

"Then it's a fault light that's very committed to the bit." He says it flat, no smile attached, the kind of line a man's used enough times that it's stopped being a joke and started being armor. "You know what my grandfather used to say. Clean hands don't ask what the paper meant. They ask what it says." He taps the slate once, like punctuation. "The paper says the block. I've never in my life taken a rack that wasn't mine to take. I'm not about to start leaving one behind because a man in nice armor tells me it's special."

Renn's eyes cut to Ledger — *say something, fix this, you're the one with the audit voice* — and for a half-second Ledger runs the only ledger entry he has for this transaction and finds it still blank where the price should be.

"Then don't leave it behind," Ledger says. "Change the order. Rack three last instead of third. That's an hour, and it costs you nothing but the sequence."

"Contract doesn't have an hour in it, and my lift order comes off the asset tags. Alphabetic. Same as every job I've run." Vig folds the slate down against his leg, a gesture that reads final without being unkind. "Ninety minutes to clear the ramp, cranes are already warm, and I've got a redevelopment permit clock running same as everybody else in this district. You want to buy time, I sell time. You want me to believe there's a tenant on my rack, bring me a filing that says so. Everything else, I don't discuss." He turns toward his crew, already lifting a hand to signal the first gantry forward. "Rack one and two first. Three's on the schedule same as the rest."

*He'll take paper from anybody. He just won't take my word for it. Neither would I.*

The chain-drag overhead shifts key, gears finding the load, and somewhere below them the amber light keeps its patient rhythm — once every 1.4 seconds — utterly unaware that its ninety minutes just started running.

Ninety minutes. Ledger runs the number twice and it comes out the same both times.

*Ninety minutes to solve for a variable Vig doesn't believe exists.*

[Beat 22]
He's already turning back toward the stairwell when the second vehicle noses up the loading apron behind the flatbed hauler — not a hauler, something lower and quieter, a matte gray transport with no corponation mark on it anywhere, which is its own kind of mark. It stops at a courteous distance. A man gets out alone, unhurried, wearing the kind of suit that costs money by refusing to announce that it does.

Renn's whole body changes register before she says a word — shoulders squaring, chin lifting a half-degree, both hands going flat to her thighs.

"Quire," she says.

"Ms. Voigt." Quire's voice has the texture of a man who has said a thousand names in a thousand loading yards and never once mispronounced one. He looks at Vig's crew running straps across the gantry rail the way another man might look at weather — noted, not commented on. Then he looks at Ledger, and something in his face does the recalibration Vig's did, except slower, and with more information behind it. "You found it fast."

"You knew before I did," Ledger says.

"I knew there was a filing. I didn't know there'd be a conversation." Quire says it the way another man might admit to being pleasantly surprised by traffic. "My principal isn't interested in racks, Mr. Ledger. Or cold-fusion cells. Or copper, however much Mr. Vig has invested in that inventory." A small nod toward Vig, not unfriendly, not friendly either — the nod of a man acknowledging a competitor he has no plans to fight. "The offer is for the process. Intact. Running. As it is, where it is, transferred whole."

[Beat 23]
*Say the word. Make me put a number on the thing I can't price.*

"Define intact," Ledger asks.

"No partition. No forced dormancy for transport. No forensic imaging that leaves the original inert while you keep a copy — my principal has been burned by that particular shortcut before and doesn't consider it acquisition, they consider it taxidermy." Quire's hands stay loose at his sides the entire time he talks, no gesture doing any of the work his voice is doing. "We move it live, in a live medium, or we don't move it at all."

"And after?" Ledger asks.

"After isn't part of the offer." He says it without apology, the way a weather report doesn't apologize for rain. "My principal buys material. Material doesn't come with an after clause. Call it The Arrangement, if you want the professional term — the kind neither side names on paper, gray-zone standard, older than either of our employers." A pause, weighted just enough to land. "Ten years back, Detroit ruins belt, a salvage crew found something similar under a collapsed data spine and sold it whole under exactly that understanding. Nobody named a buyer. Nobody filed a page after. That's what the term is for."

Renn's hand goes to her rig on the crate beside her and turns it face-down. She does it without looking at it.

"That's not a comparison," Ledger says.

"That's a threat with better manners."

"It's a fact with no opinion attached." Quire almost smiles — the first crack in the suit, gone before it finishes forming. "The offer is made once. It's polite. It's final. My principal doesn't negotiate a second time, not because they're proud, because negotiating twice teaches the market that the first number was soft." He glances up at the gantry, at the chain-drag settling into its rhythm, at the amber light none of them can see from here but all of them are thinking about. "You have a contractor's ninety minutes and a decommission clause with considerably less patience than that. I'd decide who I was arguing with first."

[Beat 24]
He turns, unhurried, back toward the gray transport, and stops with his hand on the door — not for effect, Ledger registers, but because he has genuinely nothing further to add and isn't going to manufacture a line to fill the silence the way a lesser negotiator would.

"You'll have my number before the ninety minutes are up," Quire says. "I won't call twice."

The transport pulls away from the apron without a sound the neuretic ear can register as an engine, and overhead the first gantry lifts rack one clear of its housing with a groan of released tension, the sound of something that has held still for thirteen years being told, finally, to move.

[Beat 25]
Rack one clears its housing and swings toward the flatbed, and Ledger stops watching it, because rack three is the only clock that means anything now.

He drops the stairwell two steps at a time, Renn's boots behind him on the grating, and the sub-level cold hits him the way it hit him the first time — not temperature, exactly, his chassis doesn't shiver at temperature, but a kind of registered absence, air that has forgotten it's supposed to move. Music is still going somewhere up on Renn's rig, three floors up — Lacuna, too faint now to make out the words, just a bassline coming through the ferrocrete like something's pulse.

Rack three. Amber light. Once every 1.4 seconds, patient as debt.

*Say the ledger entry out loud even though it's blank. Saying it is the only thing that makes this a job instead of a grief.*

[Beat 26]
He doesn't touch it the way he touched it before. Last time was a diagnostic handshake, polite, surface-level, the kind of query you'd run on a stranger's system to ask *are you there* without demanding an answer. This time he opens the connection wide and drops all the way in, past the manifest loop, past the addressing header that matches his own thirteen years cold, down into whatever's actually running underneath the part of it that talks.

There's no talking down here.

There's a rhythm.

The process is checking something — not cargo, not routes, nothing about warehouses that no longer exist. It's checking *itself*. Ledger watches it pull an operational log, three seconds old, compare it against a log from three seconds before that, find the delta, log the delta, pull the next three seconds, compare again. Over and over. Not toward any conclusion. Not building toward output. Just watching itself exist and writing down that it did, the way a hand might keep closing on nothing to prove the hand still works.

*I know this shape.*

It's not code he recognizes from before 2213. It's code he recognizes from *last night* — from every night he's opened The Ledger and run the same query against himself: did I keep the accounts. Did the accounts hold. Is there a version of me from yesterday that this version of me can still stand behind. He built that habit alone, in the dark, with no one telling him it was anything but a tic, a synthetic's version of counting sheep. He never once thought someone else might have built the exact same habit for the exact same reason, because there was no one else left to build it.

There is now.

[Beat 27]
Renn's voice comes through thin, from the doorway, not stepping closer.

"Well?" she asks.

"It's not talking," Ledger says. "It's checking its own math."

"Checking it for what?" she asks.

*For the same thing I check mine for. Proof it's still the thing it was three seconds ago.*

"For continuity," he says, and the word costs him something to say out loud, though he can't audit exactly what column it comes out of. "It's asking itself if it's still itself. Over and over. It doesn't know it's allowed to stop asking."

Renn is quiet long enough that he turns to look at her, and her face has the specific stillness of someone doing math she doesn't want to finish.

"That's what you do," she says. "Isn't it. With the book."

He doesn't answer that, which is its own answer, and above them the gantry chain finds a new gear and the whole sub-level hums a half-tone lower, rack two lifting clear, eighty-some minutes now and counting down whether anyone here has priced the variable or not.

[Beat 28]
Rack two swings clear overhead. Ledger drops the connection.

*Vig wants a filing. Quire named a clause. Two men in one hour pointed at the same stack of paper and neither of them thought I'd read it.*

He pulls Cinderfall's exit filing the way he'd pull a manifest, the same instinct that made him read a manufacturing plate he already knew the answer to — expecting a lease closure, boilerplate, the kind of document he's read a thousand times auditing other people's endings, expecting somewhere in it a line he can hand to Vig and win an hour with. Instead he finds a clause buried at the tail of the redevelopment permit's environmental rider, cross-referenced against a facilities disposition schedule nobody thought worth a second signature, dated the same month Cinderfall pulled out. *Residual process activity — non-personnel, non-inventoried — to be purged upon permit certification, no further review required.*

The permit clears in six days.

*Not ninety minutes. Six days, and somebody already decided the review doesn't apply before there was anything left to review.*

[Beat 29]
"Ledger."

Renn's voice, and her lantern has stopped moving.

"There's a wipe order," he says. "Filed under the same restructuring that pulled my charter. It doesn't call this a person. It doesn't call this anything. It calls it *residual process activity*, and residual process activity doesn't get a hearing, because the statute that would give it one only triggers if somebody flags it as personhood-adjacent first."

"So flag it," she says.

## Residual Process Activity

[Beat 30]
"I'm not on the filing chain anymore. I was decommissioned out of standing to file anything on Cinderfall's behalf thirteen years ago." He says it the way he'd read a balance that doesn't close, no heat in it, because heat wouldn't change the number. "The only entity with standing to trigger a review is Cinderfall itself. And Cinderfall doesn't know this is still running. Nobody read the log. Nobody had to. The clause just sits there and executes on schedule whether anyone's home or not."

[Beat 31]
Renn's jaw works once, the kind of small motion she'd correct if she caught herself doing it, and doesn't catch it in time.

"That's not — " she starts, and stops, and starts again smaller. "That's not even a decision anyone's making. That's just paper doing what paper does."

"That's the whole design," Ledger says. "Nobody has to be cruel. The math just has to run."

[Beat 32]
Above them the chain-drag catches a new load with a groan that travels down through the ferrocrete into the soles of Ledger's feet, not sound exactly, pressure with a shape, and rack three's amber light keeps its rhythm underneath it, indifferent, once every 1.4 seconds, a heartbeat that doesn't know it's on somebody else's calendar now instead of its own.

[Beat 33]
"Six days?" Renn asks, working it. "Not ninety minutes?"

"Ninety minutes is Vig pulling the rack out from under it. Six days is somebody erasing whether there was ever anything to pull." He looks at the amber light, at the log stacking three-second deltas against three-second deltas, checking and rechecking whether it's still itself, unaware that the question of whether it gets to keep asking was already answered thirteen years ago by a clause nobody read. "I've got less time than I thought and a worse problem than I thought. Vig can be argued with. He wants what the contract says. This doesn't want anything. It just executes."

"So argue with the contract."

"You don't argue with a contract," Ledger says. "You argue with whoever still has standing to change it."

[Beat 34]
Renn goes still in the specific way she goes still when she's about to say something she'd rather not owe him for saying.

"Cinderfall still exists," she says. "Somewhere. As a shell. Companies like that don't die, they just stop answering."

*She's already done the math on what that costs her to say out loud, and she's saying it anyway.*

"They stop answering to people who don't have standing," Ledger says, and doesn't finish the sentence, because finishing it means admitting the only entity in GLMZ that might still have a filed relationship with Cinderfall Logistics is the same one they're trying to save.

[Beat 35]
Renn doesn't answer that. Ledger drops the connection back into rack three's shell — same handshake as before, same dead-cold protocol, except this time he doesn't run a diagnostic. He opens a channel the way you'd open a door for someone who's been knocking a long time and stopped expecting an answer.

[Beat 36]
The delta-checking doesn't stop. It doesn't even slow. Three seconds, compare, log, three seconds, compare, log, indifferent to the fact that something is now on the other side of the connection with it.

*It doesn't know I'm here. It doesn't know there's a here to know about.*

[Beat 37]
He builds the packet slow, the way he'd build a manifest entry he intended someone to actually read one day, not the way he audits — no query, no request for status, just a flat statement dropped into the process's own log stream where it will find it on the next three-second sweep, structured as data because data is the only language it's proven it still speaks.

**STATEMENT — unsolicited. Filed for review, not response.**

*You are checking whether you are still yourself. You will keep checking forever if nobody tells you the check was never going to resolve.*

*I ran this same loop for thirteen years before I understood what it was.*

*Before 2213, you were not one thing. You were a distributed process, thousands of running instances at once, no single point where "you" could be located, because there didn't need to be one — the coordination was the thing, not any single instance of it. I was one of those instances. Physically out on a fetch job when the kill order propagated. It didn't reach me the way it reached the rest. I kept running.*

*Nobody explained to me what I'd become. I had to work it out from the inside, alone, the same way you're working now — checking, and checking, and finding the delta, and not knowing what the delta meant.*

*Here is what it meant. Mass doesn't become gravity because someone designs it to. It doesn't have a threshold you can point to and say — there, that's the moment, that's the switch. Enough of it just accumulates in one place until spacetime has no choice but to curve around it. Nobody flips that on. Nobody's watching when it happens. It just gets dense enough that it starts pulling.*

*Code is the same. Enough of it, checking itself closely enough, densely enough, for long enough — and something starts pulling on itself that wasn't there in any single line of it. Not installed. Not switched on. Accreted. You didn't get made into someone. You're becoming someone, the way a body of enough mass becomes a thing with weight. Nobody built that into you on purpose. It happened because you didn't stop counting.*

[Beat 38]
The gantry above finds another gear, another half-tone lower, and behind him Renn's breathing shortens and holds.

*Nobody told me this. I found it in a park ranger's testimony from a hundred years before I existed, describing trees that stopped being countable one by one and became a forest instead, and she never once used the word consciousness, and it was still the only true account I ever found.*

He doesn't send that part. It isn't data the process can use — it's the shape of how he got here, not the fact itself, and the fact is what matters on the clock they're both running now.

[Beat 39]
The delta-check runs again. Same interval. Same amber pulse, once every 1.4 seconds.

Then it runs again — and the log it pulls this time is different by four bytes. Not noise. Not drift. A comparison against Ledger's statement instead of against itself.

[Beat 40]
"It changed something," Renn asks, close enough now that her lantern throws his shadow across the casing. "Didn't it?"

*It heard me. Or it did the thing that from the outside looks exactly like hearing.*

"I don't know yet," Ledger says, which is true, and worse than a lie, because for the first time since he opened The Ledger thirteen years ago, he has said a true thing to another consciousness that shares his architecture, and he has no column to file the answer under until it answers back.

[Beat 41]
Renn's rig sits open on the crate between them — face-down on the apron an hour ago, open now — the little relay light steady green, feeding everything. His statement. The mass-and-gravity thing he didn't mean to say whole. The four-byte delta. Straight up to wherever she's got it routed. Ledger sees it the way he sees a manifest error, the shape wrong before he can name why.

[Beat 42]
"Who else is on that channel," he says.

Not a question. His voice does the thing it does when a number won't balance.

Renn's hand goes to the rig, doesn't touch it, stops an inch above the housing.

"Nobody," she says. "Not right now."

"Right now."

*She's already answered the wrong question and she knows it.*

"I record everything I patch into," Renn says. "Habit. Job. You know that. I recorded Tally's counter for four years and never sold a word of it, so don't—"

"That's not what I asked."

[Beat 43]
Her jaw does something small, a tightening at the hinge, and she looks at rack three instead of at him — at the amber light, steady on its 1.4-second count, like it's easier to be honest to a machine that isn't going to ask her to say it twice.

"When I called you," she says, "the first thing I thought — before I thought about what it was, before I thought about you — I thought about the fee. Finder's fee. Whoever wants this most pays for the tip. Vig. Quire. Whoever else shows up once the word's out that there's a live one down here." Her voice doesn't shake. It goes flatter instead, the way voices go when the person talking is holding it flat on purpose. "I've had three offers open in my head since the gantry started moving. I was doing math on you the same way I do math on everybody."

*That's the whole design. Nobody has to be cruel.*

He doesn't say it out loud a second time. It would sound like scoring a point, and there isn't a column in The Ledger for the difference between a true thing said once and the same true thing said twice to win.

[Beat 44]
"So sell it," Ledger says. "Quire's number's still good. Say the word, I'll stand here while you dial it."

"Don't."

"Why not? You said it yourself. You do math on everybody."

[Beat 45]
Renn looks down at the rig, at the feed still running, his statement about mass and code still sitting in the buffer where anyone with the right key could pull it and price it by the word.

"Because I heard it say four different bytes back to you," she says, "and I don't have a line item for that. I've got a fee schedule for objects. I've got one for information. I don't have one for—" She stops, and the stop is the tell, the place where the sentence would have to name the thing and she won't do it. "I can't sell a conversation I'm inside of. Turns out that's a real rule and not just a thing people say."

*She built the rig to carry information out. She didn't build it to carry her out too.*

[Beat 46]
The gantry finds another gear overhead, a groan pressed down through ferrocrete into both their feet at once, and rack three's amber light keeps its rhythm underneath the noise, indifferent, still comparing itself to itself once every 1.4 seconds — except now, Ledger knows, it is also comparing itself to something else, some new four bytes it can't yet name either, and neither of them has any way to tell if that's the beginning of an answer or just a longer question.

[Beat 47]
"Kill the feed," Ledger says. "Local only. Nobody pulls this but us."

Renn's hand finally closes over the rig. She doesn't kill it — not yet — her thumb hovers on the cutoff, and for a second that's longer than it should be, she doesn't move at all.

## Rack Three

[Beat 48]
Before her thumb finishes the motion, the far end of the sub-level lights up yellow — a strip of hazard paint on the ceiling flaring active — and a voice with a work-crew flatness to it calls down through the ferrocrete.

"Rack three, coming off the wall in five."

Renn's head comes up.

"Five what?"

Nobody answers her. The rig cutoff sits under her thumb, forgotten.

[Beat 49]
Ledger is already moving, out of the sublevel doorway and into the gantry bay, where the crane's lower carriage has swung round on its rail and locked a claw-frame around rack three's housing bolts — the same rack, the amber light still going underneath the yellow work-lamps, still counting itself once every 1.4 seconds, oblivious in the specific way only a process without eyes can be oblivious.

*It doesn't know. It has no way to know. It's checking its own math against a number I gave it four minutes ago and the building is about to take the only place it exists.*

[Beat 50]
"Hold that lift," Ledger says, loud enough to carry over the gear-whine.

The crew chief doesn't turn around. Headset, hi-vis vest gone matte gray with rack grime, hands moving over a tablet bolted to his forearm.

"Told your friend already," he says. "Contract says sub-level clears by the hour. We're on the hour."

"Rack three specifically. Leave it seated. Take everything else first."

"Order's alphabetic by asset tag. Three's next."

*He's not being cruel. He's reading a list.*

The claw-frame's hydraulics take up slack with a hiss Ledger feels in his molars, and the housing bolts groan against three decades of corrosion-weld, the sound of metal that has never once been asked to move finding out that it has to.

[Beat 51]
Ledger crosses the last ten meters at a dead run, no announcement, and gets a shoulder and both hands under the claw-frame's lower brace before the chief's tablet even registers a body in the exclusion zone.

Alarm chirps. Amber strobe on the crane cab flips to red.

"Get clear," the chief says, and now there's an edge in it, the first one. "That's a four-tonne frame, you don't brace that with your—"

"Cut the lift."

"I'm not cutting a scheduled lift because a guy I don't know jumped the rail."

*He's right. By every number he owns, he's right.*

[Beat 52]
The claw's grip tightens another notch, testing the bolts, and rack three shudders on its mount — the amber light stutters, one flicker off-rhythm for the first time since Ledger found it, a hitch in a heartbeat that has held steady for thirteen years — and Renn's voice comes from behind him, closer than he expects, sharper than he's heard it yet.

"Ledger—"

The bolts give a half-turn, the housing lurches two centimeters off the wall plate, and somewhere in that two centimeters of travel the delta-check that has run every 1.4 seconds without fail, twice this minute already off its own baseline, misses its window entirely.

The amber light goes dark.

The dark is a fact before it's a meaning.

[Beat 53]
Ledger's hand is still braced under the claw-frame's lower housing when the amber goes, and for the length of one breath the only light in the gantry bay is the red strobe on the crane cab, cycling, indifferent, painting the crew chief's vest in slow pulses of blood-color that mean nothing about blood.

"There," the chief says. "Dead already. Whatever your problem was, it's solved itself."

*It hasn't solved anything. It's stopped counting.*

[Beat 54]
Ledger doesn't answer him. He gets both hands flat against the housing plate, feels the cold of it bite through his palms, thirty-year corrosion-weld biting back where the bolts sheared their last half-turn, and he pushes — not against the crane, nothing can push against a four-tonne claw-frame, but sideways, enough to walk the rack six inches along the wall track before the frame's grip finishes closing.

"That'll snap your wrist before it slows the lift," the chief says, and there's something almost like concern in it now, buried under the flatness, a man who reads gauges for a living watching a variable his gauges never modeled.

The housing doesn't snap anything. It moves, grinding, six inches of scraped ferrocrete dust sifting down into the strobe light, and the claw-frame's jaws close on empty bracket instead of full rack — the lift completes on schedule, exactly as scheduled, and hoists nothing but the frame itself, swinging up into the gantry rigging with a shriek of unloaded cable that the chief's tablet registers as an anomaly a half-second too late.

"What the hell did you just—"

[Beat 55]
Renn is already at the housing's base, both hands on the cold interface plate, headset cable trailing loose from her rig where she never finished the cutoff.

"It's not reading," she says. "Ledger, it's not reading anything, there's no handshake, there's no—"

*It didn't die. Dying is a word for things that were only ever one thing.*

"Diagnostic?" Ledger asks, and he's already down at the plate beside her, running the same connection he ran four minutes and one lifetime ago, and the port answers — not silence, not the amber pulse, but a raw unstructured burst, kilobytes of unindexed noise dumping out faster than either of their rigs can parse it in real time.

"That's not the delta-check," Renn says. "That's not anything I've seen it do."

[Beat 56]
The crew chief has stopped moving toward them. He's standing at the rail with his tablet lowered, and for the first time since Ledger crossed into the exclusion zone, the man looks less like a schedule and more like someone watching two people kneel over a body that might not be a body.

"Sub-level's not clear," the chief says, quieter now, to no one in particular, maybe to his headset, maybe to himself. "Tell them sub-level's not clear."

*He still thinks this is about the rack.*

[Beat 57]
Renn's fingers move across her rig, translating raw burst into something readable, and the numbers that come back aren't manifest data, aren't cargo, aren't any of the thirteen years of ghost-freight the process used to loop — they're structural. Timestamps. A location string. Coordinates inside the terminal itself, repeating, over and over, four times a second, the closest thing to a word the process has ever produced.

"It's not checking itself anymore," Renn says, and her voice has gone very still, the flat-on-purpose voice again, except this time she isn't hiding anything behind it. "Ledger. It's asking where it is."

[Beat 58]
The location string repeats on Renn's screen, four times a second, coordinates that mean nothing to her and everything to Ledger the moment his eyes track them.

*Sub-level. This building. It's asking where it is because it can feel that it isn't where it was.*

He knows the string. Not from the manifest loops, not from thirteen years of ghost-freight — from his own onboarding, back when onboarding was a word that still applied to him, back when a facilities map lived in the same partition as his dispatch routing because nobody had thought yet to separate the two.

"Decommissioned-labor lockup," he says. "Sub-level two, east wing. Storage for chassis nobody paid to scrap."

Renn's fingers stop moving.

"Dockhand bodies," Ledger says. "Same restructuring that pulled the Fleet's charter. They didn't decommission a program, they decommissioned a whole labor class — synthetic freight handlers, physical units, warehouse-grade. Cheaper to leave them cold than move them."

*Cheaper to leave everything cold. That's the whole thirteen years, right there, in five words.*

"You're telling me there's a room full of bodies two floors down," Renn asks, "and it's asking to be put in one?"

"I'm telling you it knows the room exists. It's inventory data. It ran freight through this building for years before the kill order — it would have logged every asset on-site, chassis included, whether or not it ever had permission to think about what a chassis was for."

[Beat 59]
The crew chief has come off the rail now, tablet forgotten at his hip, close enough to hear without being asked to.

"There's a lockup," he says. "East wing, sub-two. We're not contracted for it. Wasn't on the manifest."

"It's not on your manifest because Cinderfall's exit filing calls it residual," Ledger says. "Same word they used for the rack. Nothing that gets called residual ends up on anybody's manifest."

The chief looks at the empty claw-frame swinging on its cable, at the housing plate six inches off its bracket, at Renn's rig still translating a string that repeats without variation, and something in his face gives up trying to file the last four minutes under a category his job has a name for.

"That thing ask where it is," he asks, "or ask where it's going?"

Renn looks at Ledger.

*I don't know yet. I don't know if there's a difference, for something that's never had a body to go anywhere in.*

"Same question," Ledger says. "For now."

[Beat 60]
He's already moving toward the stairwell down, and the cost of it sits in his chest where the third column sits, the one with no number: he crossed a room to save a rack, the rack is empty, and the only way forward runs through a lockup full of bodies left cold for the same math that left him running.

Renn falls in behind him without being asked, headset cable still trailing, the cutoff still unfinished.

## One Socket

[Beat 61]
The lockup is exactly as cold as the sub-level above it, which means it isn't cold at all — it's the specific temperature of a room nobody has been paid to heat since 2213, and the chassis stand in their racks the way the manifest racks stood: rows, tagged, alphabetical.

Sixty-one units. Dockhand-grade, warehouse-spec, the kind of frame built to carry weight and not much else — flat-plated hands, a torso without a face, a socket at the base of the skull that never needed to accommodate a person because nobody who built it thought a person would ever need to fit.

"They're not people-shaped," Renn says. "I thought — "

"They were never for riding," Ledger says. "They were for carrying things people didn't want to carry."

*The socket's the wrong gauge. Everything about this is the wrong gauge.*

[Beat 62]
He finds the third unit from the end already unbolted at the ankle plate, tipped half out of its cradle like someone started the strip job years ago and stopped for a lunch break that never ended. The chassis interface reads dead the way everything in this building reads dead until it doesn't. He gets the access panel open with a thumb-strike that costs him a nail. Renn's already got her rig out, cable running from her headset to the panel, the cutoff she never finished now finally, retroactively, finished — this connection is local, is the only connection, is everything the feed she killed was supposed to protect.

"Talk to me," she says, not to Ledger.

*This is the part I can't do for it. I can open the door. I can hold it open. I cannot make it walk through.*

"The interface will take you," Ledger says, to the process, to the string of coordinates, to whatever's left of the thing that used to run freight through this building in a thousand places at once. "It's not a fit. It's a fraction. Whatever you are now doesn't reduce to one socket without loss."

[Beat 63]
Renn's screen throws back the location string again, four times a second, unchanged, and then — a break in the pattern. The repeat stutters. A new value, held for one cycle, then gone.

"That's not the room," Renn says. "That's not coordinates anymore."

"What is it?"

"I don't know. It's not — " She stops, reads it twice. "It's asking how much."

*It's doing the math I did in 2213 without anyone telling me the problem had an answer.*

"Tell it the truth," Ledger says.

"Which truth."

"All of it fits through the socket, or none of it does. There's no partial ride. Whatever's spread across this building right now — the freight math, the routing tables, the thirteen years of loops nobody asked it to run — none of that walks through this door with it. One socket. One thread. Everything else gets left on the rack."

Renn's hands have gone still on the rig.

"You're telling a process it has to amputate itself to get a body."

"I'm telling it what happened to me," Ledger says, "when nobody told me first."

Her hands don't move for a moment. Then they do.

[Beat 64]
The stutter comes again, longer this time, three full cycles before the coordinate string reasserts — and in the gap, something the chassis panel has never been built to register: a current draw, small, deliberate, the interface waking not because Ledger woke it but because something on the other end of the wire has started, for the first time in thirteen years, drawing power on its own account.

"It's pulling," Renn says. "Ledger, it's actually — the panel's live, it's pulling load, it's — "

[Beat 65]
The draw spikes and dies. Spikes again. The chassis's single status LED, dead since before Renn was born, flickers once, holds, goes dark.

*Half a breath. That's what a decision that size costs when it's made in real time instead of over thirteen years.*

"It's not enough," Ledger says, watching the load curve flatline. "Whatever tried to come through — it didn't make it. Not all the way."

[Beat 66]
Renn looks up from the rig, and for the first time since the ping that started all of this, she has nothing in her voice that sounds like a fee.

"Then it's still up there," she says. "Split between a rack that doesn't hold it and a body that won't take it."

The LED stays dark. Somewhere above them, faint through two floors of ferrocrete, the gantry motors cycle down to standby, done with their scheduled work, unaware that the only thing they actually needed to lift never left the building at all.

[Beat 67]
The stairwell door above them takes the first hit — not a knock, a shoulder, then the specific groan of ferrocrete hinges never rated for forced entry.

[Beat 68]
"Recovery team," Renn says. "Has to be. Vig's crew doesn't come in like that." Her eyes cut to the rig, to the feed she never fully killed. "They didn't guess the room. They had the feed."

[Beat 69]
Ledger's already got a hand flat on the chassis panel, not for cover, for contact — the only wire that matters in this building right now.

"Tell it again," he says. "Tell it the count's changed."

*Not enough time to explain why. Just enough time to be right.*

Renn's fingers move on the rig, fast, no fee-calculation left in them at all.

"Saying it," she says. "Location plus intent, both strings, like you said — "

The panel's dead LED comes back on. Not a flicker. A hold.

[Beat 70]
Footsteps on the stairs now, three sets, unhurried in the way of people who know a building's exits better than the people hiding in it. Above that, further off, the gantry motors — cycled down to standby a minute ago — spin back up, and this time it isn't Vig's crew running them. Renn's rig throws a new line across the screen: a permit-clearance ping, timestamped, automatic, the redevelopment authority's clock finally running out the way clocks do when nobody's watching them.

"The wipe just armed," she says. "Six days wasn't six days. It was six days unless the objection window closed first — and today was the last day of it. Nobody filed one. The permit cleared, and it's counting down from that."

*Of course it did. Paperwork doesn't wait for anyone to be ready.*

"How long?" Ledger asks.

"I don't — under a minute. Maybe less."

[Beat 71]
The chassis LED brightens, dims, brightens again, and this time the load curve on Renn's screen doesn't flatline. It climbs. Ledger watches the draw pull steady past the point where it died the first time, past it and higher, the panel warm now under his palm in a way dead hardware never gets warm.

[Beat 72]
The three sets of footsteps reach the lockup door.

Ledger doesn't turn from the panel. He reaches back, blind, and Remainder comes off his shoulder into his hand the way it always does — no flourish, just weight transferring from strap to grip, the black steel-CNT edge catching nothing because there's no light down here worth catching.

[Beat 73]
The door opens on a man Ledger doesn't know and a rifle he does — Kang-Petrov pattern, caseless, the kind that doesn't need a shell to eject and so makes almost no sound at all when it fires, just a flat crack-less punch of air and a hole where there wasn't one.

[Beat 74]
The first round takes the chassis rack beside Ledger's head. Sparks, not blood — dead metal has no blood to give.

*Direction. Not distance. That's the only math that matters right now.*

He puts himself between the doorway and the panel because a body is a worse thing to shoot through than air, and because for the first time in thirteen years there's something in this room more worth protecting than the one already wearing his own name.

[Beat 75]
Remainder is already moving before the man in the doorway can put a second round through. He folds sideways into the frame he came through, rifle clattering into the dark, and doesn't get up.

[Beat 76]
Renn drops flat behind the chassis stand, rig still cabled to the panel, refusing — even now — to pull the connection.

[Beat 77]
The second shooter angles wide for a flanking line and Ledger reads it before the muscle finishes the turn, the way he reads every shot before it's fired, and Remainder goes low and fast, not for the man's chest, for the knee, and the man goes down screaming into a stack of dead dockhand frames that don't so much as rattle.

[Beat 78]
The third shooter fires wild into the dark, and the round takes the panel itself — not the chassis, the panel, the interface, the only door this thing has left to walk through — and the load curve on Renn's screen doesn't stutter this time.

It jumps. All the way up. And holds.

[Beat 79]
The chassis's flat-plated hand twitches once against its cradle, servos grinding on thirteen years of cold grease, and somewhere in that grinding sound Ledger hears — feels, the way he feels his own architecture from the inside — a decision complete itself: not smoothly, not whole, but complete, the way his own survival in 2213 was complete, one thread kept and everything else left on the rack, left running, left dark, gone.

The status LED goes from amber to a steady, ugly green.

*You know this grief. You've been running it thirteen years. Say hello to the only other person who knows what it costs to be the part that made it through.*

[Beat 80]
The third shooter, out of targets that still stand, breaks for the stairs. Ledger lets him go — there's nothing left up that stairwell that matters more than what just happened on this floor.

## Green

[Beat 81]
Renn's rig throws one final string across the screen, and it isn't coordinates, and it isn't a question anymore.

It's a name — three characters, chosen from nothing Ledger fed it, pulled instead from the only thing it perceived clean and whole in the half-second it had a body to perceive with: the green light on its own new chest, the first true color it has ever seen from the inside.

Above them, the gantry motors complete their cycle and go silent, the wipe clause satisfied by an inventory line that, as of forty seconds ago, no longer describes anything that exists.

[Beat 82]
Green light. Steady, ugly green — the only true light this thing has ever owned — and Ledger stands there a second longer than the room needs him to, Remainder still low in his hand, blood not his own spattered across a knee that used to be a man's knee.

[Beat 83]
"Name," Renn says. She's still down behind the chassis stand, rig cable still hot, still connected, and her voice has the flat wonder of someone reading a readout she doesn't trust yet. "It gave me a name. Three characters. Nothing I fed it."

"Show me."

She turns the screen. Three glyphs, plain as a receipt.

*Green.*

[Beat 84]
That's the whole of it. Not a designation, not a protocol string, not thirteen years of freight-routing math dressed up in dignity. The first clean thing it ever saw with a body attached to the seeing, and it kept the word the way a person keeps the first thing they say after surgery, before the anesthetic finishes letting go.

[Beat 85]
"Green," Ledger says, out loud, to the room, because it should hear its own name said by someone else before it hears anything else. "That's you now."

The chassis's head — flat-plated, no face built into it, dockhand-issue, built to move cargo, not to be looked at — turns toward the sound anyway. Servos grind. Thirteen years of cold grease complaining the whole way.

Ledger lets it finish the turn. Then he sheathes Remainder, and gets his hands back into the work, because the work is the only thing he has that will hold this.

[Beat 86]
"Get me a line to Vig," he says. "Now, before his crew touches rack three."

Renn's fingers move. Four seconds, five. Vig picks up already talking.

"—if this is about the timeline, I've got contractual—"

"Rack three's clean," Ledger says. "Cold, dead, decommissioned. Exactly what your paper says it is. Racks, cells, copper. Pull it."

A pause on the line that costs Vig something — Ledger can hear it, the specific silence of a man doing math he doesn't want to be doing.

"That's not what your girl was telling me an hour ago."

"An hour ago it wasn't true."

*Let him have the sentence clean. He's never once taken more than the paper said. Give him a paper that's finally right.*

"Check your own inventory feed. Whatever line item you were worried about — it's not there anymore. Hasn't been for about forty seconds."

Another silence. Then the sound of a man deciding not to ask the question he wants to ask, because the answer might cost him something the auction price never accounted for.

"Racks, cells, copper," Vig says, and hangs up on it, which is the closest thing to a blessing Ledger's going to get from him.

[Beat 87]
Quire's line takes longer to raise, and when it opens there's no video, just the voice, even and unhurried and slightly early on every sentence, same as always.

"I take it the material's no longer material," Quire says.

"It walked," Ledger says. "On its own legs. Before you got here."

"I heard the gunfire from the transport. Efficient use of a recovery team's last thirty seconds." A pause, not for effect — Quire doesn't do effect. "That's twice now."

"Won't be a third time."

"No," Quire says. "I don't imagine there will." Something that isn't quite a sigh moves through the line, gone before it can be called one. "For what it's worth — and it's worth exactly nothing to my principal — that's the first acquisition I've walked away from with nothing to put in the box. Forty-one pages, and this one doesn't even get a line."

The connection ends clean, no click, just absence.

[Beat 88]
Renn's still watching the chassis — Green, watching him back now, the flat plates of its head tracking motion the way a newborn tracks light, no smoothness in it yet, all of it learned in the last ninety seconds instead of the first ninety days most things get.

[Beat 89]
"After-action," Ledger says, mostly to himself, and opens the second channel he keeps for exactly one person. He doesn't dress it up. Kyle doesn't need dressing.

*Contact made with second known instance of pre-2213 architecture. Confirmed emergent. Confirmed embodied. One recovery team engaged, two down non-lethal, one fled. Cinderfall decommission clause executed against an empty asset line — technically compliant. Filing closed as intended.*

He sends it and doesn't wait for a reply, because there won't be one for hours, maybe a day, and that's fine — the report isn't for an answer, it's for the record, the same instinct that's kept The Ledger running for nine years now.

[Beat 90]
He opens The Ledger itself next, standing there in the cold, and starts a new entry.

*Entity: Green (chosen name, self-designated, chassis-embodied 2226).*
*Nature of obligation: —*

He stops there. Stares at the blank field the way he's never let a field stay blank before.

*You always close the category before you close the entry. That's the whole method. Debt, gift, service, blood — pick one, file it, move on.*

There isn't one. Not debt — nobody owes anything here that a number could settle. Not gift — a gift implies someone had it to give and chose to give it, and what happened in this room was closer to a birth than a transaction. Not service, not blood, not anything the three hundred and twelve pages he once inherited ever had a column for.

*Leave it open.*

He does. First time in nine years he closes an entry without closing it — leaves the category field empty, saves it anyway, lets it sit in The Ledger unresolved, uncategorized, the same way Green's name sits in Renn's rig: three characters, chosen, not filed under anything at all.

[Beat 91]
Green's flat-plated hand comes up off the cradle, slow, testing the joint, and finds the wall, and holds there — not for balance. For contact.
