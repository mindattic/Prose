# Chapter 10: The One Who Doesn't Stop

_Hegewisch, 11:47 PM. Kyle's neuretics is writing checks his body still has to cash._

## Hegewisch

[Beat 1]
Arcturus Civil Security — the CorpoSec stand-in for the law this city dissolved — comes at Kyle the way it always does when the invoice runs high: not one man but an entire kill team. Six of them, MilSpec reflex-ware threaded under the skin. Two matte SUVs fanning across the Hegewisch crossroads. A cadre of professional killers who do this for a salary and partial dental. They never even questioned why there was no name, just a description on their off-the-book kill contract.

The neighborhood reads the change in temperature. A noodle cart folds itself shut with a clatter of shutters. A kid on a stoop is hauled indoors by the collar. The street empties the way streets down here have learned to empty — fast, and without comment — until there is no one left standing in the middle of it but Kyle, his bike, and his bad options.

He'd known since morning that the day had a bad lean to it. At dawn he'd watched a flock of geese fly in formation but backward — beating north while they slid south, against themselves, against the air — Birds went wrong like that when The Weather was coming. He'd figured on a thin spot waiting somewhere up ahead. He had not figured on company.

His neuretics returns its first assessment in a single flat word: Run. The bio-compute is grown around and through his brain and worked by thought alone, and most people run theirs to read their messages, scroll their socials, or drone out on slop. Kyle's is a long way past military grade, and even it wants nothing to do with this fight. He overrides it and drops into engagement mode, and it goes to work: cataloguing gait and stance and micro-weight shifts, triangulating muzzle angles from shoulder geometry alone, running threat-stack priority on all five remaining operators simultaneously, painting augmented target locks in colours only his optic nerve receives — and underneath all of it, reading the hundred small tells a body leaks before it fires, handing him the next half-second a hundred and twenty milliseconds before it arrives.

The team leader's finger takes up the slack on his trigger. Kyle is already moving when the first burst stitches the air he's no longer standing in. Cacophony — his five-shot revolver shotgun — clears its throat off his left hip, and a.410 slug rides an augmented-reality line only Kyle can see straight into the nearest operator, who folds around his own sternum and sits down hard in the road. Four left in the cylinder. Five shooters still up.

[Beat 2]
The team is good. They don't bunch and they don't panic — they peel back to the SUVs and start working the angles — and good is exactly the problem, because precognition against one trigger is a gift and against five at once is a budget, to be spent carefully and never refunded. His nervous system begins to cook under the load. A second slug catches the flanker breaking left, and the man drops mid-stride. A third punches clean through the door an operator is crouched behind and folds the steel in after it.

Then Cacophony runs dry on the fourth — the hammer falling on an empty chamber with a click he feels in his back teeth — and there is no time to swing the cylinder out and feed it fresh, so Kyle simply quits solving the problem with the gun. He reaches over his shoulder and draws Silence off his back. The katana is a matte-black length of steel-CNT meta-alloy, an edge that takes no light and offers no trick but the hand that holds it, and the hand has had a long time to practice. He crosses the open ground inside the half-second his neuretics has bought him, slides under the muzzle before it can track, and takes the rifle out of the fifth man's grip at the receiver — leaving him disarmed, untouched, and loudly amazed to still be in possession of all ten fingers.

Steel for steel's problems and Cacophony for the rest: the whole of Kyle's philosophy of violence, and it has kept him breathing longer than the actuarial tables care for. Behind his eyes the overclock runs hot. In the corner of his mind's eye a small red light ticks over, the way it always does when he overdraws — a tally he stopped reading years ago — and the cost gathers somewhere out of sight, a debt he'll settle up later. A thin line of blood starts from his nose; he thumbs it off his lip without thinking, and the white creeps in at the edges of everything he can see. He ignores both, the way he always does.

Five down or out. One left — the leader, who has not moved this whole time, because the leader has been waiting.

[Beat 3]
The leader brought the solution built for men like Kyle, and he has only been waiting for the range to clear enough to use it. It is a Gauss rifle — a man-portable rail driver that throws a single hypersonic depleted-uranium dart along a line so flat it might as well have been drawn with a ruler, and cares not at all what happens to be standing behind the target. It is the one weapon Kyle's neuretics cannot beat, because you cannot dodge a thing that is already past you; you can only try not to be standing where it was aimed.

He settles it on Kyle's chest and fires. Kyle reads the shot the breath before it leaves the barrel and is simply no longer there — and the dart, finding nothing but the air he abandoned, does not stop. It crosses the crossroads dead straight and punches through the windshield of an ambulance running its lights north, and kills the driver where he sits. With no one left to steer it, the ambulance drifts through its turn and meets a stalled hauler at fifty-two miles an hour, and the impact throws its back doors wide on their hinges.

Kyle is inside the leader's reach before the man can cycle the rifle for a second shot — one clean upward cut — and the Gauss rifle clatters loose to begin a quieter life on the pavement.

He straightens in the wreck-light, and through the sprung-open bay doors of the ambulance, a small face is looking out at him. The small face lifts a hand and waves.

Kyle waves back.

[Beat 4]
"Are you the new one?" the small face asks, and Kyle understands at once that this is not the question she is actually asking.

She's eight, maybe nine. Her chest is taped under a flexible biopolymer brace, and she is, by a wide margin, more composed than the one-armed second-tier CorpoNation security officer bleeding out on the asphalt a yard to Kyle's left.

He steps up into the bay. The driver is dead. The medic slumped in the passenger seat is not coming back either. The patient tag clipped to the gurney reads IMANI OKONKWO-LINDQVIST — CRUCIBLE GENOMICS RECIPIENT — POST-OP, FIRST 6 HOURS — NORTHWESTERN MEMORIAL. Crucible Genomics prints organs to order, which means the kid is six hours into living on one of them. Kyle reads the tag twice. The math does itself.

"Hi, Imani," he says. "I'm Kyle."

"I know," she says. "My uncle Vo Dinh says you're the one who doesn't stop."

[Beat 5]
He thumbs the emergency contact on the tag. It rings once.

"Vance," a voice says, already moving.

"Your driver's dead," Kyle says. "The kid's on the floor of a south-side ditch with a bioprinted heart wired to a battery. I have a motorcycle."

A pause on the line — not shock, recognition. "Kyle." Vance says it flat, the way you say the name of a problem you already know by heart. "Of course it's you."

"What's the brace reading?" Vance asks.

Kyle looks. "Sinus, ninety-two, paced."

"You've got sixty minutes before the vascular scaffold throws a clot," Vance says. "Maybe seventy."

Kyle does not hear the maybe. The same military-grade neuretics that read the kill team a hundred and twenty milliseconds ahead of itself reads the brace now — the battery draw, the kid's color, the brute arithmetic of a six-hour-old heart — and hands back one cold number with no maybe anywhere in it. Sixty.

"Keep her calm," Vance is saying. "A new graft that races can tear right off the suture line. Those wires aren't there for decoration."

"Northwestern?" Kyle asks.

"Streeterville. Trauma Bay Nine. I'll have a slot waiting."

"I'm in Hegewisch."

Vance does the same math, almost as fast as Kyle's neuretics had. "Then it's a straight line. North. Don't stop for checkpoints, don't stop for anything. And mind The Weather — there's a thin spot parked over 35th, has been since Tuesday, and your straight line runs right under it. If the read in your skull drops out, you're in it. Steady speed, eyes on a far light, and whatever you do, don't look at the edges."

"See you in a few," Kyle says.

Vance hangs up.

[Beat 6]
He lifts Imani onto the pillion, sidesaddle, and threads the medical harness through his belt loop so the battery rides snug over his kidney instead of swinging loose. He ties her wrists loosely through the back of his coat so she won't fall if she sleeps. Then he feeds Cacophony a fresh moon-clip — five new chambers to replace the five he scattered back across the Hegewisch crossroads — and lets it settle on his left hip. Silence rides across his back, where it always rides.

Her cheek finds the back of his shoulder like it has been there before.

"Hold on," he says, which is a stupid thing to say to a kid whose hands are too small to reach around his ribs, but it is what you say.

"How fast?" she asks.

"Fast," he says.

"Good."

The kid is the only new weight he's carrying, and the only one he is doing math about. The bike turns over and catches.

[Beat 7]
The first mile is gray zone. Hegewisch sprawl, no sovereign in residence, nobody to answer to and nobody to answer for you. A decommissioned delivery automaton kneels in a drainage forecourt at the corner, the rain of so many winters streaking its chassis chalk-white, and some child has tied a festival ribbon around one of its outstretched hands; it holds the ribbon the way it would hold anything, patiently, for as long as the ribbon cares to stay. It is the kind of country a freelancer learns by heart, because the seams are where the work lives; Stash had run a load of something through here last month and said the south stretch of Avenue O was clean if you took it fast. Kyle takes Avenue O at eighty. Two long rows of collapsed light poles lie across the road like felled trees, and his neuretics flags them and lines up the gap before his eyes have finished arriving at the problem. Imani's breathing is a small warm patch against the back of his shoulder, even and slow and deliberately, carefully controlled.

"You're not scared," he says.

"I'm being efficient with my fear," she says.

Kyle almost laughs. Then doesn't.

"Where'd you learn that?" he asks.

"The lady who got me ready. She said to say it until it's true."

"Is it true yet?" Kyle asks.

Imani considers the question and answers it honestly. "No."

"Then keep saying it," he says. He does not tell her that it never quite comes true.

## Crucible Genomics

[Beat 8]
The checkpoint is two guards in soft-helmet armor and a third tucked behind a blast-shield, and all three wear the bored faces of men expecting a manifest. Kyle does not have a manifest. He has a child, a battery, and a smile, and he has learned that on the right night, presented with enough confidence, the three of them together pass for paperwork.

He brakes hard and throws both hands off the bars into the universal posture of a man holding nothing, and he gets the words out before any of the three can finish reaching for a weapon.

"POST-OP TRANSIT," he shouts. "LACUNA COOLER. NORTHWESTERN."

The left guard looks at the brace. The right guard looks at the cooler's logo. And the man behind the glass — Crucible internal, a white coat worn under his armor — pulls Imani's face up against a phenotype registry, because in a genomics zone the gate reads the person and never the plate. The bike is nobody here. It's the blood they care about. Whatever the registry hands back to him drains the color clean out of his evening.

"Let him through," he says. "Phenotype clearance."

The barrier sinks into the road.

"Have a great evening," Kyle says, and means every word of it.

[Beat 9]
A mile and a half of Crucible private artery — manicured, palm-lit, the river running black on the right. Kyle pegs the throttle. Workers in beige smocks glance up from the greenhouses and decide not to look long.

He works the geometry of it as he rides. The corporation has him tagged as a courier hauling outgoing inventory — the only reason the gate opened, and only good while he keeps moving. Nobody who wants what's wired into Imani's chest can follow him through here; they don't carry whatever got him waved in. So they won't follow. They'll call ahead. And the ones they wake on the far side won't need her breathing to collect, because a six-hour heart travels fine in a cooler.

He keeps moving.

"Why are the trees in rows?" Imani asks.

"Because the company likes to know where things are," Kyle says.

"That seems anxious."

Kyle has nothing to add. The kid is correct.

[Beat 10]
The actuator gives up its entire career at once. The slab jams a third of the way up, shrieking on a dead track, and Kyle flattens to the tank and takes them under it with inches to spare — close enough that the cold edge of the shutter combs the air over his spine. They drop off Crucible's glass-smooth pavement onto the broken asphalt of the gray zone, and the suspension finds out about Chicago all at once. Imani laughs. Small, clean, surprised out of her.

"Loud part," she says.

"Told you," he says.

## The Pullman Seam

[Beat 11]
Pullman Works glows up on the right — restored row houses, modern cameras hidden inside period housings, an Axiom company town keeping perfect time for people who don't own a single minute of it. Kyle threads the gray-zone seam along its edge, the unincorporated strip Axiom never bothered to fence, the kind of line he has spent a career learning to ride. Where the factory rain had dried, the pavement had gone soap-slick, and he fed the bike a little less lean through the stretch without thinking, the way you learn to trust a road that won't hold you.

Heads come up as the engine passes. One kid shouts something the engine swallows whole, but the shape of the word in his mouth is unmistakable — legend — and Kyle lifts a hand off the bars like a man in a parade.

Other heads come up too, and these ones don't spare a look for the bike. They're fledglings: pledges to the Vultures, the crews who scavenge the dead and the not-quite-dead, the ones who'll cut a transplant back out of your chest the day you miss a payment and sell off whatever's left of you for parts. A fledgling runs salvage on spec — no patch, no blessing — hoping to drag something home worth a permanent place in the flock. A post-op transplant riding pillion through open country isn't a target to a kid like that. It's a winning lottery ticket. The exact score that turns a fledgling into a Vulture. Two of them are already on their bikes. Already on their phones.

[Beat 12]
Imani's hand finds the side of his coat and clutches a fistful of it. She isn't afraid, Kyle realizes. She's absorbing. She has never once been outside Crucible's walls in her life, and he understands this about her without being told.

"First time off the block?" he asks.

"First time on the ground," she says.

"Well," he says. "Buckle up."

"Buckle up to what?" she asks.

"Existentially," he says.

She thinks about that one for a long moment, the city pouring past on both sides of her.

"Okay," she says.

## Ashgrave Corridor

[Beat 13]
The Ashgrave Synthesis Corridor announces itself with a wall of haze you can taste a block before you can see it. The perimeter lights glow the wrong color — too yellow, too low — the way a refinery glows when it is having a feeling about something. This is somebody's commute. A shift-change line of workers in cheap half-masks trudges the shoulder, heads down, lunch pails swinging, walking a thing they made their peace with a long time ago, because the only alternative on offer was not eating. A stall at the mouth of the corridor sells filter cartridges, and beside the cartridges a hand-painted board keeps a tally — DAYS SINCE — the number low, the way the number is always low here.

Kyle has a respirator clipped to his jacket. Imani does not. His neuretics throws the air up in front of his eyes as a number he doesn't like and a clock he likes a great deal less. He reaches back without slowing, finds her face with the flat of his palm, and presses his bandana over her mouth.

"Breathe shallow," he says. "Through this. Not your nose."

She nods against his hand. A masked worker on the shoulder lifts two fingers as the bike passes — not a wave, just the nod one night-shift gives another across a bad stretch of road — and Kyle returns it. Then he bites down on his own mouthpiece, snaps the seal, and rides into a sky the color of an old bruise like he was personally invited.

[Beat 14]
Two refinery towers exhale orange fire on either side of him, close enough that he could count the rivet patterns if he had a second to spare. Sixty feet up, a vent stack blows downward all at once, and the pressure slams sideways across the road. The bike yaws hard. He corrects before the yaw has finished happening, because the correction was already waiting in his hands a half-second before the problem arrived. Imani's grip tightens into a small steel claw at his belt.

"We're fine," he says, out loud.

"I know," she says through the bandana. "I can tell from your shoulders."

Kyle has met grown adults — armed, paid, supposedly professional — who could not read his shoulders. He is starting, against every instinct he owns, to take this kid personally.

[Beat 15]
Halfway through, the corridor's throat begins to close.

A leak somewhere upwind trips a containment lockdown, and Ashgrave's answer to a leak is a blast-rated bulkhead coming down across the only open lane on a dead-man timer — slow as a guillotine and about as open to negotiation — built to seal whoever's caught on the wrong side of it in with the very air they were trying to outrun. This is not the sally-gate. There is no actuator to find and shoot, no override, and no time to look for one, because a hydraulic spine already in motion is a different animal than one sitting at rest. His neuretics tells him so before he's even finished forming the question.

He can't beat the door. He can only beat it there.

He drops two gears and pins the throttle, and the read in his skull measures the shrinking gap against the height of a man lying flat on his tank with a child folded down his spine, and hands him back a number with no margin anywhere inside it. He takes the number anyway. Something behind his eyes gives a warning lurch for the one full second he leans that hard on the read — the body presenting its invoice in real time — then steadies.

They go under the falling edge at speed. The lip catches the battery brace and shrieks down the entire length of it in a fan of orange sparks — a hand's width higher and the night ends here, with both of them — and then the bulkhead seats home behind them with a boom he feels in the floor of his chest. Through. He throws one look down at the brace. Still green. The cooler held.

Imani isn't laughing this time. Both fists are buried in his coat, her face pressed flat between his shoulder blades, and she has gone very, very quiet. Kyle files that away with the other things tonight keeps teaching him about her.

[Beat 16]
He clears the corridor's north ramp at a hundred and ten, rolls off the throttle on the way down the far side, and spits the mouthpiece out so it swings free on its lanyard. Reaching back one-handed, he tugs Imani's bandana down off her face.

"You with me?" he asks.

She coughs once, twice, and then says, "I'm with you. Your shoulders said we were okay."

"My shoulders are working overtime," he says.

"Give them a raise."

Kyle laughs — a real one, surprised out of the bottom of him — laughs so hard he nearly loses the line.

## Englewood

[Beat 17]
Englewood at night looks like a city that has been told a joke nobody is laughing at. Kyle means to run 63rd straight across to Halsted, but 63rd dead-ends in a viaduct that finished collapsing sometime back in the spring — a long slope of broken deck and bent rebar where an underpass used to carry the road through. No way over it the easy way.

There is a hard way. A tilted slab the locals have braced and shimmed into a ramp, because in a gray zone every disaster eventually gets repurposed into infrastructure by the people who have to live on top of it. He knows the ramp is sound because Echo had run a job out this way a season back and tapped him the wave-map after, the whole broken shelf of Englewood charted in clicks and echoes the way only Echo bothers to chart anything. Some debts in this trade get paid in roads you'll never have to learn the hard way.

He takes it at speed. The bike goes light at the crest, weightless for one long held breath, Imani's battery thudding once against his kidney — and they land hard on the far side with thirty-four minutes left on the clock and a fresh ache settling in behind his teeth. Two headlights take the ramp a beat behind him. Then a third. His neuretics tags them, ranges them, and tells him the single useful thing there is to know about them: they are matching his speed on purpose.

He does the single useful thing in answer. He goes faster.

[Beat 18]
They come in a flock this time — the ones the phones woke, fresh bikes and no patience between them, fanning out across both lanes to take him at speed. The lead pair don't reach for guns. They reach for their belts and throw, and the air ahead of him fills with fat little cylinders that burst into white glare and dead static. Flashbang. Chaff. And the ugly third kind — the EMP-and-interference rounds a salvage crew carries to kill a mark's bike out from under him and blind whatever he's running behind his eyes.

It is a good plan. It would work on almost anyone alive.

Kyle's bike stumbles, coughs, catches, and keeps running on hardened ignition. And the read behind his eyes never so much as flickers — because a military neuretics is shielded against a great deal more than static. Against EMP. Against the whole spectrum of battlefield interference. And against the low, wrong pressure at the very bottom of the band that would fold a civilian rig clean in half. You do not harden a mind against that last thing unless you already know, for a fact, that it is out there waiting. Kyle has never told a living soul that his is. Where a pack of fledglings came by rounds that carry it is a question for a later night. He stops the bike — because running is the one thing they are built and ready for, and standing still is the one thing they are not.

He picks the narrow place under the 63rd Street viaduct where the lane necks down between two dead cars, thumbs the bike into auto-balance, and feels it take its own weight, the gyros holding it upright and patient at a crawl with the kid still aboard.

"Don't move," he tells her.

Imani goes still as furniture.

Then he steps off into the road and draws Silence off his back.

The first one commits to his swing — a length of chain, whipping flat — before he understands that the geometry of the whole fight just changed. Kyle reads the arc early, the way he reads everything, and simply isn't quite where it lands. The chain's tail still opens a thin red line across his forearm, the tax for standing in this close; he takes that, and on the same motion plucks the kid off his saddle, shallow and deliberate, and cuts the bandolier of grenades off his chest, and tosses it back underhand.

"Hold these."

Imani catches it against herself like a schoolbag.

The second one tries to simply run him down, which is how the second one comes to study the asphalt at close range. The third has the sense to draw a pistol — and that is the moment the neuretics paints the thing no briefing can ever quite sell you: the shot itself, a full beat before the trigger finishes pulling it, a hard red line laid from the muzzle straight through the center of Kyle's chest.

So Kyle takes his chest off the line. He drops his weight back off his heels, far enough to feel the round part the air just above his sternum and travel on to find the fourth fledgling — who had done everything right tonight except stand directly behind the man he was hunting. The shooter stares at what his own hand just did. Kyle relieves him of the pistol, and of any further interest in the fight.

The rest of the flock runs the math and doesn't care for the product. Two peel away north and don't look back. The ones already on the ground will walk home when their legs come back around to the idea.

Kyle swings onto his bike — still upright, still warm, still holding Imani exactly where he left her — kills the balance, and lets the weight settle into his hands like something he was born holding. Imani is looking down at the bandolier in her lap.

"Are these ours now?" she asks.

"Until some bigger kid wants to try and take them," Kyle says, threading her free hand back through the back of his coat.

She thinks about that, and finds it fair — which is, Kyle suspects, the most truthful thing anyone has ever bothered to tell her. He brings them back up to speed, and for the first time since Pullman the mirrors stay empty behind him. They are done being chased.

## The Cinderfall Anomaly

[Beat 19]
Halsted north of the tracks runs straight over the bones of Cinderfall Energy — eleven subterranean thermal installations whose only face to the world is a grid of steam-vent grates and the convection waves that ripple up off them, bending the streetlights into water.

"Why is the air doing that?" Imani asks.

"Because the corporation underneath us is cooking," Kyle says.

"Oh." Then, smaller, with all the cleverness gone out of it: "I don't like it here."

"Me neither," Kyle says, and doesn't dress it up, because that one is simply true.

He does not tell her that the air is about to do something Cinderfall has nothing whatsoever to do with. With the road behind him finally empty, his neuretics has nothing left to track but what waits up ahead — and two blocks on, the streetlights stop agreeing with one another about where they stand, and the read in his skull, which has had a clean answer ready for everything tonight, goes quiet. Quiet in the one specific way that means only one thing.

The Weather, Vance had called it.

[Beat 20]
He should turn west. West is Bridgeport, and Bridgeport is normal. He goes straight, because straight is what the call is for.

At 35th and Halsted the air changes temperature with nothing anywhere to account for it — a dry, settled cold, and underneath the cold the smell of a room sealed shut since before he was born — and the world quietly stops being a place. The headlight reaches out and touches something, and the something touches back. The world dims at its edges. His stomach drops a full foot without taking the rest of him along for it; the wheels are still on pavement, but the pavement is running half a second behind where his eyes swear it ought to be.

And then comes the worse thing. The thing the briefings never sell hard enough.

He knows this corner.

The neuretics returned a location stamp from a source it had no registry entry for — a fragment timestamped as origin: external. The hardware flagged it and waited. Kyle did not have a category for it.

He has never once been here, and he knows it anyway — knows it the way you know a hallway from a dream — one you have had your whole life and never once on purpose.

Behind him, Imani makes a sound he has never heard a child make. Not fear. Not pain.

Recognition.

[Beat 21]
It has a name on the forecasts and an exclusion flag on the maps. Kyle knows the names are a courtesy. Whatever this is, it isn't bad air and it isn't radiation. It is faintly and patiently aware of him.

His neuretics tries to map it and gets back nothing but garbage: distances that won't hold still, a clock running three directions at once, his own engine note reaching his ears a half-second late, off a street he's already left. The visual field isn't wrong, exactly — more like a page turned a quarter-second early, the scene slightly ahead of its own arrival. The buildings stand where they're supposed to stand, but their shadows run in the wrong direction, and the light from a window two floors up hits his eyes a moment before the window passes. So for once in his overclocked life he flies on the one instrument no build has ever managed to improve — the old animal at the base of the skull.

He fixes on a single streetlamp out on the far rim and keeps the only rules anyone keeps and survives. Constant speed. Eyes on the light. Don't look at the edges. Don't trust a turn — because in here, right is sometimes left, and left is sometimes down.

The air has a quality. A fullness — the sensation of a room where something large and very old has just turned around to look at you. Not hostile. Patient in a way that has no hurry in it, because it has already outlasted everything that ever had somewhere else to be. Whatever the maps call it, the name is too small for what it is.

"Don't look left," he says.

"I already wasn't," Imani says.

[Beat 22]
At the far edge of his vision something moves that shouldn't be able to: a shape carrying one shadow too many, and the extra shadow arrives a half-beat ahead of the shape that throws it. Where it passes, the air goes cold — a cold with no source — and it makes no sound at all, just a pressure in the ear like water at depth.

He does not look at it directly. Some animal floor of him knows that looking straight at the thing is not something a mind comes back from. Imani's breath catches against his back — his only warning — and he throws the bike right, which is the wrong word for the direction it actually goes. The miss should have cleared a foot. It clears less than an inch. He feels it pass like a current turning in deep water, and a line of cold opens along his ribs under the coat — deep, clean, not yet bleeding, because in here the blood runs half a second behind everything else.

Then the far lamp swims up close and steady and yellow, and the pavement grabs back at his tires all at once, and the engine note collapses into itself and becomes an engine again. The cold line under his ribs — the place it passed within an inch of — burns now, properly, the way real wounds do when the blood catches up to the moment. He does not look back. He rides.

And behind him, the borrowed calm finally fails her. Imani comes apart — all the way apart, the way an eight-year-old is built to — sobbing into his back, both small fists twisting in his coat. "It knew my name, it knew my name, I want to go home, I want to go home." And the brace at his kidney picks it up and starts to chirp, fast and climbing, because a six-hour-old heart was never built to run this hard. That, and not the thing back in the dark, is what can kill her now.

Keep her calm, Vance had said.

Kyle has no calm left in him and no time to counterfeit any, so he gives her the only thing he's got — his voice, flat and low and boring as a readout, naming the next ordinary thing and the next. The bike. The bridge coming up. The loud part she likes.

"You're out of it. It's behind us. It can't come where we're going."

The chirp slows. Slows again. Steadies. By the next block she has herself back, mostly, in the wrung-out way a kid does after the worst of it has finally let go.

"You're hurt," she says, very quietly.

"I'm fine," he says.

It is the first time he has lied to her.

[Beat 23]
Twenty-one minutes, and the mirrors have stayed empty since the viaduct. What he hasn't lost is the Loop — the one part of the city no CorpoNation gets to switch off, too public and too packed, the heart every zone plugs into to stay alive. So when it goes wrong, it goes wrong in the open. Tonight it is going wrong.

He hears it from the mouth of the tunnel: the flat clap of small-arms under a crowd-roar. Imani lifts her head off his back.

"Is that the loud?" she asks, a thread of the thin spot still in her voice.

"Different loud," he says. "Just people."

The long way costs eleven minutes he's already bleeding through, so he goes in. Halfway down the cathedral dark, a corporate security line has a municipal picket pinned behind an overturned cleaning rig — both sides too far gone to clock a motorcycle until it's the most interesting thing in the room. Kyle reaches back and Imani knows without being instructed to hand him a flashbang; he launches it with sword arm. It strikes the ceiling and goes off. The tunnel goes white, and he rides the median straight through the blind seconds — and one of those frightened people gets lucky. A round furrows the meat across the top of his shoulder, a hot graze that nearly tears the gun from his hand. He keeps the throttle. Adds the shoulder to the rib. Behind him Imani watches the rivers of light pour past overhead, not the blood she can't see.

"It's beautiful," she says.

Kyle gets his breath back around the burn before he answers, so it lands level.

"Don't tell the advertisers," he says. "It'll go to their heads."

[Beat 24]
River North is a thirty-second wedge of gray zone where the Loop's grid loses its nerve at the river. The bascule bridges are still there, but the river hasn't been managed in eighteen years; the lift machinery seized a decade back, and the spans sit permanently down and half-drowned, Lower Wacker a black canal beneath them where a road used to run. People live here anyway — laundry strung span to span, a man fishing the canal off a fixed deck with a hand line, a kid working a cooler of something cold down a line of cars that never moves. Down the throat of a stairwell that ran below the waterline, a crack in the wall gave off a faint blue-green light, the way the oldest blocks do once they've been wet long enough; the air that came up out of it was cold and close and smelled of a cellar that had made its peace with the river, and nobody passing it down here took that for anything but a comfort.

Imani turns her head to watch the water slide past underneath. "The street's drowned," she says — not scared, just filing it.

"Most of them down here are," Kyle says, and takes the upper deck of the Michigan Avenue span, where Tollgate Systems strung a credit-gate across a public ruin because someone always finds a way to charge rent on a thing nobody owns. He doesn't have a Tollgate badge. He has twelve minutes and an opinion about Tollgate. The barrier is a swinging steel boom; it folds off its hinge without comment, the least dangerous thing to happen to him all night.

What he thinks about instead is the camera reading his face across the span. Not the bike — the bike is nobody — it's him the system wants, and it bills the only identity it can match: a credit account that hasn't existed since 2207, one he built for exactly this kind of evening and then deleted. The invoice will draw an automated rejection, the rejection will be filed and archived and purged in a routine sweep — six weeks and seventeen credits of Tollgate's money chasing a ghost. He laughs, and it pulls at the cold line under his coat.

"Why are you laughing?" Imani asks.

"Because the most expensive thing I did to them tonight was the paperwork."

"Good," she says.

## Streeterville

[Beat 25]
Streeterville is a forest of glass towers wearing crowns of advertising, and at their feet sits the brutalist concrete slab of Northwestern Memorial. Tonight the whole district is packed wall to wall with people — the strike spilled north into the one part of the city nobody is allowed to lock down. Which is, of course, exactly why the strike chose to be here.

Kyle picks out the bay's red light six blocks out. And between him and it, wading through the crowd on six legs with the patience of a thing that has nowhere better in the world to be, is an Arcturus crowd-control unit. A crab the size of a panel van, matte riot-grey. A Bulwark. Arcturus builds everything into a crab eventually — low, omnidirectional, impossible to tip over — and a crab, it turns out, is what has been waiting at the end of his night all along.

Kyle has put this exact unit down twice before. He knows it's the same one because it slows the instant its optics find his face, and recalculates, and comes for him first out of a crowd of a thousand easier problems.

A machine can't hold a grudge. Kyle stopped believing that the second time.

[Beat 26]
The Bulwark is a crowd-control unit, built to work shoulder to shoulder with civilians, so it carries nothing that goes bang — no slugs, no rockets, nothing that doesn't care where it comes down. What it has instead is hands. Tanglefoot. Kinetic foam. A sonic throw that turns your inner ear to surf. And four hydraulic claws built to fold a person up neat and hold them there for collection. It wants Kyle folded up. It does not care about the kid at all, except as part of the same package.

That is the opening. It is a thin one.

Kyle frees Imani's hands from the back of his coat without setting her down — she stays on the pillion, she stays with the bike — and pulls two fat cylinders from the bandolier she's carried since the viaduct. The fledglings' own flash and chaff. Finally good for something.

"It sees in light, and it hears in sound, and it can't shoot back," he says. "Left one's flash — that blinds it. Right one's chaff — that makes it deaf and stupid. Pull the tab, count two, throw it at the face."

Imani looks at the crab the size of a panel van. Then Imani looks at the two grenades in her own two hands.

"It's a really big crab," she says, in the voice of a kid who has just been handed the best and worst job of her entire short life.

"It is," Kyle agrees. "Throw at the face."

[Beat 27]
Then everything is very loud and very fast at once.

Imani's first flash goes off a half-second early and a yard wide — and the Bulwark flinches from it anyway, optics whiting out — and Kyle is already moving, not away from the thing but under it, because the one place a crab cannot reach is the spot beneath its own belly. He lays the bike flat and slings the two of them through the gap between its legs, drawing Silence as the world tips sideways, and opens a leg out clean on the way through. The Bulwark drops a quarter of its height with a sound like a building deciding to sit down.

Then, for the first time all night, the read fails him.

Whatever the thin spot did to him at 35th, the overclock picks now to cash the bill. The light in the corner of his eye flares red, and the half-second he's leaned on since Hegewisch just doesn't come — and his body, built to arrive early, arrives late instead. The world greys at the centers. His knee buckles half a step. For one full second he is only a tired man with a sword, slow as everyone else in the world.

So he never sees the claw. It whips back over the fallen flank, closes on the bandolier across Imani's chest, and tears it loose — lifting her half a foot into the air before the strap gives and drops her hard.

Something bigger had wanted them. Exactly the way he told her it would.

But she'd pulled the chaff's tab a heartbeat before it took her, and the two-count runs out now — point-blank, in the crab's own grip, bursting in the cluster of sensors the claw reached out with. The legs stop agreeing with one another and start hunting for a floor that isn't there.

Kyle gets her down behind the engine block and puts himself between her and the machine, and finishes it the slow way — on grip and memory and the bare animal at the base of the skull, with nothing out in front of him at all. A claw widens the cut on his forearm. The bad rib tears on a blind pivot. He eats both, because he never saw them coming. That is the difference. The Bulwark folds into the intersection — blind, deaf, beached, legs working and going nowhere. The bike does not survive the dismount.

But the bay's red light is three blocks off now. And three blocks is nothing.

"Good count," he tells her.

Imani — shaking, fierce, wrung all the way out, staring at the claw that took her grenades and choked on them — says, "I know."

[Beat 28]
The bike is dead and the bay is three blocks off, and three blocks is nothing; he has bled a great deal more than this for a great deal less. He sheathes Silence across his back. Holsters Cacophony on his hip. And picks her up — harness, battery, the whole small weight of her gathered in against his chest.

Imani's hand fists in his collar. "You're hurt," she says again, and this time he doesn't bother with the lie.

"Yeah," he says.

He carries her. A red light in the distance growing larger and brighter.

[Beat 29]
Vance is already out in the lane with the gurney — tall, brown-skinned, hair tied back, scrubs hanging off a body that has clearly run a long way today — and he does not waste a single syllable on hello.

"On the gurney."

Kyle is already lowering her onto it. The harness comes off in two practiced motions. Imani's hand stays knotted in his coat a half-second after the rest of her is down, and when he gently pries it loose, she asks him the question every kid eventually has to put to somebody.

"Will I see you again?"

Kyle does not lie to her. He has been lied to by kinder people than himself, and it never once helped.

"No," he says. "Probably not."

She takes it the way she has taken everything else tonight — straight, no flinch, filing it away somewhere. Good, he thinks. Better she learns it now, while she's still young enough for the scar to set smooth: that sometimes the person who comes for you is a stranger, who does the thing, and then is gone. He learned it late himself. It set crooked.

"Thank you for the loud," she says.

"Anytime, kid," he says — the gentlest lie he has told all night, and the only one she lets him keep. "Tell the heart I said hi."

[Beat 30]
Vance reads the brace, the wires, the cooler — and then reads him. The hand pressed flat to his side. The dark spreading at the forearm and the shoulder. The boot that won't quite take his full weight.

"Forty-one minutes," he says. "I had you at fifty-five."

"You bet against me?"

"I bet the over. Paid for the gurney you just put her on." Vance snaps his fingers twice at a trauma nurse, points at Imani, and the bay swallows her the way good bays do — fast, and from all sides at once. "Eleven years, Kyle. You keep bringing me work in worse shape than the patients."

"I cheated," Kyle says.

"You're bleeding into my ambulance lane," Vance says. "Stay. Half the people in my bay tonight are in better shape than you are."

Kyle is already turning away. "Don't worry about me," he says. "I'll just rub some dirt in it."

[Beat 31]
He walks.

The bay door drops shut behind him and its red light goes white, which is the hospital's quiet way of saying they have her now — which is the only confirmation he was ever going to get that the run worked. No alarm follows him out into the street. No name. Whatever he tore open tonight to get her here has already closed up seamless behind him, like it was never there at all.

[Beat 32]
He turns south, on foot, the long way, because south is no longer urgent and there's no bike left to make it quick. The read has come back — slow, shaky, but back. The white that rode the edges of his sight all night is pulling away, the bleed at his nose gone tacky and dry. The body, at least, has stopped sending up its bills for the evening.

The bad news keeps its own count. Where a civilian rig would show a battery bar, the build in his skull shows one small light. Every time he runs the read past the red, that light goes red with it. He doesn't look at it while he's working. He looks now, and counts what he can stand of tonight's — and the thin spot alone is most of it.

Then the read, freshly back and not yet asked, hands him something he didn't request: a board notification, pulled off the open band as he walks. New posting, south side. Escort work, clean spec, full retainer. No broker. No client that resolves to anything.

A kill team that never asked why their contract carried only a description and no name. A job too clean to have a careless human behind it. And now this — arriving the hour he finished, like a receipt.

He has been seeing the shape for months. He has a word for it now. He puts the word in the drawer next to the buyers' list and the relay logs and the other things he has not finished deciding about, and he declines the posting, and walks south through the rain, and lets whoever is watching watch him do it.
