# Lieutenant Lyra, Sinterkin Slayer

_At the edge of the map, a lieutenant four months into her first command reads a killing ground the way she reads everything: for what does and doesn't add up.

A girl is missing from the frontier village of Aldford, her crown found mangled at the wood's edge. Lieutenant Lyra takes her unit up to the ford expecting a straightforward kill — and gets one: a five-strong pack and a fourteen-foot horror that speaks directly into her mind mid-fight, offering her exactly what she wants most — the death of the sergeant who's made her life miserable, and a command no one will ever question. She refuses in the same breath she kills it.

But the evidence at the kill site doesn't add up to a girl actually taken. No blood. No cloth. Just a crown — and a trail that was staged. What Lyra finds when she follows the real one is smaller, sadder, and far more human than the monster she just put down: two young people who chose a fabricated death over a marriage neither of them wanted, and a truth she decides, on her own authority, not to bring home.

A tightly-coiled Entos standalone — soft with children, loyal to a man who doesn't deserve it, merciless with monsters, and merciful with people, often within the same page._

[Beat 1]
Aldford was the kind of village that existed because the ford did - stone and thatch on the safe bank, close enough to the garrison's watch fires to sleep easy, far enough from the treeline that most of its children had never seen a harrower and meant to keep it that way.

Officially the garrison held the last mapped mile of Calyx's own frontier. Unofficially, everyone from the miller to the youngest recruit understood that Calyx's maps and the world shared very little past the ford, and called what began on the far bank the Untamed - plainly, the way you'd name a dog you didn't trust, so it couldn't surprise you by living up to it.

That week Aldford smelled of woodsmoke, pressed apple, and the marigold and late heather the girls were weaving into crowns for the Crowning.

The Crowning fell at first hard frost, when the year's crop of children old enough to be promised in marriage - or old enough to refuse it, though the songs oversold how often that happened - stood in the square and had a wildflower crown set on their head by the village's youngest child. A child gives it, the saying went, because a child is the last one still permitted to believe the world means well by you. Lyra had heard the saying three times that week and had not once asked what became of the belief afterward.

She came down to the square the morning before the rite because Baast said the miller's wife had been asking after the Templar by name, and a Templar who let a village ask after her twice was doing something wrong.

A child met her at the well before she'd gone twenty paces - Sage, six, missing a tooth she was proud of and a mother who kept losing track of her - with a crown of her own making, lopsided, one marigold already dropping its head. She held it up with both hands like it weighed something.

"For you," Sage said, in the tone of a transaction, not a gift, "so the wolves don't get you."

"There aren't wolves this far south."

"For the other things, then."

Lyra crouched to let the girl set it on her, which took some doing given the girl's aim and Lyra's height, and didn't tell her the crown wouldn't do a thing against a harrower's reach. She thanked her the way she'd once thanked Vega, badly, over ruined marigolds in a kitchen garden three hundred miles and a lifetime from any ford, with the same specific gravity she'd have used thanking a superior officer, which seemed to satisfy the girl more than any softness would have.

Across the square, the year's crop was gathering for the fitting - five of them this year, and Heather Marsh stood tallest among them, seventeen, in a dress her mother had let out at the seams twice that autumn, her first Crowning as a woman grown and not a child playing at one. Beside her stood Corwin Vell, her intended, a young man handsome in the specific symmetrical way that made strangers stop in the street and then feel briefly foolish for having stopped. He held Heather's hand with the correct amount of warmth and looked, without seeming to notice he was doing it, toward a knot of young men unloading firewood by the mill.

The drift toward the mill snagged in her attention and didn't sit right, though she couldn't yet say why. She set it aside the way she set aside everything that wasn't currently trying to kill her, and went back up to the treeline before the frost burned off, which at the edge of Calyx in late autumn meant she went up and it didn't.

[Beat 2]
The runner came up from Aldford before the frost burned off the second morning, which meant someone had been climbing in the dark, which meant it was already bad.

Heather Marsh had not come home from the Crowning fires.

Osgood Marsh met them at the wood's edge already grey with it, his wife Enid past speaking, and it was Baast - not Lyra - who found the crown first, snagged low in a hawthorn where the path gave out into scrub: marigold and heather, the twin of the one Sage had set on Lyra's head two mornings before, except this one was crushed flat on one side and black with something that wasn't blood, though it wanted to be mistaken for blood at a glance.

Ichor. Old iron and rot, the same smell that came off a kin-pack an hour before you saw one.

The ground told the first half of a story easily enough - drag marks, a scuffed heel, a handprint too shallow to hold - and Lyra crouched over all of it for longer than she needed to, because something in the read didn't sit the way a taking usually sat. She didn't have the word for what was wrong yet. It matched something she'd felt at the well. She went on.

Corwin Vell stood a little apart from Heather's parents, correctly grieved, his hand resting once, briefly, on Enid's shoulder before he withdrew it as though remembering he was owed the gesture rather than moved to it. He asked Lyra twice, evenly, whether there would be anything to recover. He did not ask whether Heather had suffered. That omission sat next to everything else that didn't add up, unresolved.

"Kin sign runs up toward the ford," Baast said, low, so the family wouldn't hear the count. "Same ground we've been watching. If something's holding the leash up there -"

"Then it's already had a full night's head start on a girl it dragged uphill through hawthorn." Lyra stood. "That's not how a kin-pack carries anything. They don't carry. They run it down where it falls."

"Maybe it didn't run her down."

"Maybe." She didn't believe it and didn't say so. "You're with me as far as the treeline. Nobody else past the hawthorn line - I don't need this sign taken apart by boots before I've read it."

Ferris, nineteen and trying not to look at the crown, said nothing. It was the kind of silence she'd come to expect from him, and still trust.

She took the crushed crown with her. Not for sentiment. Evidence didn't stay evidence long once a village decided to grieve on top of it.

[Beat 3]
Lieutenant Lyra was twenty-five, four months into the posting, and she still weighed her own rank on her tongue like a coin that might be counterfeit. She had asked for a garrison this small, out at the edge of Entos, where the maps stopped naming things and started describing them instead. Nobody believed she'd asked for it. On the bad mornings, neither did she.

Sergeant Osric Baast had buried two lieutenants before her and made no secret of budgeting for a third. He'd worked out early that the fastest way to remind a twenty-five-year-old officer of her place was to make her wait for every word he gave her, so he made her wait, every time, on principle, and didn't trouble himself to explain the habit to somebody he didn't expect to keep.

Baast's count from the hawthorn line held all the way up: kin sign thickening toward the ford, four sets, maybe five, moving with a coordination that still didn't sit right against everything the manuals said about kin behaving like scattered vermin instead of a directed pack. "Kin don't run in fives unless something bigger's holding the leash," she'd told him at first light, over the crushed crown still in her hand, and he hadn't argued, which from Baast was its own kind of agreement.

She took two out with her toward the treeline. Baast, because whatever else he was, he'd forgotten more about harrowers before breakfast than she was likely to learn in a year, and pretending otherwise would have gotten someone killed. And Tam Ferris, nineteen, six weeks into a garrison coat that still smelled of the tailor's chalk. Not because Ferris was ready. Because he needed to watch one done right before the morning came when he would have to do it wrong, and alone, the way they all eventually did.

They left before dawn, into cold that hadn't broken yet.

The track down to the water ran through birch scrub gone black with wet. Ferris talked for the first half mile - his sister, a debt, the weight of the coat - then stopped on his own without being told. She liked him better for it. The cold at the bottom of the draw was a different cold. It came up out of the ground rather than down out of the sky, and it got into the boot leather, and it stayed.

At the treeline above the ford she raised a fist and both men stopped without a sound. Baast from twenty years of practice. Ferris from six weeks of copying Baast, which was not the same thing but would do.

Below them the water ran low and quick over grey stone. The ice shelved out from both banks and was broken through the middle, and the break was too wide for one set of shoulders. She counted the sign in the mud twice. Four narrow. One that was not narrow.

"I go in. You hold this line unless I come back wrong, or don't come back."

Ferris opened his mouth. Baast set a hand on the boy's arm without looking at him, and that was the end of that.

Baast rarely argued anymore, not because he'd warmed to her, but because arguing would have meant admitting he wanted her to come back, and he still expected to bury her before that admission became necessary.

The first of the kin found her at the ford, and it made the mistake of announcing itself.

She heard the ice crack as it came off the bank, turned, and took it across the snout with the flat of her blade - not a killing stroke, just enough to see what she was dealing with. Membrane-grey hide, too many joints in the forelegs, eyes the pale yellow of old fat. It shook its head, which was the wrong response, and she hit it again with the edge, which was the right tool for what followed.

Three kin. Then two more, from the treeline in sequence, moving like one thing - which they hadn't planned, kin didn't plan - but the membrane does something to group instinct. She killed four of them in the time it took to cross the ford. The fifth got her left shoulder, not deeply, the pauldron caught most of it, but she'd feel it in the cold for days. She killed the fifth against the bank and stepped back to check her footing.

The harrower was in the tree line. She could smell it before she saw it: old iron and something beneath iron, the particular rot-stink of membrane-heavy tissue, the kind that doesn't fully decompose because the membrane has replaced the biology with something it didn't bother to translate cleanly. The smell came in off the creek and sat in the back of her throat.

She heard it breathe.

Not loudly - that was the wrong thing to expect from harrowers. The size of them, you expected loud. What you got was low, a sound that lived under hearing, that she felt in her back teeth and the base of her spine before she registered hearing anything at all. The creek stones ahead of her fogged, a thin vapor rising where the cold came off it before the thing had moved at all.

It stepped out of the tree line.

She spent three seconds looking at it. That was all the time she had.

It was fourteen feet at the shoulder, maybe more, grey as the stone and water around it - which was probably how it had survived to that size. It moved wrong. Not badly - efficiently, the extra joints doing work she couldn't account for. Each foot came down with enough weight to crack ice she'd crossed without cracking it.

It looked at her.

The eyes did it. Yellow, like the kin, but too large, and the membrane-glow in them pulsed with something like a heartbeat that matched nothing else she could track.

It would have been an easy thing to become one of them. She didn't.

It could help her, Lieutenant. That was the offer, though nothing out there had a mouth to make it.

It wasn't a sound. It arrived the way a draft arrives through a door you hadn't noticed was open, already inside her before she'd registered anything had crossed the distance between them, and it had shape enough to be words though nothing out there had moved to make them.

She needed no help from the likes of it. That much was reflex, not conversation - the same reflex that already had her weight shifting onto the balls of her feet.

Baast didn't want her here, it went on. He never had. Spare it, and it would be done with him before he finished deciding her sergeant's next silence was worth the breath - and she would walk home the only officer left standing on this line. No one questioned a lieutenant who outlived what killed her sergeant.

All of it fit inside the same three seconds she'd already spent looking at it, because that was apparently all the room the thing needed. She spent the rest of them picturing a garrison without Baast in it, and didn't like the picture any better than she liked him.

No.

She adjusted her grip and walked toward it.

The trick with harrowers - the thing no one learned until they'd been through a dozen of the smaller ones, that the Old Watch manuals mentioned once and then refused to elaborate on - was that the reach was the problem, not the weight. The weight was manageable, eventually, when it came down. The reach was what killed you. Long neck, longer forelegs than you'd estimated, the jaw coming in from angles you'd planned wrong. You had to be inside the reach. All the way inside. It was uncomfortable. The alternative was worse.

She went in at an angle, sword low, slipped inside the first jaw-sweep, and drove the pommel hard into the joint where the foreleg met the shoulder. The harrower came down on that side - a controlled fall, not a stumble - and she was already under the neck, blade reversing, driving the point up through the soft tissue at the underside of the jaw and through the palate into the skull.

She had to brace both feet against the neck to get the leverage.

The harrower made a sound she had no name for - the kind that kept almost becoming something else and never quite did.

Then it stopped making any sound at all.

She stood there a moment in the silence - just the creek and the crows lifting from the trees above - looked at what she'd done, and thought: that's a lot of head to carry.

She checked the bodies one by one while the crows worked up their courage - four kin, the harrower, nothing else. No cloth. No boot. No blood that wasn't animal. A kin-pack that ran a girl down through hawthorn and killed her somewhere between the village and the ford should have left more of her behind than a crushed crown, even accounting for what the crows and the cold water would have taken by now.

It should have left more of her behind than nothing.

She didn't go down to the garrison. She went back the way she'd come, slower this time, reading the ground instead of crossing it.

[Beat 4]
The hawthorn line told two stories if you were willing to crouch in the mud long enough to find the second one, and Lyra was.

The first story was the one everyone had already agreed to: drag marks, a scuffed heel, the crushed crown, kin sign thickening toward the ford. It held together right up until it didn't - right up to the point, forty yards short of where the kin trail properly started, where the drag marks simply stopped being drag marks.

No heel-scuff past that point. No weight dragged, no weight carried. Just two sets of boot prints, side by side, evenly spaced, walking - not running, not stumbling, not being pulled by anything - off at an angle that took them away from the ford entirely, toward the low saddle that led, eventually, to the Millbrook road.

One set was Heather's height. The other was taller, broader through the stride, cutting fresh through bracken that hadn't been touched by anyone in a hurry.

Lyra crouched over the place where the two stories split and did not, for a moment, do anything at all.

Then she went back to the kin, because the kin were still telling her something and she hadn't finished listening.

The fifth kin - the one that had come at her out of sequence, the one the pauldron had eaten the worst of - still wore a lead around its neck when she rolled it over. Not garrison rope. Not anything a hunter carried for game this size, because nobody hunted kin for game. A belt. Decent leather, the buckle stamped with a tooling pattern she'd seen on exactly one kind of goods in Aldford's market stalls - the kind that came upriver from Millbrook, where the leatherworkers cured hide a shade darker than Aldford's own tannery managed.

No other kin wore one. Not the harrower. Not the other four.

One kin. One lead. One young man's belt, worn thin at the second hole the way a belt wears when its owner has lost weight recently, or grown lean on nerves, or both.

She sat back on her heels at the ford's edge with a dead thing's collar in her hand and asked herself the only question she trusted - not why, never why, that wasn't hers to solve - just what it would have taken, mechanically, for it to be true.

Let it go at the wood's edge. Let it run for the only thing it knew how to run for - its own kind, its own harrower, upstream where the real sign already was - and let its tracks braid into a story that told itself, given a crushed crown and a smear of its own blood, without anyone having to lie out loud.

It was, Lyra thought, a better piece of tactical thinking than half the garrison could manage sober.

She left the belt on the dead kin. It wasn't hers to take, and there was nothing written on it that a parent would recognize as their daughter's hand.

The two sets of boot prints were still there when she stood, walking off at their unhurried angle toward the Millbrook road, in no more hurry now than they'd been an hour ago. That was the part she couldn't close on her own - whether a plan this careful had accounted for the one variable it couldn't control, which was her.

She followed the second path instead of turning back toward the garrison. Not to bring anyone back. Only a fool marched into a conclusion without checking whether the ground agreed with it first.

[Beat 5]
The second trail didn't run either. It walked the whole way - past the low saddle, along a brook she didn't have a name for - until it stopped being a trail at all and became two people sitting under a willow with their boots off, drying bare feet in what was left of the daylight, as if the last day and a half hadn't happened to anyone.

Heather Marsh looked up first. Whatever she saw in Lyra's face took what color she had left.

The boy beside her came up onto one knee before he'd worked out there wasn't a version of this where standing helped him. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Hands too calloused for someone who'd spent the morning doing nothing more dangerous than walking.

"Sit down," Lyra said. Neither of them did. She hadn't expected them to.

She didn't raise her voice. A raised voice let people believe the anger would pass. "You leashed a sinterspawn. Tame or not, young or not, that's a mouth that takes a man's arm off at the elbow before he's finished being surprised by it. You walked it through hawthorn in the dark, close enough to a live pack that it could have turned around and come back through both of you instead of running home. And you did that so I'd spend a morning inside a harrower's reach confirming a story you built out of your own blood - while your mother stood at the wood's edge not speaking, because she already knew what a crown like that meant."

"We knew the ford sign was already there," the boy said. "We didn't put you in it. That fight was going to be walked either way."

"That's not the part I asked you to answer for."

Neither of them answered for it, and the silence told her plenty on its own.

"How," Lyra said. Not why - she'd already told them why didn't interest her. "How does a boy your age leash a live sinterspawn and keep the arm he leashed it with."

Teague answered like a man who'd been waiting for someone competent enough to ask the right question. "They go home. Whatever they are when you catch them small, whatever they turn into after - they still go home. Every one I've ever taken apart or watched taken apart, first thing it does hurt or scared, it runs for whatever it answers to. I didn't teach mine anything. I just never let it forget where home was supposed to be, and then I pointed it there myself."

"You've done this before."

"Once. Not like this." Something went out of his face that hadn't been performance a moment ago. "Three weeks back, checking a trapline past the second treeline, I met the thing that hound used to answer to. Talks. Not out loud - you feel it try to get in behind your eyes, the way you'd feel someone's hand on your shoulder before you saw them. It spent the better part of an hour telling me it wasn't what I thought it was. That the membrane wasn't a sickness, it was a gift nobody had asked it to stop giving. That everything it touched, it was only trying to finish."

"And you believed it."

"I believed it wanted me to." His jaw worked once. He shook his head once, hard, like a man throwing off water, and didn't say anything else about it.

"Fourteen feet at the shoulder," Lyra said. "Too many joints in the forelegs. Eyes that pulsed instead of blinked. Something that got in behind my own eyes for about as long as it takes to draw one breath, and wanted something from me that wasn't my death."

Teague went very still. "That's it. That's exactly it."

"It's dead. I put it down at the ford this morning, the same as four kin that came with it - yours among them, near as I can tell, once its lead ran out and it went home to the only home it had left to go to." She said the rest of it plainly, because there wasn't a way to say it that made it smaller. She'd had three seconds and had spent them reading a reach and a stance instead of whatever it had actually been trying to say, because reading a reach and a stance was the only kind of reading that kept a person alive at that range. That didn't make the choice go away. It just meant she'd made it before she'd known there was one to make, and would have made it again the same way, knowing.

Lyra tried to set the rest of it aside, the way she set aside everything that wasn't actively trying to kill her. It didn't take - not all the way, not this time.

"The hounds," she said.

"Used to be dogs." He said it flatly, the same recitation-voice Heather would use a minute later for her own worst fact. "I knew one of them. Miller's old bitch, the one that used to run the cart road before she went missing last spring - I know her by the notch out of the left ear, still there under everything else it grew instead. Whatever's in the membrane doesn't build new things when it can take something that already knows how to run and hunt and love a person, and just - finish it differently." He looked, for the first time, like he wanted to be somewhere else more than he wanted to be here with Heather. "So no. Not all monsters, if you want to split it that fine. But that one wasn't lonely and it wasn't misunderstood. It was a corruption in a shape smart enough to argue for itself, and now it's a shape on the ground at the ford instead, and I can't say I'm sorry for it."

She let the quiet sit until Heather broke it, which took less time than Lyra had expected from a girl who'd apparently spent a week planning nothing else.

"If I'd stood up in that square and said his name instead of taking the crown," Heather said, "I'd have been shorn in front of the whole ford by the second bell, and my father would have spent the rest of his life paying back three times a bride-gift he never had a third of to begin with. That's not a story where I get to leave quietly with anyone. That's a story where the mill goes to Corwin's family by winter and I go with it shorn, so everyone downriver knows what an oath-breaker looks like buying her own flour." She said it flat, a recitation - the kind of thing a person only delivers like that once they've already said it to themselves a hundred times, alone. "This way nobody pays for anything. Sinterspawn don't send a bill."

The boy hadn't moved his hand from hers the whole time. Teague, Lyra would learn - Teague Wynn, out of Millbrook - and never anything more of him than that.

She had taken her own vow young too - seventeen, the same year Heather stood in that square - except nobody had measured her for a betrothal first, and she had never once had to fake her own death to keep the life she'd chosen. It was not a small one.

Lyra looked at the two of them a long moment, and didn't like what she saw any better than she had crouched over a belt.

"You understand I could walk you both back to that yard right now," she said. "Nobody would stop me. It's the kind of thing a Templar's oath actually covers."

"I understand it," Heather said, and didn't look away.

Lyra stood. The cold had found her shoulder again, the old ache the fifth kin had left there, and she rolled it once before she spoke.

"Then get off Calyx ground before some Templar with less patience finds this brook. Millbrook doesn't report its own missing to a garrison that isn't looking for them. Keep it that way."

She was three steps up the bank when Heather said her name - not Lieutenant, not Templar, the name nobody outside the garrison used for her anymore. Lyra didn't turn around.

"Thank you," Heather said, "for not writing us down."

Lyra kept walking. It was as close as either of them was going to get to saying the rest of it out loud, and it would have to be enough.

[Beat 6]
She came in through the north gate at the seventh hour with the harrower's head lashed to her pack by a rope through the nostrils, dragging behind her on the frozen ground because there was no dignified way to move something that large, four kin-pelts bundled under her arm, a belt she hadn't mentioned to anyone folded small in an inside pocket, and Sage's crown still riding crushed under the edge of her pauldron, where it had been since the well and she'd never once had a free hand to take it off.

The crows on the gate posts lifted. The ones already in the yard didn't; they'd found the blood trail an hour before she had and were making the most of it.

Osgood and Enid Marsh were waiting in the yard with the kind of stillness that had stopped being patience sometime around dawn. Corwin Vell stood a half pace behind them, dressed as correctly as he had been at the hawthorn line, his composure by now less a performance than a habit he'd worn long enough to forget was one.

She dropped the head in the center of the yard. It hit the stone like a dropped millstone.

Enid made a sound that wasn't a word. Osgood held his wife up with one arm and asked, in the flat, careful voice of a man who had already decided he could survive one more true answer that day, whether there'd been enough of her left to give the sept a name to bury.

Lyra looked at the ford water still drying black under her nails and told him there wasn't.

It was the only lie she told that day, and the only one she meant to keep telling.

Corwin didn't ask a second question. He put his arm around Enid's shoulders the way a well-brought-up young man was supposed to, and over the top of her head his eyes went, once, to a stableboy pretending very hard to be busy with a bridle by the gate - the same drift she'd noticed at the Crowning, the same absence of hunger a real grief for a real wife-to-be should have carried. She set it aside with the rest of what wasn't hers to fix, and let him keep his correct, empty face. Whatever he was guarding behind it wasn't going to cost this yard a second lie today. One was already more than enough for anyone here to carry.

Somewhere upriver past Millbrook a boy who'd never been reported missing was walking a road that didn't run past any garrison, with a girl whose crown nobody would ever find because it had gone the whole way to Millbrook still on her head, and Lyra intended to keep it that way for exactly as long as it took the two of them to become somebody else's problem to marry off.

"Is that all of it?" the watch captain asked, toeing the head.

"Write it up," she said, and went inside.
