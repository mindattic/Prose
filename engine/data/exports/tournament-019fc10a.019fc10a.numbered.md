# Tournament

_For three days a year, Three Points forgets there's a war. A landless Pallor axeman fighting to buy his sister's freedom is paired with an Atrament rig pilot for the tournament of a lifetime — and stumbles into a plot to kill the one man both their Houses need alive._

[Beat 1]
The coast road empties into Three Points the way it does every truce season - carts creaking under bunting nobody's bothered to take down since last time, a fiddle somewhere getting tuned badly, the smell of tar and roasting meat rolling out to meet the road before the gate itself comes into view. Men who'll be trying to kill each other again inside the week walk the same rutted track shoulder to shoulder, unbothered, the way they do every three years.

The boards had already failed him. This is the next thing.

The gate at Three Points is timber and cable, and it does not care who you are.

Kaeric comes off the coast road with the war-axe slung across his back, no cloak clasp, no banner-pin, nothing at his collar to say whose man he is. The toll-master at the near post has a ledger chained to his belt and does not look up until Kaeric's shadow falls across the page.

Coin, he says.

Kaeric puts the coin down. Copper, mostly. A few silver marks worn thin at the rim.

The toll-master counts it with two fingers, sliding each piece off the pile like he's done it ten thousand times, because he has. He looks up once. At the collar. At the bare wrists. At the axe.

No House? he asks.

Not a question. Kaeric doesn't answer it like one.

No.

The toll-master marks something in the ledger anyway — a tally column with no crest beside it, the way you'd log a stray dog that still has to pay for the road same as the hounds. He waves Kaeric through without another word.

Past the post the fair opens up the way it always does, sudden and too loud, like walking into a wall of sound after the quiet coast road.

Verlaine blue-and-silver hangs off the merchant stalls on the left. Atrament's black iron sigil flies not thirty feet from it, over a forge-master's tent selling coupling grease and file sets. A Pallor banner, faded fjord-grey, snaps in the wind between them both, close enough that the ropes nearly cross.

Soldiers walk past each other close enough to touch shoulders. A Verlaine man in gate-livery laughs at something an Atrament quartermaster says, both of them holding cups from the same wine-seller. Three days from now some of these men will be putting spears through each other on the Rhine line and calling it liberation, or calling it defense, depending which bank they wake up on.

Here they haggle over the price of dried fish.

*Three days of this. Then the river again.*

Kaeric has seen it enough seasons running. It catches behind his sternum anyway.

He walks past a Pallor recruiting table with no flag of his own to plant on it. A boy selling roasted nuts calls out a price without looking at his collar, without caring, because at Three Points a copper mark spends the same no matter whose banner you didn't bring.

That should feel like relief. It has, other seasons.

Today he keeps his hand flat over the pouch at his belt and walks on.

[Beat 2]
The customs-house throws a long shadow across the boards even at midday, the timber gone black with damp and old grease. Kaeric stands in it because he stood in it not an hour ago, because the coin in his pouch is the same coin, because nothing about the number should have changed twice in one morning.

It has changed anyway.

The old figure is still there if he looks close — a paler ghost of chalk under the fresh line, the shape of a number he'd memorized on the walk in. The new one sits over it, thicker, pressed hard enough to gouge the wood grain. Higher by a margin no surcharge should account for.

Someone scrubbed it and wrote it again. That is not a clerk's mistake.

He counts it twice. The second count doesn't fix the first.

A clerk different from the last one works the board with a rag and a chalk-stick, updating a name three columns down. Younger. Ink on both hands, not just the writing one.

Kaeric puts a finger to the wood, under Sennah's name, not touching the number.

This one's wrong.

The clerk doesn't turn around.

Numbers don't come from me.

They come from somewhere.

Sinter office, the clerk says, still not turning. Recalculates the surcharge every fair-week. Something about yield on the quarantine stock. I just write what they send down.

*Yield. Like she's a crop.*

Kaeric doesn't say that part. He says the other part, the one that costs him less.

When was it sent down?

This morning.

The clerk finally turns, and there's no cruelty in the face, which is worse somehow than if there had been. Just a boy doing sums for coin, the same as anyone.

You want to lodge a complaint, there's a window for that. Third tent past the quartermaster's. Line's long, though. Everyone's board went up today.

Everyone's board. Not hers, picked out. Not aimed at anyone at all.

It does not help.

Kaeric looks at the number again.

Coin he has, against coin he needs — the gap wider now than it was an hour ago, and the hour before that it had already been too wide.

He could stand in the complaint line and get told, politely, by someone else with clean sums, that the quarantine costs money and the money comes from somewhere and today it comes from her.

He could walk to the Bond Market instead, where debts change hands for reasons that have nothing to do with fairness, where a man with an axe and a name for himself in the Pit might find someone willing to talk price instead of policy.

Neither one puts coin in his pouch. Both of them cost time he didn't plan to spend.

His jaw sets, unbidden, before he's decided anything — the same tightening that comes before the rage discipline, the breath he hasn't started yet gathering low in his chest like a held door.

Not here. Not at a chalk board.

He breathes it back down, one long pull through the nose, the way his father taught him before he ever swung the axe for coin instead of for the house that never gave him one.

The clerk has already turned back to his column, waiting for Kaeric to be someone else's problem.

Kaeric reads the number one more time, like memorizing a wound before you let the tincture close it.

Then he turns from the board, toward the Pit.

[Beat 3]
The ore comes down the chute in fists of black-red rock, and Sennah Dunmoor's job is to know which fists are worth the furnace and which are worth nothing.

Her hands do it before her eyes finish looking. Weight first. A good chunk sits heavy for its size, dense enough that the wrist knows before the mind does. Color second, the rust-dark vein against the dull grey waste, though the color lies half the time and the weight almost never does.

She sorts. Left hand to the good bin, right hand to the waste chute, and the chute never stops feeding no matter which hand is busy.

Fourteen days on this line and the callus still hasn't set right on the left thumb.

The furnace room breathes heat at her from thirty feet away, a wall of it that never drops even when the doors are open to the yard. Sweat runs down between her shoulder blades and pools at the belt of her smock, and the air tastes like hot iron filings, a taste she has stopped noticing except in the mornings, when it hits new again for the first ten breaths.

Her hands know the ore. Her back knows the bench. Her feet know the exact worn groove in the floor planking where four other women have stood before her doing this same sort, going back longer than anyone will say out loud.

She does not let herself think about time in hours. She thinks in fists of ore instead — so many fists to the bin before the noon bell, so many after.

A chunk comes down heavier than it looks. She takes it in both hands to be sure, feels the dense pull of it, good ore, and drops it in the left bin with the flat clack of rock on rock.

The woman beside her, older, gap-toothed, missing two fingers on the sorting hand from some year before Sennah arrived, leans in without breaking her own rhythm.

You hear the tally this morning?

Sennah doesn't look up. Looking up costs a fist of ore's worth of time.

Which tally?

Sinter surcharge. Went up again. Whole board, they're saying, not just ours.

It always goes up. It has never once gone down.

Sennah's hands don't stop moving. Stopping is the one thing the foreman watches for, more than slow hands or wrong sorts. A mark against the day's count runs the debt one day longer.

How much?

Enough that old Ferrin did the sum twice and didn't like it the second time either.

The older woman shifts a chunk of waste rock into the chute with the heel of her palm, the missing fingers curling automatically to protect the stumps from the belt edge, a motion so practiced it looks like nothing at all.

Sennah does her own sum, the one she does every night by the light through the dormitory shutter slats. She has done it since the day the clerk read her indenture terms aloud and she understood that quarantine costs get passed downhill.

*If the surcharge climbs at the same rate three more weeks running, the debt outlives the ore.*

She doesn't say that part. Saying it costs breath.

Heard something else, the older woman asks, quieter now, pitched under the grind of the belt so the foreman's man by the door won't catch it. About your brother.

Sennah's hands stop. One half-second, no more, a chunk of good ore held too long in the left palm before she remembers herself and drops it in the bin.

What about him?

Word came through with the fish cart, the one runs the fair road. Big man, axe, no banner-pin — signed up under a hired flag for the Duel-teams at Three Points. Name close enough to yours that the cart driver asked around.

Kaeric. Nobody else fights with an axe and no house to call his own.

The rumor sits in her chest like a coal she can't decide whether to bank or let go cold. She does not let her face answer either way. The foreman's men have learned to read hope on an indentured woman, and they use it.

Could be some other man with an axe, she says, and keeps her hands moving, weight first, color second, left bin, right chute. Common enough trade.

Could be, the older woman says, in the tone of someone who doesn't believe it either but has decided not to push.

The belt keeps feeding. Sennah keeps sorting, fists of ore ticking down toward the noon bell. She does not say the name out loud.

[Beat 4]
The Pit throws its own sound across the fair — a low roar from a chalk-ringed square, men three-deep at the ropes, a bookmaker's boy running a slate up and down the line.

Kaeric stands at the outer ring and does not go in yet.

Someone at that customs-house board wrote her figure twice in one week. That is not a fair-week recalculation. That is a hand that knows her name.

He turns back toward the customs-house before the thought finishes forming.

The same clerk works the board, rag in one hand, chalk-stick in the other, three names further down the column now.

Kaeric plants his finger beside Dunmoor's line again.

When did this figure move?

The clerk doesn't look up.

Told you already. This morning.

Before this morning?

Now the clerk looks up, and there's the first flicker of something behind the boy's eyes — not fear, just the particular weariness of a person doing sums for a man who won't accept the sum.

You asking if someone singled her out?

I'm asking when the number changed.

The clerk sets the chalk down, wipes both ink-stained hands on the rag, buys himself a second he doesn't need.

It moved eight days back. Same week Sinter re-ran the quarantine yield. It moved again today. Same reason.

Same reason?

The clerk's voice picks up a little speed now, the cadence of a man reciting something he's said forty times since dawn.

Every board with an Atrament quarantine holding took the same jump. Not hers alone. Not picked out. Look at the column next to hers if you don't believe me — Halstead's girl, Meraq's boy, six names under the same recalculation. It's the whole block of holdings. Not her.

Kaeric looks down the column himself now, and there it is — name after name, the same fresh chalk, the same gouged pressure in the wood, a ledger's width of other people's daughters and sons all carrying the same new weight this morning.

So there's no complaint to lodge?

The clerk almost looks sorry.

Complaint window's for clerical error. This isn't error. This is policy. You can stand in that line all afternoon and they'll tell you the same thing I just did, only slower, and charge you a copper for the privilege of hearing it twice.

Kaeric says nothing for a moment.

His jaw works once, the old tightening, and he lets the breath go out through his nose before it can become anything else.

How much further, he asks, and his own voice sounds strange to him, flatter than he meant it.

The clerk turns the board slightly, so the lamp catches the fresh figure clean.

Kaeric reads it.

The gap that was already too wide this morning has widened again. Enough that two rounds in the Pit no longer close it. Enough that he needs the third — the House Champion round, the one he wasn't sure his body would survive before the number moved.

*I built the count around two silver and four. I didn't build it around needing all six.*

The clerk has already turned back to his rag and his column, done with him, on to the next name that needs its weight written down.

Kaeric stands there one breath longer than the boy expects him to.

Then he turns from the board a second time, toward the Pit.

[Beat 5]
The broker's tent is canvas the color of old bruises, and the man behind the slate table wears House Pallor grey the way a soldier wears it after the war stops caring about him. No banner over the flap. Just a hand-lettered sign: PRIVATE PAPER, INDENTURE CLASS ONLY.

Kaeric doesn't sit. He puts the whole purse on the slate, all of it, the copper and the two silver he'd been saving in case the fair itself demanded a toll he hadn't planned for.

This. For Dunmoor's line. Whatever's owed.

The broker doesn't count it. Doesn't need to — his eyes have already priced the leather it's sitting in.

You know the name.

Doesn't matter if I know the name. Coin spends the same either way.

The broker picks up the purse, weighs it once in his palm like a man testing an egg for a crack, and sets it back down. Not pocketed. Not refused.

I could take this off you and you'd walk out feeling robbed either way, he says. Doesn't change the paper. Dunmoor's line is Atrament charter, not fair charter. Locked to quarantine status.

Locked how?

Fixed figure till Sinter's declared closed. Full stop. Doesn't move for coin, doesn't move for a good story, doesn't move for a face I recognize from the Pit, which yours is, for what that's worth.

And when does Sinter get declared closed?

The broker looks at him the way a man looks at someone who has asked whether the tide comes in on a schedule he can bribe.

It doesn't. Not this year. Not the one after.

*Not a number. A wall shaped like a number.*

Kaeric's hand is already back on the purse before he's decided to move it.

Someone set that charter. Someone can unset it?

Atrament did. Over their own dead in the quarantine zone, near as I understand it. Not a policy they revisit for coin from a stranger with an axe.

I'm not asking them to revisit it for a stranger.

No, the broker says, and there's no edge in it, which is the part that lands wrong. You're asking for someone. That's worse leverage, not better. Man asking for himself, I can respect the greed. Man asking for someone else, I know exactly how far he'll go and exactly how little that's worth to me.

He slides the purse two inches back across the slate, toward Kaeric, gentle as returning a borrowed tool.

Keep your coin. Spend it somewhere it moves something.

Kaeric stands there with his hand flat on the purse, jaw already tight, the breath he hasn't let out yet stacking up behind his teeth.

Whose debt is it, he asks. Not the number. The name behind the name.

The broker's face doesn't change, but something in the shoulders does — a small settling.

That's paper I don't sell either.

[Beat 6]
The purse goes back into his coat with the same motion he'd use to holster a blade he's ashamed of drawing.

Kaeric walks the length of the market before he lets himself stop, past the leather-stall, past the man selling roasted nuts in twists of oiled paper, until the crowd thins to the place where the fair's noise starts giving up its edges. He stands at a hitching post nobody's used today. Runs a thumb along the grain of it, an old habit.

He does the sum again anyway. Not because it will change.

Two rounds. Six silver.

That was the plan when the debt was smaller.

He takes the purse back out — four silver, eleven copper, coin that was already short of what the boards wanted this morning, before the second recalculation, before the wall the broker described that doesn't move for coin or story or a face from the Pit. He counts it into his palm anyway, copper by copper, the metal cold and slick with the sweat of his own hand.

Four silver, eleven copper, plus whatever the Pit pays.

Two for hired steel. Four for the beast rounds. That's ten and eleven, if I live through both.

Not enough. Not against a number that jumped twice in eight days.

Unless there's a third round. Unless he beats whoever they put up as House Champion, and that purse is fat enough to close what's left.

He doesn't know the Champion round's number yet. Nobody posts it before the bracket clears the second round — the sergeant told him that much, offhand, the way a man mentions weather that's still two days out. Could be enough. Could be nothing close. He's building a bridge over a gap he can't measure from this side of it.

The third round is the one where men don't walk out the same as they walked in.

A cart rattles past on the packed dirt, wheel-iron loud against a stone somebody hasn't cleared, and for a second the sound is enough to stop his thinking cold.

It starts again anyway.

*Sennah's medicine can't wait for a fourth round that doesn't exist. There's no second purse behind this one.*

He closes his hand around what's left of the first purse, feels the coins bite faint lines into his palm, and doesn't open it again.

He already signed his name at the registration table, steadier than he expected to. The sum was never going to change his hand.

He puts the purse away for the last time before the Pit, tucks it flat against his ribs where a blow won't easily find it, and looks toward the ropes, where the crowd noise has picked up — first bout of the afternoon starting without him.

Kaeric walks toward the sound.

[Beat 7]
The registration tent is smaller than he remembers from an hour ago, canvas gone slack in the noon air, a single lamp burning inside though the sun's still up. A different clerk sits the table now — older, gray at the temple, ledger open to a fresh page already half-scored with names.

Duel-team bracket, the clerk says, before Kaeric's opened his mouth. Pair or single entry?

Single.

Then you're hired banner or you're paying stray-rate again, and stray-rate on team brackets runs three, not two. Organizers don't like a man alone on the card. Bad optics for the sponsors.

Three. Not two.

I paid stray-rate already, at the first table.

That was individual bouts. This is the team roster. Different board, different fee.

Kaeric's jaw sets before he decides to let it. He breathes it down the way his father showed him, slow through the nose, four counts.

I don't have a banner.

Then you take one. House Draught's short a man on the roster — lost one to the infirmary tent this morning, chest wound gone bad. They'll take a stray and split the purse standard, seventy-thirty, their favor. Or Verlaine's auxiliary line's got a slot, same split. Your call.

Draught, he says, because the name means nothing to him.

The clerk dips the pen, writes it without looking up. Kaeric Dunmoor, hired to House Draught, unaffiliated origin.

*My name and hers on paper an hour's walk apart, and nobody in this tent will ever set the two boards side by side.*

That's three silver, the clerk says. Or you don't fight the team round, only your singles.

He already knows what's in the purse. Four silver, eleven copper. He's counted it enough times today.

Brigg's voice comes from just behind his elbow, quiet, careful, the voice he uses when he's decided not to be a problem.

You said save some for eating.

I did say that.

Kaeric doesn't look down at him. Doesn't trust his own face right now to be worth looking at.

He puts three silver on the table. The coins ring flat and small against the wood. One silver and eleven copper left, and the fair's cheapest meal runs four copper.

The clerk marks the ledger, blots it, turns the book around so Kaeric can see his name sitting under House Draught's column, third from the bottom, ink still wet enough to smear if a thumb strayed near it.

Bracket posts at the ropes come sundown, the clerk says. You're in the second slate. Don't be late — Draught's captain doesn't hold a line for a man who isn't there.

Kaeric stares at the wet ink a moment longer than he needs to.

He turns from the table. Brigg falls in beside him, quiet, matching his stride the way he does when he's decided this isn't the moment to ask questions, and the fair noise closes back around them both like water over a dropped stone.

[Beat 8]
The draw hall smells of tallow smoke and wet chalk, and Kaeric has learned to stand where the crowd thins, at the back, near the pillar with the crack running up it like a dry riverbed.

He watches the drum instead of the faces.

The faces are the same every season. Hungry men wanting sponsors. Sponsors wanting names they can bet against safely. He wants nothing from any of them.

Brigg stands close enough that Kaeric can feel the boy vibrating.

"That's the Verlaine clerk," Brigg says. "The fat one. He spun crooked last season, everyone says so. Spun it so House fighters drew House fighters."

"Everyone says a lot of things."

"I'm just saying. If he spins it crooked again—"

"Then it spins crooked." Kaeric doesn't look down at him. "Watch the drum. Not the man."

The clerk is Ocipheus livery under a Verlaine sash, on loan for the tournament the way half the clerks are, nobody's man and everybody's for three days. He cranks the drum's iron handle. The slats inside clack against each other, dry wood on dry wood, a sound like a cart going over rough cobble.

Names get called. Men Kaeric half-recognizes step forward, get their tokens, drift back into the murk near the tent flaps. A Calyx fighter with a shaved scalp draws a Loera woman half his size and the crowd laughs, not unkindly, because she broke a man's collarbone in the qualifying bouts and everyone in this room knows it.

Kaeric's name doesn't come.

He waits.

*The drum doesn't know Sennah's name.*

Brigg shifts his weight foot to foot, working up to something. Kaeric can feel it coming the way he feels weather.

"If you draw somebody bad," Brigg says, "somebody with a House behind them who doesn't want you there — you'll still fight fair? Even if they don't?"

"I fight the way I fight. Doesn't change with who's across from me."

"But what if they've got friends in the crowd. What if the crowd's against you."

"Then the crowd's against me." Kaeric's eyes stay on the drum. "The Pit doesn't care about the crowd either."

The clerk's hand goes into the drum's mouth again, gloved to the wrist against splinters, and this time when he pulls the slat out he squints at it and reads the name twice before he calls it, like he doesn't trust his own reading.

"Kaeric. Dunmoor."

The hall doesn't go quiet for him. It never does. A few heads turn, the way heads turn for a stray dog that's wandered somewhere it shouldn't.

He steps forward anyway. Boots on packed dirt, not sand yet, that's for the Pit floor itself. Brigg trails a half-step behind, close enough to catch anything that gets thrown.

The clerk reaches for the second drum — the paired names, the man Kaeric will stand beside instead of across from, because this bracket is duel-team, two bodies moving as one against whatever the Pit sends next. He cranks it once, twice, the wood ticking over inside like a held breath let out slow.

He draws the slat.

He reads it, and something crosses his face that Kaeric has seen before on clerks who'd rather be reading anyone else's name.

"Kressida Haun," the clerk says.

The name doesn't land soft in the room. Somewhere near the front, a man laughs — short, ugly, cut off fast.

Kaeric doesn't know the name. Everyone else in the hall seems to.

Brigg tugs at his sleeve, quiet now, quiet in the way he only gets when something's actually wrong.

"That's her," Brigg says. "By the second post. The one who's not clapping."

Kaeric follows the boy's chin toward a woman standing apart from a knot of Atrament fighters, arms crossed, watching the drum the way Kaeric watches a wound to see if it'll keep bleeding. She isn't smiling. She isn't scowling either. She doesn't move.

She looks up. Finds him across the hall without seeming to search for him at all.

She knew before the clerk called it.

The clerk moves on to the next slat, the moment already behind him, already somebody else's problem. The crowd's attention slides off Kaeric and Haun both, back to the drum, back to names that matter more to more people.

Kaeric doesn't move toward her yet.

She hasn't moved toward him either.

Between them the hall keeps turning, chalk dust drifting down through the lantern light.

[Beat 9]
Haun crosses the floor first.

She doesn't hurry. She walks the way a woman walks toward a debt she intends to collect in full, boots flat on the packed dirt, arms still crossed until she's three steps from him and drops them to her sides.

Brigg edges back half a pace. Kaeric doesn't.

"You're the axe," she says. Not a question.

"Kaeric Dunmoor."

"I know your name. I asked the clerk before he called it." Her eyes go over him once, boots to shoulders, the way a quartermaster checks a crate for the shipping marks. "I wanted to know what I was getting."

"And?"

"An unsponsored fighter with a two-handed axe and no House to bury him if the Pit finishes what it starts."

Kaeric doesn't answer that. There's nothing in it that's wrong.

"Vantric," Kaeric says. "That's your partner."

Something moves under her stillness, quick, gone before it settles into anything he can name.

"Was."

"Heard he took a bad cut in the qualifying bouts. Heard he's still in the infirmary tent."

"Heard a lot for a man who stands at the back of the hall."

"Brigg hears things. I listen when he talks."

Brigg, behind him, goes very still at being mentioned, the kind of still that means *don't say anything, don't ruin this.*

Haun's jaw works once, like she's deciding whether to spend the next sentence on him or save it.

"Vantric's not fighting again this season," she says. "Maybe not next. The healer won't say which, and I've stopped asking, because the answer doesn't change what's in front of me." Her eyes cut to the drum, to the clerk already cranking it for the next name, already forgetting them both. "What's in front of me is you."

"I fight the Pit the way I fight anything. One line, one weight behind it."

"Duel-team doesn't work like that." Flat. Final, the way a clerk reads a rule he's tired of explaining. "Two bodies, one animal. That's the whole bracket. That's the whole reason it's not just two men fighting in the same sand."

"Then teach me the animal."

She looks at him a long moment, and he watches her decide something, the way he's watched men decide whether a debt is worth collecting by hand or letting go cold.

"You don't know what you're offering."

"I know I don't have a choice, same as you don't." Kaeric nods once at the roster board, the chalk already smudging where someone's sleeve brushed it. "Drum called it. Doesn't ask either of us first."

Brigg's hand finds the hem of Kaeric's coat and grips it, small and sure, the way he does when he's decided which grown-up he's staying near.

Haun's gaze drops to the boy's fist in the cloth, then back up.

"Bring the boy where I can see him," she says. "I don't like things behind me I haven't counted."

She turns before he can answer, already walking toward the tent row, already assuming he'll follow.

[Beat 10]
The tent row runs behind the draw hall, canvas after canvas staked into ground still hard from the night's cold. Haun doesn't slow at the flap of hers. She counts three tents past it, stops at a fourth, and pulls the flap wide with the flat of her hand.

"Here," she says. "So we're not doing this in front of the whole roster."

Kaeric ducks in after her. Brigg stays at the flap, one hand still on the canvas, not quite inside, not quite out.

Inside, the light is amber through the canvas, the smell of oiled leather and something medicinal underneath it. A cot. A footlocker with a name burned into the lid that isn't hers.

"This is his," Kaeric says.

"Was." Haun crosses her arms again, the same stance she wore across the hall, like she's built the posture specifically for this conversation and can't put it down. "He's in the infirmary tent now, third row, cot by the brazier. Took a bad fall in the qualifying bout, landed wrong on the sand, tore something in the hip that doesn't want to heal straight. Healer says maybe next season. Maybe not."

"Nobody blames him falling."

"Nobody blames anybody. That's not the point." Her voice doesn't rise. It goes flatter instead, each word set down like a coin counted onto a table. "The point is I trained four years to move with that man without looking at him. Four years so his hand on my shoulder means one thing and his hand on my hip means another and I never once have to turn my head to know which. That's gone. That's in a cot by a brazier."

"And now you've got me."

"Now I've got you." She says it the way she'd name a debt she didn't ask to inherit. "An axe man who's never stood in a duel-team bracket, who's never had to move so another body could move around him, drawn out of a drum because the drum doesn't care what either of us needs."

*She wanted somebody good. She got somebody the House would let go without asking questions.*

"I didn't ask for you either," Kaeric says.

"I know." For the first time something in her face loosens, not softer, just tired, the way a rope goes slack when the weight comes off it wrong. "That's the only thing about this morning I don't have to hate."

Brigg, at the flap, shifts his weight, and Haun's eyes cut to him, sharp again.

"He stays where I told you," she says. "Where I can count him."

"He's not fighting your bracket."

"He's standing close enough to matter if something goes wrong in it." She looks back at Kaeric, and there's no apology in the look, none owed and none offered. "Everything near this pairing matters now. That's what you signed onto when the clerk called your name and didn't have the decency to call somebody better."

Kaeric doesn't answer that either. There's nothing in it that's wrong.

"Show me the bracket rules," he says. "Not what you tell me. What's written."

Haun's jaw sets, and for a moment he thinks she'll refuse him that too, the way she's refused him everything else this morning except her name and her grief. Instead she reaches into the footlocker — Vantric's footlocker — and pulls out a folded paper gone soft at the creases, and her hand lingers on it before she holds it out.

"Read it yourself," she says. "Then tell me if you still think a drum that did this to both of us deserves to be obeyed."

[Beat 11]
The paper is thinner than it looks, the fold gone brown where thumbs have worried it. Kaeric takes it the way he'd take a blade someone else has already drawn blood with.

He reads.

Forge Marshal, she said.

I don't want it, Kaeric said.

[Beat 86]
The cage smelled the way they always smelled — wet iron, and under it the sweeter rot Corrik had stopped noticing years ago, the way a tanner stops noticing the tannery.

He hadn't planned on coming out here tonight.

Three Points had gone quiet in the wrong way. A Forge Marshal's men had walked the Pit's holding yard twice this week, once with a ledger and once without, and the second time was the one that meant something. They hadn't asked him anything yet. That was the part that itched.

*They're not asking questions because they already know which ones to ask.*

The specimen in the last cage was smaller than the truce-fair crowds liked, which was usually good news for Corrik — a small one sold cheap and moved fast, no one asking why it hadn't fought yet, no beastmaster wanting a demonstration bout before the coin changed hands. This one he'd caged nine days back, up in the ash country where the Sinter approach still steamed after rain, and he hadn't sold it, and he'd told himself that was because the season's bracket was full, nothing more particular than that.

It watched him now the way they all did. Flat. Unhurried. He'd handled forty of them this year and told himself the eyes meant nothing, that a thing with no tongue behind its jaw couldn't be doing anything you'd call thinking.

He crouched by the latch anyway and did not look at its eyes.

His hand found the pin before he'd decided to move it, the same motion he used a hundred times a season loading stock onto the transport cart, except the cart wasn't here and neither was a buyer and neither, tonight, was anyone at all.

The latch gave with the small flat click it always gave, no different from any other night he'd opened this cage to feed it or water it or check the chain-collar hadn't rusted through.

He didn't say anything to it. He wasn't the kind of man who talked to his stock. Ichor-proof plate didn't come with a heart — that was the joke the older haulers told, and Corrik had laughed at it enough years running that he mostly believed it about himself.

The door swung open on a hinge he'd oiled that morning for no reason he'd examined.

It didn't bolt for freedom the way the frightened ones did. It went the way the calm ones went, unhurried, out past the cart-ruts and into the dark where the ash country started, and Corrik watched it go the way he'd watch any transaction complete.

If it's true Sinterkin and he's just put it back where it can do what true Sinterkin do, that's on him and no one else.

If it's not, he's lost one cage's worth of coin and no one will ever know the difference.

He told himself the second thought last, because it was the one that let him stand back up.

The other cages held stock enough for next season's bracket, and he checked their latches out of habit, out of the same flat competence that had kept him alive on the Sinter approaches longer than most haulers lasted, and none of it felt any different than it had an hour ago.

He didn't write it in the manifest. There was nothing to write — a cage that had never held anything, as far as any ledger in Three Points would ever say.

The ash-country dark took the shape of it within a dozen strides, and Corrik latched the empty cage shut behind nothing, the same care he'd have given it full, and walked back toward the yard lights without once looking over his shoulder to see if it looked back.

[Beat 87]
Meraq makes his own lines before he lets himself stop moving. He sets the tube on the folding table inside his tent, upright in the rack, and only then does he sit down and take the glove out of his coat.

It has not dried on the walk. It will not dry tonight either, not in canvas leaking in three places, not with the fire outside guttering under a groundsheet the aides rigged too late. He lays it on the table beside the tube and looks at both of them the way a man looks at two figures he's been given and cannot yet make add to anything.

The tube first. He unscrews the cap and draws the truce out by its ribbon, and the paper has gone soft at the fold, translucent where the wet found the ink, but the words hold — fourth day, dawn, same field, both parties withdraw a half-mile and no further violence offered or answered. His own hand, his own signature, blotted twice on purpose so that no one can later swear he hadn't meant it. Vaen's mark below it, the one he always makes instead of a name, three strokes that could be anything and are, by long agreement, understood to be Kesric Vaen.

The terms hold. Whatever else changed in that tent, the terms hold exactly as they were an hour ago.

He rolls the paper again, careful of the softened fold, and puts it back in the tube and caps it, and sets it upright in the rack where it will stay dry enough, or as dry as anything stays in a camp that has been rained on for six days running.

Then there is only the glove.

He turns it over once. The knuckle-blackening, the bone toggle, the stitching he's already priced at a glance and not needed to price twice. It tells him nothing new the second time. It is leather and thread and a shape that has been a hand's shape for years before tonight, and it will go on being that, sitting on a folding table in a wet tent, whether or not Meraq decides what it means.

It means nothing. A man forgets a glove. Men forget gloves every day of every war that has ever been fought in weather like this.

He does not believe it saying it any more than he believed it standing in the mud watching Vaen's bare hand press flat against his own chest.

Three days.

He counts them the way he counts everything — not on his fingers, not aloud. Tomorrow. The day after. The day after that. Then the dawn on the field, the half-mile withdrawn, the truce expiring the way all truces expire, on schedule, without ceremony, into whatever comes next.

Vaen will ask about the glove before he asks about Kaeric. That is how it will go, and Vaen has already decided it will.

Meraq picks the glove up again. He does not put it in his coat. He does not put it in the strongbox with the requisitions and the dead men's names and the one letter he has never managed to burn — that is where a man puts things he means to stop thinking about, and he is not going to lie to himself twice in the same hour.

He sets it on the table instead, in the open, where he will see it every time he sits down to write an order, and he takes a fresh scrap of paper from the stack the aides keep for dispatches and writes four words on it in his own hand: *Held. Not returned. Waiting.*

He does not sign it. He folds it once and lays it under the glove's cuff, tucked into the wrist opening the way a man tucks a note into a boot he means to put back on, and he sits back and looks at what he's made — a glove with a message inside it, addressed to no one, answerable to no one, sitting three feet from a truce that expires in three days whether either of them has asked the other anything by then or not.

If Vaen comes back for it before the fourth day, he will find the note. If he doesn't, Meraq will have kept a thing he cannot explain to any man who asks why a Champion writes riddles into another king's glove.

Either way the coin is spent now. He cannot un-write it.

Outside, the rain has not eased. It comes down on the tent roof in the same flat, patient rhythm it has kept since dusk, indifferent to truces, to gloves, to four words folded into a wrist that might never be read by the one hand they were meant for.

Meraq sits a while longer with his hands on the table, empty now, and does not reach for anything else.

[Beat 88]
The strongbox lock takes three tries because Meraq's hands are still cold, and he counts that too — a number to add to the others he keeps.

Rain finds the tent seam again where he'd wedged the oilcloth wrong the first time, a thin cord of water walking down the center pole and pooling near the leg of the folding table. He watches it a moment before he does anything about the leak, because the water is honest in a way the last hour was not.

Three days.

He unrolls the truce document once more, though he has already read it twice and signed it once, because rereading things is cheaper than thinking about the glove now sitting inside the strongbox with the requisitions and the names of men who are dead because he miscounted something once, years ago, and never again.

Withdraw a half-mile from the field, both parties, dawn to dawn, three days, no exceptions, no messengers under flag closer than the marked stones.

Clean terms. Terms he wrote himself, in the second hour, when Vaen had gone quiet in the way that meant he was letting Meraq believe he'd won something.

Outside, iron footfalls cross the mud in a rhythm too even for a man's stride — a Heloth courier-construct picking its way toward the officers' row, venting a short hiss of steam-chemical exhaust that hangs a moment in the cold air before the wind takes it. Meraq does not look up. He has heard that gait ten thousand times and it tells him nothing except that someone, somewhere, needs a message carried faster than a man on foot could carry it.

He rolls the document, seats it in the tube, and seals it with the wax he keeps warm in his palm for exactly this reason. The wax takes his thumbprint instead of a signet because he has never trusted a stamp not to be lost, stolen, copied. His own skin he can account for.

Analog.

The word sits in him like the wax sits in the tube — pressed in, cooling, taking a shape he did not choose.

He has been called worse. He has been called nothing at all, for years at a stretch, by men who found his name harder to say than his function. But Vaen has never once, in eleven years of this — the map table, the field, the slow grinding business of who yields what — reached for that word before the tent flap closed behind him.

He used it before he left. Not after. Before.

Meraq sets the tube down and turns it so the wax seal faces him, a small habit, checking that the impression took clean. It did. Everything he does tonight, he is checking that things took clean, because the one thing he cannot check is what took hold in that tent when the King pressed his bare hand flat to his own chest and said nothing at all.

*Kaeric.*

The name comes up in him unbidden and he lets it surface exactly long enough to recognize it before he pushes it back under, the way he has pushed it under for three years. The question underneath the name is not where the boy is. It is what it cost to make him, and that is a question with only two answers, and Meraq does not have three days' worth of patience for either one.

The rain gets into the tent again, the same cord of water finding the same low place in the canvas, patient as a slow leak always is.

He wedges the oilcloth tighter this time. He does not check the strongbox again. He blows out the lamp, and in the dark he counts the things he is certain of — one truce, one seal, one glove he has not yet decided whether to call evidence or confession — and comes up one short, the way he has come up one short every night for three years, and lies down anyway to wait for morning.

[Beat 89]
Second day, dawn coming grey through the canvas, and Meraq is already awake when the Heloth on watch outside changes its weight from one foot to the other — a sound like a kettle settling.

He has not opened the strongbox since the first night.

That is its own kind of counting.

Half a mile away, across ground neither side is walking, Vaen is doing — what. Eating standing too, maybe. Kaeric.

[Beat 91]
Three Points comes apart the way it went up: fast, loud at first, then quieter than seems possible for a place that held this many people three sunrises ago.

The Lists go first. Iron cressets that burned through both nights of the joust are dumped and left to cool in their own ash, hissing once when a boy tips rainwater over the nearest one to save himself a wait he isn't paid to take. The fence rails come down in sections, hauled to a cart already stacked four rows deep, and nobody troubles to sweep the churned lane where the Heloth mounts drove their long, patient circuits — the ground there is torn to a black paste that will take a season to close over, hoofless divots and skid-marks already filling with standing water the color of weak tea.

The Pit empties slower. Rope rails get coiled by hand, one man walking backward, another feeding the line, the same unhurried economy of motion that men use on things they expect to use again. The chain-stakes where the beastmasters worked their Sinterspawn come up out of the mud with a sound like a tooth pulled, and are stacked, and are not cleaned. Something in the corner of the arena wall is still dark with what it's dark with. No one is assigned to look at it. The tarpaulin that shaded the mercenary bracket flaps once in a gust and is folded, badly, by two men who want to be somewhere else.

The Bond Market strikes its canvas last of all the trade rows, because it did the most business and packed the tightest crowd, right up until a bell some House or other rings at these things to mean *done, actually done, stop pretending you have one more hour*. The ledgers close. The chalked prices on the boards are wiped with a wet rag, not erased so much as smeared into an illegible gray, and the boards themselves go onto a cart with the others, indistinguishable now from a hundred other flat boards that held a hundred other numbers this week.

By midafternoon the wagons are strung out along both roads leaving the field, a slow double column of houses' colors mixing at last, Atrament brass fittings creaking behind Verlaine oiled wheels, Pallor's undyed canvas rolled next to somebody's dyed and expensive silk, all of it just cargo now, all of it just weight to be gotten off this ground before the ruts set in for good.

What they leave is what a fair this size always leaves.

Torn bunting, three colors of it, snagged on a fence post that nobody thought worth the extra half-minute to salvage, lifting and falling in a wind that has nowhere else to push it. A heap of stall-refuse — bruised fruit, a split cask stave, a boot with no partner — dumped where the food-row used to run its whole noisy length, and already the crows have found it, four of them, then eleven, working the pile with the unhurried confidence of things that have done this before and will do it again in three years when the ground calls them back. A pair of lean dogs, camp-followers of no house and every camp, trot the perimeter of the old cook-fires nose-down, finding what the haulers didn't think was worth the wagon space.

The Pit's mud is already skinning over at the edges, a thin gray crust forming where the sun gets to it, the way any wound starts to close the moment the pressure comes off it.

By evening the last cart is a shrinking shape on the eastern road, and the field under where Three Points stood is doing the only thing it has ever known how to do, which is nothing — holding its churned ruts and its cold cressets and its torn bunting up to a sky that does not care what stood here, waiting the way it always waits, quiet, empty, patient for three years, for the next truce, the next fair, the next enormous temporary thing that will rise here loud and bright and certain of itself, and come down again just like this one did, into crows and dogs and mud going still.
