# 1381: The Uprising

_In the summer of 1381, England's poorest rose up and nearly brought a king to his knees.

For three decades, the crown had squeezed the peasantry with impossible taxes and laws that bound them to their lords. When collectors came for a fourth poll tax, the rage that had been simmering in the fields and villages exploded. From Essex and Kent, tens of thousands marched on London. They burned the Savoy Palace, stormed the Tower, and forced a boy-king to grant them charters of freedom. For six days, the realm hung in the balance.

This is the story of that uprising, told through the documentary record that survived it — the chronicles, court rolls, and indictments that captured what the rebels wanted, who led them, and what happened when the crown turned to settle accounts. It is a history of manorial bondage and plague-driven labor shortage, of poll-tax revolts and a radical priest's vision of a leveled world, of Wat Tyler and John Ball and the nameless thousands whose names we will never recover. It traces how a medieval kingdom responded to the largest popular insurrection of its age, and what the Rising changed — and what it did not.

The historical record holds genuine gaps and competing readings. This account follows the evidence where it leads, and leaves the questions open.

For most people living in the English countryside in the first half of the fourteenth century, the authority that mattered day to day was not the king in Westminster but the lord of the manor — and the single fact that shaped nearly everything else about a household's life was whether its land was held freely or in villeinage [1]. A villein tenant could not leave the manor without the lord's permission, could not marry off it without paying a fee, and owed a fixed number of days of labour on the lord's own land — the demesne — before any work on his own strips began [1]. This was not a metaphorical bondage. It was recorded, manor by manor, in court rolls that specified exactly how many days a named tenant owed and what happened when he didn't show up.

The manor itself was usually organized as an open-field system: two or three large fields, divided into narrow strips distributed among tenants so that good and poor soil were shared out roughly evenly, worked in rotation so a third (in the three-field system that had spread across much of midland England by 1300) lay fallow each year [1]. The lord's own demesne land was mixed in among the tenant strips or held as a separate block, worked using exactly the compulsory labour this chapter describes. None of this was designed with anyone's comfort in mind; it was a machine for extracting a surplus from land and turning it into a lord's income, with the village community's own subsistence built in only because a starving tenant could not work.

The labour a villein owed had names, and the names mattered because each one was a separate thing to negotiate, resist, or resent. "Week-work" was the routine obligation — commonly two or three days a week on the lord's demesne, year-round [2]. "Boon-work" was extra, seasonal, and unpaid in the sense that mattered: additional days demanded at harvest and other peak moments, when the lord's own need for labour spiked and a tenant's own strips needed the same hands at the same time [2]. On top of the labour itself sat a set of fees that fell only on the unfree: a merchet, paid for a lord's permission when a tenant's daughter (and sometimes a non-inheriting son) married; a leyrwite, a fine for a child born out of wedlock; a heriot, the lord's right to seize the best beast a tenant owned at his death, collected from the widow or heir before anything else was settled [2]. None of these fell on a free tenant. That distinction — fees and labour service tied to unfree status specifically, not simply to poverty — is why villeinage was a legal category and not just an economic one.

The legal machinery enforcing all of this ran almost entirely inside the manor itself. A villein's disputes over land, inheritance, or service were heard in the lord's own manorial court, presided over by the lord's steward, not the king's judges — and by the working legal theory of the age, laid out at length in the treatise attributed to the thirteenth-century justice Henry de Bracton, an unfree tenant had no standing to bring most kinds of action in the royal courts against his own lord at all [3]. A villein was, in the blunt formulation later English lawyers used, "the lord's man" — his person as well as his labour, in the specific sense that his lord could recover him if he fled, the way a lord could recover any other piece of property that had wandered off the manor [3].

None of these obligations rested on force alone; they rested on paper, and English manors kept an unusually thorough paper record of them. By 1381 most manors maintained court rolls -- year-by-year parchment registers naming who held what land, on what terms, and what was owed -- alongside custumals (written statements of a manor's own specific customs) and rentals [50]. Crucially, these documents recorded personal status as well as tenure: whether a named individual was free or unfree. Villeinage was hereditary and provable by exactly this kind of record -- if the roll said a tenant's grandfather had been a bondman of the manor, the tenant was one too, and so, in turn, were the tenant's own children, the status passing down attached to a name in a register rather than to any physical mark or public ceremony [50].

Beyond week-work, boon-work, merchet, leyrwite, and heriot, two further customary dues are worth naming because they recur later in this book: chevage, a fee a villein owed simply for permission to live away from the manor, and the miller's monopoly -- the obligation to grind one's own grain at the lord's mill, at the lord's price, rather than at a hand-quern of one's own -- enforced, on at least one manor this book will return to, by physically confiscating the offending equipment [50]. None of it depended on force in the moment of collection. It depended on a written line that could be produced, on demand, in the lord's own court, before the lord's own steward -- which is exactly why, eight chapters from now, burning that same paper becomes a weapon in its own right, not simple vandalism.

None of this happened in a static world. England's rural population had grown for roughly two centuries before 1300, and by most modern estimates had reached a level — historians debate the exact figure, with proposals for England alone ranging from around four to six million — that would not be matched again until the eighteenth century [4]. Growth on that scale, without a matching growth in cultivable land, meant more households competing for the same strips, holdings splitting smaller with each generation, and marginal land — thin soil, steep ground, land that needed constant clearing to stay arable — brought under the plough simply because better land had run out [4]. Real hunger followed: the Great Famine of 1315 to 1317, driven by catastrophic wet weather across northern Europe, killed on a scale later chroniclers struggled to describe, and it struck a population that later research suggests was already showing signs of nutritional stress from decades of pressure on the food supply [5]. By the 1340s, in other words, rural England was a crowded, land-hungry, already-strained place — not a stable old order about to be disturbed from outside, but a system already leaning on its own limits when something did arrive to break it entirely.

Then and Now

The strangest thing about villeinage, to a modern reader, is not the poverty — plenty of people have been poor — but the specificity of the unfreedom: a real fee, with a real name, charged for a real event in your own family, payable to a man who did nothing to earn it except own the ground your house stood on. A merchet for a daughter's wedding is a strange enough idea that it is worth sitting with rather than rushing past: not a tax on wealth or a tax on the event in the abstract, but a toll on a specific person's specific joy, priced item by item the way a modern reader might price a wedding venue.

What has not changed is the shape of the underlying argument, even where the vocabulary has: who owes what to whom for the right to use land they did not create, and who gets to write the rules for that arrangement. A medieval villein negotiated week-work and boon-work against a lord who held the court that would hear the dispute; a modern tenant negotiates a lease against a landlord who — in most jurisdictions — no longer also sits as the judge. That specific reform, court and landlord no longer being the same person, is one of the quieter inheritances of the story this book is about to tell.

The plague reached England by sea in the summer of 1348, put ashore — chroniclers disagree on the exact date and even the exact port, with Melcombe (modern Weymouth), Bristol, and Southampton all named as candidates — sometime between late June and late summer of that year [6]. It moved inland from there with a speed contemporaries had no framework to explain and modern epidemiology still debates the precise mechanism of, reaching London by autumn and much of the rest of the country within about a year. Modern estimates of the death toll, reconstructed from manorial records, ecclesiastical vacancy registers (a parish priest's death is one of the more reliably documented facts in this period, since a replacement had to be formally installed), and other administrative traces, converge on a range of roughly 40 to 60 percent of England's population dead within the first wave, 1348 to 1350 [6]. There is no comparable event in the rest of this book, or in most of recorded English history: a population loss on that scale, inside two years, driven by a cause no one present could identify or treat.

The demographic shock landed directly on the system Chapter 1 described. A manor that had, the year before, more tenant households competing for strips than there was good land to go around now had the opposite problem: labour was suddenly the scarce resource, and land — someone's, somewhere, now standing empty — was not. Wages for hired labour began rising almost immediately as employers competed for a smaller pool of workers, and villein tenants who had spent a generation negotiating from a position of surplus labour discovered they were negotiating from a position of shortage instead. The change happened too fast for the legal and customary structure of the manorial economy to adjust to on its own terms.

The plague should have settled the labour question in the survivors' favour outright: with somewhere between a third and a half of the workforce gone, the tenants who remained held a bargaining position no generation before them had enjoyed. Instead, lords went back to the same court rolls this book's Chapter 1 described and began reviving servile dues and labour obligations that had, on many manors, quietly lapsed or gone uncollected for years before 1348 -- reasserting on parchment a level of control the plague's demographic arithmetic no longer supported in practice [51]. Parliament's response, the Ordinance and then the Statute of Labourers this chapter turns to next, was the same instinct applied at the national level: use written law to hold the old price in place against a population too small to be made to accept it forever.

The crown's answer arrived within a year, before Parliament had even reconvened to make it statute. The royal Ordinance of Labourers, issued 18 June 1349 while the first wave of plague was still active in parts of the country, ordered every able-bodied man and woman under sixty, not otherwise occupied in a trade or living off their own land, to accept work when offered it — and to accept it at the wage rates that had prevailed in 1346 or the several years before, not whatever a labour-starved market would now bear [7]. Refusing an offer of work, or leaving a position before an agreed term was up in search of better pay elsewhere, was made a punishable offense. The ordinance did not create new obligations for villein tenants specifically — its language reached the whole labouring population, free and unfree — but its practical effect was to try to freeze in place, by royal command, an economic relationship that plague had already broken.

Parliament made the policy more precise and considerably more enforceable in February 1351, in the Statute of Labourers, passed at the first parliament held after the plague specifically because members were hearing complaints that, in the statute's own accusatory phrase, wages had risen "out of singular covetousness" rather than because the labour market itself had changed [8]. The 1351 statute set out graduated penalties — fines, imprisonment, and the seizure of goods — for labourers who took more than the old rate and, on paper at least, for employers who offered it. It also created a new local office to enforce all of this: justices of labourers, drawn from the county gentry, empowered to hear and punish violations directly rather than leaving enforcement to the ordinary machinery of manorial and royal courts.

The justices of labourers were, by design, the same class of men who benefited most directly from wages staying low — local landholders enforcing a law that protected local landholders' interests, against the very labourers whose bargaining position the plague had just improved [9]. Modern historians reading the surviving court records find a pattern of selective enforcement consistent with that structural conflict of interest: prosecutions falling disproportionately on wage labourers and smallholders, while larger employers who broke the same wage cap to secure scarce workers faced enforcement far less often. The statute was, in practice as much as in intent, class legislation — a labour market forcibly held at its pre-plague price by the people who owned the demesnes that price benefited.

It did not work, not in the sense of actually holding wages down for long; real wages for agricultural labour rose substantially over the following decades regardless of what the statute said, because a legal wage cap cannot repeal a genuine shortage of hands. What it did do, and did successfully, was generate three decades of accumulated resentment among exactly the population it targeted — a resentment with a specific legal instrument to point to, a specific class of local official to blame for enforcing it, and a specific grievance (being punished for asking to be paid what the market would now bear) that needed no abstract theory of justice to explain. When the poll tax commissioners arrived in Essex in the spring of 1381, they were not the first arm of royal and local authority this population had reason to resent on economic grounds. The Statute of Labourers is named explicitly, by the chroniclers who wrote closest to the events, as one of the standing grievances behind what followed [9].

Then and Now

There is something almost administratively familiar about the Statute of Labourers to a modern reader, and that familiarity is worth pausing on rather than rushing past: a government responding to a sudden shock in the labour market not by asking what changed, but by trying to legislate the old price back into existence. The instinct to freeze a relationship in law rather than let it renegotiate itself is not a medieval one specifically; it is a recurring one, whenever the party who benefits from the old price also happens to write the law.

What is genuinely alien is the scale of the shock the statute was responding to, and how little time anyone had to process it. A modern labour-market disruption unfolds over years, reported on, debated, studied while it happens. The Black Death killed close to half the people some of these villages had ever known, in roughly eighteen months, with no institution capable of explaining why, and the survivors were expected — by the same June, in some places — to simply resume the old arrangement as if nothing about the underlying facts had changed. That gap, between the speed of the catastrophe and the speed of any honest legal or social adjustment to it, is the real subject of this chapter, more than the plague itself.

Four years before the events most of this book describes, in 1377, villagers across roughly forty communities in south-eastern and south-western England tried a different tactic against the same underlying grievance: not refusal by force, but refusal by argument, made through the courts [10]. Tenants on a number of manors stopped performing labour services and told their lords they were no longer bound to, on a specific legal theory — that their manors were "ancient demesne," land that Domesday Book, the great survey commissioned by William the Conqueror in 1086, recorded as having been held directly by the Crown at that date, and that tenants of ancient demesne manors were entitled by custom to lighter, fixed obligations rather than whatever labour a lord chose to demand [10]. Historians have named the resulting wave of test cases and petitions the Great Rumour, after the language contemporary records used for the claim spreading manor to manor.

It is worth noticing what this was not. It was not a spontaneous riot and it was not, in its own self-understanding, an attack on the legal order — quite the opposite. The Great Rumour's tenants were making a documentary argument: that a specific eleventh-century record, still physically consultable, proved a specific legal status that their lords were currently violating. They petitioned Parliament and pressed suits in the royal courts rather than simply stopping work and waiting to see what happened. This was a peasantry that already, four years before the more famous rising, understood its grievance well enough to frame it as a claim about documents and precedent rather than only as a claim about fairness in the abstract [10].

The 1377 tenants did not simply assert their ancient demesne claim; they went and got paperwork of their own to prove it. More than forty villages are recorded sending for exemplifications -- certified, sealed copies of the relevant Domesday Book entries -- and at least some tenants organized formally to pursue and pay for them: in Wiltshire, a group bound themselves by oath not to work until their exemplification arrived, and one man, John Godefray, was brought before the county's justices specifically accused of having advised villeins that an exemplification "by record of the book of Domesday" would prove them free [52]. Of roughly twenty exemplifications historians can trace as having been requested in 1377, only one is recorded as having actually succeeded in supporting a tenant's claim [52]. The tenants of the Great Rumour, in other words, met the manor's own weapon -- a document that could be produced in court -- with a document of their own, sought through entirely proper legal channels, and still lost.

The argument failed. The courts and Parliament rejected the ancient demesne claims made in 1377, and the villages that had stopped performing labour services on the strength of them were, in the historical record, simply defeated — no charter, no settlement, no acknowledged victory of any kind [11]. What they had instead, by the time the poll tax commissioners arrived four years later, was practice: a demonstrated willingness to organize collective, coordinated refusal across multiple manors at once, a shared vocabulary for the grievance, and — just as significantly — a working knowledge of exactly which legal channels had already been tried and closed off. The historian Rosamond Faith, examining the Great Rumour's court records and the ideology behind them, argues that the episode shows fourteenth-century peasant communities capable of sophisticated, documentary legal argument well before 1381, not a population radicalized from nothing by a single tax in a single spring [11].

The connection to what followed is not speculative. Several of the specific counties active in the Great Rumour — Essex among them — were also early and vigorous participants in the 1381 rising, and some of the same underlying grievance (labour obligation understood as illegitimate, provable by reference to a document or a legal category the tenants believed protected them) resurfaces in 1381's own demands, most directly in the rebels' insistence at Mile End on "the law of Winchester" as the only law that should bind them — a phrase historians still debate the precise meaning of, but which gestures at exactly the kind of appeal to an older, better legal order that had already failed once, in 1377, when it was made through the courts instead of through direct action [11].

Then and Now

The Great Rumour is easy to skip past in a short retelling of 1381 because it didn't work and nothing burned. That is exactly why it belongs here: most attempts to fix an unfair arrangement are not the dramatic one that finally succeeds, they are the several quieter ones before it that fail and get forgotten, and a population's later willingness to act more forcefully is rarely a first idea — it is usually what's left after the polite version has already been tried.

What has not changed is the reflex the Great Rumour represents: reaching for a document, a precedent, a technicality that might make an unfair arrangement not just wrong but also, provably, illegal. What is genuinely different is how that reach has fared since — a modern tenant with a documentary grievance against a landlord has, in most developed legal systems, actual standing to bring the claim in a court that is not also the landlord's own court. The 1377 villagers had the argument. They did not yet have anywhere neutral to make it.

England fought the Hundred Years' War against France for most of the fourteenth century, and by the 1370s the war was going badly and costing a great deal regardless. Garrisons at Calais and Brest alone ran to tens of thousands of pounds a year, and a single expedition could consume tens of thousands more in a few months, at a time when French raids were reaching the English coast itself rather than staying safely across the Channel [12]. Parliament, asked in November 1380 to find on the order of £160,000 for the war effort, reached for an instrument it had already tried twice before and found administratively simple, if deeply resented: a tax charged per head rather than on land, income, or property — a poll tax [12].

The first of the three, granted in 1377, was a flat groat — four pence — on every lay person over fourteen, excepting genuine beggars, with some allowance for married couples; it raised roughly £22,000 [13]. The second, in 1379, tried to answer the obvious objection to a flat per-head charge — that four pence meant nothing to a lord and a day's wages to a labourer — with a graduated scale across roughly seven social bands, from six pounds thirteen shillings and four pence at the top down to four pence at the bottom; it raised only about £18,600 against a hoped-for £50,000, undermined by widespread evasion and, in some records, simple failure to properly assess the wealthy at their true band [13].

The third tax, granted at the Northampton parliament in November 1380 and collected in the spring of 1381, went back to a flat charge — nominally one shilling, three times the 1377 rate, per person over fifteen, with no allowance for married couples — but built in a local-averaging rule meant to soften the flat rate's unfairness in practice: a community's wealthier residents were supposed to pay more than a shilling and its poorer residents less, so long as the community's overall average came out to twelve pence a head, and so long as no one paid more than roughly twenty times the minimum [14]. It was projected to raise around £66,666. Comparison of the surviving 1381 return membranes against the 1377 returns for the same communities — the fullest modern edition of which, compiled by Carolyn Fenwick, spans three volumes of return-by-return detail — shows a shortfall too large to explain by the one-year change in the minimum taxable age alone: in some counties, the number of recorded taxpayers in 1381 runs to only around two-thirds of the 1377 figure for the same places [14].

Historians read that gap chiefly as organized evasion rather than an actual population collapse — communities under-registering household members, particularly unmarried young women, who fell outside a household head's usual tax liability and were harder for an outside assessor to verify against local knowledge [14]. The Crown's own response was to treat the shortfall as fraud rather than exemption: in March 1381, royal commissioners were sent into the counties specifically to reinvestigate local returns and press officials and communities to explain the missing names — the direct administrative act, arriving in Essex two months later, that this book's next chapter follows into a specific village and a specific confrontation.

Then and Now

A flat tax that charges a duke and a day-labourer the same shilling is, on its face, a strange thing for any government to attempt twice, let alone three times in five years — and the 1381 government's own fix, the local-averaging rule meant to make richer households absorb more of the burden inside their own community, is a recognizably modern instinct: if the flat rate is unfair, adjust the distribution rather than the principle. It did not work here any better than it usually does, because the people asked to administer the adjustment locally were often the same people who stood to benefit from under-assessing their wealthier neighbours.

What hasn't changed is simpler and less comfortable: a state under fiscal pressure from a war going badly abroad reaches for the tax that is easiest to administer, not the one that is easiest to bear, and the resentment that follows attaches not to the war that made the tax necessary but to the tax collector standing in the village square. Richard Lyons and John Legge, whose deaths this book will return to before the revolt runs its course, were not responsible for the Hundred Years' War going badly. They were responsible for how its bill got collected, and that turned out to be the more dangerous job.

John Ball was, by the time the revolt reached Blackheath in June 1381, already a familiar and repeatedly punished figure to the church authorities of Kent — not a preacher who emerged from nowhere in the crisis, but one with a documented record stretching back roughly two decades. Archbishop Simon Islip excommunicated him sometime between 1362 and 1366 for preaching doctrine the church judged heretical; his successors Simon Langham (1366) and, later, Simon Sudbury himself confirmed the sentence, with a specific record from December 1376 referring to Ball's continuing "manifest contumacy" — defiance of the church's own judgment against him [15]. He held no fixed parish living for most of this career, preaching instead as what this book's Glossary calls a hedge-priest: unbeneficed, itinerant, answerable to no single bishop's ordinary oversight in the way a settled incumbent would be, and reaching, by the same token, whatever audience happened to gather to hear him in a market square or a churchyard rather than a fixed congregation.

He was in custody again when the revolt began — committed to the Archbishop's own prison at Maidstone, in Kent, probably around the end of April 1381, weeks before the Essex poll tax confrontation that conventionally opens the story [16]. Chroniclers differ on the exact mechanics of what happened next: some describe Ball being deliberately freed by the Kentish crowds when they broke open Maidstone's prison in early June; others suggest he may already have regained some liberty by other means before the crowd reached him. What is not disputed is that by 12 June he was at Blackheath, preaching to the assembled rebels on the eve of their entry into London.

Hostile chroniclers writing after the revolt, chiefly the St Albans monk Thomas Walsingham, present Ball as a follower of John Wycliffe and the Lollard movement — the Oxford theologian's followers, already under suspicion for challenging clerical wealth and orthodox eucharistic doctrine. The timeline makes the claim awkward to sustain as a simple cause-and-effect story: Ball's own excommunication predates Wycliffe's rise to real prominence as a controversial figure by roughly a decade, which means Ball cannot have been radicalized into heresy by a movement that had barely begun when the church first moved against him [17]. Modern historians read the Wycliffe association chiefly as a polemical convenience for Walsingham and like-minded chroniclers — a way to discredit two separate threats, the popular preacher and the Oxford don, by tying them together as a single movement, regardless of whether the doctrinal or organizational link was ever real [17].

What Ball's own preaching actually argued, independent of any borrowed Lollard theology, sits in a older and broader English tradition of clerical social criticism: an egalitarian reading of Scripture's account of humanity's common origin, used to challenge the legitimacy of hereditary rank and unfree labour specifically. Later tradition credits him with a rhyming couplet delivered at Blackheath — "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?" — though the fullest surviving text of the sermon built around that line comes down through a chronicler's later reconstruction rather than a transcript taken at the scene, and this book treats the couplet as authentically attributed but the surrounding sermon text as reconstructed rhetoric rather than verbatim record [18].

One piece of evidence from this world survives in a form no reconstruction is needed for: a small set of coded letters, preserved by the chroniclers Henry Knighton and Thomas Walsingham, that appear to have circulated among rebel sympathizers in the weeks around the rising, at least one addressed to "all manner of men" and several attributed, with varying confidence, to Ball himself [19]. Only about half a dozen of these messages survive at all, and they are strange documents to read even now: short, deliberately cryptic, packed with allegorical names — "Piers Plowman," borrowed directly from William Langland's contemporary poem of the same name, alongside figures like "Hobbe the Robber" — that clearly meant something specific to their intended readers and mean, to a modern reader, only that a shared symbolic vocabulary existed among at least some of the rising's participants before June 1381 [19]. The literary scholar Steven Justice, examining these letters closely, argues they show something more specific than generalized unrest: a rural population with real, if informal, literacy and a working familiarity with contemporary vernacular writing, organizing and communicating through text rather than purely through rumour and word of mouth [19].

That detail matters for how this book asks its reader to picture 1381 as a whole. A revolt whose participants could read and circulate coded allegorical letters referencing a specific recent poem was not a leaderless eruption of blind fury with no capacity for organization or shared ideas — whatever else it was, it had, in John Ball and in the letters connected to his name, a genuine intellectual and rhetorical apparatus behind it, built over two decades of preaching that the authorities had tried and repeatedly failed to suppress.

Then and Now

There is a particular modern reflex worth noticing in how Walsingham handled John Ball: link the popular agitator to the establishment heretic, whether or not the connection is real, because the linkage itself does discrediting work neither name could do alone. That move does not require plague, poll taxes, or villeinage to make sense to a modern reader; it requires only two inconvenient people and an audience willing to accept that association is argument.

What is harder to reach for now, and worth sitting with, is the sheer duration of Ball's unsuccessful suppression: excommunicated by one archbishop, the sentence renewed by two more of his successors in turn, repeatedly imprisoned, and still at large and still preaching when the revolt finally arrived nearly two decades later. No record gives his age or birth, so this book will not invent one — but the length of the institutional record alone is the point. The church had every formal tool available to it and simply could not make him stop, which is its own quiet fact about the actual limits of institutional power in this period, regardless of how absolute that power looked on paper.

The revolt's conventional start date is 30 May 1381, in the Essex village of Fobbing, and the man at the center of it is one this book cannot introduce with full confidence, because the record itself is uncertain about who he was. A royal commissioner — most secondary accounts give his name as John Bampton, though at least one careful modern review of the Essex poll tax commission's actual membership has questioned whether a Bampton by either that first name or "Thomas" (which some sources use instead) held a formal seat on the commission at all — arrived to press Fobbing and neighbouring villages for poll tax arrears the March reassessment commissions had flagged as underpaid [20]. A Fobbing villager, named in the record as Thomas Baker, told him the village had already paid in full and would pay no more. When the commissioner tried to enforce collection anyway, the confrontation turned violent: several officials — clerks and jurors from the earlier Brentwood proceedings — were killed, and the commissioner himself escaped back toward London [20].

This book states the identity gap plainly rather than picking a name to sound authoritative: whether the man Fobbing turned away was "John" or "Thomas" Bampton, and whether he held the formal commission the popular story assigns him, is a real, unresolved question in the documentary record — not a settled fact this account is simplifying for readability. What is not in question is the sequence that followed. Word spread from Fobbing to neighbouring villages within days, and by early June the unrest had moved from a single confrontation over tax arrears into a coordinated regional rising.

The commissioner summoned village representatives back to Brentwood on 1 June to answer for the shortfall directly, an escalation rather than a resolution — the crown's response to being refused was to demand a formal accounting, not to quietly drop the matter. By 4 June, Essex rebels were gathering in coordinated assembly at Bocking, well beyond the scale of a single village's dispute with a single tax official, discussing what to do next as a regional movement rather than as scattered local grievances [21]. The unrest crossed into Kent within days of that, carried by the same news of successful, if violent, defiance that had traveled out of Fobbing in the first place — a genuine information network operating at speed across county lines, not a series of unrelated local flare-ups that happened to occur in the same season.

None of this required central coordination from London or from any single named leader across both counties; Wat Tyler had not yet been elected to lead anyone, and the Essex rising that began at Fobbing ran on its own organizational logic, distinct from and slightly ahead of the Kentish rising this book turns to next. The two would converge on London within the same fortnight, but they began as separate regional movements responding to the same tax, at nearly the same moment, for reasons this book's earlier chapters have already laid out: three poll taxes, three decades of resented wage law, and — in some of the same counties — the memory of the Great Rumour's failed legal argument four years before.

Then and Now

It is worth sitting with how ordinary the trigger actually was: not a massacre, not a famine arriving that week, but a tax collector being told, by one named villager, that the village had already paid — a bureaucratic dispute over arrears that any modern reader would recognize as an argument with a revenue agency, not the opening scene of a national uprising. History rarely announces its hinges in advance; Thomas Baker almost certainly did not think he was starting anything larger than a local argument about a receipt.

What has changed least is the speed and unpredictability of escalation once an ordinary grievance meets a population already primed by other resentments to read it as one more instance of the same pattern. A modern tax dispute stays a tax dispute because the surrounding institutions — courts, media, formal appeal processes — absorb it. Fobbing had none of those buffers standing between "the village won't pay" and "the village down the road hears about it and stops paying too."

Kent rose within days of Essex, and its own trigger was a specific arrest rather than a specific tax collector: a man named Robert Belling, whose detention — the record does not fully clarify by whom or on exactly what charge, beyond that it was connected to the same poll tax enforcement sweeping the southeast — drew a crowd to Dartford around 5 or 6 June 1381 [22]. From there the crowd moved to Maidstone, the county town, and broke open its prison, freeing Belling along with other prisoners held there. The same or an accompanying crowd then marched on Rochester Castle, a royal fortress on the road toward London, which surrendered without any recorded resistance and released its own prisoners in turn [22]. Two royal strongholds had fallen to an unarmed crowd inside a matter of days, without a single defender named in the record as having died defending either one.

It was at Maidstone, on 7 June, that the Kentish rebels formalized what had so far been a crowd acting on shared outrage into something with a named leader: a mass gathering elected Wat Tyler to lead them, according to the fullest surviving account of the moment, the Anonimalle Chronicle [23]. Almost nothing in the documentary record explains who Tyler was before this date. Later chroniclers offer competing, uncorroborated details — that he was from Essex or from Kent, that he had served as an archer in the French wars — none of which this book can verify against an independent record, and all of which it therefore treats as unconfirmed tradition rather than established fact. A related, equally unresolved question is whether "Jack Straw," named by some chroniclers as a separate rebel leader active in these same weeks, was a distinct person or simply an alias for Tyler himself; the surviving record does not settle it either way.

With Tyler elected, the Kentish force turned toward Canterbury, entering the cathedral city and its castle on 10 June without resistance [24]. Archbishop Simon Sudbury — the same man whose office as Lord Chancellor had made him a face of the crown's fiscal policy, and who had personally excommunicated John Ball years before — was not present; he had already withdrawn toward London and the Tower. In his absence, the rebels compelled the cathedral's own monks to swear an oath of loyalty to "the true commons of England," a formula this book will see repeated at other stops on the march, and opened the castle's prison, executing at least one man identified in the crowd's own judgment as an enemy or informer [24].

By the time the Kentish force left Canterbury, the pattern for what would happen at each subsequent stop was already set, and it is worth naming plainly, because it recurs at Rochester, Canterbury, and later London alike: royal and ecclesiastical authority did not fight back. Castles and prisons opened because the men holding them chose surrender over resistance, again and again, against a force that had, as far as the documentary record shows, no siege equipment and no standing army — only numbers, momentum, and an opponent unwilling or unable to test whether those numbers would actually attack a defended position.

Then and Now

The oddest fact in this chapter, to a modern reader used to fortresses meaning something, is how little Rochester Castle's walls actually did. A royal castle is exactly the kind of hard target a besieging army spends weeks or months reducing in most medieval warfare this book's readers might already know about — and this one opened its gates to an unarmed crowd because the men inside decided, correctly as it turned out, that resistance would cost them more than surrender would.

What hasn't changed is the basic calculation behind that decision: authority backed by a credible threat of force behaves very differently from authority that has run out of people willing to test that threat. Kent's castles in June 1381 did not fall because the rebels were strong in any conventional military sense. They fell because, for one specific and temporary window, nobody on the other side of the gate believed defending it was worth dying for.

The Kentish force reached Blackheath, an open heath southeast of London, on 12 June 1381 — the same day, by most reconstructions, that news reached the young king at the Tower that the Essex rebels had also arrived north of the river, converging on the capital from two directions at once. Chroniclers' figures for the size of the assembled crowd vary wildly and were, by the standards of any real medieval headcount, essentially guesses: numbers as high as fifty or sixty thousand appear in the chronicle tradition, while modern historians, working from what population estimates for the relevant counties can actually support, generally treat a figure closer to ten thousand or somewhat more as far more plausible for the combined Kentish and Essex forces at their peak [25]. Even the more conservative modern estimate describes a crowd larger than any single force London had had to reckon with inside living memory.

It was at Blackheath, per chronicle tradition, that John Ball delivered the sermon this book discussed in Chapter 5 — the "When Adam delved" address to the assembled host, on the eve of their entry into the city. Whatever its exact wording, the sermon's timing at this specific moment, immediately before the crowd moved on London itself, gave it a function beyond rhetoric: it was, in effect, the last formal statement of purpose before action, delivered to a force about to test whether the capital would open its gates or fight.

Richard II, fourteen years old (born January 1367), made one attempt to negotiate before the crowds actually entered London. On the morning of 13 June he traveled downriver by barge to Greenwich, intending to meet the Kentish leadership face to face on the riverbank near Blackheath — and then, on the advice of the men accompanying him, declined to actually go ashore once he saw or heard the scale and temper of the crowd waiting for him [26]. The meeting that might have happened at Greenwich did not; the king's barge turned back toward the Tower without him setting foot on land, and the rebels, denied the audience they had assembled to seek, resumed their advance on the city instead.

It is difficult to overstate how much of what follows in this book traces back to that single reversed decision. A king willing to negotiate in the field, before any gate had been tested, might have produced a very different outcome than a king who withdrew and then had to negotiate twice more, at Mile End and Smithfield, only after the city had already been entered, a palace burned, and an archbishop killed. Whether staying on the barge was cowardice, sound tactical caution, or simply bad advice poorly weighed under pressure is not a question this book can settle from the record — the councillors' actual reasoning is not preserved in any detail worth trusting — but the consequence of the choice is traceable with much more confidence than the reasoning behind it.

Then and Now

Every crowd-size estimate from this period deserves the same skepticism a modern reader would bring to a viral claim about attendance at a rally: chroniclers writing about their own side's opposition had every incentive to inflate the threat, and this book's own preference for the lower, population-constrained modern estimate over the chronicle figure of fifty or sixty thousand is not pedantry, it is the same discipline a careful modern reader already applies to a suspiciously round number in a news report.

What is harder to import from the present is the position Richard found himself in on that barge: fourteen years old, being advised by adults whose own political survival was tied up in the advice they gave him, about whether to step onto a riverbank and meet, in person, tens of thousands of his own subjects who had already killed several of his officials. No modern head of state faces that specific calculus at that age, with that little institutional buffer between the decision and the consequence.

London's gates opened from the inside on the afternoon of 13 June 1381. The city's own poorer residents — sympathetic to the rebels' cause on their own account, by most historians' reading, rather than coerced into it — let the Kentish force across London Bridge, while the Essex contingent entered separately through Aldgate on the city's eastern wall [27]. Once inside, the crowd moved with clear direction rather than random destruction: the Marshalsea prison in Southwark was broken open, the Fleet and Newgate prisons in the city itself were attacked and their prisoners released, and the Temple — the Inns of Court, London's legal quarter — had its buildings damaged and its legal records deliberately burned, a symbolic strike against the profession most directly responsible for enforcing the tenurial and labour law this book's earlier chapters described [27].

The single most systematic act of destruction that day was reserved for one building: the Savoy Palace, on the Strand, the London residence of John of Gaunt and reputed the grandest private house in the kingdom. The chronicler Henry Knighton, describing what the rebels found and did there, records that the quantities of silver plate alone — "without counting the parcel-gilt and solid gold" — were so great that "five carts would hardly suffice to carry them away" [28]. They were not carried away. Knighton and other chroniclers are explicit that the goods were smashed, burned, or thrown into the Thames rather than looted — a distinction the rebels themselves appear to have enforced, since at least one man caught attempting to pocket a piece of Gaunt's silver was, according to the chronicle tradition, killed by his own fellow rebels for it [28].

The distinction between destruction and theft matters for how this book asks its reader to understand the day's violence. Burning John of Gaunt's silver rather than stealing it is not the behaviour of a crowd interested in personal enrichment; it reads, in the framing most modern historians take from the chronicle evidence, as a deliberate act of punishment against a specific, named man too unpopular and too widely blamed for the crown's fiscal policy to be attacked directly — Gaunt himself was safely in Scotland — so his house and his wealth stood in for him instead [28].

The same afternoon and the days immediately following saw a second, much less disciplined kind of violence: a sustained hunt for Flemish immigrants living in London, culminating in mass killings — one chronicle account describes forty Flemings executed in a single ward, another describes thirty-five killed inside the church of St Martin Vintry where they had taken refuge [29]. The historian Rodney Hilton has suggested this violence may have been organized in part by London's own weavers' guilds, in economic competition with Flemish textile workers rather than purely spontaneous xenophobic rage, though this remains an interpretation rather than a documented fact, and this book states it as Hilton's reading rather than settled record [29]. Whatever coordinated the killing, its victims were chosen by nationality and accent rather than by any individual act, in sharp contrast to the named, specific targeting of men like Richard Lyons for their own conduct — a distinction this book will not blur, because the difference between punishing a named man for what he did and killing a stranger for how he sounded is exactly the difference between the two kinds of violence this single day contained. Lyons himself did not survive it: dragged from his own house and beheaded in the street that same day, one more specific man marked for a specific reason.

Then and Now

The Savoy's destruction is, in its way, one of the more disciplined acts of violence in this entire book: a crowd angry enough to burn the grandest house in England chose, collectively and apparently by enforced consensus, not to keep a single piece of it for themselves. That kind of discipline under those conditions is not the default behaviour most modern readers would predict from a description like "mob violence" — it is closer to a deliberate statement, made in the one currency (destroyed wealth) that could not be dismissed as ordinary theft.

What is harder to look at directly, and this book will not soften it, is the killing of the Flemish immigrants in the same days — violence organized, whether by guild rivalry or simple mob momentum, around nationality and accent rather than any individual's actions. A crowd capable of the Savoy's specific, targeted discipline was also capable of the opposite: killing people for what they were rather than for anything they had done. Both things happened in London that week, sometimes within the same afternoon, and neither one explains the other away.

On the morning of 14 June, Richard II left the Tower of London with a small bodyguard to meet the assembled rebels at Mile End — the meeting this book's next chapter follows in full. He left behind, inside what was supposed to be the most secure fortress in the kingdom, his mother the Princess of Wales, his half-sister, the young Henry of Bolingbroke (John of Gaunt's fourteen-year-old son and heir), and the men the rebels held most directly responsible for the crown's policy: Archbishop Simon Sudbury, in his other capacity as Lord Chancellor, and Sir Robert Hales, Prior of the Knights Hospitaller and Lord Treasurer, the officer who had overseen the poll tax's actual collection [30].

A separate rebel force — not the same body of men who had gone to Mile End with the king, but a distinct group, numbering perhaps 400 by later estimate — approached the Tower that same morning, while Richard was still occupied at Mile End. The gates opened. No garrison resistance is recorded at all; whether through negligence, sympathy, confusion about who was actually in command with the king absent, or simple unwillingness to defend the fortress against a force that size, the men who should have held the Tower did not [30]. The rebels moved through the fortress largely unopposed, searching room to room for the men they had come for.

Sudbury and Hales were found together in the chapel of St John, within the White Tower — Sudbury, by one chronicle tradition, had just finished celebrating Mass. Along with them the rebels took Brother William Appleton, a Franciscan friar serving as John of Gaunt's personal physician, and John Legge, a royal sergeant-at-arms blamed for devising the poll tax's most invasive enforcement method: physical inspection of households to check for unregistered, and therefore untaxed, young women [31]. All four were dragged from the Tower to Tower Hill, just outside the walls, and beheaded there without any trial of any kind — no royal court, no ecclesiastical tribunal, nothing resembling the due process either man's office would ordinarily have commanded. Sudbury's death reportedly took several strokes of the axe to complete. The heads of all four were set on poles and carried through the city before being fixed to London Bridge, Sudbury's own archbishop's mitre nailed to his skull in place of a normal display spike [31].

In the same rooms, the rebels found Henry of Bolingbroke — Gaunt's son, and the future Henry IV, though neither he nor anyone else in that room could have known that in June 1381. Chronicle tradition holds that he was recognized as Gaunt's heir and about to be killed for it when a royal guard named John Ferrour intervened and successfully argued for his life; the princess and Richard's half-sister were found separately, mocked, and released unharmed [32]. The Tower was thoroughly looted of armour and regalia before the rebels withdrew, though — as at the Savoy — the chronicle tradition again distinguishes deliberate, symbolic violence against specific named men from indiscriminate plunder of the building's contents generally.

Then and Now

The detail that lands hardest, of everything in this chapter, is not the executions themselves but the mundane administrative reason given for John Legge's presence on the list: a man killed specifically for how he had designed the mechanics of a tax inspection, for the exact bureaucratic cruelty of checking whether an unmarried young woman in a household had been properly registered for taxation. Legge did not levy the tax or set its rate. He built the enforcement procedure, and the crowd that found him in the Tower treated that distinction — designing how a policy would be inflicted, rather than deciding that it would exist — as no distinction at all worth sparing him for.

What has not changed, six and a half centuries later, is how quickly an institution's physical security turns out to depend entirely on whether the people guarding it believe defending it is worth their own lives. The Tower of London in June 1381 was, on paper, the single most defensible position in the kingdom. It fell to roughly four hundred men because nobody inside chose to make them prove it would cost them anything to take it.

While the Tower was falling, Richard II rode out to Mile End, an open field east of the city walls, to meet the assembled rebels directly — chiefly, by most accounts, the Essex contingent rather than Tyler's Kentish force, and it is genuinely unclear from the surviving record whether Tyler himself was present at this specific meeting at all [33]. This was the negotiation the failed Greenwich attempt the day before had denied both sides; this time the king actually met the crowd face to face, with only a small bodyguard, and heard their demands directly rather than through an intermediary.

The demands, as the record preserves them, were specific and coherent rather than a vague cry for mercy: the surrender of named officials for punishment (a request already overtaken by events, since Sudbury and Hales were being killed at the Tower at roughly the same hour); the abolition of serfdom and unfree labour service outright; land rent fixed at a low cash rate rather than owed in labour; freedom to buy and sell in open market without the restrictions unfree tenure imposed; and "no law within the realm save the law of Winchester" — a phrase this book's Chapter 3 already flagged as unresolved in its precise meaning, generally read as an appeal to an older, more localized, self-regulating standard of justice rather than a specific identifiable statute [33].

Richard agreed. Charters bearing the royal seal were drawn up on the spot and in the days immediately following, announcing the abolition of serfdom across the realm, and clerks were dispatched to disseminate copies to the assembled crowd and, in principle, to the wider country [34]. It is worth being precise about what the king did and did not concede: he granted the substantive demands — an end to unfree tenure, fixed low rents, free trade — while declining to personally surrender the named officials the rebels wanted punished, instead promising that justice against wrongdoers would be his own to administer going forward. Whether Richard intended these charters as a genuine settlement or as a tactical concession to buy time and disperse the crowd is a question the surviving record cannot answer with confidence — his later conduct, which this book's Chapter 14 follows in full, is far easier to document than his intentions on this particular afternoon.

Whatever the intention, the charters worked as tactics: a substantial part of the Essex rebel force, satisfied with the king's promises and the document now in their hands, began dispersing that evening, heading home rather than remaining in London [34]. The Kentish force under Tyler, considerably less satisfied, and with its own leader apparently absent from this specific negotiation, remained in the city — still hunting John of Gaunt's associates, foreigners, and figures connected to the legal profession, and still, as of that evening, without a settlement of its own.

Then and Now

There is a particular kind of institutional move on display at Mile End that any modern reader will recognize instantly: grant the substantive policy demand while declining to hand over the individuals blamed for the old policy, and offer, instead of accountability, a personal promise to do better going forward. It is a settlement that costs the person granting it very little in the moment and defers the actual reckoning to a later date that may or may not arrive.

What is different, and worth sitting with, is how completely that promise could be revoked once the immediate pressure was gone — a modern charter, statute, or settlement generally survives the crisis that produced it, at least on paper, protected by institutions built specifically to make reversal costly. Richard's charters at Mile End had no such institutional protection at all. Their entire force came from the crowd standing in front of the king when he sealed them, and once that crowd went home, so, within weeks, did the charters.

By the morning of 15 June 1381, Tyler's Kentish force — unsatisfied by the Mile End settlement it had no part in — remained in London, and Richard II agreed to a second meeting, this time at Smithfield, an open market ground just outside the walls beside St Bartholomew's Priory. He went first to Westminster Abbey to pray, then rode to Smithfield in the late afternoon with a royal party of perhaps two hundred men, positioned east of the priory; Tyler's remaining rebel force, numbering in the thousands, massed to the west [35].

The Anonimalle Chronicle preserves the fullest surviving account of what happened when Tyler rode forward to meet the king, and it is worth quoting its substance directly rather than smoothing it into indirect summary, because the specific manner of the exchange matters to what followed. Tyler greeted Richard with a familiarity chroniclers clearly found presumptuous — addressing him as "brother" and promising him friendship — before setting out a further set of demands beyond what Mile End had already granted: a new charter, the abolition of the entire episcopal hierarchy beyond a single bishop for all of England, the redistribution of church wealth among the laity, and a single law for the whole realm [35]. He then, by the chronicle's account, called rudely for drink and rinsed his mouth out in front of the king — a small, specific detail that later readers have taken as evidence of exactly the kind of contemptuous informality that provoked what came next.

An altercation broke out — chroniclers' accounts vary on the precise trigger, whether words exchanged with a member of the royal party or some further gesture from Tyler read as a threat toward the king — and Tyler made a motion that royal attendants took as an assault. Lord Mayor William Walworth struck the first blow, and squire Ralph Standish delivered further wounds that finished the killing [36]. Tyler fell, mortally wounded, in full view of the massed rebel crowd across the field — the man who had led the largest single force in this uprising, cut down within arm's reach of the fourteen-year-old king he had just addressed as "brother."

What happened in the next few minutes decided whether London would erupt into open battle or not, and the record credits it entirely to Richard himself. With the rebel crowd beginning to react to Tyler's fall — nocking arrows, by some accounts, and moving forward — the king rode alone toward them, reportedly declaring some version of "I am your captain and your king, follow me," and led the crowd away from the confrontation toward open fields at Clerkenwell [36]. Whatever combination of genuine composure, quick political instinct, or sheer desperate improvisation produced that ride, it worked: the crowd followed him rather than avenging Tyler on the spot, and Walworth used the interval to summon London's militia in force. By the time reinforcements arrived, the moment for retaliation had already passed.

Then and Now

Tyler's head, like Sudbury's before it, ended up on a pole — the revolt's own logic of display, turned now against its own leader rather than the officials it had killed. Richard knighted Walworth on the spot and, within days, was actively restoring the very order the past week had nearly overturned; the Smithfield confrontation, more than any single moment before it, is where the revolt's momentum visibly reverses.

What is genuinely striking, and difficult to reconstruct with confidence from the record, is the calculation a fourteen-year-old boy made in the seconds after watching a man he had just been negotiating with killed a few feet away from him. Riding alone toward an armed, furious crowd of thousands is not an obviously safe choice under any modern crisis-management framework; it is closer to a bet, made instantly, with no time to consult anyone. It happened to pay off. This book cannot tell its reader what Richard was actually thinking in that moment, because no source records it — only that he did it, and that it worked.

London was never the whole of 1381, even though it produces most of this book's most dramatic single scenes. News of the capital's fall traveled fast, and it triggered independent, locally driven risings across a wide arc of England, each with its own specific target and its own local grievance layered on top of the shared complaint about tax and tenure. At St Albans, Hertfordshire, townsmen under William Grindecobbe had been in long-running dispute with their own Benedictine abbey over feudal privileges the abbey held over the town; on hearing of London's fall on 13 June, Grindecobbe traveled there himself, secured Wat Tyler's personal authorization for direct action against the abbey, and returned home to lead the opening of the abbey's prison and the burning of its manorial records in the town square, forcing Abbot Thomas de la Mare to grant a charter of civic rights within days [37].

At Cambridge, on 19 June, the target was the University itself — a corporate body enjoying royal privileges townspeople had long resented. Corpus Christi College was ransacked, and the University's library and archives were burned in the town square, an act one chronicle records accompanied by the cry "Away with the learning of clerks, away with it," with a local woman, Margery Starre, remembered by name as one of those leading the burning [38]. The specific choice of target — records and books, not people — echoes the Temple's burned legal documents in London: an attack on the documentary apparatus of privilege and obligation, not simply on wealth or persons.

The clearest single illustration of what burning a record actually meant to the people doing it comes from St Albans, and it involves stone rather than parchment. The abbey held a monopoly on milling grain within the town, enforced in part by confiscating villagers' own hand-querns -- small household millstones that let a family grind its own grain without paying the abbey's mill -- and had then, in what reads as a calculated piece of institutional gloating, had the confiscated stones set permanently into the paving of the abbot's own parlour floor, to be walked over daily as a standing reminder of whose monopoly this was [53]. In 1381 the St Albans townsmen dug the stones back up, smashed them, and distributed the fragments so that a share went to as many households as possible -- a division of a formerly humiliating object into individually owned pieces that at least one modern account reads as a deliberate echo of the distribution of blessed bread at Mass. The reversal did not last: once royal authority was restored, the rebels were compelled, on 12 July 1381, to bring back every stone they could recover and reset it in the parlour floor exactly where it had been taken from -- how many of the original stones actually came back, whole or in fragments, is not precisely recorded [53].

Read against that, the destruction that ran through this book's account of London and its outlying towns -- the Temple's legal archive, Cambridge's university library and charters, the manorial rolls at St Albans itself, and (in its own way) the Savoy's silver, destroyed rather than looted -- shared a single logic: destroy the record and the burden of proof does not disappear; it shifts, at least for a while, onto living memory and disputed testimony, ground where a tenant facing a lord's steward had, for the first time, something closer to an even footing.

East Anglia saw the most sustained and, ultimately, most violently suppressed regional rising. In Suffolk, John Wrawe — a chaplain — led forces that stormed Bury St Edmunds Abbey; Prior John Cambridge fled but was caught two days later and beheaded, and Sir John Cavendish, Chief Justice of King's Bench and Chancellor of the University of Cambridge, was tracked down at Lakenheath and killed [39]. In Norfolk, a dyer named Geoffrey Litster assembled rebel forces outside Norwich around 17 June. Henry Despenser, Bishop of Norwich — a former soldier before he was a churchman — responded without waiting for any royal commission: marching from Stamford with a small force of men-at-arms and archers, he relieved rebel-held Peterborough - which had risen in the same wave as Bury St Edmunds, though this book has not traced its own local rising in detail - reclaimed Cambridge and then Norwich, and met Litster's army directly at North Walsham around 25 to 26 June, defeating it decisively [40].

Despenser executed Litster and other captured rebels on the spot, without the formal trial a royal commission would ordinarily have provided — technically outside his own legal authority as a bishop rather than a royal officer, but never punished for the irregularity given how the engagement ended. His campaign was the only regional suppression carried out by direct military engagement rather than by the slower machinery of royal commissions and courts that would deal with the rest of the country over the following weeks [40]. Further west, unrest reached as far as Bridgwater in Somerset, where local risings targeted an Augustinian house and a royal official's property in patterns recognizably similar to what had already happened at Bury St Edmunds and St Albans — the same underlying grammar of grievance, expressed independently in a region the London events had reached only as distant news.

Then and Now

What the risings outside London share, more than any single tactic, is a preference for burning the record over burning the person or even the building wholesale — manorial rolls at St Albans, university archives at Cambridge, legal documents at the Temple in London itself. A modern reader used to thinking of unrest as directed at property or at people might miss how much of 1381's violence was aimed, quite precisely, at paper: the specific artifacts that made an obligation provable, re-assertable, and enforceable against a community that could not read them for itself.

What hasn't changed is how unevenly the state's response to unrest scales with how directly that unrest is confronted. Bishop Despenser, acting entirely on his own initiative with a handful of soldiers, ended East Anglia's rising in a single battle before London had even finished restoring its own order. The rest of the country would wait weeks longer for the same outcome, delivered not by a sword but by commissions, courts, and the much slower machinery this book's next chapter follows.

Royal authority reasserted itself within days of Tyler's death, and it moved faster and more thoroughly than the revolt itself had. Robert Knolles, Nicholas Brembre, and Robert Launde were appointed to restore order in London immediately; a force of roughly four thousand soldiers was mustered and dispatched into the counties still in unrest [41]. On 20 June, barely five days after Smithfield, Thomas of Woodstock — the king's uncle — and Robert Tresilian, the newly appointed Chief Justice, received a special commission covering Essex, with military support behind them, empowered to try captured rebels directly rather than through the ordinary machinery of the courts. Tresilian's commission conducted mass trials at Chelmsford; one widely cited figure puts the executions there at around thirty, though the precise count varies across sources, and Tresilian went on to St Albans for further trials, including that of William Grindecobbe, using methods later historians have criticized as legally coercive [41].

By November 1381, when Parliament formally reviewed the crisis, at least 1,500 people were dead by execution across the country — a figure that does not include those killed during the fighting itself, only those put to death afterward by royal justice [42]. The scale of that number is worth sitting with against the revolt's own body count of named officials: Sudbury, Hales, Appleton, Legge, Lyons, Cavendish, Prior Cambridge — a handful of specific men, killed in specific circumstances the rebels themselves could each explain. The crown's reckoning killed roughly two hundred times that many people, most of them never individually named in any surviving account this book can point its reader to.

The Mile End charters did not survive the reassertion of order. On 30 June, the king ordered serfs and villein tenants back into their previous service conditions; by 2 July, the charters granting the abolition of serfdom were formally revoked [43]. Whatever Richard's actual intentions had been at Mile End — genuine concession or tactical concession bought under duress, a question this book's Chapter 11 already flagged as unresolvable from the surviving record — his later conduct removed the ambiguity retroactively. A general pardon was eventually extended to most participants, but explicitly excluded certain categories: the men of Bury St Edmunds specifically, anyone involved in the killing of the king's officials, and fugitives still on the run from prison at the time of the pardon [44].

John Ball, the last major named leader still at large, was caught at Coventry on 13 July 1381, tried the following day, and executed — hanged, drawn, and quartered — on 15 July, reportedly with Richard II himself present [45]. John Wrawe, tried separately, attempted to buy leniency by giving evidence against roughly two dozen of his own former associates; the evidence was accepted, and Wrawe was executed anyway, on 6 May 1382, the following spring. By that point the last regional pockets of unrest — a civic dispute in York, explained more fully in this book's Glossary, chief among them — were also being wound down, though full order was not restored there until 1382, and isolated property-compensation disputes stemming from the revolt were still being resolved in the courts as late as 1387.

Tradition attributes to Richard II, in an encounter with Essex petitioners asking him to honour the Mile End promises after the charters' revocation, a line chroniclers report but which this book cannot verify as a verbatim quotation: that the petitioners were "rustics" then and would remain "rustics" still, held now not as before but "incomparably" more harshly [54]. Whether Richard said anything close to those words is unconfirmable from the surviving record -- the line appears in chronicle tradition marked, even there, as reported rather than witnessed speech -- but the sentiment it expresses is not in doubt at all, because the documented policy that followed the revocation matches it exactly: not a return to the pre-1381 status quo, but a deliberate reassertion of lordly control harder than what had existed before the revolt began.

Then and Now

There is a specific, cold mathematics to how this ended that deserves to be stated plainly rather than folded into a general narrative of "suppression": roughly 1,500 executed by the state against a handful of officials killed by the rebels, a ratio that looks less like justice restoring balance and more like a lesson being deliberately overtaught. Wrawe's fate — giving up his own associates and dying anyway — is the starkest single data point in that arithmetic: cooperation with the reckoning bought him nothing at all.

What has not changed since 1381 is how much faster and more efficiently organized state violence can be than the popular unrest it responds to, once the state actually decides to apply itself. The revolt took roughly six weeks, county by county, village by village, to build the momentum it reached at its height. The reckoning against it, once Smithfield removed the one obstacle in its way, needed barely a month to kill more people than the entire uprising had, in all its violence, managed across its whole span.

This book has, up to now, called the events of the summer of 1381 "the revolt," largely for convenience, and it owes its reader an accounting of that choice before closing, because the name itself is contested and the contest is not a trivial one. No contemporary chronicle gives the rising a single collective title at all. "The Insurrection of Wat Tyler" was the common eighteenth- and nineteenth-century label; "the Peasants' Revolt" is a later coinage, and the historian Paul Strohm, searching for its origin, traced an early use to J. R. Green's Short History of the English People (1874) without being able to identify who actually coined the phrase [46]. Modern historians, including Strohm and Miri Rubin, have criticized the name on two separate grounds: many participants were demonstrably not peasants in any technical sense — clergy like Ball, urban artisans, London tradesmen, and at least one former member of Parliament appear among named participants — and "revolt" implies a coherence and singular purpose that the actual, county-by-county, sometimes locally distinct pattern of unrest does not fully support [46]. Alternative labels — "the Great Revolt," "the English Rising of 1381," simply "1381" — each solve one problem and leave another; this book has used "the revolt" and "1381" as convenient shorthand throughout, not as a claim that either name settles what the event actually was.

That naming dispute is not pedantry. It maps directly onto a genuine, long-running historiographical disagreement about how much the events of this book actually accomplished, and that disagreement is where this final chapter spends most of its attention.

Three broad schools of interpretation are worth naming plainly, because a reader encountering any single popular account of 1381 is likely encountering one of them dressed as the whole truth. An older, largely Whig-constitutional reading treated the revolt as a genuine milestone in England's long walk toward representative liberty and the end of feudal bondage — Richard's Mile End promise, even revoked, read as an idea that could not be fully put back in its box once granted. Rodney Hilton's Marxist social history, Bond Men Made Free (1973), reframed the same events as a class conflict continuous with earlier peasant movements across Europe, reading 1381 as one instance of a recurring structural struggle between peasant producers and a landholding class rather than a uniquely English constitutional drama [47]. Steven Justice's Writing and Rebellion (1994), already discussed in Chapter 5, represents a later, more textually and archivally grounded turn: less interested in what the revolt proves about class conflict in the abstract, and more interested in what its participants' own surviving words show about their literacy, organization, and self-understanding [47].

The economic historian M. M. Postan offered a considerably more deflationary reading still, describing the uprising, in a phrase later historians have returned to repeatedly, as in many ways a "passing episode" — dramatic in the moment, but not the hinge on which England's subsequent social and economic development actually turned [48]. Postan's own scholarship on medieval demography and labour supply supports a specific claim this book can state with more confidence than it can state which historiographical school is "right": villeinage's decline over the following century tracks primarily with continued labour scarcity, rising rural wages, and lords' growing preference for cash rents and leasehold arrangements over compulsory labour service — economic forces already visible before 1381 and continuing on much the same trajectory after it, largely independent of whether the revolt had happened at all [48].

What the revolt does appear to have changed, with more confidence, is narrower but real. No English government levied another poll tax in anything like the 1381 form for over six hundred years — not until 1990, when the Community Charge, popularly nicknamed the "poll tax" by opponents specifically to invoke this book's own subject, provoked widespread refusal and, eventually, its own political collapse [49]. The revolt also appears to have permanently altered how English governments approached raising war revenue more broadly: subsequent parliaments, wary of provoking a repeat, leaned more heavily on other forms of taxation, one of several factors historians credit with constraining England's capacity to fund the remaining century of the Hundred Years' War at the scale earlier campaigns had achieved [49]. And the revolt has had a long afterlife entirely independent of its immediate legal consequences, adopted repeatedly across the centuries since as a symbol for movements far removed from fourteenth-century villeinage — the nineteenth-century socialist and writer William Morris drew on it explicitly in his own utopian fiction, and it remains, in Britain, a standing reference point for popular resistance to taxation perceived as unfair, invoked again during the 1980s Community Charge debates by name [49].

None of that resolves the Whig/Marxist/revisionist disagreement this chapter opened with, and this book will not pretend it can. What can be said plainly is this: a tax collector was refused in an Essex village on 30 May 1381, and six weeks later an archbishop was dead, a king had negotiated in the street twice, and the largest crowd London had assembled in living memory had come and gone — and on the narrow, specific question of whether any of that changed the law of villeinage in England, the honest answer is that it did not, not directly, not durably, and not for reasons the revolt itself controlled. What changed instead was slower, economic, already underway, and would very likely have arrived regardless. That gap — between how much the summer of 1381 felt like a hinge and how little of the actual legal record turned on it — is, more than any single scene this book has described, the real subject of the whole account.

One irony belongs on the record before this book closes, and it involves the boy this book has followed at Mile End and Smithfield. Richard II survived the summer of 1381; he did not survive his own kingship. Two decades of increasingly autocratic rule and open feuding with his own magnates culminated, in 1399, in his confiscation of the Lancastrian inheritance from the exiled Henry Bolingbroke -- the same Henry of Bolingbroke this book's Chapter 10 found, as a boy of fourteen, nearly killed in the Tower for being John of Gaunt's son and heir [32]. Bolingbroke returned from exile, deposed Richard, and took the throne as Henry IV. Richard was moved to Pontefract Castle before the end of 1399 and died there, probably in mid-February 1400; the official account holds that he starved, though chroniclers disagree on whether that starvation was self-inflicted despair, deliberate neglect by his captors, or something closer to a quieter version of the executions this book has already described in full. No source this book treats as reliable settles which [55]. Deposed by the son of the man whose burning house he had once watched from the Tower, Richard died at roughly thirty-three, having outlived the crisis that made him briefly, genuinely heroic, but not the one that came after.

And on the narrow, material question this book has returned to throughout -- who owed what to whom, and on what proof -- the poll tax and, eventually, villeinage itself did give way, but not because the rebels' Mile End demands were ever honored: not from Richard, not on any charter's authority, and not for reasons the revolt itself controlled. The poll tax was never levied again in its 1381 form. Villeinage did not survive the following century intact, not because any king signed it away, but because the parchment recording who owed what could not, in the end, out-argue a labour shortage: lords who tried to enforce the old rolls against tenants who now had somewhere else to go simply watched those tenants leave. The document that had once proven a man's unfreedom for life turned out, over a hundred years rather than six weeks, to matter less than whether anyone was left to enforce it.

Then and Now

The 1990 Community Charge's opponents reaching back six centuries for a nickname is, in its way, the most direct proof this book can offer that 1381 never actually ended: a government tried a flat, per-head tax again, and the public reflex to name it after this book's own subject was instant and required no explanation to land. That kind of durability — a specific historical grievance surviving as usable political shorthand for six hundred years — is not something most events, however dramatic in the moment, ever achieve.

What is harder to sit with, and what this chapter has tried to state without softening, is how little the actual participants of 1381 got, in the terms they themselves asked for, in exchange for everything this book has described. Wat Tyler, John Ball, and roughly 1,500 executed afterward did not win the abolition of serfdom; serfdom wound down anyway, later, for reasons largely indifferent to their sacrifice. A modern reader wants events to earn their outcomes. This one, on the most careful reading the record supports, did not — and that is a harder thing to leave a reader with than a tidier story would be, but it is the honest one.

What Survives

Nearly everything this book has told its reader rests on a small number of surviving texts, none of them written by anyone who fought at Blackheath, burned the Savoy, or stood in the crowd at Smithfield. It is worth closing with a plain account of what those texts actually are, because the confidence of a narrative account like this one can too easily obscure how thin, in absolute terms, the surviving documentary base for 1381 really is.

The Anonimalle Chronicle, an Anglo-Norman and Middle-English chronicle associated with St Mary's Abbey, York, supplies this book's fullest single narrative source for the revolt's central events -- Tyler's election at Maidstone, the dialogue at Smithfield -- but modern scholarship treats it as a secondary compilation, assembled close to the events rather than a strict eyewitness account, and its own manuscript history is itself a specialist field of study.

Henry Knighton's Chronicon, written by an Augustinian canon of Leicester Abbey, and Thomas Walsingham's Historia Anglicana, written at St Albans -- the same abbey whose own townsmen this book's Chapter 13 follows in open revolt -- are the two other principal English narrative sources, each with its own institutional vantage point and its own visible biases: Walsingham in particular writing with open hostility toward John Ball and an evident interest in tying him to Wycliffe. Jean Froissart's Chroniques adds a French, more distant perspective, valuable for its wider context and its illuminated manuscript illustrations of the rising, but written well after the fact and treated by historians with more caution on points of specific detail.

Beyond the narrative chronicles, a genuinely different kind of source survives: the administrative record. The poll tax return membranes themselves -- the actual parchment rolls recording, community by community, who was assessed and what they owed in 1377, 1379, and 1381 -- are held today at The National Archives, Kew, catalogued under series E 179, and their modern edition, compiled across three decades by Carolyn Fenwick, is the single resource that made this book's Chapter 4 analysis of evasion and undercounting possible at all. Separately, the King's Bench indictment rolls and gaol delivery records of the post-revolt trials -- the paperwork of the reckoning this book's Chapter 14 describes -- preserve names, like Johanna Ferrour's, that appear in no chronicle narrative whatsoever; without the court record, entire participants in the events of June 1381 would have left no trace at all.

The six short rebel letters this book's Chapter 5 discussed at length -- preserved only because Knighton and Walsingham happened to copy them into their own chronicles rather than because anyone thought to preserve the originals -- are, in a real sense, the closest this book can come to the rebels' own words in their own hand, surviving by the same accident of hostile chroniclers' curiosity that preserved so much else in this account. Nothing written by Wat Tyler survives at all, in his own words, from any source. Everything this book has told its reader about what he actually said comes down through the mouths and pens of men who were, at best, watching him from the other side of an argument he lost.

1 — Villein tenure and the open-field system

Christopher Dyer, Making a Living in the Middle Ages: The People of Britain, 850–1520 (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2002). Dyer's economic history of medieval Britain sets out the basic mechanics of manorial tenure: villein status tied a household's land to fixed, unpaid labour service on the lord's demesne, restrictions on leaving the manor, and marriage fees, distinct from free tenure; the open-field system organized cultivation in scattered strips across two or three large fields worked in rotation, with roughly a third left fallow each year under the three-field system that had spread across midland England by 1300.

Cited in: Chapter 1.

2 — Week-work, boon-work, merchet, leyrwite, and heriot

Christopher Dyer, Making a Living in the Middle Ages (Yale University Press, 2002), on the specific customary payments and labour obligations that marked unfree tenure: routine "week-work" (typically two to three days per week on the demesne), seasonal "boon-work" (additional labour demanded at harvest and other peak periods), a merchet fee for a lord's permission when a villein's child married, a leyrwite fine for a child born out of wedlock, and a heriot — the lord's right to the best beast of a deceased tenant's estate, collected from the heir.

Cited in: Chapter 1.

3 — Villein legal status and manorial jurisdiction

Paul R. Hyams, King, Lords and Peasants in Medieval England: The Common Law of Villeinage in the Twelfth and Thirteenth Centuries (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1980). Hyams's study of the common law's treatment of villeinage documents that royal courts generally refused to hear a villein's suit against his own lord, leaving manorial courts (presided over by the lord's steward) as the effective forum for most disputes; the treatise attributed to the thirteenth-century justice Henry de Bracton discusses a lord's right to recover a fled villein and the narrow, procedurally specific circumstances (an uncontested long absence "without writ") under which a fugitive villein could begin to acquire standing to appear in court as though free.

Cited in: Chapter 1.

4 — Population growth and land pressure before 1348

Bruce M. S. Campbell, English Seigniorial Agriculture, 1250–1450 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2000), Cambridge Studies in Historical Geography no. 31. Campbell's national-scale study of medieval English demesne agriculture documents the sustained population growth of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, land pressure that pushed marginal, poor-quality land into cultivation as better land ran out, and the resulting strain on the food supply by the early fourteenth century — a population level, by most modern estimates, not matched again in England until the eighteenth century.

Cited in: Chapter 1.

5 — The Great Famine, 1315–1317

William Chester Jordan, The Great Famine: Northern Europe in the Early Fourteenth Century (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1996). Jordan's study — awarded the Medieval Academy of America's Haskins Medal — documents the catastrophic run of wet growing seasons across northern Europe from 1315 to roughly 1317 (with effects lingering into the early 1320s in some regions), and the resulting mass mortality and nutritional crisis that struck a population already under strain from decades of growth against a fixed land base.

Cited in: Chapter 1.

6 — The Black Death's arrival and mortality in England, 1348–1350

Ole J. Benedictow, The Black Death 1346–1353: The Complete History (Woodbridge: Boydell Press, 2004). Benedictow's study — the most exhaustive quantitative reconstruction of the epidemic's spread and death toll — traces the plague's landfall in England to the summer of 1348, with chroniclers disagreeing on the exact date and port (Melcombe/Weymouth, Bristol, and Southampton are all named candidates), and its rapid spread inland over the following year. Modern estimates converge on roughly 40 to 60 percent of England's population dead in the first wave (1348–1350), reconstructed from manorial records and ecclesiastical vacancy registers rather than any contemporary national count.

Cited in: Chapter 2.

7 — The Ordinance of Labourers, 18 June 1349

The royal ordinance, issued while the first wave of plague was still active in parts of England, ordered every able-bodied person under sixty not otherwise occupied in a trade or self-sufficient farming to accept work when offered, at the wage rates prevailing in 1346 or the several years before — not whatever a labour-scarce market would now bear — and made both refusing work and abandoning a position before an agreed term punishable. See the Ordinance of Labourers (1349) as printed and discussed in standard collections of English statutes; its provisions and dating are not seriously disputed among historians, unlike some of the events later in this book.

Cited in: Chapter 2.

8 — The Statute of Labourers, February 1351

Passed at the first parliament held after the Black Death specifically in response to complaints that wages had risen "out of singular covetousness," the statute elaborated the 1349 ordinance in far greater enforcement detail: graduated fines, imprisonment, and seizure of goods for labourers taking more than the old rate, and, on paper, for employers offering it, enforced through a new local office of justices of labourers drawn from the county gentry. See the Statute of Labourers (1351) as printed and discussed in standard collections of English statutes.

Cited in: Chapter 2.

9 — Selective enforcement and the Statute of Labourers as a named grievance behind the 1381 revolt

The pattern of enforcement falling disproportionately on wage labourers and smallholders rather than the larger employers who also broke the wage cap to secure scarce workers is documented in the surviving justices-of-labourers court records and discussed in the historiography of the statute's social effects (see, e.g., the discussion of enforcement bias in Bertha Haven Putnam, The Enforcement of the Statutes of Labourers During the First Decade After the Black Death, 1349–1359 (New York: Columbia University, Studies in History, Economics and Public Law, vol. XXXII, 1908), still the foundational court-record study of the statute's early enforcement). Contemporary chroniclers writing about the 1381 revolt name resentment of the labour statutes among the standing grievances behind the rising, alongside the poll tax.

Cited in: Chapter 2.

10 — The Great Rumour, 1377: ancient demesne and Domesday Book

Rosamond Faith, "The 'Great Rumour' of 1377 and Peasant Ideology," in R. H. Hilton and T. H. Aston, eds., The English Rising of 1381 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1984), pp. 43–74. Faith's study of the 1377 protest movement documents tenants across roughly forty manors in south-eastern and south-western England refusing labour services on the legal theory that their manors qualified as "ancient demesne" — land Domesday Book (1086) recorded as held directly by the Crown, entitling tenants to lighter, fixed customary obligations — and pursuing the claim through petitions to Parliament and suits in the royal courts rather than through direct action alone.

Cited in: Chapter 3.

11 — The Great Rumour's failure and its continuity with 1381

Rosamond Faith, "The 'Great Rumour' of 1377 and Peasant Ideology" (in Hilton and Aston, eds., The English Rising of 1381, Cambridge University Press, 1984, pp. 43–74), argues that the 1377 ancient demesne claims were rejected in the courts and Parliament with no settlement in tenants' favor, but that the episode demonstrates a peasantry already capable of coordinated, multi-manor, documentary legal argument well before 1381 -- evidence against reading the later revolt as sudden radicalization caused solely by the poll tax. Several counties active in the Great Rumour, including Essex, were also early participants in the 1381 rising; the precise meaning of the 1381 rebels' demand at Mile End for "the law of Winchester" remains debated among historians, but is generally read as a similar appeal to an older, supposedly more just legal order.

Cited in: Chapter 3.

12 — The financial cost of the Hundred Years' War in the late 1370s

Standard accounts of English royal finance in this period document garrison costs at Calais and Brest running into the tens of thousands of pounds annually, and individual military expeditions consuming comparable sums in a matter of months, against a backdrop of French raids reaching the English coast in the summer of 1377; Parliament's November 1380 request was for roughly £160,000. See, e.g., the financial background summarized in J. J. N. Palmer, England, France and Christendom, 1377–99 (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1972), on the fiscal pressures of the period's war effort.

Cited in: Chapter 4.

13 — The 1377 and 1379 poll taxes: rates and yields

The 1377 poll tax levied a flat groat (4 pence) on every lay person over fourteen, excepting genuine beggars, raising approximately £22,000; the 1379 tax replaced the flat rate with a graduated scale across roughly seven social bands (from 6s. 8d. at the top to 4d. at the bottom), raising only about £18,600 against a hoped-for £50,000 amid widespread evasion and under-assessment. See the summary of rates and yields in Carolyn C. Fenwick, ed., The Poll Taxes of 1377, 1379 and 1381 (Records of Social and Economic History, new series, British Academy/Oxford University Press, 3 vols., 1998–2005), the standard modern edition of the surviving return membranes.

Cited in: Chapter 4.

14 — The 1381 poll tax: rate, local averaging, and the evasion gap against 1377

Carolyn C. Fenwick, ed., The Poll Taxes of 1377, 1379 and 1381 (Records of Social and Economic History, new series, British Academy/Oxford University Press, 3 vols., 1998–2005). Fenwick's edition of the surviving return membranes documents the 1381 tax's structure -- a nominal flat shilling per person over fifteen with a local-averaging rule (a community's per-head average had to reach 12 pence, with no individual paying more than roughly twenty times the minimum) -- and shows that recorded taxpayer counts in 1381 fall, in some counties, to only around two-thirds of the equivalent 1377 figures for the same communities, a gap too large to be explained by the one-year change in minimum taxable age alone. Historians read the shortfall chiefly as organized local evasion (notably the under-registration of unmarried young women) rather than genuine population decline, consistent with the Crown's own response: royal commissioners dispatched to the counties in March 1381 specifically to reinvestigate returns and press local officials for the missing names.

Cited in: Chapter 4.

15 — John Ball's excommunication and disciplinary record

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1983), the standard documentary sourcebook, prints and discusses the surviving ecclesiastical record of Ball's discipline: excommunicated by Archbishop Simon Islip between roughly 1362 and 1366, with the sentence subsequently confirmed by Archbishop Simon Langham (1366) and again referenced under Archbishop Simon Sudbury in December 1376 for Ball's continuing "manifest contumacy."

Cited in: Chapter 5.

16 — Ball's imprisonment at Maidstone, spring 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), discusses the record placing Ball in the Archbishop of Canterbury's prison at Maidstone, Kent, probably from around the end of April 1381, and the chroniclers' differing accounts of whether he was freed by the crowd when the prison was broken open in early June or had already regained some liberty beforehand.

Cited in: Chapter 5.

17 — The Wycliffe/Lollard association as chronicler polemic

Modern historiography (surveyed in, e.g., the introduction to R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed., Macmillan, 1983) treats Thomas Walsingham's linking of Ball to John Wycliffe and the Lollard movement with skepticism: Ball's documented excommunication predates Wycliffe's rise to real prominence as a controversial Oxford theologian by roughly a decade, making a direct doctrinal-recruitment narrative chronologically awkward, and the association is generally read as a polemical convenience letting a hostile chronicler discredit both a popular preacher and an academic heretic by treating them as one movement.

Cited in: Chapter 5.

18 — The Blackheath sermon and the "When Adam delved" couplet

The couplet is widely attributed to Ball's Blackheath preaching (12 June 1381) across the chronicle tradition and later popular memory, but the fullest surviving text of a sermon built around it is a later chronicler's reconstruction of the occasion's rhetoric rather than a verbatim transcript taken at the scene -- a distinction discussed in the historiography surveyed in R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983). This book treats the couplet's attribution to Ball as well-established tradition and the surrounding sermon text as reconstructed rather than verbatim.

Cited in: Chapter 5.

19 — The rebel letters preserved by Knighton and Walsingham

Steven Justice, Writing and Rebellion: England in 1381 (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1994). Justice's study focuses on six short, cryptic Middle English letters preserved in the chronicles of Henry Knighton and Thomas Walsingham, ascribed to figures including "Jack Milner," "Jack Carter," "Jack Trewman," and John Ball, several referencing William Langland's contemporary poem Piers Plowman by name. Justice argues the letters demonstrate a rural population with real, functional literacy and a shared vernacular textual culture, organizing and communicating in writing rather than through rumour alone -- evidence, in his reading, against treating the rising as an inarticulate, purely spontaneous eruption.

Cited in: Chapter 5.

20 — Fobbing, 30 May 1381, and the Bampton identity gap

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), prints the relevant record of the Fobbing confrontation and the killing of officials that followed. The commissioner's identity is inconsistently given across secondary literature -- "John" and "Thomas" Bampton both appear -- and this book treats his precise identity and formal commission status as a genuine, unresolved gap rather than asserting a first name the record does not clearly settle.

Cited in: Chapter 6.

21 — The Brentwood recall (1 June) and the Bocking assembly (4 June)

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the commissioner's summons of village representatives back to Brentwood on 1 June 1381 to account for the tax shortfall, and the subsequent coordinated Essex rebel assembly at Bocking on or around 4 June -- evidence the unrest had moved from a single village's confrontation to organized regional coordination within days.

Cited in: Chapter 6.

22 — The Kent rising: Robert Belling, Dartford, Maidstone, and Rochester Castle

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), prints and discusses the record of the Kentish rising's opening days: a crowd gathering at Dartford around 5-6 June 1381 in connection with the arrest of Robert Belling, the subsequent storming of Maidstone's prison, and the surrender of Rochester Castle without recorded resistance, releasing its prisoners in turn.

Cited in: Chapter 7.

23 — The election of Wat Tyler at Maidstone, 7 June 1381

The Anonimalle Chronicle (see the discussion and translated excerpts in R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed., Macmillan, 1983) is the source for Tyler's election as leader of the Kentish rebels at a mass gathering in Maidstone on 7 June 1381. Nothing in the surviving record independently corroborates the later chroniclers' claims about Tyler's origin (Essex or Kent) or his supposed military service as an archer in France; this book treats those details as unconfirmed tradition.

Cited in: Chapter 7.

24 — Canterbury, 10 June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the Kentish rebels' entry into Canterbury and its castle without resistance on 10 June, the compelled oath of loyalty "to the true commons of England" sworn by the cathedral monks in Archbishop Sudbury's absence, and the opening of the castle prison with at least one summary execution recorded.

Cited in: Chapter 7.

25 — Crowd size estimates at Blackheath

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), surveys the wide range of chronicle figures for the assembled rebel force (as high as 50,000-60,000) against modern historians' considerably more conservative estimates, closer to 10,000 or somewhat more for the combined Kentish and Essex forces at peak, based on what the relevant counties' actual fourteenth-century population could plausibly supply.

Cited in: Chapter 8.

26 — The failed Greenwich negotiation, 13 June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents Richard II's barge journey to Greenwich on the morning of 13 June 1381 to meet the rebel leadership, and his decision, on his councillors' advice, not to go ashore once he observed the scale of the assembled crowd -- the meeting did not take place, and the king returned to the Tower while the rebels resumed their advance on London. The specific reasoning behind the councillors' advice is not preserved in detail the record can independently verify.

Cited in: Chapter 8.

27 — London Bridge, Aldgate, and the prison-breaking of 13 June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the rebels' entry into London on 13 June 1381 -- the Kentish force across London Bridge (its defenses opened from inside by sympathetic Londoners) and the Essex force through Aldgate -- and the same-day breaking-open of the Marshalsea, Fleet, and Newgate prisons and damage to the Temple's legal records.

Cited in: Chapter 9.

28 — The destruction of the Savoy Palace

Henry Knighton's Chronicon (see the translated excerpts and discussion in R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed., Macmillan, 1983) records the destroyed quantity of silver plate at the Savoy Palace -- "such quantities of vessels and silver plate, without counting the parcel-gilt and solid gold, that five carts would hardly suffice to carry them" -- and states explicitly that the goods were destroyed (burned or thrown into the Thames) rather than stolen, including the tradition that a rebel caught pocketing a piece of silver was killed by his fellow rebels for it. Historians generally read the Savoy's destruction as deliberate, targeted punishment of the absent John of Gaunt rather than opportunistic looting.

Cited in: Chapter 9.

29 — The killing of Flemish immigrants, 13-14 June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), prints chronicle accounts of the mass killing of Flemish residents of London, including a figure of roughly forty executed in one ward and thirty-five killed after taking refuge in the church of St Martin Vintry. Rodney Hilton, Bond Men Made Free: Medieval Peasant Movements and the English Rising of 1381 (London: Methuen, 1973; new introduction by Christopher Dyer, Routledge, 2003), suggests the violence may have been coordinated in part by London's weavers' guilds in economic competition with Flemish textile workers -- offered by Hilton as an interpretation of likely motive, not as an independently documented fact, and presented here as such.

30 — The Tower's fall, 14 June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents Richard II's departure for Mile End on the morning of 14 June leaving Sudbury, Hales, and other officials in the Tower, and the entry of a separate rebel force of roughly 400 into the fortress that same morning without recorded resistance from its garrison.

Cited in: Chapter 10.

31 — The killings of Sudbury, Hales, Appleton, and Legge

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), prints the chronicle record of Archbishop Simon Sudbury and Treasurer Robert Hales being found in the Tower's chapel of St John, taken with William Appleton (John of Gaunt's physician) and John Legge (a royal sergeant blamed for devising the poll tax's household-inspection enforcement method) to Tower Hill, and beheaded without trial -- Sudbury's execution reportedly requiring several strokes -- with all four heads subsequently displayed on London Bridge, Sudbury's own mitre nailed to his skull.

Cited in: Chapter 10.

32 — Henry of Bolingbroke's near-killing and the royal women's release

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), records the chronicle tradition that Henry of Bolingbroke -- John of Gaunt's fourteen-year-old son, later Henry IV -- was found in the Tower and nearly killed as Gaunt's heir before a royal guard, John Ferrour, intervened on his behalf, and that Richard's mother and half-sister were found separately, mocked, but released unharmed.

Cited in: Chapter 10.

33 — The Mile End meeting and rebel demands, 14 June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), prints the fullest surviving record of the Mile End meeting and its demands: surrender of named officials for punishment, abolition of serfdom and unfree labour service, fixed low cash rents, free trade, and "no law within the realm save the law of Winchester." Dobson and other historians note it is uncertain who specifically spoke for the rebels at this meeting and whether Wat Tyler was present at all, since Tyler's own Kentish force appears to have remained separately engaged elsewhere in the city that day.

Cited in: Chapter 11.

34 — The Mile End charters and their immediate effect

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the sealed charters announcing the abolition of serfdom drawn up during and immediately after the Mile End meeting, Richard II's refusal to personally surrender the named officials the rebels sought (a demand already overtaken by events at the Tower), and the dispersal of a substantial part of the Essex rebel force that same evening, satisfied with the charters, while Tyler's Kentish force remained in London unsatisfied.

Cited in: Chapter 11.

35 — The Smithfield meeting, 15 June 1381

The Anonimalle Chronicle (translated and discussed in R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed., Macmillan, 1983) is the fullest surviving source for the Smithfield meeting: Richard II's prayer at Westminster Abbey beforehand, the royal party of roughly 200 positioned east of St Bartholomew's Priory against thousands of rebels massed to the west, Tyler's overly familiar greeting of Richard as "brother," his additional demands (a new charter, abolition of the episcopal hierarchy beyond a single bishop, redistribution of church wealth, one law for the realm), and his rinsing his mouth out with drink in front of the king.

Cited in: Chapter 12.

36 — The death of Wat Tyler and Richard's ride to Clerkenwell

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the chroniclers' varying but broadly consistent accounts of the Smithfield altercation: Lord Mayor William Walworth striking the first blow against Tyler, squire Ralph Standish delivering the fatal further wounds, and Richard II's ride alone toward the reacting rebel crowd, reportedly declaring himself their captain and king and leading them to Clerkenwell Fields, defusing the immediate threat of violence while Walworth summoned London's militia. The king's own reasoning in the moment is not preserved in any source this book treats as reliable.

Cited in: Chapter 12.

37 — St Albans: William Grindecobbe and the rising against the abbey

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the St Albans rising: William Grindecobbe's journey to London on hearing of the capital's fall, his securing Wat Tyler's authorization for direct action, and the subsequent opening of the abbey prison and burning of manorial records in the town square (13-16 June 1381), forcing Abbot Thomas de la Mare to grant a charter of civic rights.

Cited in: Chapter 13.

38 — Cambridge, 19 June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the Cambridge rising against the University's privileged corporate status on 19 June 1381: the ransacking of Corpus Christi College and the burning of the University's library and archives in the town square, with one chronicle recording the cry "Away with the learning of clerks, away with it," and Margery Starre named as a leading participant.

Cited in: Chapter 13.

39 — Bury St Edmunds: John Wrawe, Prior Cambridge, and Sir John Cavendish

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents John Wrawe's Suffolk rising and its storming of Bury St Edmunds Abbey, the flight and capture two days later of Prior John Cambridge (beheaded), and the killing of Sir John Cavendish, Chief Justice of King's Bench and Chancellor of the University of Cambridge, tracked down at Lakenheath.

Cited in: Chapter 13.

40 — Geoffrey Litster, Henry Despenser, and the Battle of North Walsham

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents Geoffrey Litster's Norfolk rising, gathering forces outside Norwich around 17 June 1381, and Henry Despenser (Bishop of Norwich)'s unilateral military response without royal commission -- marching from Stamford, relieving Peterborough, reclaiming Cambridge and Norwich, and defeating Litster's army at the Battle of North Walsham around 25-26 June, with captured rebels including Litster executed on the spot without formal trial.

Cited in: Chapter 13.

41 — Royal reassertion of control and the special commissions, June 1381

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the rapid post-Smithfield restoration effort: Robert Knolles, Nicholas Brembre, and Robert Launde appointed to restore London control, roughly 4,000 soldiers mustered for the counties, and the special commission granted to Thomas of Woodstock and Chief Justice Robert Tresilian on 20 June 1381 covering Essex, whose mass trials at Chelmsford (commonly cited at roughly thirty executions, with the precise figure varying by source) and subsequent proceedings at St Albans used methods later historians have characterized as legally coercive.

Cited in: Chapter 14.

42 — The death toll of the post-revolt suppression

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), cites the figure, current by the time of Parliament's November 1381 review of the crisis, of at least 1,500 people executed across the country in the suppression that followed the revolt's collapse -- a figure separate from any killed during the fighting itself.

Cited in: Chapter 14.

43 — Revocation of the Mile End charters

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents Richard II's order of 30 June 1381 returning serfs and villein tenants to their previous service conditions, and the formal revocation of the Mile End charters by 2 July 1381.

Cited in: Chapter 14.

44 — The general pardon and its exclusions

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents the general pardon eventually extended to most revolt participants, explicitly excluding the men of Bury St Edmunds, those involved in killing the king's officials, and fugitives still at large from prison.

Cited in: Chapter 14.

45 — John Ball's capture, trial, and execution

R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), documents John Ball's capture at Coventry on 13 July 1381, his trial the following day, and his execution -- hanged, drawn, and quartered -- on 15 July 1381, reportedly in Richard II's presence. John Wrawe's execution on 6 May 1382, despite testifying against roughly two dozen former associates in an apparent bid for clemency, is also documented there.

Cited in: Chapter 14.

46 — The "Peasants' Revolt" naming dispute

Paul Strohm's search for the origin of the phrase "Peasants' Revolt" identified an early use in John Richard Green's A Short History of the English People (1874); secondary accounts of Strohm's own conclusion vary between crediting Green as the coiner outright and reporting that Strohm considered the actual originator undeterminable, and this book states that uncertainty rather than resolving it. The critique of the name on grounds that many participants were not peasants (clergy, urban artisans, London tradesmen, at least one former MP among named participants) and that "revolt" overstates the rising's coherence is associated with Strohm and with the historian Miri Rubin; see the discussion of the naming question in the historiographical surveys of the revolt, e.g. in R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983), and Juliet Barker, England, Arise: The People, the King and the Great Revolt of 1381 (London: Little, Brown, 2014), whose own title reflects a deliberate alternative naming choice.

Cited in: Chapter 15.

47 — The Marxist and revisionist historiographical schools

Rodney Hilton, Bond Men Made Free: Medieval Peasant Movements and the English Rising of 1381 (London: Methuen, 1973; new introduction by Christopher Dyer, Routledge, 2003), reads 1381 through a Marxist class-conflict lens, situating it within a recurring European pattern of peasant movements against landholding elites. Steven Justice, Writing and Rebellion: England in 1381 (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1994), discussed at length in Chapter 5, represents a later, more textually and archivally grounded historiographical turn focused on the participants' own literacy and self-representation rather than class conflict as the primary analytic frame.

Cited in: Chapter 15.

48 — Postan's "passing episode" characterization and the economic decline of villeinage

M. M. Postan, The Medieval Economy and Society: An Economic History of Britain in the Middle Ages (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1972), sets out the economic-historical case that villeinage's decline over the fifteenth century tracks primarily with continued labour scarcity, rising real wages, and landlords' shift toward cash rents and leasehold arrangements -- trends visible before 1381 and continuing afterward largely independent of the revolt's political outcome. The characterization of the revolt itself as a "passing episode" is widely attributed to Postan in the secondary historiographical literature (e.g., in surveys of the revolt's long-term impact); this book has not been able to verify an exact page citation for the phrase within Postan's own text and states that limitation rather than inventing a page number.

Cited in: Chapter 15.

49 — Long-term legacy: no repeat poll tax until 1990, war finance, and cultural afterlife

England levied no tax in the 1381 poll tax's specific per-head form again until the Community Charge of 1989-90, popularly and pointedly nicknamed the "poll tax" by its opponents; its own widespread refusal and political collapse are documented in standard modern British political histories of the period. William Morris's use of the revolt (most directly in his utopian romance A Dream of John Ball) as a touchstone for later socialist political imagination is well documented in literary-historical scholarship on Morris. The revolt's dampening effect on subsequent Parliaments' willingness to fund Hundred Years' War campaigns at earlier scales is discussed among the revolt's constitutional-historical consequences in standard surveys, including R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983).

Cited in: Chapter 15.

50 — Manorial court rolls, custumals, rentals, chevage, and the mill monopoly

Christopher Dyer, Making a Living in the Middle Ages (Yale University Press, 2002), documents the manorial record-keeping apparatus that made villein status provable and hereditary: court rolls recording land transfers and personal status manor by manor, custumals setting down a manor's own particular customs, and rentals; and further customary dues beyond week-work and the fees discussed in Note 2, including chevage (a fee for living away from the manor) and the miller's monopoly, by which tenants were required to grind grain at the lord's mill at the lord's price rather than at a household hand-quern.

Cited in: Chapter 1.

51 — Post-plague lordly revival of lapsed servile dues

Christopher Dyer, Making a Living in the Middle Ages (Yale University Press, 2002), and M. M. Postan, The Medieval Economy and Society (Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1972), both discuss the documented tendency of manorial lords, in the years immediately following the Black Death, to reassert and re-collect servile labour dues and customary obligations that had lapsed or gone uncollected on many manors before 1348 -- a documentary reassertion of control running directly counter to the demographic reality of a sharply reduced labour supply.

Cited in: Chapter 2.

52 — Domesday Book exemplifications sought during the Great Rumour

Rosamond Faith, "The 'Great Rumour' of 1377 and Peasant Ideology" (in Hilton and Aston, eds., The English Rising of 1381, Cambridge University Press, 1984, pp. 43-74), documents that more than forty villages are recorded sending for exemplifications -- certified copies of the relevant Domesday Book entries -- to support their ancient demesne claims, including Wiltshire tenants who bound themselves by oath to withhold labour until their exemplification arrived, and the case of John Godefray, brought before Wiltshire justices accused of counselling villeins that such an exemplification would prove their freedom. Of roughly twenty exemplifications historians can trace as requested in 1377, only one is recorded as having succeeded in supporting a tenant's claim.

Cited in: Chapter 3.

53 — The St Albans parlour-floor querns

Rosamund M. Gammie, "Memory, materiality, and trauma: revisiting the parlour floor, St. Albans, 1381," Rethinking History 29, no. 2 (2025): 384-406. Gammie's study documents the abbey's confiscation of villagers' household hand-querns to enforce its milling monopoly, their installation in the paving of the abbot's own parlour floor, the 1381 rebels digging up and smashing the stones and distributing fragments among townspeople, and the forced restoration of the recoverable stones to the parlour floor on 12 July 1381, once royal authority was reasserted; the article notes it is not precisely recorded how many of the original stones were ultimately restored intact versus in fragments.

Cited in: Chapter 13.

54 — Richard II's reported remark to Essex petitioners

The chronicle tradition attributing to Richard II, in an encounter with Essex petitioners after the Mile End charters' revocation, a remark to the effect that they were "rustics" and would remain so, in bondage "not as before, but incomparably" worse, is discussed (with appropriate caution about its status as reported rather than verbatim speech) in the historiographical surveys of the revolt, e.g. R. B. Dobson, The Peasants' Revolt of 1381, 2nd ed. (Macmillan, 1983). This book treats the line as chronicle tradition rather than a confirmed direct quotation.

Cited in: Chapter 14.

55 — Richard II's deposition and death, 1399-1400

Nigel Saul, Richard II (New Haven: Yale University Press, Yale English Monarchs series, 1997), the standard modern biography, documents Richard's escalating autocracy and conflict with the baronage, his 1399 confiscation of the exiled Henry Bolingbroke's Lancastrian inheritance, Bolingbroke's return and Richard's deposition the same year, and Richard's death at Pontefract Castle, probably in mid-February 1400 -- officially recorded as starvation, though whether self-inflicted, imposed by his captors, or something else again remains genuinely disputed in the surviving record.

Cited in: Chapter 15.

RICHARD II

King of England, crowned 16 July 1377 at age ten on the death of his grandfather Edward III, governed in his minority by a council dominated by his uncle John of Gaunt. Fourteen years old during the revolt, he personally met the rebels twice -- at Mile End on 14 June, where he granted charters abolishing serfdom, and at Smithfield on 15 June, where he rode alone toward the crowd after Wat Tyler's killing and defused the immediate threat of violence [26][33][34][36]. The royal council revoked the Mile End charters within weeks [43].

Cited in: Chapters 8, 11, 12, 14.

JOHN OF GAUNT

Duke of Lancaster, Richard II's uncle and the wealthiest magnate in England, widely blamed by contemporaries for the fiscal policy that funded the Hundred Years' War, including the 1377 poll tax [12][13]. His London residence, the Savoy Palace, was deliberately destroyed by the rebels on 13 June 1381 as a targeted act against him personally; he himself was in Scotland at the time [28].

Cited in: Chapters 4, 9.

WAT TYLER

Almost entirely undocumented before 7 June 1381, when the Anonimalle Chronicle records his election as leader of the Kentish rebels at Maidstone [23]. Led the Kentish force into London, presided over the Savoy's destruction and the Tower killings, and met Richard II at Smithfield on 15 June, where an altercation ended with Lord Mayor William Walworth and squire Ralph Standish killing him [35][36]. Claims about his origin and prior military service are unconfirmed chronicle tradition, not independently verified fact.

Cited in: Chapters 7, 8, 9, 12.

JOHN BALL

A Kentish "hedge-priest" (see HEDGE-PRIEST) with a documented radical preaching career stretching back to the 1360s, excommunicated by three successive archbishops of Canterbury for "manifest contumacy" [15]. Imprisoned at Maidstone when the revolt began [16]; preached to the rebels at Blackheath on 12 June, traditionally credited with the couplet "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?" [18]. Later chroniclers' claims linking him to John Wycliffe and the Lollards are treated by modern historians as polemical rather than factual, given the chronology [17]. Captured at Coventry, tried, and executed on 15 July 1381 [45].

Cited in: Chapters 5, 8, 14.

JACK STRAW

Named by several chroniclers as a rebel leader in the early Essex and Kentish phases of the revolt. Modern historians treat his identity as a genuine open question: it is unclear whether the name refers to a real, distinct individual or is a chronicler's alias for Wat Tyler or John Wrawe. This book states the uncertainty rather than resolving it.

Cited in: historiographical discussion throughout.

SIMON SUDBURY

Archbishop of Canterbury from 1375 and Lord Chancellor from 1380, bearing direct administrative responsibility for the poll tax; had earlier excommunicated John Ball [15]. Found in the Tower's chapel of St John on 14 June 1381 and beheaded without trial on Tower Hill, his head displayed on London Bridge [31].

Cited in: Chapter 10.

ROBERT HALES

Prior of the Knights Hospitaller of England and Lord Treasurer from 1380, the officer directly administering poll tax collection; the Hospitallers' Clerkenwell Priory was destroyed by rebels for this reason. Beheaded alongside Sudbury at Tower Hill, 14 June 1381 [31].

Cited in: Chapter 10.

WILLIAM WALWORTH

Lord Mayor of London during the revolt, a wealthy fishmonger by trade. Struck the first blow against Wat Tyler at Smithfield on 15 June 1381 and subsequently raised the City militia to disperse the rebel crowd; knighted on the spot by Richard II [36].

Cited in: Chapter 12.

RALPH STANDISH

A royal squire present at Smithfield who delivered the further, fatal wounds to Wat Tyler after William Walworth's initial strike, 15 June 1381 [36].

Cited in: Chapter 12.

JOHN WRAWE

A chaplain who led the rising as it spread from Essex into Suffolk, storming Bury St Edmunds Abbey; Prior John Cambridge and Chief Justice Sir John Cavendish were killed under his forces' campaign [39]. Captured after the revolt's collapse, he gave evidence against roughly two dozen associates in an apparent bid for clemency but was executed anyway, 6 May 1382 [45].

Cited in: Chapters 13, 14.

GEOFFREY LITSTER

A Norfolk dyer who led the East Anglian rising, gathering forces outside Norwich around 17 June 1381; defeated and executed by Henry Despenser at the Battle of North Walsham, c. 25-26 June [40].

Cited in: Chapter 13.

HENRY DESPENSER

Bishop of Norwich and former soldier, who suppressed the East Anglian rising without royal commission -- relieving Peterborough, reclaiming Cambridge and Norwich, and defeating Geoffrey Litster's forces at North Walsham, executing captives including Litster without formal trial [40].

Cited in: Chapter 13.

WILLIAM GRINDECOBBE

Led the St Albans townsmen in their dispute with the town's Benedictine abbey; secured Wat Tyler's authorization for direct action and led the opening of the abbey prison and burning of manorial records, 13-16 June 1381 [37]. Tried and executed once royal authority was restored, despite believing he had secured a pardon.

Cited in: Chapter 13.

THOMAS OF WOODSTOCK

Richard II's youngest uncle (Earl of Buckingham, later Duke of Gloucester), given a special commission alongside Chief Justice Robert Tresilian on 20 June 1381 to suppress the revolt and try captured rebels across Essex [41].

Cited in: Chapter 14.

ROBERT TRESILIAN

Appointed Chief Justice shortly after the revolt; conducted mass trials at Chelmsford and later at St Albans (including William Grindecobbe's), using methods later historians have criticized as legally coercive [41].

Cited in: Chapter 14.

THOMAS BAKER

A villager of Fobbing, Essex, who told the poll tax commissioner on or around 30 May 1381 that the village had already paid and would pay no more -- the confrontation conventionally treated as the revolt's opening spark [20].

Cited in: Chapter 6.

BAMPTON (POLL TAX COMMISSIONER)

The royal commissioner whose attempt to collect poll tax arrears at Brentwood triggered the Fobbing confrontation of 30 May 1381. His first name is given inconsistently across secondary accounts ("John" and "Thomas" both appear), and this book treats his precise identity and formal commission status as a genuine, unresolved gap in the record [20].

Cited in: Chapter 6.

RICHARD LYONS

A wealthy London vintner and royal financial adviser, previously convicted of corruption in the Good Parliament of 1376; killed by rebels in London on 13 June 1381, one of several officials specifically targeted rather than victims of indiscriminate violence.

Cited in: Chapter 9.

JOHN LEGGE

A royal sergeant-at-arms blamed for devising the poll tax's household-inspection enforcement method (checking for unregistered, untaxed young women); beheaded at Tower Hill alongside Sudbury and Hales, 14 June 1381 [31].

Cited in: Chapter 10.

WILLIAM APPLETON

A Franciscan friar serving as John of Gaunt's personal physician; killed at the Tower on 14 June 1381 alongside Sudbury, Hales, and Legge, targeted for his connection to the absent Gaunt [31].

Cited in: Chapter 10.

HENRY OF BOLINGBROKE

John of Gaunt's fourteen-year-old son, present in the Tower when rebels overran it on 14 June 1381; nearly killed as Gaunt's heir before a royal guard, John Ferrour, intervened on his behalf [32]. Later deposed Richard II and reigned as Henry IV, a history outside this book's scope.

Cited in: Chapter 10.

JOHANNA FERROUR

Named in post-revolt indictment records as a leading participant in London's violence, including accusations tied to Richard Lyons's killing and the Savoy's burning -- one of very few women named individually in the revolt's court records; no evidence survives of her being punished as severely as male leaders [42].

Cited in: Chapters 9, 14.

FOBBING

A village in south Essex on the Thames estuary, site of the 30 May 1381 confrontation between villager Thomas Baker and the poll tax commissioner conventionally treated as the revolt's opening spark [20].

Cited in: Chapter 6.

BRENTWOOD

An Essex market town where the poll tax commissioner recalled village representatives on 1 June 1381 to account for tax shortfalls, escalating the Fobbing incident into regional unrest [21].

Cited in: Chapter 6.

MAIDSTONE

County town of Kent; site of the prison-breaking that freed John Ball and others, and of Wat Tyler's election as leader of the Kentish rebels, 7 June 1381 [22][23].

Cited in: Chapter 7.

ROCHESTER

A royal castle in Kent that surrendered to the rebels without recorded resistance in early June 1381, releasing its prisoners [22].

Cited in: Chapter 7.

CANTERBURY

Seat of the Archbishopric held by Simon Sudbury; entered by the Kentish rebels 10 June 1381 without resistance, its cathedral monks compelled to swear loyalty to "the true commons of England" [24].

Cited in: Chapter 7.

BLACKHEATH

An open heath southeast of London where the Kentish rebels mustered on 12 June 1381 and John Ball preached; the surviving text of his sermon is a later chronicler's reconstruction rather than a verbatim transcript [18][25].

Cited in: Chapters 5, 8.

SAVOY PALACE

John of Gaunt's London residence on the Strand, reputed the grandest private house in England; systematically destroyed by rebels on 13 June 1381 -- goods burned or thrown into the Thames rather than stolen, a fortune substantial enough that chroniclers struggled to put a figure on it [28].

Cited in: Chapter 9.

TOWER OF LONDON

The royal fortress overrun on 14 June 1381 by roughly 400 rebels with no recorded garrison resistance; Sudbury, Hales, Appleton, and Legge were taken from here to Tower Hill for execution [30][31].

Cited in: Chapter 10.

MILE END

An open field east of London's walls where Richard II met the rebels on 14 June 1381 and agreed to charters abolishing serfdom -- charters revoked within weeks [33][34][43].

Cited in: Chapter 11.

SMITHFIELD

An open market ground beside St Bartholomew's Priory where Richard II met Wat Tyler on 15 June 1381; the meeting ended with Tyler's killing and the effective end of the revolt's London phase [35][36].

Cited in: Chapter 12.

BURY ST EDMUNDS

A Suffolk town dominated by its wealthy Benedictine abbey; stormed by John Wrawe's forces, its Prior beheaded and Chief Justice Cavendish killed nearby [39].

Cited in: Chapter 13.

NORTH WALSHAM

A Norfolk market town, site of the battle (c. 25-26 June 1381) in which Henry Despenser defeated Geoffrey Litster's Norfolk rising -- the only regional rising suppressed by direct military engagement [40].

Cited in: Chapter 13.

ST ALBANS

A Hertfordshire town in long dispute with its Benedictine abbey; townsmen under William Grindecobbe opened the abbey prison and burned its records, 13-16 June 1381; John Ball was later tried here [37][45].

Cited in: Chapters 13, 14.

CAMBRIDGE

University town where a rising on 19 June 1381 targeted the University's privileged corporate status, ransacking Corpus Christi College and burning its library and archives [38].

Cited in: Chapter 13.

YORK

A northern city where unrest distinct from the southern rising's causes broke out in late June and again on 1 July 1381, when John de Gisbourne attempted to seize civic control from Mayor Simon de Quixlay; order was not fully restored until 1382.

Cited in: Chapter 13 (brief).

THE ANONIMALLE CHRONICLE

An Anglo-Norman/Middle-English chronicle associated with St Mary's Abbey, York, and this book's fullest single narrative source for Tyler's election at Maidstone and the Smithfield dialogue; treated by modern scholarship as a secondary compilation rather than a strict eyewitness account [23][35].

Cited in: throughout; discussed directly in "What Survives."

WALSINGHAM'S HISTORIA ANGLICANA

A hostile Latin chronicle by the St Albans monk Thomas Walsingham, chiefly responsible for linking John Ball polemically to Wycliffe and the Lollards; a valuable if partisan source for the St Albans rising against Walsingham's own abbey [17][37].

Cited in: Chapter 5; discussed directly in "What Survives."

KNIGHTON'S CHRONICON

A Latin chronicle by an Augustinian canon of Leicester Abbey, source for the Savoy Palace's destroyed plate and, with Walsingham, for the surviving rebel letters [19][28].

Cited in: Chapters 5, 9; discussed directly in "What Survives."

FROISSART'S CHRONIQUES

A French-language chronicle by Jean Froissart, written retrospectively and at a remove from events; valuable for wider international framing and illuminated illustrations, but treated with more caution on specific detail than the English chronicles.

Cited in: discussed directly in "What Survives."

THE STATUTE OF LABOURERS (1351)

An act of Parliament elaborating the 1349 Ordinance of Labourers, freezing wages at pre-plague levels and criminalizing both employers who paid more and labourers who demanded it; enforced by justices of labourers drawn from the county gentry and named by chroniclers as a standing grievance behind the 1381 revolt [7][8][9].

Cited in: Chapter 2.

POLL TAX RETURN MEMBRANES (E 179)

The surviving administrative returns recording assessment and collection of the 1377, 1379, and 1381 poll taxes, held at The National Archives, Kew; comparison across years reveals the organized evasion this book's Chapter 4 discusses [14].

Cited in: Chapter 4; discussed directly in "What Survives."

KING'S BENCH INDICTMENT AND GAOL DELIVERY ROLLS

The surviving legal record of the post-revolt trials, preserving names such as Johanna Ferrour's that appear in no chronicle narrative [42].

Cited in: Chapter 14; discussed directly in "What Survives."

VILLEINAGE

The unfree tenurial status binding a large share of the medieval English peasantry to a lord's manor: fixed unpaid labour service, restrictions on leaving or marrying without licence, and subjection to the manorial rather than royal courts [1][3]. Not formally abolished by the revolt; declined over the following century chiefly for economic reasons [48].

Cited in: Chapters 1, 15.

THE GREAT RUMOUR

A 1377 protest movement across roughly forty villages in south-east and south-west England, refusing labour services on the legal theory of ancient demesne; rejected in the courts, but demonstrating organizational capacity historians read as a dress rehearsal for 1381 [10][11].

Cited in: Chapter 3.

ANCIENT DEMESNE

A legal status attaching to manors recorded in Domesday Book (1086) as held directly by the Crown, entitling tenants to lighter, fixed customary obligations; the basis of the Great Rumour's rejected 1377 legal claim [10].

Cited in: Chapter 3.

POLL TAX

A tax levied per head rather than on land, income, or property; England levied three in rapid succession, 1377-1381, the third's enforcement directly triggering the Essex and Kent risings [13][14].

Cited in: Chapter 4.

HEDGE-PRIEST

An unbeneficed, itinerant priest without a fixed parish living, such as John Ball -- operating outside normal parish structure, with less institutional protection but a wider, more socially mixed audience than a settled incumbent [15].

Cited in: Chapter 5.

"PEASANTS' REVOLT" (NAMING DISPUTE)

A likely nineteenth-century coinage with no fixed contemporary title; criticized by modern historians for implying a coherence and a purely peasant composition the actual, county-by-county rising does not fully support [46].

Cited in: Chapter 15.
