# The Tissue Ledger

_Gault Musa solo. The ex-Vulture works the father's organ-harvest fear into the death economy and finds the Cermak Reclamation Crew front. He clears Celeste from it -- but finds husks coming through brain-blank, paid for in stolen compute, and the thread of a cheap cat-ear job (Sol) that went in and never came out. The informant Feliksas wants out._

[Beat 1]
THE TISSUE LEDGER

Gault Musa, ex-Vulture. Nine years on the pickup circuit. Now he reads the missing for Cold Read Investigations, six partners out of a third-floor walk-up at the Gray Zone edge, buzzer that works when it wants to.

The case: a father is certain someone harvested his daughter. Gault's job is to check the ledger.

[Beat 2]
The father, Douglas Hartley, had talked for eleven minutes without saying the word "daughter." He said "tissue," "inventory," "provenance." He said "these operations have specific sourcing requirements." A man who had run shadow-economy instruments his whole working life had finally found a shadow economy he was afraid of.

Gault wrote down what Douglas said and what he didn't say and handed the notes to Rennick after the intake. Then he went to work the ledger.

[Beat 3]
The organ market has three tiers and he knows all three. Top tier: licensed hospital systems buying from licensed procurement chains — Neuretics Surgical, Venn Medical, the Arcturus-adjacent corpo shops on the North Shore. Mid tier: gray-market brokers who ask the right questions slowly and not too loud, matched pairs, matched blood types, time-critical sourcing with a premium for discretion. Bottom tier: the scrape-what-you-can operations that buy from whoever brings a cooler.

If someone harvested Celeste Hartley, she entered this system at one of those three doors. She did not enter at the top door — he could verify that from public procurement ledgers by morning. The bottom door was where he was going first.

[Beat 4]
He took the Red Line south at seven in the evening, which was the hour the corridor started sorting itself out. Pilsen to Bridgeport, Bridgeport toward the Cermak interchange, the stretch where the licensed zone thins to a thread and the unlicensed corridor runs underneath it, close enough that a checkpoint scanner on the platform could read your neuretics for the price of the licensed side and find nothing on you worth the read.

He looked out the window and let the stops count down.

[Beat 5]
Devereux Osei — no relation to Rennick, different Oseis entirely, Gault had verified this his first week because the world is small and GLMZ is smaller — ran a Vulture sub-depot out of a former auto-glass shop on 26th and Kedzie. The sign still said SAFELITE in faded paint four layers down, and in certain slants of light you could read it under the newer paint like a watermark.

Dev met him at the rollup door with a coffee in each hand and handed one over handle-first without being asked. This was the Vulture custom when you'd run together. It meant you were still in the ledger.

Gault took it left-handed. He always took with the left and worked with the left; the right palm he kept clear, fingers a little open, the way a man keeps one tool clean. Dev had stopped noticing years ago. Everybody who'd run with Gault knew he'd put a price on anything you set in front of him except a body, and that he wouldn't shake a stranger's hand because his palms ran warm and read the living the same way they read the dead, and he didn't like knowing a man's temperature before he knew his name.

[Beat 6]
"North Shore girl," Gault said. "Twenty-two. No priors, no flags, no reason to end up in the system. Gone four days. Father's theory is harvest."

Dev made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "Father's theory is always harvest. Half the time it's Scatter."

Gault looked at him.

"The mod communities," Dev said. "Some of them have been losing people sideways. Not the body — the body shows up. The mind's just gone. We pick them up and there's nothing wrong with the hardware."

"How many?" Gault asked.

[Beat 7]
Dev pulled a stool over and sat on it sideways, the way he always sat when he was thinking about volume. "In the last eight months I've logged eleven pickups with the full kit — heart, lungs, kidneys, working neuretics pulled and sold — where the neuretics came in wiped. Not damaged. Wiped. The rest of the hardware is fine. Somebody ran them empty before we got there, or whoever gave us the bodies ran them empty first."

He sipped his coffee.

"You know what a wiped neuretic stack sells for," he said.

"More than a working one," Gault said.

"Three times more. The compute substrate is intact. You sell it to whoever needs quantum-capable cycles and doesn't want to explain where they got them."

[Beat 8]
"Who's sourcing the bodies," Gault said. It wasn't a question.

Dev turned his coffee cup in his hands once, the way he did when he was deciding what was worth what. "There's a shop on Ashland. Used to be a licensed clinic — you can see where they pulled the signage, left the anchor bolts in the brick. They're running a cheap-mod front. Cat ears, basic sensory packages, starter-tier stuff. Walk-in price is four hundred Φ below market."

"Below market by how much?"

"Enough that anyone doing the comparison knows what the subsidy is," Dev said. "But the people walking in aren't doing the comparison."

[Beat 9]
Gault set his coffee down on the workbench beside a row of sealed coolers, each one tagged with a Carrion Enterprises routing sticker and a printed inventory sheet tucked under the lid handle. Dev ran a clean operation — this was not that shop.

"The cat-ear mod," Gault said. "The directional-hearing package. That's what the girl my client is looking for was researching."

Dev didn't say anything for a moment. Then he said, "She North Shore all the way? Money, resources, good research habits?"

"Yes."

"Then she probably went to Ines. The Ashland shop isn't who people like that end up at." He picked up his coffee again. "It's who people like everyone else end up at."

[Beat 10]
Gault rode down to the Ashland stop and walked two blocks north against a wind that came off the lake and smelled faintly of the sump. He found the building without effort — the missing signage left a ghost on the brick, a rectangle of slightly cleaner concrete where the letters had been, and the light from the window display was the particular white of a procedure room, which a mod shop did not need for cosmetic consultations.

He stood across the street for six minutes with his hands in his pockets and his palms registering ambient temperature through the fabric. He was not timing himself. He was reading the loading dock on the east side of the building.

[Beat 11]
The loading dock had been retrofitted from the building's clinic days — the ramp grade was right, the door width was right, the overhead clearance was right for a gurney stack. But the hours were wrong. A licensed cosmetic practice takes deliveries before noon because its supply chain runs on the licensed corridor's schedule. The dock behind this building had taken a delivery at eleven-seventeen at night, Gault's count, because the van parked beside it still had its engine running and the heat signature from the wheel wells was twenty-two minutes old — his palms, pressed flat against his thighs, gave him that for free.

A mod shop that works at noon does not load at eleven at night. A cold-storage operation does.

[Beat 12]
The refrigeration unit on the roof was a Castellan 900 series — he recognized the compressor casing, the footprint, the industrial-grade condenser fins. A Castellan 900 runs a seven-hundred-liter cold chamber at four degrees Celsius continuously. A cosmetic practice doing cat-ear mods needs perhaps a hundred and twenty liters of refrigerated storage for its biologics and a standard residential unit handles that fine.

A Castellan 900 is for quantity. It is for inventory you turn over on a schedule but never run dry.

[Beat 13]
He crossed the street and went in through the front. The window display had three cat-ear demo units under glass — the standard directional package, the cosmetic-only shell variant, and the hybrid with the sensory-augment chip. The pricing card was handwritten on stock paper and the prices were, as Dev had said, four hundred Φ under the going rate for the sensory-augment package and two-eighty under for the standard.

The person behind the counter was a young man with his own ears modded to the hybrid package, the right side sitting slightly higher than the left in the way of a fresh install still settling into the cartilage. He looked up and said, "Consultation or walk-in?"

"Pricing question," Gault said.

[Beat 14]
He asked about the standard package and the young man recited the procedure from memory — install time, integration window, the two-week softness in the auditory channel while the neuretic pathways finished wiring in. It was correct. The biology was correct, the aftercare window was correct, the pricing was wrong in the way that only made sense if the front end was a subsidy for something the back end collected.

He pressed one palm flat against the glass counter for a moment, as though steadying himself to think. The counter was cold. Not room temperature. The refrigeration system in the back wall was running through the floor into the display case and the counter surface, keeping the room at a temperature you only needed if you were doing serious tissue work.

"I'll think on it," he said, and left.

[Beat 15]
He had what he needed from the outside. What he needed from the inside would take a contact.

He walked south on Ashland for three blocks, turned east, and called a woman named Priya Chandrasekaran-Webb from a corner with a working streetlight and a fried-plantain cart whose owner had been watching the Ashland building for two years for reasons of his own. Gault knew the owner's name — Tomás — and had bought plantains from him before and would again, but tonight he stood downwind with the phone to his ear and watched Tomás watch the building and waited for Priya to pick up.

[Beat 16]
Priya had been a Vulture before him, before Dev, back when the territory included everything south of the 18th Street corridor and they ran seven vans instead of four. She knew every unlicensed clinic in a four-kilometer radius the way a contractor knows load-bearing walls — not because she'd studied them, but because she'd carried things in and out of them for years.

"The shop on Ashland," he said when she answered.

"I was wondering when someone was going to ask about that," she said.

"How long."

"Eight months since it changed. Whoever bought the clinic kept the front operating and swapped the back. The community's been trying to push it out for two years — the journalists only come when something bleeds on camera."

"Has something bled on camera."

"Not yet," she said.

[Beat 17]
"The inventory," he said. "What goes in and what comes out."

She was quiet for a moment. He heard traffic on her end and the compressed static of her working-class neuretics relaying the call through the corridor's spotty mesh. "Bodies in, parts out, same as Dev told you. But some of the ones coming in aren't bodies the way we mean it. Hardware intact, person gone." A pause. "And the shop pays its back-door suppliers in something I won't say on an open line. Come see me when you've got a name to trade. Not before."

She hung up before he could close the rate.

[Beat 18]
Gault stood at the corner with the dead phone in his hand and watched Tomás turn a plantain with a long-handled tong and not look at the Ashland building directly, the practiced peripheral attention of a man who has been watching something dangerous for long enough that he's learned not to let it see him looking.

A name to trade. He had one. He just had to be willing to say it inside a clinic that took deliveries at eleven at night.

[Beat 19]
He thanked her and hung up and bought a paper bag of plantains from Tomás, because he was going to be standing here a while and also because he owed the man the business.

"The shop," Gault said, without pointing.

Tomás lifted his chin in a fraction of a nod and did not stop turning the batch on the griddle. "My niece went in there fourteen months ago," he said. "Cat ears. Discount package. She came out with the ears." He lifted the tong and set the next batch down. "Three weeks later she stopped answering the phone. A month after that I saw her on the street and she looked at me like she was behind glass." He slid the finished batch into a paper sleeve. "She's still alive. She's just not her. Doctors say neuretics are functional. Feed is active. She just — drifts."

Gault took the plantains and put sixty Φ on the cart's rail. "What happened to her integration stay," he said.

"She did it somewhere else," Tomás said. "Somewhere she found through the cat-ear community."

[Beat 20]
He filed Tomás's niece in the column he was building — alive but not present, neuretics functional, drift not damage — and walked back toward the Ashland building from the south end, staying on the opposite side of the street. He needed to know if Celeste Hartley's tissue had entered this operation. To know that, he needed the intake log.

He did not have the intake log. What he had was nine years of knowing that every illegal tissue operation keeps one, because without a log you can't prove to your buyers that what you're selling is what you say it is, and buyers in this market do not pay without provenance. Even the people who claim not to ask questions ask questions. The log existed. He needed someone inside to read it.

[Beat 21]
There was a man named Feliksas Burokas-Okafor who had worked the Cermak operation's intake for six months and who had, five weeks ago, reached out to Priya through a mutual contact to say he wanted to get out of the business and needed to know if anyone was building a case. Priya had not yet committed. She had mentioned him to Gault once, carefully, the way you mention a resource you're not sure is real.

Gault called her back.

"The intake man," he said.

"I told him I'd need something concrete before I'd vouch for him with anyone."

"I have a name to check against his log. That's something concrete."

She was quiet.

"What's it worth to him," Gault asked, "to know that someone with an agency is listening?"

[Beat 22]
Feliksas met him an hour later in the back booth of a noodle shop on 25th, two blocks from the Ashland building and worlds away from it in the way that corridor geography works — one block can be inside and the next block outside and the threshold is invisible until you've walked it enough times to feel the seam.

He was a medium-built man in his early forties, Lithuanian-Nigerian heritage from the planes of his face, wearing a thermal undershirt and a jacket with Carrion Enterprises freight division patches on the sleeves that he had turned inside-out and was wearing backward-inside, which was the corridor's version of a disguise. He was not good at this. He was probably excellent at intake logs.

[Beat 23]
"I want immunity," Feliksas said, before Gault sat down.

"I can't give you immunity. I'm a private investigator."

"I want someone to know what I know before something happens to me."

Gault sat down. "Then tell me what you know and I will make sure it goes to people who can use it. That's what I can offer."

Feliksas looked at him for a long time. Then he took a folded piece of paper from his inside jacket pocket and set it face-down on the table. Not the intake log itself — a handwritten copy of six entries. Six names, six dates, six abbreviated intake notes in a cramped shorthand that Gault recognized as the Vulture system's standard notation, which meant whoever designed this operation had learned the craft from the licensed side before flipping it.

[Beat 24]
Gault read it without picking it up. The shorthand was what he remembered. The notation NR in the neuretics column meant neuretics removed. The notation NRW — neuretics removed, wiped — meant the stack had been cleaned before or at the point of intake. Five of the six entries carried NRW. The sixth carried NR without the W, which meant the stack had been pulled working and was being sold as a functional unit rather than a compute substrate.

There was no entry for a Celeste Hartley. No entry matching her description — twenty-two, North Shore biometrics, no corridor history. He ran his eye down the six dates. The most recent was eleven days ago.

"Is this the full log," he asked.

"Last six months," Feliksas said. "There are more before that. I only started keeping copies two months ago."

[Beat 25]
"The girl I'm looking for," Gault said. He described Celeste without using her name — age, build, background, corridor-inexperienced, cat-ear mod research. "Is she in the full log."

"No," Feliksas said, without hesitating. "I would remember her. People from the North Shore stand out in the intake record. Their biometrics come through cleaner, the tissue histories are longer. She's not in anything I've processed."

Gault sat with that for a moment. It was the answer Douglas Hartley needed. It was not a clean answer — the operation was real, it was processing people, just not this particular person — but it closed the specific fear that had walked through Cold Read's door. Celeste Hartley's tissue had not entered this economy.

That was the job Rennick had sent him to do. It was done in an hour and forty minutes.

[Beat 26]
He ordered a bowl of noodles because the booth cost something and because he was going to sit here a while longer.

"The NRW entries," he said. "The wiped stacks. Who delivers those bodies."

Feliksas wrapped both hands around his tea. "They don't come from our usual supply. The regular bodies — accidents, shootings, the people we were supposed to be collecting for Carrion — those come in through the dock in the standard way. The NRW bodies come in through the basement entrance. Three or four at a time, irregular intervals. Different vehicle every time. No Carrion routing stickers."

"Different payment too," Gault said.

"Yes," Feliksas said. He looked at his tea. "Cash doesn't cover it. They pay in compute-blocks. Anonymous quantum-cycle leases. The boss calls it 'institutional Φ' when he talks about it, which he almost never does."

[Beat 27]
"Institutional Φ," Gault said.

"That's the word he uses."

Gault ate a forkful of noodles and ran the rates the way other men hum. A matched-pair liver from a healthy twenty-year-old, eighteen to twenty-two thousand Φ on the gray market. Functional kidneys off the same donor, fourteen more. A working quantum-capable neuretic stack, thirty to thirty-five, because the licensed market backorders six months and the people who can't wait pay double and don't ask. A wiped stack sold as pure compute substrate brings the same or better, because the substrate is the rare thing and the mind that ran on it is already gone.

All of it spends as Φ. Somebody was choosing not to take the Φ. Somebody was paying for dead people in raw compute-time and walking away from the cash, which only made sense if the cash was no use to them and the cycles were.

[Beat 28]
Tomás's niece was still alive. Still drifting, neuretics functional, ambulatory and empty — and she was not in this intake log, because the log only counted the ones who'd gone all the way to dead. The drifters were an earlier stage of the same thing. The NRW bodies were where the stage ended.

Whatever ran that procedure took the mind and left the meat for the crew to sell. Gault could read the shape of it the way he read a harvest scene — what was taken, what was left, in what order. He just didn't have a name for a buyer who needed minds and paid in time.

[Beat 29]
"The cat-ear mods," he said to Feliksas. "The NRW deliveries — do any of them have fresh mod installs? Directional-hearing package, the sensory-augment variant?"

Feliksas thought about it. He had a careful mind; he was the type who replayed intake entries like a ledger in his head because that was how he'd survived doing this job without drinking himself into a problem. "Four of the eleven I've logged personally," he said. "All four with fresh cat-ears. Two of the others I'm less certain — I didn't do the full intake on those."

"Four out of eleven," Gault said. "So roughly a third."

"Maybe more. The ones I didn't intake could be the same."

Gault put down his fork. "Is there a name in your partial log. A young woman. Nineteen, corridor background, two jobs, the cheap package from your front shop?"

[Beat 30]
Feliksas looked at his tea for a long time.

"There was a girl," he said. "Maybe five weeks ago. Nineteen or twenty. She'd had the cheap package — I recognized the install signature, it's a shorter incision line than the licensed version. She came in through the basement with the NRW batch, which means she wasn't a regular source body — she was one of theirs." He paused. "She was small. Dark hair, two-job build, the kind of thin that's not a choice. Her neuretics were wiped like the others but the cat ears were still active. I noted it because you don't usually see fresh installs come in that way. The ears were tracking sound when we brought her in."

Gault held that for a moment. The ears working. The mind gone. Something had read her off the stack and left the hardware running.

"Was there a name," he said.

"No ID. She was a Jane Doe."

[Beat 31]
"Where is she now," Gault said.

Feliksas looked out the window of the noodle shop. "The basement goes down," he said. "Below the sub-level. There's a corridor that opens into the under-city — the flooded section, below the old drainage infrastructure. The bodies that can't be sourced go down there. She went down there."

Gault knew the under-city the way he knew the tiers of the organ market — from the outside, as a system, as the place where the math stopped adding up and the bodies went when the ledger had nothing left to say about them. He had never been below the sub-level drainage. The crews who worked that far down were a different category of Vulture, and they did not share their contacts lightly.

"Is she recoverable," he asked.

"No," Feliksas said. "Not from down there."

[Beat 32]
He paid for both bowls and walked out. The wind had shifted and the sump smell was stronger now, coming up through the storm grating at the corner. Two blocks south, below his feet, below the old drainage mains and the pre-city infrastructure, there was a flooded sub-level where the Cermak crew sank the bodies they were done with, and in that sub-level somewhere there was a nineteen-year-old girl who had wanted cat ears and worked two jobs and had not been able to afford the safe version, and no one had hired a detective agency to find her.

He did not name what he felt about that. He filed it in the column with Tomás's niece and kept walking.

[Beat 33]
The double-cross was the part he sat with on the Red Line north.

Feliksas kept the Cermak intake log. Feliksas was also the man Priya's contact had flagged as wanting out — and wanting out was not being out. He was still inside, still processing the NRW bodies through the basement, building the copied record he'd handed across the noodle-shop table the whole time he did it.

Gault ran the source the way he ran a body on the table: value on one side, cost on the other, decide which is which before you cut. The value was the log — names, dates, the compute structure he'd named without being led to it. The cost was that he was still in the building, and every day inside was a day the crew could read the leak in him. They had a way of handling that. They'd already used it once on a licensed operator who tried to quit, and dressed it as a suicide, and one day Rennick would get to read that scene.

[Beat 34]
He messaged Rennick from the train. Short, flat, factual: Celeste not in the ledger. Confirmed. Real operation at Ashland/Cermak — organ harvest and NRW compute-substrate sourcing. Carrion Enterprises infrastructure, under-city disposal. Jane Doe five weeks ago, cat ears, corridor background, nineteen. No ID. Gone. Have a source inside. Source is double-sided. Need to talk tonight.

Rennick's reply came back in under a minute, which was how Gault knew he'd been waiting for the Celeste answer. One word: confirmed. Then, after a pause: keep the source warm. Don't burn him before we need him.

[Beat 35]
Keep the source warm. As though Feliksas were a tissue sample and not a man who'd handed over a paper list in a noodle shop and asked not to die for it.

Gault had run alongside Rennick long enough to read the shorthand. Warm meant protected — meant Rennick was already working out what Feliksas could testify to and in front of whom and in what order. An efficient instruction, written by two men who kept the same kind of ledger. It would be Gault's job, not Rennick's, to make the man last long enough to spend.

He put the phone away and watched the South Side pass in lit windows and dark gaps and the occasional pillar of steam venting up through a sidewalk grate.

[Beat 36]
His brother's name had been Adil. He'd died four years ago in a Bridgeport corridor collapse — a building Arcturus Civil Security had cleared for occupancy without reading the load-bearing column report. The report had been filed. It had been stamped. Someone had calculated that the liability window was shorter than the inspection turnaround, and Adil had been on the third floor when the east wall agreed with the report.

The registry had his preferences on file. Two kidneys, the liver, the corneas, the heart. The heart went to a cargo dispatcher in Wicker Park. Gault had decided in the first week not to learn the man's name, and he kept the decision the way he kept his right palm clear — deliberately, every day, by not reaching. Somewhere across the city Adil's heart was keeping time in a stranger, and Gault had priced every part of the city except that one.

[Beat 37]
He got off at the 35th Street stop and walked west instead of transferring north. He wanted to see the Cermak building from the under-city side, which meant he wanted to find the access point.

The under-city in this stretch ran below the old Sanitary and Ship Canal drainage infrastructure — pre-reconstruction tunnels that had been sealed when the corridor was rebuilt in the 2190s and that everyone with a license pretended were sealed still. The corridor knew better. The under-city was where you put things the surface couldn't account for, and things had been going down there long enough that there were people who knew the routes, who had mapped the water levels after the last pump failure, who moved through the flooded sub-levels for reasons that ranged from storage to refuge to disposal.

Gault was not going in. He was going to find the door.

[Beat 38]
The access was a utility corridor cut behind a row of ground-floor commercial units on the east side of Ashland — a half-level below grade, reached by a ramp that had been poured as a loading accommodation and repurposed as an approach. The ramp was wet. Not from rain; the temperature was wrong for condensation and the city had been dry three days. The wet was coming up from below, the slow seep of a water table that had been rising for forty years in this stretch of corridor because the pumps that were supposed to handle it had been on a maintenance backlog since 2218 and the relevant corponation had decided the liability calculation still favored delay.

The door at the bottom of the ramp was a standard freight door with a cipher lock that had been changed in the last month — the wear pattern on the keypad buttons showed fresh oil around four of the twelve digits, which was enough to tell him it had been changed but not enough to tell him the combination.

[Beat 39]
He pressed both palms flat against the door surface and held them there for twenty seconds. The door was cold — four degrees on the inside face, which meant the refrigeration system from the Ashland building extended down here, which meant the under-city access wasn't just disposal access. It was cold storage. The under-city below this corridor was running as a secondary cold chamber, which explained the Castellan 900 on the roof running at capacity: it was cooling two floors simultaneously.

The operation was larger than it looked from the street. The street-level clinic was intake and front. The sub-level was processing. The under-city was archive.

[Beat 40]
He heard someone on the ramp behind him — footsteps, one person, no hurry, which was more alarming than hurry would have been. He turned around.

The man on the ramp was medium height, wearing a Carrion Enterprises freight jacket without the inside-out modification, which meant he was not ashamed of the affiliation or he hadn't thought about it. He had a work tablet in one hand and he was looking at Gault with the flat assessment of someone whose job includes deciding what category a problem falls into.

"Lost," Gault said, before the man could speak. "Looking for the 34th Street meter access. My neuretics show it two blocks east but the city grid's wrong down here."

The man looked at him for four seconds. Then he said, "Two blocks east, one block south, blue door." He said it the way you say something you've said before to people who weren't supposed to be where they were, with the weary efficiency of a man whose job also includes redirecting the mildly wrong instead of escalating them.

Gault thanked him and walked up the ramp without hurrying.

[Beat 41]
When he was two blocks north and the ramp was no longer visible behind him, he stopped and wrote down everything his palms had told him: door temperature four degrees Celsius, consistent with a refrigeration system running through the building's foundation into the sub-level, door changed in the last thirty days, four active digits on the cipher pad, one person with tablet access to the ramp at eleven-forty-one at night.

A cold-storage operation at this depth was not improvised. It had been engineered. The Castellan 900 on the roof, the sub-level processing, the under-city cold archive — this was infrastructure. This was capital investment. This was a Carrion Enterprises body-man's budget, not a crew boss's side project.

[Beat 42]
He took the train north and got off two stops early and walked the rest through air that turned almost clean at the Gray Zone border, where the licensed corridor's atmospheric systems started scrubbing the particulate load and you could feel the line as a fraction of a PSI — like being inside instead of outside, like the air had been bought.

He let the cleaner air do nothing for him and climbed the stairs.

[Beat 43]
He climbed three flights to the Cold Read office and found Rennick at the intake desk with two coffees already poured, because Rennick read a scene that way — knew from the message that Gault would walk through the door in the state he was in and that two coffees were the correct response.

Gault sat down and put both hands flat on the desk for a moment, letting the ambient temperature of the room ground the thermal sense. Room temperature. No bodies here. He picked up a coffee.

"Celeste Hartley is not in that operation," he said.

"The father is right that the operation exists," Rennick said.

"Yes. Just wrong about his daughter."

Rennick nodded once. "Tell me the rest."

[Beat 44]
He told it in the order it had happened, which was the order the ledger assembled itself: the three tiers, Dev's eleven pickups, the Castellan 900 on the roof and what it meant about volume, the compute-payment anomaly, Feliksas and the copied log entries, the NRW pattern, the cat-ear connection in four confirmed cases, and last, the Jane Doe.

Nineteen. Two jobs. Corridor background. Cheap package from the Ashland front. Cat ears still active when she came in through the basement. Neuretics wiped. Under the under-city now, in the cold, in the archive.

Rennick listened to all of it without interrupting. When Gault finished, he was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "She has a name."

"She has a Jane Doe number. I don't have the name yet."

"Find the name," Rennick said. "Before we hand anything to anyone."

[Beat 45]
He spent the next four hours on it. The Jane Doe had been logged by the Cermak crew five weeks ago, which put her disappearance roughly six weeks back — long enough that a missing-person flag would have aged off the corridor's informal network and gone cold in the community's own tracking. Long enough that whoever was supposed to miss her had either stopped looking or never been able to start.

He worked the cat-ear community's boards through channels Priya could access, looking for a missing person who matched: young, corridor, two jobs, the cheap package. He cross-referenced against the community's own missing-and-unaccounted list, which was not an official document but was more accurate than the official document for this stretch of corridor, because the community tracked its own and the officials tracked the cases with addresses.

He found her in the community list at two-eleven in the morning. A handwritten entry, probably someone's neuretics-to-text, dated forty-three days ago: *Sol, 19, Pilsen-adjacent, worked Lakeshore kitchen and the 31st Street packaging floor. Went for integration after cat ears. Nobody's heard from her. If you know anything please reach out to the community desk.*

Her name was Sol Castellanos-Park.

[Beat 46]
He sat with her name for a while. Sol. The community list entry had no photograph — just the text, the age, the two jobs, the note about integration. Whoever had written it had not expected an agency to find it. They had written it because the community desk was the only desk that answered for people like Sol, and the community desk answered by keeping the record, because the record was the only thing left to keep.

He added her name to his report file. He tagged it: Jane Doe, Ashland/Cermak intake, NRW batch, five weeks prior, cat ears, Pilsen-adjacent, 19. Sol Castellanos-Park.

[Beat 47]
He messaged Rennick: Found the name. Sol Castellanos-Park, 19, Pilsen. Community list has her missing forty-three days. Same corridor, same cheap package, same integration window, same gone. She's the Jane Doe. She has a name now.

Then he sat in the dark office with the night pressing in through the third-floor window and let himself think the thing he'd been keeping at arm's length since the noodle shop. Whatever bought Sol's body hadn't wanted money. It had wanted cycles — continuously, at scale, the way a body wants oxygen — and it had been buying them by walking girls with fresh cat ears into a procedure that emptied them and left the meat for the crew. That was a hunger with a budget. He had never logged one of those before.

[Beat 48]
He had a shape, a rate, and a direction, and no column to file the buyer in. So he did the thing the ledger taught: when a number won't resolve, you hand it to the person who reads the layer you can't. He flagged the buyer-profile for Corvin and the analog-sourcing for Voss, and he wrote one line of his own under it — buyer needs minds, pays in time, scaling — because that was the part he was sure of and the part that scared him.

[Beat 49]
Rennick replied at two-forty-seven: Good. She gets a file. We open one tonight.

Gault started it. Her name at the top, her age, her two jobs, the community-desk entry word for word. The Cermak intake date and the NRW notation and the detail that her cat ears were still tracking sound when Feliksas logged her. The under-city access, the cold archive, the Castellan 900 on the roof holding her and however many others at four degrees below the corridor that had never counted them.

Douglas Hartley's question had been answered three hours ago. Everything in this file was Sol Castellanos-Park's, and nobody had hired anybody to open it. Gault set the agency rate at the bottom of the page anyway — symbolic, twenty Φ, billed to Cold Read — because a case you work for nothing is a case you can talk yourself out of finishing, and he was not going to be able to talk himself out of this one.

[Beat 50]
Then Feliksas. Still in the building on Ashland, logging bodies morning or night as they came, keeping copies that would get him killed the day the crew read the pattern in him before Cold Read had a case worth the copies. Keep the source warm. The correct tactical reading. Also the reading that left a man with a cipher lock at his back and a cleaner down the hall who handled loose ends.

Gault did the math on it one more time and it came out the same way it always did, which was uneven, so he stopped doing the math and made a call instead. He messaged Priya: I want Feliksas pulled inside two weeks, case or no case. Build me a reason to move early. And he sent Feliksas himself a single line, the kind you send a source you intend to keep alive and not just keep: Three more entries, then you stop copying and you wait for me. Don't get ahead of it. Then he set a marker in Sol's file — extraction of source, hard deadline, fourteen days — so that the next person who opened the ledger would find the cost of Feliksas written down where it couldn't be skipped.

[Beat 51]
He walked home through the Gray Zone with both hands in his pockets, the right one clear out of habit even there, palms reading the street — cold, consistent, no bodies near him.

Douglas Hartley would get his answer in the morning and pay it and be relieved and never know how close the corridor had come to him. That file would close. The other one was open now: a girl named Sol, nineteen, four degrees Celsius under a flooded sub-level, and a man named Feliksas still upstairs on Ashland with a cipher lock at his back and fourteen days on the clock Gault had just started for him.

Two names he hadn't been hired to carry. He let himself into the dark apartment carrying both, and did not set them down.
