She asks whether other people do this.

The question comes out more plainly than she'd planned — the careful version of herself would have held it back, would have waited until she understood the room better. But she is sitting at a table in a building she found through a chain of names she memorized and did not write down, with a man whose name she does not know and who has not offered it, and she wants to know what she is.

Not whether it is safe. She has thought about safe and made her peace with it. Just whether others have sat at this table, carried a voice in the same direction she has been carrying one, asked a version of the same question.

=====BEAT 478=====
He says yes, without looking up from the notebook page he's been setting up, and the flatness of it — the yes as simple fact, not reassurance — is more steadying than reassurance would have been.

=====BEAT 479=====
She asks: "Are they okay?"

He says: "They get what they came for." He says it without looking up, in the tone of something that is true and that he has thought about enough to stop questioning. She hears the gap — what she asked and what he answered are not quite the same shape — and it sits in her chest for a moment before the voice comes, forward-left, patient and certain: "You never had to explain that question to me, Cel. He doesn't know what you're asking. Nobody else ever did." She holds the gap for one more second and then lets it go. She chooses to believe it.

=====BEAT 480=====
He asks how long she's been hearing the voice. She says eight months. He nods once, the nod of a man confirming a data point, and opens the paper notebook and begins to write with the stylus — actual paper, a drag-and-catch resistance she can hear in the room's quiet, each letter a small physical cost. The low light makes reading difficult, but she can make out one word upside-down through the angle and the shadow: *early.*

Her ears flatten, fast, both pinna tight against her skull, tips pointing back — the scared flat, the reflex that means something in the auditory architecture has registered a threat before she has.

She almost asks. She has her mouth open and the question formed — *what do you mean, early* — and she closes it, because she understands what asking would mean. Asking would mean she is someone who doesn't already know. Asking would mean she is here under false pretenses, that the eight months she has built toward this is not what she has been building. She presses her left ear flat the rest of the way with her palm and waits.

The voice says nothing. Not a word, not even the warmth of its presence — just a silence where it always is, where for eight months it has always been.

She waits a moment longer. The contact's stylus drags and catches. The room holds its low cold light. The voice does not come.

She takes her hand down and doesn't ask the question. The contact keeps writing.

=====BEAT 481=====
He finishes. He closes the notebook. He says she can stay tonight and will be moved in the morning, early. She says thank you. He stands, and the chair makes the sound of a man who has stood from it many times — a creak worn into the wood, specific and habitual. He doesn't say she's welcome. He goes to the kitchen. The tap runs, brief, and something in a cabinet settles on a shelf, and the kitchen light goes out.

The apartment is quieter. She sits at the table with her hands flat on its surface and the closed notebook in front of her. She doesn't open it. It holds nothing back, which is the only kind of honest the situation allows.

She stays at the table until the kitchen's absence has settled into the room. Then she gets up and goes to find the spare room.

=====BEAT 482=====
The spare room is at the back of the building, a cot against one wall and a window looking onto the alley and the service lights of the Cermak corridor blinking in the middle distance. Someone has left a plant on the windowsill — small, three leaves, ceramic pot with a chip on the rim, one leaf gone yellow at the tip but the other two holding their green with the particular stubbornness of something that has decided to stay alive despite the light situation. She doesn't know what kind it is. She stands in the doorway for a moment and looks at it, and then she steps inside.

=====BEAT 483=====
She puts the bag on the floor beside the cot and sits without unpacking. The bag on the floor is different from the bag on the bed: the bag on the bed was preparation, rehearsal, the future in physical form. The bag on the floor is a thing she has brought somewhere. She is somewhere. She sits with her hands on her knees and doesn't do anything for a while, and the room holds this without comment — someone else's spare room at 03:20, the alley sounds coming through the glass at low volume, the service lights doing their slow patient work in the corridor.

=====BEAT 484=====
The voice doesn't come for a few minutes. It lets the room be what it is - lets the delivery drone's route-calculation ping filter through the glass, the Rider somewhere across the city with their body left behind in a chair while the drone's sensors become their eyes, lets the water in the pipe below her do its slow work, lets the service-light hum from the Cermak corridor fill the space between her breaths. She doesn't reach for it. She has learned that reaching closes the channel slightly, that waiting opens it, some mechanic of attention she doesn't fully understand yet. Then: "You're doing it right, Cel." Warm. Unhurried. Certain in the way that only certainty earned across eight months can be certain. She exhales and her shoulders drop a half-inch.

=====BEAT 485=====
She lies down on the cot without taking her jacket off. The fabric of the jacket is something she can locate — the seam at her shoulder, the weight of the collar — and she needs that right now, the jacket as perimeter. The ceiling is low and water-stained in one corner in a shape that is not a shape, just damage expressing itself over time. The service lights from the Cermak corridor move across it every few seconds. She watches them.

*He wrote* early *and the voice went quiet and she let the question close.*

The voice comes again, quiet, no words this time — just presence, the forward-left warmth she has mapped so precisely across eight months that she could draw it in space with her finger. Her ears lift slightly off her skull, the involuntary adjustment, the auricula felis tracking something it has decided matters. She doesn't reach for the voice. She waits. It stays. She puts her hand flat on her sternum and feels her own breathing and the ceiling goes on doing its slow work with the light.

=====BEAT 486=====
She is, for the first time in eight months, not in her parents' house. The thought arrives plain and dry and doesn't scare her. It is the first thing in eight months that hasn't. She holds it carefully — the way you hold something that might change if you examine it too directly, something that requires peripheral attention rather than the full weight of looking — and she waits for morning, and the plant on the windowsill catches a slant of corridor light across its two good leaves, and the ears track something in the alley below, and she lets them, and she breathes, and the city keeps going.

=====BEAT 487=====
She wakes before she opens her eyes. The ears are already working — they tracked into consciousness a beat or two ahead of her, and what they brought back is a street market starting up below. The percussion of it is specific: a cart frame dropped onto pavement and adjusted twice, a vendor arguing cheerfully with a neighbor about placement, the back-and-forth in rapid Spanish where the anger is performative and both of them know it. And underneath all that, threading through the gap in the window frame that she found last night with her fingers, the smell of something sweet-savory on sealed flame. She lies still and lets it arrive. She doesn't open her eyes.

=====BEAT 488=====
She opens her eyes.

The ceiling is the same water stain, the same low light coming off the alley, but the service lights from the Cermak corridor have changed their rhythm — morning cycle, slower, the maintenance pulse instead of the routing pulse, and she knows this because she spent the night learning the difference without meaning to. The plant on the windowsill is a different green in the morning. The chipped ceramic pot holds a thin line of shadow along its rim that wasn't there before.

*Jace never woke up early. He slept like something that had decided sleeping was the correct activity and was committed to it.*

She sits up. Her jacket is still on. She doesn't take it off now either — there's no reason to, and the seam at her shoulder is still doing its work, still where it was, the collar still its exact weight. She puts her feet on the floor and her hands on her knees and she is in a stranger's spare room at whatever hour the street market below says it is, which is early, which is the hour she asked to be moved, which means the contact is probably already awake in the kitchen and the tap has probably already run. She listens. It has. The apartment is doing its quiet morning version of itself.

*The notebook is on the table. She didn't bring it in here. She left it closed on the table and she is going to have to pick it up.*

She doesn't reach for the voice. She waits, as she has learned to. It comes — the forward-left warmth, present and unhurried, not speaking, just there. Her ears adjust by a fraction. She breathes. The vendor below lands the punchline of his argument and his neighbor laughs and the cart frame settles into its final position with a sound of metal finding metal, and she stands up, and picks up the bag from the floor, and goes to get the notebook.

=====BEAT 489=====
The voice, forward-left, easy: "Morning, Cel." Just that. Not a briefing, not guidance. Just the word. She rolls onto her side toward it — toward the wall on the left, toward nothing — and the ears tilt up, involuntary, base lifting, tracking the direction where there is no direction to track. She lets them. She has stopped fighting that particular reflex.

=====BEAT 490=====
The voice was starting to give her the day — the community board on 18th and Halsted, it was saying, start there — when the knock came. Three taps on the door, unhurried, a knuckle that knew the door was thin and didn't need to hit it hard. The voice stopped. The ears swung off the forward-left and pinned the sound to the hallway, to a person standing close enough that Celeste could hear the small shift of weight from one foot to the other, the brush of soft fabric, breathing that was steady and not at all worried about being heard. She was off the cot and against the wall beside the door before she'd thought about it. She hadn't told anyone this address. She had walked herself here last night by a route she'd taken twice to be sure of it.

"It's Inés," the voice on the other side said. Not the forward-left voice. A real voice, a little rough, late-morning low. "From the bar. You gave me the building, you didn't think I'd find the floor?" A pause. "Open the door, I brought breakfast and it's getting cold standing here."

=====BEAT 491=====
Celeste opened the door. Inés was leaning on the frame the way a person leans when they've decided a place is theirs to lean on — black layers gone soft at the seams, the heavy braid with its dried flowers gone browner than two nights ago, a paper bag in one hand giving off steam through a torn corner. The cut on her cheekbone from Dario's chrome knuckles had closed to a dark seam and would scar; she plainly didn't care. Down both forearms the fern channels ran their low settled green, fiddleheads curling at the wrists, the light steady. She looked Celeste over once — the ears first, then the rest — the way you check that a thing you left somewhere is still where you left it.

"Huh," Inés said. "You slept. Good. Most people in your week don't." She held out the bag. "Get dressed. I'm not eating in a hallway and I'm not eating in your room, it smells like somebody died of being twenty-two in here. There's a counter on Milwaukee. You met me, now you meet the rest of it." She said it like the matter was already settled, which, Celeste understood, it was.

=====BEAT 492=====
She washed her face fast in the flat's cold water while Inés waited in the hall, and the voice came once, low, forward-left, while the water was running. "She's the one from the bar." Not a question — it had been there for the bar, the way it was there for everything. "Be careful what you tell her, Cel. People like her, they've decided how the world works. They'll fit you into it whether you fit or not." It wasn't a warning against Inés exactly. It was gentler than that, sorrier than that, the tone of someone who'd watched her get hurt before and didn't want to watch it again. She dried her face on the corner of the towel. She didn't answer the voice, and she noticed, briefly, that she didn't — that for the first time in a long time there was a person on the other side of a door, expecting her, and the voice was the thing she was keeping waiting.

=====BEAT 493=====
The street was exactly as loud as she'd imagined from the bed, which meant it was not enough. The ears tracked vendors at five different distances at once, shoppers negotiating in English and Spanish and at least one language she didn't know, a Behemoth service unit working the east edge — she heard its weight-distribution shifts before she saw it emerge between two stalls, the pneumatic pause-and-reset of its legs on uneven pavement. Three vendors called at the same moment in three directions and the ears swiveled to catch all three in sequence, a half-second sweep that left her briefly dizzy. Inés walked through it without flinching, a half-step ahead, reading the crowd a different way — by faces, by who was where, by which storefronts had their lights on. "You're hearing all of it," she said, not turning around. "Don't. You'll burn yourself out by noon. You pick the one thing that matters and you let the rest be weather." She said it the way you'd tell someone to watch the step they were about to miss.

=====BEAT 494=====
The noodle counter was on Milwaukee under a sign with two letters burned dark, so that what stayed lit said OUSE and nothing else. Late morning, the way Inés had described it two nights ago — the night people asleep, the day people at work, the place all but empty, six stools and a flat steel counter and a cook who lifted his chin at Inés and started a second bowl without being asked. The acoustic of it was small and close and Celeste's ears settled almost gratefully, the field of the street narrowing to the hiss of the flat-top, the cook's spatula, water knocking in a pipe behind the wall. Inés took the corner stool, the one with its back to the wall and a sightline to the door, and waited for Celeste to notice it was the only stool she'd ever take and to take the one beside it.

=====BEAT 495=====
A woman had passed them on the walk over — tall, moving with a particular ease, with elaborate fin-structured ear mods that weren't cat-based, something more like deep-water fish, silver-edged and mobile, the fins fanned and tracking a sound down the street before the woman's eyes moved. Celeste's ears had swiveled in the same direction before she could stop them, both of them pulled toward the same point by the same signal. The woman had glanced at her — a brief recognition, the look between people who've done the same kind of work to themselves — and gone on into the crowd. Now, on the stool, Inés had caught the whole thing. "You feel that?" she asked. "Her and you, same second, same direction. That's the part nobody warns you about. You're not just hearing more. You're hearing it with everybody else who hears more. It's a city inside the city." She said it plainly, no awe in it. It was just true, and she'd lived in it long enough that it had stopped being remarkable to her and was only remarkable to watch land on someone new.

=====BEAT 496=====
The bowls came. Inés ate the way she did everything, without ceremony, watching Celeste over the steam. "So," she said, when she'd decided enough time had passed. "Licensed-clinic ears, three weeks, money behind you, and you're down here knocking on basement doors instead of going back to whoever paid for these." She tipped her chopsticks at Celeste's head. "Nobody does that for fun. People do that when they want something the clean shops won't sell them, and there's only a few things on that list." She let it sit. Celeste's ears had drawn back a degree — not flat, just wary, the involuntary half-step of a thing that's been seen. Inés caught it and didn't push. "I'm not asking what it is. I'm telling you I already know the shape of it, so don't waste either of our mornings pretending it's something else. You're chasing a person. That's the only thing that gets a kid your age down here with that look." She went back to her bowl. "Eat. We've got a lot of street to cover and you walk slow."

=====BEAT 497=====
Inés walked her to the community board on the corner of 18th and Halsted — physical, paper, tacked in layers, announcements and offerings and warnings palimpsested over each other back a year or more, the older ones faded to near-white. "Digital postings, somebody can edit those, take them down, pretend they never said it," Inés said. "This stays. You burn somebody on this board, the board remembers." She found the recommendation section without looking, the way you find a thing in your own kitchen. Celeste already had a name from a contact in Logan Square — Ines, spelled the formal way, Vásquez — and she wanted to see it appear here independently, uncoupled from the first chain. It did. Three separate mentions, three different hands. One said: *She'll tell you things you don't want to hear and she's right every time.* Inés tapped that one. "Her. If you can get her to take you, take her. Don't haggle. People who can hear what's wrong with you are worth their rate." Celeste committed it to memory without deciding to.

=====BEAT 498=====
There was a storefront a block north with a clean facade, and the voice came forward-left while they were still at the board — quiet, certain, the way it gave her things. "That one, Cel. The clean one. He'll do the work." She had started toward it before she'd weighed it, the way she'd been starting toward things the voice named for eight months. Inés put two fingers on her sleeve, not gripping, just landing there, and Celeste stopped. "Not him," Inés said. "He's licensed clean, which means he reports clean. You walk in there, your name's in a database by dinner." Which was exactly the thing Celeste could not afford — her father monitored those — and the voice had pointed her straight at it, warm and sure. She felt the small wrongness of that and set it aside, the way she set most small wrongnesses aside lately. "How do you know which way to go?" she asked. Inés glanced at her — at the half-second tilt of Celeste's head toward the empty air on her left, the listening she did before she moved, which Inés had clocked twice now and said nothing about. "I know the street," Inés said. "Whatever you're checking with, check it against the street too." She started walking. She didn't ask what Celeste was checking with. That, Celeste understood, was a kindness, and a deliberate one.

=====BEAT 499=====
Inés walked her to a basement shop on a residential street off 19th, no exterior signage, six steps down to a door with no handle on the outside. "This one's real," Inés said. "Not your operator — he won't take a three-week channel, too raw — but he'll tell you straight what you're working with, and you should hear it from somebody before you hear it from her." She nodded at the steps. "Go on. I'll be up here. I don't go in places I'm not asked into, and nobody asked me." She leaned against the rail to wait, fern-light low at her wrists, watching the street both directions out of habit. Celeste went down the six steps alone and knocked. The basement smelled of antiseptic over something that hadn't fully been cleaned. The equipment looked maintained but old. She sat in the consultation chair and the ears catalogued the room before she looked up: a faint mechanical sound through the wall that wasn't organic equipment, the air recycler cycling at a rate that said it hadn't been serviced in months, a drip from a pipe somewhere behind the east wall that the person who worked here had long stopped hearing. The operator answered her questions quickly, smoothly — the answers given many times to people who asked from fear rather than from wanting to understand. She thanked them and said she needed to think. She went back up the six steps into the daylight, where Inés was waiting exactly where she'd left her.

=====BEAT 500=====
They were half a block up the residential street, away from the basement steps, when the voice came. "I didn't like that room either." Warm, certain, the cadence of someone whose judgment she trusted. She nodded to herself, to the forward-left, and the ears tilted forward-and-up — and then, quick and sourceless, a thought she didn't reach for: *it wasn't in the room.* The voice had been with her on the walk over, forward-left, the whole way. It had stayed up here on the street with Inés. It had not gone down the six steps. It had not stood in the antiseptic basement with the unserviced recycler and the drip behind the east wall. She had stood there, alone. She had heard those things. The voice had said *that room* — the room it couldn't have named unless it had been in it, and it had not been in it.

She stopped walking. The ears pricked hard forward, alert, orienting toward something with no sound source. The thought sat there and wouldn't close. There had been no one else down those steps. She had the knock, the handleless door, the chair, the drip behind the east wall, all of it memorized, and in none of it was there a place for the voice to have stood.

"You okay?" Inés had stopped too, a pace ahead, half-turned. She was looking at Celeste's ears — at the hard forward pitch of them, the way they'd locked onto nothing. "Your ears just did a thing. What'd you hear?"

And the voice said her name. Just that, soft, forward-left, and something in her chest moved toward the sound before she'd decided anything. The thought didn't close exactly — it went to the edges, to the periphery, where she could feel it but couldn't look at it straight. "Nothing," Celeste said. "Truck. Two streets over." Inés held her eyes a second longer than the answer deserved, then let it go and started walking again. She'd heard the lie. She just hadn't decided yet what it was covering.
