The bar was called the Hereafter, which Sasha had taken for a joke until she'd been inside an hour, after which it stopped being one.

She had come for the quiet. Not the room's quiet — the room was loud in the ordinary way, a gray-market generator chugging under the floor, a cracked public screen over the bottles running the Consensus loop, the city's machines explaining in a warm voice whose fault the holes in the world were and how it was, reassuringly, the fault of people who could hear things. Sasha could hear things. She did not look at the screen. She had come for the other quiet, the one that did not come cheap and never came for long.

The hum had been with her since she was small. A low note under everything, the way a refrigerator runs in a room you're not in, except the room was the whole world and the note never stopped and sometimes, under it, the distinct sensation of being looked at through a wall by something with no particular opinion about her. Everyone else's head had been closed at the factory. Hers had been left open. She had stopped being angry about the reason and stayed angry about the fact.

There was exactly one thing loud enough to drown it, and it was expensive, and a man at the end of the bar had been generous enough to volunteer.

She gave him a second smile with the bottom of a bottle. For the length of it — the reach, the turn, the wet surprised sound he made discovering his new face — the hum went away. It was the only silence she got. It lasted, this time, about as long as it took him to fold up against the rail, and then it came back, right on schedule, the way it always did, and she was alone inside her skull again with the note and the feeling of being watched, and furious about both, which was how she knew the evening was over.

Nobody followed her out. People in the Hereafter had a good instinct for the difference between a fight and a service being rendered.

Outside was Joliet, which the GLMZ kept around the way a man keeps a tooth he means to pull. The towers were a hundred kilometers northeast, a smear of light low on the sky like a sunrise that had gotten stuck and given up. Out here the freight rails ran flat and dead in both directions, forty years since anything rode them — everyone took the Pulse now, or the Loop, and the rails just lay there going chalk-white where the repair strata had started knitting them back together one decade and then stopped, mid-thought, when the money left. She wanted a road with no people on it. The rail bed delivered. She walked it north because north was the only direction with a light at the end of it.

The rail bed also, it turned out, had a camel on it.

Sasha stopped.

The camel stopped too, a few meters off, and regarded her with the long sideways patience of an animal that has nowhere to be. On its back sat a man. He was dressed for no weather in particular and sat the camel like he'd been doing it a thousand years and might do it a thousand more, and the important thing, the thing her body understood before the rest of her caught up, was that there are no camels in southern Illinois. There had never been camels in southern Illinois. The fact arrived in her stomach as a small cold drop and she filed it where she filed everything and kept her face shut.

"Where did you get the camel?" she said. It was not the first question. It was the only one her mouth would make.

"The camel is not my thrall," the man said. He did not look down at it. "It is free to come and go as it pleases. We just happen to be going the same way."

"Which way is that?"

"We just arrived."

Her hand had gone to Noise on her left hip — the wrong hand for a stranger, the right hand for a threat, and her body had not finished deciding which he was.

"Leave it, or take it out," the man said, pleasantly. "It is the same to us. You have already done both."

"I haven't done anything."

"Not yet. Also already. We are not good with your tenses."

The camel took a step toward her. Not the man steering it — the camel, on its own business, came and put its great soft ridiculous head down near her shoulder and breathed at her, the way the strays did, the way every dumb animal in the Glooms did the second she stopped moving, like she gave off a smell that said *safe* in a language she had never agreed to speak. She did not like animals. She especially did not like that they liked her. She stepped back. The camel followed. She let it; it was that or shoot a camel, and she had standards.

"Why are you talking to me?" she said.

"The others are shut." The man tipped his head at the dark, the whole dark, the dead town and the dead rails and the smear of city light behind it all. "Talking to them is talking to a wall that believes it is a window. You were left open. Someone should have closed you, and did not." A pause that wasn't a pause, more like a frame dropped from the middle of a thing. "We are glad of the oversight."

It got in. She felt it get in — the breath she didn't take, the held second — *someone should have closed you and did not,* nineteen years of it said back to her flat as a weather report by a man on a camel who wasn't there. Then she shut the door on it, hard, the way she'd had to learn to shut everything.

"Don't," she said.

"We have already stopped," the man agreed.

She made herself look at the towers instead of at him. "Something's making holes. Out here. And up there." She had felt it for weeks, the difference: the old hum out here was a cracked bell still ringing the note it broke on, but the new pull, the one under the city, did not ring. It *drew*. "What is it."

"You make a hole in a thing," the man said, "and the thing is on both sides of the hole. They drill toward us. We arrive toward them. Where you would put the middle, there will be no middle." The camel chewed at nothing, sideways, content. "We are arriving. You felt it. That is why you are walking north and telling yourself it is because of the light."

"I'm walking north because there's nothing behind me."

"Yes," the man said, kindly, as though she had agreed with him.

"Are you the thing that's been taking people?" "The kids."

"No. That one does not know what a person is." For the first time something moved in the voice, or under it, the *we* widening to take in more *we* than one man could hold. "We know. That is worse, and it is better. You will decide which."

It pointed northeast. At the light. At the only place she'd spent her whole life trying not to go.

"We just arrived," the man said again, like it was good news, and the camel turned to follow the rails toward the city as though the two of them had settled something, and Sasha, against every instinct she had ever trusted to keep her alive, fell into step.
