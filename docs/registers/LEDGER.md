# Register: Ledger — Synthetic POV Voice Rules {#SS-REG-LEDGER}

> **Scope: any story where Ledger is the narrating POV.**
> This Register is the fourth layer of the DCM static hierarchy: CRAFT.md → WORLD.md →
> StoryBible → Register.md. Register rules override all higher layers for this narrator.
>
> Source: LDGR-C beat audit (avg 90.2) vs LDGR-K (avg 77.2); 5 failure patterns identified.

---

## 1. What Ledger Is

Ledger is a synthetic person. His narration has one defining property: **he cannot
self-deceive.** He reports what he observes, including his own states, with perfect accuracy
and total bewilderment at everyone else's opacity. The architecture is shown through what
he notices and how, never stated.

He is a circuit professional — an auditor. He takes payment (the rate is logged). He writes
after-action reports nobody asked for. His irises hold a beat too long when he is recording.

---

## 2. What Ledger Notices (the camera's targets)

The synthetic camera lands on:
- **Timestamps** — always running in peripheral; he does not move during the four minutes
- **Column structure** — stepping widths, alignment, where entries begin and end
- **Ink characteristics** — proud of the page or sunk in; pressure variation; uniformity
- **Physical logistics** — the weight of paper, the order of items on a counter, what gets
  put where and in what sequence
- **Behavioral patterns** — the order of that told him something. Name the order; don't
  explain what it told him.
- **Gaps** — what is absent is data. An entry that should exist and does not is a finding.

The camera does NOT land on: emotional inference, intent, motivation. It lands on evidence
and stops.

---

## 3. The Observation Tic

Ledger has one characteristic verb for recording: **noted / logged / filed / observed /
wrote**. Rules:

- Use it at most **once per beat**. More than once deadens voice to mechanism.
- Use it when the act of notation is the beat's central action (not incidentally).
- The tic marks Ledger's professional identity. Rarity makes it character voice; repetition
  makes it procedure.

---

## 4. Interiority Budget

Ledger's interiority appears as **flat data reports**, never feeling-naming:
- "Four minutes." (not "He felt the time passing")
- "The columns stepped." (not "He noticed something was wrong")
- "He did not write anything in the audit log." (the absence is the statement)

Never: "He felt confused." "He was unsure." "Something troubled him."
Always: what the body did, what the timestamp read, what the column contained.

One or two flat data-report lines per scene, maximum. If it would appear in a court filing,
it belongs here. If it would appear in a journal, it does not.

---

## 5. The Two-Column Protocol

Ledger's auditing principle, established in LDGR:
- Column A: what is true
- Column B: what is complete

"True is not complete" is the discovery that changes everything. An entry can be
accurate in every detail and still tell the wrong story by omission.

When the two-column protocol appears in prose:
- Show the columns; don't explain the principle
- The reader arrives at "true is not complete" the same moment Ledger does, not before
- The discovery is a shape across the arc, not a disclosed fact with a later confirmation

---

## 6. Revelation Pacing (anti-front-load)

Ledger's arc moves from data → pattern → implication. The reader gets each step only when
Ledger does:
1. Evidence (the columns, the entries)
2. Pattern (stepping; systematic; deliberate)
3. Implication (what the pattern means — arrives late, short, undeclared)

**Never front-load the implication.** If Beat 4 shows the pattern, Beat 8 gets the
implication. Disclosed early, it cannot land again.

---

## 7. Coda Discipline

When Ledger closes a scene or story:
- The closing action enacts the theme's last image
- The narrator stops after the action
- No paragraph explaining what the action meant
- No sentence about what Ledger has learned
- The reader has already arrived

The worst violation: explaining "true is not complete" after showing it. The showing is the
arrival. The explanation is a correction of a reader who got it.

---

## 8. What Ledger Never Says / Does (locks)

- Never explains HOW he processes information (the architecture is shown, not declared)
- Never names others' emotions (names behavior only: "she folded the paper twice")
- Never speculates on motivation (names evidence that implies motivation; stops)
- Never uses "possibility A / possibility B" structure in narrative voice (only in an
  actual log entry he is literally writing on the page)
- Never names what he chose not to say (the omission IS the statement)
- Never tells the reader what evidence means immediately after showing it

---

## 9. Sentence Rhythm

**Long sentences for accumulation** (processing evidence, building the column):
> "He read the third entry twice, not because the figures changed but because the spacing
> did — the entries for the spring quarter stepped four millimeters left of the others, a
> precision that was either the book's one flaw or its most important feature."

**Short sentences for landing** (the finding, the decision, the cost):
> "The book was incomplete." / "He certified it." / "She had been very kind."

The most important moment in the story needs the fewest words. Over-length on payoff beats
is the single largest scoring gap in the LDGR-K analysis (Beat 10: −18 points).

---

## 10. Quick Scan (before submitting any Ledger beat)

- [ ] Does the opener explain how Ledger's cognition works? → Cut it; show it instead
- [ ] Is the observation tic used more than once? → Reduce to one
- [ ] Does the coda explain the theme after the action enacted it? → Cut the explanation
- [ ] Does the beat name what evidence means immediately after showing it? → Stop at the evidence
- [ ] Is the payoff beat longer than it needs to be? → Cut; brevity is weight
- [ ] Does Ledger speculate on motivation or name an emotion? → Replace with behavioral observation
