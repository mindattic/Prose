# The Twelve Apostles — who were they, besides "a follower of Jesus"?

The question this document asks: strip away "follower of Jesus," and what independent
biographical fact — birthplace, occupation beyond the fisherman clichés, family, grave,
descendants — actually survives for each of the Twelve? If these were real, historically
significant people, they should leave the kind of trail real people leave: named relatives,
identifiable burial, some trace outside a text written to make a theological case for them.

## 1. The Scriptural Claim

The New Testament gives **four apostle lists that do not fully agree with each other**:

| Matthew 10:2–4 | Mark 3:16–19 | Luke 6:14–16 | Acts 1:13 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simon (Peter) | Simon (Peter) | Simon (Peter) | Peter |
| Andrew | James son of Zebedee | Andrew | John |
| James son of Zebedee | John | James | James |
| John | Andrew | John | Andrew |
| Philip | Philip | Philip | Philip |
| Bartholomew | Bartholomew | Bartholomew | Thomas |
| Thomas | Matthew | Matthew | Bartholomew |
| Matthew | Thomas | Thomas | Matthew |
| James son of Alphaeus | James son of Alphaeus | James son of Alphaeus | James son of Alphaeus |
| Thaddaeus | Thaddaeus | Simon the Zealot | Simon the Zealot |
| Simon the Zealot | Simon the Zealot | Judas son of James | Judas son of James |
| Judas Iscariot | Judas Iscariot | Judas Iscariot | — (replaced by Matthias, Acts 1:26) |

The rosters agree on nine names and disagree on the tenth slot: Matthew and Mark's
**"Thaddaeus"** is almost certainly the same person as Luke and Acts's **"Judas son of
James"** — different names for one man, or a manuscript-tradition variant (some manuscripts of
Matthew read "Lebbaeus" instead of "Thaddaeus" at this exact point), but the Gospels never
explain the discrepancy or acknowledge it. "Bartholomew" is widely, though not universally,
identified by scholars with "Nathanael" (who appears only in John 1:45–51 and is never called
an apostle there at all, and never appears in any of the four Twelve-lists by that name).
Judas Iscariot's own fate is given two incompatible endings: hanging himself (Matthew 27:3–10)
versus falling headlong and bursting open in a field (Acts 1:18) — the two accounts do not
describe the same event and no harmonization is attempted by either text.

## 2. The Spectrum of Scholarship

- **Traditional / confessional (Catholic and Orthodox hagiography):** full historicity of all
  Twelve as individuals, their traditional missionary fields (India, Ethiopia, Persia, Armenia,
  Georgia, Spain, etc.), and the physical relics/tombs venerated for each, understood as an
  unbroken historical chain of apostolic succession.
- **Mainstream historical-critical** (Bart D. Ehrman, Raymond E. Brown, John P. Meier's *A
  Marginal Jew* project, E. P. Sanders): high confidence that a group of close followers existed
  and that **Peter, James son of Zebedee, and John** were real, active, identifiable figures in
  the earliest Jerusalem church — this rests not on the Gospels but on **Paul's own letters**
  (Galatians 1:18–19, 2:9), written within roughly two decades of the crucifixion, which is
  independently our earliest surviving Christian source and names Peter ("Cephas"), James, and
  John as the Jerusalem "pillars" Paul met personally. Confidence drops sharply for the other
  eight to nine names: they were very likely real people, but essentially unknowable as
  individuals beyond the bare name in a list. The missionary-journey and martyrdom narratives
  for most of them are read as later pious literature, not history.
- **Skeptical / minority-academic** (e.g., Richard Carrier and similar writers, a position
  rejected by the working mainstream of historians, including non-religious and explicitly
  atheist historians in the field): questions whether "the Twelve" existed as a fixed, named
  body at all during Jesus's lifetime, proposing the roster may be a later church-organizational
  retrojection built to mirror the twelve tribes of Israel symbolically. This is distinct from,
  and should not be conflated with, "Jesus mythicism" (denial that Jesus existed at all), which
  is a separate and even more fringe position outside this document's scope.

## 3. The Independent Record, apostle by apostle

- **Peter:** the best-attested by far. *1 Clement* (Rome, c. 96 CE, i.e., within living memory)
  references Peter's martyrdom; Ignatius of Antioch (early 2nd century) references him as an
  authority. The Vatican announced in 1968 (under Pope Paul VI) that excavations beneath St.
  Peter's Basilica (the Vatican necropolis, excavated 1940s–60s) had recovered bones judged
  "consistent with" a man of Peter's approximate age — this identification is contested by
  independent archaeologists and rests on inference and unbroken-veneration tradition, not
  epigraphic proof; there is no first-century chain of custody that can be independently verified.
- **James, son of Zebedee:** the *only* apostle whose death is recorded in the New Testament
  itself (Acts 12:2 — executed by Herod Agrippa I, c. 44 CE), making this the single
  best-attested apostolic death by scripture's own account. The Spanish tradition that his body
  was later transported to Compostela is a **medieval legend with no ancient source**, first
  appearing centuries later and closely tied to the political and religious needs of the
  Reconquista-era Iberian church — a textbook case of legendary accretion serving a later
  institution's interests.
- **John:** tradition conflates several figures who may not be the same person — "John son of
  Zebedee," "John the Evangelist" (traditional author of the Fourth Gospel), "John the Elder,"
  and "John of Patmos" (author of Revelation). The Ephesus tomb tradition is attested no earlier
  than the 2nd century (via Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, as later quoted by Eusebius in the
  4th century), long after any living memory of the man himself.
- **Andrew:** his legendary martyrdom on an X-shaped cross is a **medieval invention** (no
  source earlier than roughly the 10th century); his main ancient biographical source is the
  apocryphal *Acts of Andrew* (2nd–3rd century), a genre of text written to give later
  communities an apostolic founder, not a contemporary record.
- **Thomas:** the strongest non-canonical secondary tradition of any apostle besides Peter. The
  *Acts of Thomas* (3rd-century Syriac) claims a mission to India, and the **Saint Thomas
  Christian community of Kerala, India**, maintains a continuous tradition of this origin to the
  present day. This is worth taking more seriously than most apostolic legends because the
  underlying *possibility* is genuinely plausible — an active Indo-Roman trade route via the
  Malabar pepper coast existed in the 1st century CE — but the specific narrative is still a
  later (centuries-removed) hagiographical composition, not a contemporary attestation of the
  events it describes.
- **Matthew, Bartholomew/Nathanael, Philip, Simon the Zealot, Thaddaeus/Judas son of James,
  James son of Alphaeus:** for all six, there is effectively **zero independent first-century
  attestation** beyond appearing on a list. Everything else claimed about them — missionary
  destinations (Ethiopia, Persia, Armenia, Georgia), martyrdom methods, relics, tombs — comes
  from apocryphal Acts composed 100–300+ years after the fact, geographically scattered in a
  pattern that closely tracks which later regional churches wanted an apostolic founding claim
  ("apostolic succession" as an institutional asset), rather than tracking any transmitted
  historical memory.
- **Judas Iscariot:** uniquely, the NT gives him two mutually exclusive deaths (see §1). Beyond
  the betrayal narrative and its two endings, nothing external about him survives at all.

**Relics and tombs, as a pattern:** nearly every apostle has at least one, and often multiple,
**rival** cities or churches claiming to hold his authentic tomb or relics — directly comparable
to the well-known problem of multiple churches each claiming the true head of John the Baptist.
The existence of *competing* claims is not neutral evidence; it is itself a strong indicator
that these are later institutional inventions rather than a single preserved historical fact
being merely disputed.

**Family and lineage:** essentially none survive for any of the Twelve — no genealogies, no
named children, no descendants claimed by anyone, ever. This is a genuinely striking asymmetry:
the Gospels bother to construct **two different, mutually incompatible genealogies for Jesus
himself** (Matthew 1:1–17 versus Luke 3:23–38, which disagree on the line of descent from David
onward and even on the name of Joseph's father). No comparable effort was ever made for any of
the Twelve. Structurally, the Twelve function in the text more as a **symbolic number** —
deliberately mirroring the twelve tribes of Israel (made explicit in Matthew 19:28 and Luke
22:30) — than as twelve fully individuated historical biographies.

## 4. The Gap Table

| Attested (independent sources / physical evidence) | Assumed (plausible, widely repeated, rests on scripture alone or inference) | Legendary Accretion (added later, for a traceable reason) |
|---|---|---|
| Peter, James (Zebedee), and John as real, named, active figures in the earliest Jerusalem church (Paul's own letters, Galatians 1–2, our earliest Christian source) | That "the Twelve" existed as a fixed, stable, named roster during Jesus's ministry exactly as later recorded — the four lists themselves disagree on one slot | Missionary-journey and martyrdom narratives for 8–9 of the Twelve, composed centuries later in apocryphal Acts, geographically distributed to match later regional churches' institutional needs |
| James (Zebedee)'s death by Herod Agrippa I, c. 44 CE (Acts 12:2 — the one apostolic death the NT itself records) | Vatican necropolis bones (1968 announcement) as Peter's actual remains — plausible tradition, no independently verifiable first-century chain of custody | The Compostela (Spain) James tradition — a medieval Reconquista-era invention with no ancient source |
| A continuous Saint Thomas Christian community in Kerala, India, with genuine 1st-century Indo-Roman trade contact making the underlying claim plausible | That Thomas personally, specifically, founded this community as narrated | The *Acts of Thomas*'s specific India narrative details (3rd century Syriac composition) |
| Two independent, non-harmonized NT accounts of Judas Iscariot's death exist | That either account alone is "the" historical event, absent a way to adjudicate between them | Any later tradition that quietly resolves the Matthew/Acts contradiction without flagging that one is being preferred over the other |

## 5. Open Questions

- Whether "the Twelve" as a bounded, numbered body is a genuine feature of Jesus's own ministry
  or a later organizational construct is not settled — Paul's letters attest "the twelve" as a
  known group (1 Corinthians 15:5) very early, which weighs against the more skeptical reading,
  but do not independently confirm the specific roster.
- Almost nothing about the actual lives — occupations beyond the Synoptic fisherman framing for
  a few of them, ages, hometowns beyond Galilee generally, marital status (Peter's mother-in-law
  is mentioned in Mark 1:30, implying he was married, but this is the exception, not the rule) —
  is recoverable for most of the Twelve, and this is a genuine evidentiary gap, not a solvable
  puzzle.
- Whether "Bartholomew" and "Nathanael" are the same person is a longstanding, unresolved
  identification, not a settled fact.
