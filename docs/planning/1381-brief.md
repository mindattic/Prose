## 1. Series Position {#SS-BRIEF-1381-§1}

**Universe:** NONFICTION. **Story type:** Standalone NONFICTION book, third production line after the
Gospel tetralogy (Matthew/Mark/Luke/John) and NEPH (*Sons of God, Daughters of Men*). Each of
those was a text/tradition-vs-history examination of a religious subject; this book applies the
identical citation-grounding method to a **secular historical event**: the English Rising of
1381, conventionally called the "Peasants' Revolt." **Node code: `1381`.**

**Book(s) this story serves:** None as a prerequisite. Fully standalone.

**Timing:** Real-world history, no in-fiction continuity. The book covers, in order: England's
manorial economy before 1348; the Black Death and the Statute of Labourers (1349–1351); the
Great Rumour (1377); the three poll taxes (1377–1381); the revolt itself (30 May – 15 July 1381,
with suppression running into 1382); and its long-tail legacy and historiography to the present.

## 2. Mission Contribution {#SS-BRIEF-1381-§2}

Which part of NONFICTION's mission does this book advance?

- **[x] The "George Washington's dentures" standard, applied to a national founding myth rather
  than a religious one.** "Peasants' Revolt" is itself a 19th-century coinage (see §5, Naming) not
  used by any contemporary chronicle — the popular version (leaderless rural mob, "peasants,"
  spontaneous violence) is heritage; the record shows organized urban and rural artisans,
  named leadership, written demands, and selective, targeted violence. Making that gap explicit,
  chapter by chapter, is this book's version of the Gospel books' historicity work.
- **[x] Spectrum-of-scholarship as historiography, not confessional camps.** NONFICTION's usual
  Jewish/Christian-confessional/mainstream-critical/empiricist spectrum (`docs/NONFICTION.md` §2)
  doesn't apply to secular political history. The equivalent spectrum here runs **Whig/liberal
  constitutional readings** (the revolt as proto-democratic milestone) through **Marxist social
  history** (Hilton's *Bond Men Made Free* — class conflict as the primary lens) to the
  **revisionist/archival turn** (Dobson's documentary sourcebook, Justice's *Writing and
  Rebellion* on rebel literacy and rhetoric, Barker's narrative synthesis, Prescott/Liddy's
  record-office work) that treats the revolt's causes and impact as more locally contingent and
  less nationally decisive than either older school assumed. A chapter is built around watching
  this spectrum disagree about the same event, never adjudicating who's "right."
- **[ ] None** — not applicable.

## 8. Thematic Complement {#SS-BRIEF-1381-§8}

**Theme:** A tax collector gets run out of an Essex town, and six weeks later the Archbishop of
Canterbury is dead on Tower Hill and a fourteen-year-old king is negotiating, on horseback, with
the largest crowd London has ever seen — and within a month the whole thing is over and almost
nothing the rebels won survives on paper. The book's spine is the gap between how fast it moved
and how little of it lasted in law, set against how much it actually changed underneath the law.

**Register:** Same as the rest of NONFICTION — curious, not adversarial; dry wit per `docs/NONFICTION.md`
§3b (rate-limited, never at the crown's, the rebels', or the dead's expense); "Then and Now"
closer per chapter (§3c); no invented specifics; genuine open questions (Jack Straw's identity,
the Bampton first-name confusion, whether Ball's Blackheath sermon text is contemporary or later
literary reconstruction) rendered as real uncertainty, not smoothed over.

**No dramatization — the one explicit departure from the Gospel/NEPH prose model.** Per the
user's direct instruction: this book stays in expository/narrative-nonfiction register throughout
— reconstructed scene-setting grounded entirely in the documentary record, never invented
dialogue, interiority, or sensory detail beyond what a chronicle or record actually attests. Where
a chronicle *does* record direct speech (Tyler's Smithfield demands per the Anonimalle Chronicle,
Ball's "When Adam delved" line), it is quoted and sourced as reported speech, not staged as scene.

**What would be duplicated if this book didn't exist:** nothing — no other NONFICTION work examines
secular English constitutional/social history; this is the template for future non-religious
NONFICTION subjects.

## 9. Structural Blueprint Seed {#SS-BRIEF-1381-§9}

**Resolution mode:** External/situational — the revolt is crushed and its named leaders executed,
but its stated grievance (the poll tax) is never levied again in this form, and villeinage does
erode over the following century. Both the defeat and the longer-run vindication are real and
neither cancels the other; the book does not resolve which mattered more (see §2's revisionist
pole).

**No dramatization, no moral-polarity/POV apparatus** — unlike TFAH, this book carries no
committed narrative "side." It is not building toward a POV thesis; it is reconstructing a
sequence of events and stating the range of ways serious historians have weighed them.

**Chapter spine (15 numbered chapters, chronological, + 2 trailing structural chapters + Notes +
Glossary):**

1. The Realm Before the Plague — manorialism, villeinage, the pre-1348 baseline
2. The Black Death and the Wage Shock — Ordinance (1349) and Statute (1351) of Labourers
3. The Great Rumour — the 1377 ancient-demesne test cases, the legal dress rehearsal
4. Paying for France — the three poll taxes, 1377/1379/1380–81
5. John Ball, Hedge-Priest — his preaching career before the revolt, the Wycliffe-connection dispute
6. Fobbing and Brentwood — the Essex spark, 30 May – 4 June
7. Kent Rises — Maidstone, Rochester, Canterbury, the election of Tyler, 5–10 June
8. Blackheath — the march on London, Ball's sermon, 11–12 June
9. The City Opens — the Savoy burns, the killing of the Flemings, 13 June
10. The Tower Falls — Sudbury and Hales, 14 June

**Event-type palette:** legal test case / fiscal policy / crowd action / siege-without-resistance
/ urban insurrection / summary execution / negotiated charter / battlefield suppression / show
trial — following the real event sequence, not an invented dramatic palette.

**Intertextual anchors (grounding the spectrum, not narrative style):**
1. R.B. Dobson, *The Peasants' Revolt of 1381* (2nd ed., Macmillan, 1983) — the documentary
   sourcebook every subsequent study cites
2. Rodney Hilton, *Bond Men Made Free: Medieval Peasant Movements and the English Rising of 1381*
   (Methuen, 1973; new intro. by Christopher Dyer, Routledge, 2003) — the Marxist class-conflict
   reading
3. Steven Justice, *Writing and Rebellion: England in 1381* (University of California Press, 1994)
   — the revisionist turn on rebel literacy, rhetoric, and textuality
4. Juliet Barker, *England, Arise: The People, the King and the Great Revolt of 1381* (Little,
   Brown, 2014) — the most recent full narrative synthesis, "the Great Revolt" naming choice
