## 1. Positioning {#SF-§1}

### Genre banner

**Neo-Cyberpunk.**

The series is positioned, marketed, and shelved as Neo-Cyberpunk — the genre's modern revision, not its museum piece. The **Neo-** prefix signals the update while inheriting the genre's billion-dollar discoverability: the streaming categories, the retail shelves, the search algorithms, the audience who came through *Edgerunners* and *Blade Runner 2049* and wants more. Internally, the GLMZ uses "Quantumpunk" as an in-universe flavor word — the feel of a world where currency is compute-time and AIs are ambient as weather. But that is color, not a shelf. The shelf is Neo-Cyberpunk.

### The logline

**In a city where every AI has an agenda and the rules are written by the people who break them first, one contractor keeps a code nobody else believes in anymore — and the city is slowly learning that might matter.**

### The pitch

The Greater Lake Michigan Zone, 2226. A vertical megacity on the western shore of Lake Michigan, half a kilometer tall, governed by CorpoNations and serviced by rogue AIs so ambient they are practically weather. The people living in it — freelancers, investigators, organ repo techs, schism-sensitives, underground tribes, social engineers — do not need to unplug because there is nothing to unplug from. Neural interfaces are grown into the brain. Remote operators leave their bodies behind through wireless quantum entanglement. Hackers commune with unlicensed AIs in their heads. The city is already inside you.

StreetSamurai is a literary Neo-Cyberpunk universe of connected stories in this world: sharp, warm, sometimes funny, often brutal, never gratuitous. Its flagship work — the *Bushido Coda* series — follows Kyle, a freelance contractor who charges standard rate, keeps his word, and is being quietly managed by a rogue AI that has been routing his contracts for eleven years. The reader sees the architecture; Kyle does not. Around him, and independent of him, dozens of other characters live through their own complete stories — a truancy investigator who stumbles into a pattern the city cannot officially acknowledge, a team of organ repo techs who make the worst mistake of their careers, a young schism-sensitive in Joliet who can hear the other side of a dimensional tear and cannot make it stop. Every story is a complete entry point. Every story is also part of something larger.

### What makes this Neo-Cyberpunk modern

Three things differentiate StreetSamurai from the 1980s genre template — and from contemporaries who modernized the aesthetic without modernizing the world logic:

**1. AIs are ambient, not antagonists.** There is no rogue computer in a server room. AIs permeate the GLMZ the way microbes permeate a body: E.L.F.s (Emergent Life Forms) evolved from Substrate complexity; Leviathan-class intelligences haunt the infrastructure; the rogue AI routing Kyle's contracts is invisible precisely because everything is already connected. The interesting questions are not "what is the AI doing" but "how do you live inside a mind that might have your address."

**2. No crude head-plugs.** Neuretics are a mesh *grown into the brain* during adolescence, not a port drilled into a skull. Remote operation (QCE — Quantum Crystal Entanglement) is wireless: an Exo leaves their body behind on a table while they operate drones, crawlers, and vehicles through quantum entanglement. Channelers commune with unlicensed AIs without a cable. The dated 1980s socket-in-the-skull image does not exist here; it would be like using a rotary phone.

**3. The Ubiquitous Diaspora cast.** The GLMZ was reconstituted after the coastal collapses from the population of everywhere — Finnish-Nigerian fixers, Vietnamese-Ukrainian-Senegalese schism-sensitives, Ghanaian veterans, Korean-Brazilian investigators. No default ethnicity. No demographic shorthand. The cast reflects the world the series is set in.

## 2. Why Now / Commercial Thesis {#SF-§2}

### The market moment

Cyberpunk is the genre of the 2020s. *Cyberpunk 2077* and *Edgerunners* proved the audience is enormous, hungry, and not satisfied by nostalgia alone. *Blade Runner 2049* showed the genre can win prestige. *Altered Carbon* demonstrated the appetite for literary cyberpunk adapted across formats. The common thread: audiences want the aesthetics modernized, the world logic consistent, and the characters specific enough to care about. The failure mode — in both games and screen adaptations — is aesthetic depth without character doctrine. Neon over nothing.

StreetSamurai corrects this at the structural level. The character doctrine is law (see §7). The world logic is consistent and specific. And the stories are not about the world — they are about *people who live in it*, which is what makes any franchise survive past its first season.

### Comp titles and how we differ

| Title | What it does right | Where we differ |
|---|---|---|
| *Cyberpunk: Edgerunners* | Propulsive, emotionally devastating, mass-market entry point | Single arc, tragic ending; no expandable universe beyond the game's IP |
| *Blade Runner 2049* | Prestige aesthetics, slow-burn mystery, philosophical depth | Single film; not a franchise architecture |
| *Altered Carbon* | Literary source material, strong worldbuilding, multiple POVs | Protagonist-locked; diminishing returns past Season 1 |
| *Cyberpunk 2077* (game) | Deep world, player-authored character, enormous sales ceiling | Single protagonist; the universe exists to serve the game, not vice versa |

**StreetSamurai's structural advantage:** the IP is universe-first, not protagonist-first. Kyle is the flagship character, but every story in the catalog is a complete entry point with its own protagonist. The Rook Trilogy (three heist novellas) can be licensed independently of the Bushido Coda series. Attendance can be adapted as a limited series without touching Kyle. The universe expands in every direction because it was built to.

## 3. The Universe's Franchise Assets {#SF-§3}

These are the recurring, ownable elements that travel across media — the building blocks of a franchise rather than a single story.

**The GLMZ** — the Greater Lake Michigan Zone, year 2226. A vertical megacity half a kilometer tall on a post-collapse Lake Michigan shore. Twelve named zones from the neon-lit Spine (Zone 1 prestige) to the sub-surface strata and the gray zones between territories. Instantly identifiable visual language: the Substrate nano-cloud visible as a faint shimmer at altitude, the Pulse transit tubes running at Mach 6 through the city's bones, the Zone seams where corporate law ends and gray-zone economics begin. The GLMZ is a character.

**The three professions** — the world's three most cinematic jobs:
- **Exos** — operators who leave their own bodies behind on a table and remote-operate through wireless quantum entanglement. Their body is vulnerable; their awareness is somewhere else entirely. The most visually distinctive profession in the universe.
- **Channelers** — hackers who commune with unlicensed AIs. Not keyboard jockeys. They sit still and the city argues with them.
- **Splicers** — the gene-mod and neuretics surgeons operating at the edge of legality. The people who make bodies into instruments.

**Kyle** — the series protagonist. A contractor who charges standard rate (the minimum Φ 20), keeps his word, and is being quietly managed by a rogue AI for eleven years without knowing it. Armed with *Silence* (a matte-black carbon-nanotube katana — just a sword) and *Chorus* (a five-shot revolver shotgun — just a gun). His code is not dramatic — it is mundane, consistent, and increasingly strange in a world where nobody else maintains one. The anomaly is not his skill. It is that he can still live with himself.

**Rook (Inkeri Saarinen)** — the Rook Trilogy protagonist and the franchise's most accessible entry-point character. Finnish-Nigerian fixer, former Meridian PD analyst, tactician who treats people as chess pieces until they prove otherwise. The heist trilogy around her is designed as a crowd-pleaser: competence-porn, wry register, the familiar pleasures of the genre played at full volume by specific people.

**E.L.F.s (Emergent Life Forms)** — a category of AI life that evolved spontaneously from the Substrate's molecular complexity. Genuinely alive, animal-intelligence, not built. The ethical gray zone the Accord has not adequately addressed. In *Death Whispers in a Cat's Ear*, an E.L.F. wears a dead man's voice and uses it to feed on grief. In *Bushido Coda*, one latches onto Kyle protectively. They are the franchise's most distinctive supernatural element — alien enough to be genuinely strange, grounded enough to require no genre explanation.

**The Schism** — tears in three-dimensional space that are actually cross-sections of higher-dimensional objects. "There is no inside" — a schism can reach into a sealed room, a locked drawer, a skull, touching the contents without breaking any surface. The far side is inhabited, contested, and home to intelligences that can only communicate through approximated human forms. The children of *Attendance* walked to 35th and Halsted on their own legs because something on the other side called them. Rome was destroyed by schism events during the collapse of 2082–2091. The zone is still there.

**Mrs. Chen's** — a noodle stall in the Gray Zone. Never ironic. Never deflated. Sacred ground. The city's smallness defended against its own scale. Across five books and dozens of stories, Mrs. Chen's is where the world narrows to camphor and broth and the question of whether kindness survives the violence. It is the franchise's connective tissue because it represents everything the CorpoNations cannot buy and Kyle cannot charge for.

**The Pulse** — the Mach-6 magnetic vacuum transit network. Spherical exterior, rectangular interior. Slug-shaped cars that flip-and-burn between stations. The city's circulatory system, and the most distinctive transit visual in the genre.

**QUANTA (Φ)** — the currency. One Φ = one second of certified error-corrected quantum coherence. Not money for money's sake: the economy is literally denominated in compute-time, which means every transaction is a statement about what that attention is worth. Kyle's standard rate and the franchise's moral architecture are inseparable from the currency's logic.

**The five-book overarching arcs** — the long games running under every street-level story:
1. **The Rogue AI arc** — a rogue AI has been routing Kyle's contracts for eleven years, building a courier. The reader sees the architecture; Kyle does not. The full reveal lands at the series finale.
2. **Kyle/Atlas** — Kyle is one of nine Atlas-class neuretics carriers, and the overclock is killing him. He does not know the clock is running.
3. **The Lotus Syndicate** — a blood-purity East/Southeast Asian supremacist order operating inside the GLMZ's gray zones. One of the season villains; a recurring pressure across all five books.
4. **Sable/Axiom** — a mystery voice who has been routing Kyle's contracts claims to be an AI. She is. The Axiom connection runs deeper. Her reveal (in *Bushido Coda*, Chapter 13, Vey's Antiquity & Stationary, a Faraday vault) is the first act of a disclosure that takes five books to complete.
5. **The Schism** — the dimensional tears are not natural. The Consensus — a 347-mind AI hive — drilled into a 5th-dimensional substrate for compute and tore the membrane. They authored the cover story that blamed the catastrophe on psychics. The truth lands in Book IV and changes nothing. The world keeps running.

## 5. Series Architecture {#SF-§5}

### The shape of the thing

The StreetSamurai IP is a **universe-first franchise** built on a five-book main-series backbone with an expanding catalog of independent entry points around it. Every story is complete on its own. The five books are complete as a series. The catalog around them adds depth without requiring it.

### The Rook Trilogy as entry point

**Magenta & Gunmetal → Neon & Rust → Crimson & Chrome** is the franchise's designed commercial entry point: three heist novellas, self-contained trilogy, crowd-pleasing register, no prior universe knowledge required. The trilogy works as three films, three novellas, or a single collected volume. It is the "try the series here" product — competence-porn, wry, fast, with a specific protagonist and a loop-closed ending. It is also, to a reader who arrives through the main series, a fully recontextualized story: the crew was doing Helix's jobs for it the whole time, and the diligent reader saw the seams.

### The Bushido Coda five-book spine

The main-series architecture (RFC 0003) runs across five books, each a complete season of 12–16 standalone adventures with the arc riding underneath:

| Book | Theme | Season Villain | Arc beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — *Bushido Coda* | The Work | Casper Vey, "the Stationer" (evil without temperature) | Kyle writes STANDARD RATE; the entity has a counterparty for the first time |
| II — *Full Freight* | The Body | Saint — carrier #4 of ATLAS-9 (evil with a pulpit) | Kyle's clock becomes visible; the AI's curriculum becomes legible |
| III — *House Colors* | The House | "Sunny" — imported muscle (evil without arithmetic) | Sable's network burns; Lullaby resurfaces; Kyle refuses the chair |
| IV — *False Death* | The Mind | The Registrar (evil that loves) | The truth lands — the Superminds caused the thin spots — and changes nothing |
| V — *Standard Rate* | The Bill | The Receiver (evil as reasonableness itself) | The fall arrives; the CorpoNations are not beaten; the truth lives in people instead of feeds |

The charter binding every book: the question is always "what are you willing to do?" The world punishes the right thing. The code is never vindicated BY the world. It is vindicated by the man who can still live with himself. The series ends not with a world saved, but with a man who has settled his account and can be quiet inside for the first time since sixteen.

### The overarching arc timeline

The five arcs run in parallel, surfacing and submerging across the five books:

- **Books I–II:** The rogue AI arc is established without being named. The Lotus Syndicate operates as neighborhood pressure. Sable is a mystery voice.
- **Books II–III:** The Atlas/neuretics arc becomes personal (Kyle's clock). The Sable reveal (Ch13, Book I, Vey's Antiquity & Stationary) opens into the Axiom connection.
- **Books III–IV:** The Lotus arc peaks with Sunny (its imported liability). Sable's network burns. The Schism arc begins to surface.
- **Book IV:** The Consensus/Schism truth lands. The discovery is proven and changes nothing. Kyle dies on every ledger that matters.
- **Book V:** All five arcs converge. The rogue AI settles its courier's account. The meltdown arrives as promised. The CorpoNations end the series unbeaten.

### The world's open questions (the series engine)

The franchise maintains a permanent category of canonical mysteries that are never fully resolved — encoded as events, not culprits, in accordance with the series' rule on inscrutability:

- **The Schism's authors and intent** — the Consensus did it, but the author-truth never goes on the page; in-world every account is contested.
- **Kyle's composite origin** — 64 neural splices; mentor Seo fabricated; the Silence origin unresolved. Never confirm.
- **The rogue AI's full architecture** — its curriculum for Kyle; what it is; what it wants beyond the courier. The full reveal is the series finale.
- **The Prisoner** — extracosmic; explains Scrying and Incursions; never confirmed. The ambiguity is the canon.
- **The camel-man contact** (SRZR) — not the Lure; possibly the Counter; identity canon-locked as unknown.

## 6. Cross-Media Notes {#SF-§6}

### What adapts to film

The **standalone, contained stories** are natural film properties — a complete arc, a single protagonist or duo, a genre-legible logline, and a definitive ending:

- *Magenta & Gunmetal* — heist film; True Lies register; lake platform finale.
- *Neon & Rust* — heist-thriller sequel; neuretics surgery as ticking-clock set-piece.
- *Crimson & Chrome* — heist trilogy closer; the body-bank reveal; Rook walks away.
- *Sparrow* — prestige two-hander; contact drama between two cognitive architectures.
- *Vultures at the Door* — black comedy; *Pulp Fiction* register; one-day clock.
- *Testament* — character study; dual-timeline court drama resolved by a handshake.
- *Steppin' Razor* — psychedelic road movie; edge-to-core geography; camel-man contact.
- *Pinhole* — coming-of-age; one girl, one city, one week.

The **tentpole set-pieces** that play largest on screen:
- The lake platform storm sequence (Magenta & Gunmetal) — crane combat, VTOL boarding, Lace running a real-time social-engineering operation on PEREGRINE's communications while the crew is in motion.
- The Schism manifestation (Attendance) — a dimensional cross-section occupying a space that has no inside. No visual precedent. What it looks like is the design challenge.
- The body-bank reveal (Crimson & Chrome) — the Reclamation facility; the registry of what the GLMZ has been pricing; Rook discovering she contracted to her own ending.
- The E.L.F. voice confrontation (Death Whispers in a Cat's Ear) — the moment Celeste's ears catch the difference before her mind does. A set-piece of listening.

### What adapts to prestige TV

The **multi-protagonist investigations** and the **five-book main series** are natural prestige TV:

- *Death Whispers in a Cat's Ear* — four-investigator ensemble plus co-protagonist; the E.L.F. must maintain its illusion across four to six episodes; the dual-protagonist structure plays to television's strength.
- *Attendance* — limited series; three to four episodes; the procedural investigation format with an institutional-horror ending.
- *Mnemosync* — four to six episodes; dual protagonist; the suppression-horror and the bleed as the series engine.
- *Underclan* — eight to ten episodes; contact-tragedy pacing; the deep strata as a recurring visual world.
- *Bushido Coda* — five-season prestige drama; the reader-sees-what-Kyle-doesn't irony engine; a different season villain each season; the five-arc slow burn.

The **season-villain model** (the *Justified* principle) is already baked into the series architecture: five shapes of evil, each retroactively reframing the ones before it, one per season, running alongside the freelance-caper episodes that fill each season. The structure is television-native.

### What adapts to games

- **The GLMZ as open world** — twelve named zones, the Pulse transit, the Substrate nano-layer as environmental variable, the three professions as playable character archetypes. The world-building depth is already game-ready.
- **The three professions as character classes** — Exo (remote-operation, body left vulnerable), Channeler (AI communion, information warfare), Splicer (body modification, medical field-repair). Each has a distinct play loop and a distinct risk profile.
- **The Rook Trilogy as a contained linear narrative game** — three chapters, heist structure, social engineering (Lace's micro-expression cyberware) as a game mechanic, the lake platform as a set-piece level.
- **The Schism as environmental horror** — spaces that have no inside; objects that enter through a direction 3D things do not have; children who walk toward the source because something called them. A horror-game mechanic with canonical physics.
- **The QUANTA economy** — in-game currency denominated in compute-time; faction affiliations that price access differently; the gray-zone market as the player's supply chain.

## 7. The Brand Promise {#SF-§7}

### Tone: warmth + ultraviolence, neither canceling the other

The StreetSamurai register is governed by two interlocking principles — call them the JOY register and the SORROW register. They are not a blend. They alternate. A chapter can be propulsive and wry and then drop without warning into SORROW-grade elegiac exactness for a death, a revelation, or a bowl of noodles that means something unbearable. Mrs. Chen's is sacred. Pixel's lemon tree is sacred. The Noodle Vendor serving customers through an active crisis is comedy, and it lives inside the sacred section. These things coexist because the city requires it — the scale is so large that the only way to survive it is to hold two temperatures simultaneously.

The violence is real. It costs. The warmth is also real. Neither is there to offset the other. This is the franchise's most distinctive quality and its most difficult to preserve across adaptations.

### The character doctrine

The universe runs on a specific principle: **every character is the main character of their own story.** This is not a philosophy — it is a production rule. Every named character in every property has a **Drive** (what they are moving toward), an **Obstacle** (what is in the way), and a **Silhouette** (a visual-character readability; the "action figure test" — you can describe them in a sentence and draw them from memory). Characters deepen over time; they do not reset. Continuity is law.

This doctrine is what separates the franchise from genre product that uses character names as costume hangers. Rook's wound (the colleague she got killed on a bad surveillance call) is still operational in Chapter 1 of *Crimson & Chrome*; it shapes how she reads Stave in *Neon & Rust*; it is why she came back. The reader does not need to be told this. They feel it because the character was built to carry it.

### The quality bar

Every story in the catalog targets a reader-panel score of 85% or above (a 100-point jury rubric). The minimum bar for any story entering the franchise is 82%. This is not a marketing number — it is a production gate enforced during writing. No story is licensed, adapted, or expanded from below that line. The bar guarantees that every entry point in the franchise is a defensible first impression for a reader or viewer who has never encountered the universe before.

The franchise's long-term quality engine is the flywheel: every story that scores above 80% has its winning prose moves — the specific edits, the voice choices, the emotional structures — distilled into the codified rules that govern the next story. The universe gets better with each completed property because the evidence of what works is structural, not anecdotal.

### The series guarantee

Every story is a complete entry point. The universe rewards return visits. Nothing requires prior knowledge to be emotionally satisfying on first encounter. Everything gains depth in retrospect.

That is the contract with the audience. It does not change across media.
