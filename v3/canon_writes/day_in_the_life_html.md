# Day in the Life

*Protagonist: Kyle Ellen Corbin*

## Part I: The Ride Back

The rain had moved past percussion into resignation by the time Kyle was riding north out of the Gray Zone. The Pulse line that ran the eastern strut threw its low-frequency hum up through the road surface — a sound below sound, the kind of vibration you stopped noticing after a year of riding the surface routes and never stopped feeling. The bike took it without complaint. The bike never complained. The bike was, Kyle had concluded several years ago, the single most reliable presence in his life, which was a sentence he had once tried to say out loud to himself and had abandoned halfway through because it had begun to sound like the kind of sentence that meant something he did not want to mean.

The towers leaned over him as he climbed out of the strut. From the bottom of the canyon you could not see the sky; the sky was a private resource at this depth, available only to the residents of the upper hundred floors and to the VTOL traffic that crossed between them. From the elevation Kyle was now at — sixteenth-floor surface road, the tier they had cut into the side of the structure when the original road grid drowned in 2174 and the city had decided to keep going — the sky was a slot. A vertical slot of slate-grey weather between two buildings he had not learned the names of, because the buildings at sixteen did not have names you needed to learn unless you worked in them, and Kyle did not work in any of them, and that was something he had also decided he was going to call a blessing.

He rode the long way. The short way would have taken him up the West Town freight ramp and dropped him at the Damen lift in twenty minutes. The long way ran him through three tier crossings he did not strictly need to make, through the Wicker boundary surveillance grid he made a point of crossing with different plates each time, through the kind of residential block where the windows above the alley had laundry strung between them and somebody was always cooking something that did not smell like ration paste. The long way was forty-eight minutes. Kyle had stopped counting trips on the long way. The long way was where he did the work the array did not do for him — the work of letting the evening settle into a place where it would not produce decisions until he had asked for the decisions.

Tonight the work was: he had collected three teeth from a man whose face was already wrong, delivered them to a daughter who had not been twelve years old in any of the ways that mattered, and watched a woman behind a noodle counter try to give him a thing she could not afford to give and could not afford not to give. The work was not the loading dock. The work was the conversion of all of that into something he could carry home without having to put down.

He did not put it down. He carried it. He rode.

---

The bike found the Damen lift at 22:01. He took the lift up to the eighteenth-floor surface (The Pivot's tier — somebody had hand-painted PIVOT in matte black on the lift's interior at some point in the building's earlier life and the CorpoNation maintenance crew had never quite gotten around to scrubbing it). The doors opened on rain that had been waiting for him at this elevation specifically, a heavier rain than the strut had been throwing, the kind that hit the lift's threshold in flat percussion before he had even rolled the bike out onto the deck.

He rolled the bike out anyway. He walked it the last forty meters to the alley behind The Pivot. Habit. The bike could ride that distance under its own power and Kyle was the only one who would know he had walked it instead, but the habit was the habit, and the habit was that you arrived home the way you wanted the room to know you were arriving. Quietly. With your weight off the engine. Without telling anyone above the third floor that anyone had come back.

The Pivot was a converted insurance archive that had stopped being an insurance archive in 2189. The building had a flat brick face and a service alley that ran along its east side and a fire-escape lattice that was older than half the operating buildings in West Town and structurally sounder than most of them. The window on the second floor at the back of the alley belonged to apartment 2E. The light was on. The kind of soft yellow light that came from the workbench lamp Pixel kept aimed at her current project. Music inside, very faint through the building's old skin, something with strings.

He kicked the stand down. He locked the wheel hub. He covered the plate. He walked the bike's stillness into the back service door, into the freight elevator, up to the second floor, and the freight elevator's gate had not stopped clattering when Pixel's door opened.

She had heard the bike pull into the alley. Of course she had. She had a window that looked at exactly the right slice of the building's approach geometry, and she had spent four years tuning her ear to the specific frequency of Kyle's motorcycle, and the bike's idle was as recognizable to her at this point as her own door's hinge. He had stopped trying to sneak in roughly the third month.

"Get in here."

She had said it before she saw the back of his shirt. She knew. She always knew. The bike sound was diagnostic the way the step on the third stair was diagnostic — Kyle's bike sounded a particular way when he had not been shot and another particular way when he had, and Pixel could tell the difference at a distance she would never publicly admit to.

He went in.

## Part II: The Kitchen

Apartment 2E was three rooms and a fire escape: a main room with the workbench under the window, a galley kitchen, a small bedroom, and a bathroom that had been retrofitted by someone who had not entirely thought through the plumbing run. The main room was where Pixel worked. The kitchen was where Pixel fed people, including Kyle, on the rare occasions she could trick him into being fed. Tonight the kitchen was where she did her field-medic work, because the kitchen table was the only flat surface in the apartment large enough to lay a man across, and the kitchen had the best light.

She pointed at the chair. He sat. She put on gloves. She turned the music down, not off — Pixel did not work in silence, Pixel worked with the music doing the thinking she did not need to do consciously — and she came around behind him.

"Coat off, shirt up."

He took the coat off and laid it across the next chair. The coat was wet. The kitchen floor did not care. He took the shirt off entirely because he had learned, over four years of these visits, that *shirt up* and *shirt off* produced the same result when Pixel was the one doing the work and the shirt off was thirty seconds less negotiation.

She let out one long breath through her nose. Not commentary. Inventory. "Three?"

"Two for sure. The third might be a graze, might be in there."

"Lie down."

He lay down on the table. The table was warm because Pixel had been working at it before he arrived; her current project was scattered along its far edge, a half-disassembled neural reader pulled apart in the specific organized disorder of someone who intended to return to it. The wood was old. Pre-2200 oak, oiled to a depth that nothing the building's atmosphere did to it could ruin. Kyle had bled on this table before. The table had not held the stains. The wood was, like a lot of things in Pixel's life, somehow not what it should have been by any reasonable accounting and yet better for not being.

She got out the forceps.

The forceps were the long pair. She had four pairs of forceps; the long pair was the pair she used for Kyle specifically, because Kyle's musculature ran lean enough that the entry track on most rounds was deeper than a normal-bodied retrieval, and the long forceps reached. The short pair was for the regular clientele Kyle did not ask about. Kyle had stopped asking about Pixel's regular clientele around the same time he had stopped asking why she lived across the hall from him, which was around the same time he had decided that whatever the answer was, it was the kind of answer that did not survive being asked for.

She started talking. This was always the way. Pixel did not work in silence on a person; she narrated, low, the running commentary doing the work of telling him she was there and the work was happening and the next ten minutes had a person at both ends of it.

"Upstairs bird's been making sounds again. Three this morning. Same minor key, three different intervals, I think it's learning the building's HVAC."

"Yeah."

"There's a new noodle stand opened on Damen. Five blocks west of Chen's. I went past it on Saturday. They have a poster up, full CorpoNation logo, and they are calling themselves *Damen Authentic* in three languages, and I do not need to tell you that an authentic anything that has a CorpoNation logo printed on the poster is not authentic in any of the three languages."

"No."

"I think they are trying to do to Chen what they did to the old man on Ashland."

"They will fail."

"I think they will, but they will hurt her some first. You should know."

"I do now."

He felt the first fragment come out. She had it. She held it up where he would not see it directly but where his peripheral vision could register that something had been removed, because she had learned years ago that he hated the moment more if he did not have a visual confirmation that the procedure had progressed. The fragment hit the small metal dish at the edge of the table with a sound he had heard so many times now that the sound was not a sound. It was vocabulary.

"There's a song I like," she said, sliding the forceps in for the second one. "I'll play it for you. After. Not during. During is yours."

"Mm."

He let the array route the pain. Not off — the facility had been clear that fully partitioning pain was a long-term injury — but down, attenuated, the channel volume dropped to a setting where it was information rather than emergency. The trick was old. The trick was the trick the facility had built him to do. He used it the way other men used coffee or whiskey or prayer, with the same automatic relationship to the underlying need.

She got the second one.

"Damen's bird theory," she said, transitioning without seam to a topic she had been waiting to get to all evening, "is that the building's HVAC plays it specific tones at specific times of day depending on which apartments are running their kettles. The bird memorizes the tones. The bird then sings the tones back when the tones are not playing, which is, if you really think about it, the bird trying to negotiate with the building. The bird is trying to bring the kettle back."

"That is a theory."

"I think the bird is smarter than the building. I think the building's HVAC is not actually responding to the kettles, but the bird thinks it is, and the bird is doing field experiments. There are worse epistemological projects." She moved the lamp. "Hold still."

He held still. She found the third fragment. The third was the graze — half-buried, the depth ambiguous until she had it under the light. She took it out clean. The fragment hit the dish. Three.

"Done."

She set the forceps down. She removed the gloves. She put a strip of biotape over each entry, slowly, with the specific patience she put on things that mattered — the project on her bench, the bird's epistemology, Kyle's back — and then she stepped back and washed her hands at the sink for longer than the procedure had required.

He sat up.

She was at the counter when he turned around. She had a kettle going. She had been heating it the entire time, he realized, with the specific timing she put on things she did not announce, so that it would be ready exactly when the procedure ended. She poured two cups. She handed him one. The handle was on the side without the chip.

"Drink it slow."

He drank it slow.

## Part III: Across the Hall

He left her apartment at 23:47. He did not feel the time pass. The kitchen had been warm and the tea had been hot and Pixel had played the song she had mentioned — a thing he did not recognize, sung in a language he did not speak, the kind of song that did not need to be in a language you spoke to do what it was doing — and she had not said anything for the last six minutes of the song because the song had been doing what it needed to do without help.

He crossed the hall to 2F. He unlocked the door. He set Silence on the rack inside the entryway and Chorus next to it, the holster open, the cylinder still loaded with the one slug he had not fired at the dock. The bird's-head grip caught the light from his apartment's one functional ceiling fixture and threw a small red glint off the crosshatch texture, the only color in the room besides the matte black of everything else Kyle had brought home in his life.

He did not turn the light off. He did not undress. He went into the bedroom and lay down on the bed in the clothes he had been wearing since the apartment with the camphor smell, and he reached for Silence on the rack across the room without standing up, because the rack was where Silence lived but the bed was where he lived, and he had learned years ago that the gap between the two was the kind of gap he did not want to leave open on nights where the work had been the kind of work this night's work had been.

He brought Silence to his chest. He held the saya across his sternum the way he had held it in the trench in A Borrowed Hand and the way he would hold it through the next decade of nights when the work had been too heavy to put down clean. The weight was the weight. The discipline was the discipline. The blade did not glow, did not hum, did not register him in any way the blade was capable of registering him. The blade was steel. The steel was cold against his shirt. The cold against his shirt was the answer to the camphor smell and the daughter's hand closing around the teeth and Mrs. Chen's offer of forever, and the answer was not a refutation, it was an accompaniment. It said: *you are not alone with this. The blade is here. The blade has been here.*

He slept.

## Part IV: The Morning

The rain was still falling at 06:34 when his eyes opened. The light in the bedroom was the colour of bad weather coming through a window that had not been cleaned since the building was still an insurance archive. The world had not paused for him to recover. The world had taken the night off in the sense that the night had ended; it had not taken the night off in the sense that anyone had decided to be merciful. Kyle had stopped expecting mercy from the world somewhere in his early twenties. It was not a grievance. It was just a calibration.

He sat up. He felt the three biotape strips on his back move with his shoulder blades. He felt the cut on his left forearm where the boss's follow-through had grazed him — a thin line, scabbed clean, no infection signal from the subdermal mesh. He felt the array come up from its overnight low-power state and find him in the room and start cataloguing things that did not need to be catalogued. He let it.

He stood. He put Silence back on the rack. He put a kettle on. He looked out the bedroom window at the alley below, which was full of the same rain it had been full of when he had come up the night before, the alley itself unchanged in any of the ways that mattered. The bike was where he had left it. The bird from upstairs was, as Pixel had reported, singing — a low minor-key thing Kyle had now heard with his own ears for the first time, three different intervals, three different tries at coaxing something out of the building's HVAC. The bird's epistemological project was ongoing. Kyle could respect that.

The kettle clicked. He poured. He held the cup in both hands and stood in his kitchen and did the thing he did most mornings, which was sit with the awareness that he was alive and uninjured beyond the catalogued, and that the day in front of him was going to be its own day, and that the work he did would not save the world and was not intended to and that the work was, anyway, his to do.

He drank the tea.

The phone in his coat — left on the chair across the kitchen — chimed once. He set the cup down. He walked over and read the message. Sable's pipeline. A new contract had landed. The terms read clean. The pay read fair. The job, on the surface, was the kind of job Kyle did, which was the kind of job the pipeline had been delivering for two months without any of the contracts giving him a reason to question the pipeline.

The bird upstairs sang again. The HVAC did not answer. The bird tried a different interval.

Kyle filed the contract for later. He finished the tea. He went to put on a clean shirt.

The day was going to be its own day. He had no idea, walking across his apartment to the closet, what the day was going to be. He did not know that he would be at Mrs. Chen's by 19:30 that evening for the second bowl of his usual two. He did not know that a hooded woman would be at the counter three stools down, watching him pretend not to watch her, until she tilted her head *come*. He did not know that the contract on his phone had been routed to him for reasons the contract's own metadata could not legibly describe, by a pipeline he trusted because the pipeline had not yet given him a reason not to. He did not know that *not yet* was about to become *eleven hours ago*, in language he did not have the vocabulary for, in a shielded room he had not yet agreed to walk into.

He did not know any of that. He buttoned the shirt. He put the cup in the sink. He locked Silence and Chorus into their respective sheaths and harnesses. He shrugged the coat on, the one that had been wet last night and was now dry from sitting against the radiator, and walked out of his apartment into the day the day had become.

Pixel's door was closed. The hallway smelled the way hallways smelled. The bird upstairs was still working on the kettle.

He took the long way.
