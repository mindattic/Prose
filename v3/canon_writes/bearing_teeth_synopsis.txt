A working family pays Kyle in damp credits at half-rate to break the man who broke them — specifically the badly-done jaw man whose face was rebuilt cheap and tight on the left, the one who actually broke the husband's jaw. Kyle takes the loading dock at the bottom of the Gray Zone's eastern strut: walks in through the front door because he wants the moment when six men who have spent weeks feeling like weather look up and see something worse than weather standing in their doorway. Four ruthless kneecap shots from Chorus in three seconds — buckshot, slug, slug, slug — and four men go down. The fifth, a chrome-arm operator with hydraulics and reach, swings as the count is still resolving and meets Silence at the shoulder seam in a clean down-angle cut that opens the motor bus the only way Silence opens anything: by being a sword that was put in the right place at the right time. He sits and does not get up. The boss, who carries his own katana and has the training to use it, stands up unhurriedly because watching his crew dismantle has only sharpened his interest. Four exchanges. The boss tests the line, then deflects rather than parries, then commits, then thrusts — and on the thrust Kyle slides Silence's edge along the boss's blade from tip to tsuba and lets the cut finish at the wrist. No glow, no charge, no special effect. Just a sword that was sharper than the boss's training was current. Kyle collects three teeth from the badly-done jaw man — two loosened by the fall, one requiring grip and twist — and delivers them to the daughter at the ventilation unit. He cleans the blade. The tremor starts in his right hand four minutes after the work ends; he has learned to use the window. He walks northeast to Mrs. Chen's noodle stall and refuses her offer of free food forever, insisting on standard rate every time — the ritual that defines their bond for the next several years. The bike is parked two blocks south. He will get to it eventually.
