# The Interview

*Protagonist: Kyle Ellen Corbin-Vister*

## Part I: The Geometry

The NeoCortex cataloged it at 02:14:33 — six positions, half a second, clean geometry. One elevated on the fire escape above the panel van, the rifle's barrel finding the angle through the grating with a calmness that read as professional rather than confident. One behind the van itself, the electromagnetic signature of augmented limbs bleeding through the chassis like a second heartbeat. One behind the concrete planter at the eastern corner, the dead tree throwing no shadow worth using, the operative's breathing controlled in the way that takes years to learn and costs more than years to maintain. One at the parking structure mouth — augmented legs, dense chest plating, the kind of layered armor that says someone spent real money to make this person hard to stop. One flanker behind him, unaugmented, which meant either they were confident or they were bait. And one non-organic quadruped near the dumpster, pit-bull-sized, jaw emitter cycling at low draw, no BCI signal at all.

The bike was three blocks west, parked behind the pharmacy on Ohio, plate covered. He had ridden it down from West Town and walked the last six blocks on foot because the cross-street he was about to engage in was a corridor and bikes did not do well in corridors. He had assumed he might end this evening on foot. He had assumed correctly.

He gave them ten more steps. The wet asphalt held the streetlight in long orange ribbons and he watched his own reflection walk through them — the katana a dark vertical line against his coat, Chorus riding his left hip. The towers above the cross-street leaned inward at this elevation; the canyon between them framed a strip of sky the rain was falling through, and the strip was, by his rough estimate, just wide enough for a single VTOL to thread through if a VTOL had any business being this far down, which they didn't. The space above the alley was dark. The light came up from the windows, not down from the sky, and the windows here were mostly out. The cross-street was a place CorpoNations had agreed not to maintain at the same time they had agreed not to surveil, which made it the kind of place an ambush worked. He had walked the cross-street tonight because his route home asked him to, and because he had not yet been the kind of man who refused a route his discipline had approved.

The broth Mrs. Chen pressed into his hands three hours ago, protein-heavy and too hot, the way she always made it too hot so he would have to sit down and wait instead of taking it and walking. He had sat. He had eaten half. He had meant to finish it. He had stood up because the contract on his phone had called in its time window, and the contract had wanted him eight blocks west, and Kyle had paid the chip and walked out into the rain because the discipline was the discipline. He had thought, briefly, on the way to the contract, that the broth he was leaving behind was a thing the discipline would not have approved of. The discipline had not been consulted. The night had moved on.

The flanker behind him was breathing through his nose, which meant he wasn't scared, which meant he hadn't done this many times or had done it so many times it no longer registered as the kind of event that required fear. Kyle filed that. He filed the spacing — the six positions triangulated into a containment pattern he had seen before, or almost seen, something at the edge of the catalog that had been sitting unresolved for two months since a Bucktown engagement where the opposing crew's restraint pattern had matched something he couldn't name. They had let him leave that night. He had not resolved why. The NeoCortex had flagged it and he had filed it under *unresolved* and left it there because filing it under *threat* would have required action and he had not been ready to take action and that was the truth of it, the specific cowardice that lived in the gap between his code and his capacity, and he had been carrying it quietly, as a fact he hadn't yet decided what to do about.

They committed on the eighth step.

The quadruped cleared the dumpster first — fast and low, the jaw emitter cycling up from idle to active draw in the three-quarters of a second it took to cross the gap, and the sound it made was a cacophony of grinding noise. The fire escape operative dropped into position. The panel van operative stepped into the street with the unhurried confidence of someone who had been told the geometry was already settled. The planter operative stood. The armor at the parking structure mouth did not move at all, which was worse than if it had moved, because stillness at that position meant it was the door — not the attack but the seal — and Kyle understood in the 0.3-second window the NeoCortex gave him before Ballistic Precognition shifted from passive to active that this had never been an ambush in the way he understood ambushes.

*They need me breathing.*

The bio-battery read seventy-two percent. The rain was cold against the back of his neck. Five chambers in Chorus — four buckshot moon-clip, one slug seated in the top chamber because he had loaded it that way on the off chance he encountered the kind of door tonight a slug would solve. Six positions in front of him. One question he had been refusing to ask for two months. And the quadruped's jaw emitter fully cycled now, and the flanker behind him had stopped breathing through his nose.

## Part II: Now

"Now."

Mira steps from behind the planter and Kyle is already moving — not away, *through*, the draw completing itself in the space between her word and the rain hitting his right shoulder as the blade came over it. Silence cleared the saya in one continuous motion, no flourish, no signal, the kawagane catching the orange streetlight only at the edge where the edge always caught light. He catalogs her in the half-second he has: BCI at the left temple, close-cropped hair, machine pistol indexed high — Lotus south-arm, captain-grade, the kind of woman who runs containment because she trusts the geometry. He does not answer her. The blade answers for him.

KT is faster than the arm. The chrome Tier 2 prosthetic — Lotus-subsidized, the kind of debt that walks into every room before the person does — comes out elbow-to-fingertip in a full extension that telegraphs the angle, and Kyle reads the machete in her unaugmented right before she commits to the swing. He drops his shoulder and the blade passes the air where his neck was and he turns the geometry into his, Silence coming across low and redirecting the prosthetic's mass rather than meeting it, the edge scoring chrome instead of flesh because he needs her functional for the next four seconds and because the arm isn't hers, not really, and that matters to some part of him that isn't the hardware. She staggers. He steps past her toward the van.

Dex closes from behind — unaugmented, both handguns drawn, the flanker whose breathing he had cataloged as *controlled* rather than *afraid*. The NeoCortex tracks him at the edge of the predictive window, seven degrees outside the primary engagement cone, which means Dex is the variable the formation is counting on. Rook hasn't moved from the structure mouth. Nines on the fire escape has the angle but hasn't fired, which means the order is still *alive*, which means Kyle has exactly as long as it takes someone to decide the geometry is no longer worth the constraint. The quadruped rounds the van at low profile, jaw emitter at full draw now, the pre-sound filling the wet air with something between pressure and intent.

He keeps moving.

## Part III: The Seam

KT's arm comes horizontal and he doesn't step back. He steps *into* it — not to meet it, to find the angle the arm cannot intercept — and Silence comes up not to her chrome but to the shoulder seam where the chrome socket met flesh, the same seam Kyle had found on a chrome-arm man at a Gray Zone loading dock several years ago, the same seam every shop-grade chrome arm presented in a slot of unprotected actuator housing roughly a thumb-width wide. The edge passed through the gap the way the edge passed through anything when the angle was right and the line was committed. Bone, tendon, the cable bundle that carried the motor bus from the spinal interface — Silence did not care about any of it because Silence was a sword and what swords cared about was the geometry of where the edge met the body, and the geometry was correct.

KT's chrome arm did not fall off. The arm did not need to fall off; the arm needed to stop receiving signals. The motor bus was severed. The arm hung dead from the shoulder, hydraulics holding it in approximate position but no longer responding to anything KT was telling it to do. She made a sound that was less than screaming and more than nothing, the involuntary exhalation of a body that had just lost a limb it was counting on, the specific noise of *circuit breaker tripping* that Kyle had heard before and would hear again because shop-grade chrome was always going to surrender the seam to anyone who knew where to look. The Lotus subsidy on that arm represented, by Kyle's rough estimate, between three and five years of monthly payments KT had been making since the install. The seam Kyle had just found had taken less than a second.

Laugh or you'd cry. He did not laugh. He stepped past her.

Mira's burst from the planter is controlled — two rounds, tight grouping, the machine pistol indexed exactly where he was standing 0.4 seconds ago. He reads the muzzle before the report and steps left, hears the rounds clip the car door behind him, the metallic percussion of rounds punching sheet metal. He catalogs the angle. He catalogs that she fired at all, which means the geometry shifted faster than she expected, which means the formation's tolerance for *alive but damaged* just compressed. He also catalogs that her call to Nines arrives late. He hears it — two words, clipped, the cadence of someone issuing an order that should already have been issued — and the rifle on the fire escape does not fire when the angle opens. The angle opens clearly. Nines has the shot. Nines does not take it. The NeoCortex flags the lateness and he files it: *Bucktown*, two months, a crew that held back when the geometry said engage. The same restraint. The same specific hesitation that lives between *capability* and *permission*. He does not resolve it. He files it again, under the same heading, and keeps moving toward the van while the quadruped's jaw emitter fills the wet air with pressure and Chorus rides his hip like a question he already knows the answer to.

## Part IV: The Dog

Rook comes out of the structure entrance the way a man comes who has done this in rooms where there was no room — two-hand grip, heavy pistol indexed at center mass, moving left to close the angle. Kyle reads the geometry. Inside three meters the katana's arc needs space to generate and Rook knows it, which means Rook has been briefed, which means someone studied him before tonight. He gives Rook the approach. Keeps the blade low. Lets the man believe the geometry is his.

The quadruped rounds the sedan's nose fast and low, articulated legs finding wet asphalt in the efficient way that has nothing to do with traction and everything to do with mechanical patience, the jaw emitter fully drawn, the pre-sound arriving ahead of it like a promise. Kyle hears the leg-cadence shift — weight transferring forward, the rear actuators loading for the lunge — 0.2 seconds before the lunge happens. He goes up. Left boot on the sedan's hood, right boot finding the windshield frame, and the jaw emitter discharges into the air under his boot sole with a sound like a circuit board clearing itself, the inductive pulse arcing through empty space because there was nothing under it to deliver to.

*Threshold. Not past it.*

He catalogs it in the half-second he has in the air. A real combat drone would have throttled that emitter to stack — would have hit the nerve bundles in his calf and let the cascade do the rest. This one passed at exactly the level that registers as a near-miss and continued cycling. The Bucktown crew held back the same way. Two months of *unresolved* and the catalog closes the file now, quietly, without ceremony: *they need the product undamaged*. He does not act on it. He drops Chorus into his left hand — bird's-head grip, the recurve handle seating itself the way a handle only seats when it was fitted to the hand that holds it — thumbs the hammer back on the slug chamber that had been waiting in the top position since he loaded it that afternoon, and puts the first round through the rear actuator housing as he comes off the hood. The legs collapse. The dog drags itself forward on its front pair, jaw still cycling, the threat halved and still present, and he steps past it because *halved* is enough.

Rook fires at three meters. Kyle reads the shoulders before the trigger breaks — the left shoulder pulling back fractionally, the way a man pulls back when he is trying to account for movement he expects — and steps the angle, the round passing through the space between his right arm and his ribs with a percussion he feels in his teeth but not in his side. The NeoCortex registers *wound: none*. The four buckshot chambers in Chorus's cylinder are still loaded, four shells live, the slug chamber empty, the hammer indexing the next chamber on the next pull. Rook is already adjusting his grip for the second shot and Kyle is already inside the correction, and Nines on the fire escape still has the angle, and still has not fired.

## Part V: KT

KT comes around the planter at a sprint and she is screaming something that used to be words. The dead arm swings useless at her left side, the chrome catching the orange streetlight in a way that makes it look decorative, a trophy she is carrying rather than a limb. The machete is overhead, both-handed grip because she has one hand that works, and Kyle reads the arc before she commits to it — the shoulder angle, the lean, the way a person runs when they have already decided the outcome and are simply delivering the body to the decision. He steps inside. Not back, not sideways, *inside*, into the space the arc cannot reach, and Silence moves the way it moves when he is not thinking about it, which is the way it moves best. The edge finds her throat at the closest point. The cut is clean.

*The kind of clean the people who built him would have liked.*

He dislikes the thought the moment it lands. He files it where he files those thoughts — not deep, not hidden, just set aside in the way you set aside something you will not examine tonight. The blade carries through the full motion and rings off the concrete planter on the follow-through, a hard clean note in the wet air, steel on stone, the kind of sound an old katana makes when it meets an old material at the right angle. KT's machete hits the asphalt. The sound it makes is smaller than it should be.

Dex, from somewhere behind the van: *"He cut KT's head off, man. He just—"*

"Shut up."

Mira's voice. Kyle catalogs the tightness in it. Not the tightness of a captain whose geometry is failing — he has heard that, knows the cadence of tactical recalculation, the compression that happens when a plan starts costing more than projected. This is something else. Something that has a name he does not have access to from this angle, at this distance, with this much blood on the wet asphalt between them. KT had been on her cell fourteen months — he does not know this, cannot know this, but the Lotus-subsidized arm is a fact in the world and facts have histories, and the tight voice is the sound of someone who recommended that arm, who designed the audition, who called *Now* knowing what the word would cost. He files the tightness. He does not know what it means. He will not know what it means until he knows more about what she wanted from tonight, and he does not know that yet, and Rook is still adjusting for the second shot.

## Part VI: The Wrist

Rook adjusts and Kyle does not let him finish the adjustment.

The bandoleer pouch opens on the first thumb-snap — the leather worn to the exact softness that means it opens without thought, which is why Seo built it that way. The fresh moon clip is heavier than the half-spent one in the cylinder; he reads the weight with his thumb as he slides it free of the pouch. Five rounds, all slug, fragmentation tips. He thumbs the cylinder release on Chorus. The cylinder swings out, the spent slug chamber and four live buckshot chambers exposed in the rain. He turns Chorus barrel-up. The half-spent moon clip drops into his off-hand by gravity, four buckshot still on the clip, ringing as it dropped because the rain was on the metal and the rain made everything sound clean. He drops the spent clip into the bandoleer's catch pocket — not the cleanest holster work the discipline had ever asked of him, but the discipline did not have a category for *replace your live ammunition mid-fight to put a fragmentation round through a man three stories up* and Kyle had improvised the category twenty years ago and had not improved it since. The fresh moon clip went into the cylinder. He snapped Chorus closed. He thumbed the hammer back. The cylinder seated. The action engaged. Six seconds. He had practiced six seconds, in dry weather, against a wall in 2F, on evenings when the discipline asked for something to do besides cleaning a blade.

He raises Chorus toward the fire escape. Nines is three stories up, in profile, the cybernetic wrist-brace catching the orange streetlight at the inner forearm where the steel shows through the sleeve — the kind of brace that means nerve damage, old injury, the arm rebuilt around the loss. Kyle puts the slug through the brace junction. The explosive fragmentation takes the wrist apart the way a sentence ends when the grammar collapses — not gradually, all at once — and the rifle comes off the grating and drops three stories to the alley below, and Nines goes down on the fire escape screaming in the way that means the arterial bleeder has already started, the kind of bleed that needs a tourniquet inside two minutes or it becomes a different kind of problem. Kyle does not climb. He does not call up. He does not send Mira, who has the closest angle and the most reason to move. He knows what he is leaving on that fire escape. He files it with the other things he files — the same drawer, the same quiet act of not examining — and thumbs the hammer back a second time on the next slug in the cylinder.

Dex breaks from behind the van and runs south. Kyle tracks him for 1.4 seconds, the NeoCortex painting the probability cone of the sprint, and then he lets the cone collapse. Dex is running in the way that means he will not stop running tonight, and the things Dex saw will travel faster than Dex can carry them, and that is already true and cannot be made less true by the expenditure of a round he is going to need. He keeps the muzzle low. The quadruped is dragging itself toward the sedan on its two functioning front legs, jaw emitter still cycling, and Nines is bleeding out on the fire escape three stories above the alley, and Mira has not moved from somewhere near the planter, and Rook is still in the geometry, and the night is not finished with him yet.

## Part VII: Rook

He drops off the sedan hood as the quadruped drags itself the last two meters — jaw still cycling, the front actuators grinding wet asphalt, the thing's persistence purely mechanical, no calculation in it, just the forward instruction running until it runs out. He lets it close. At one meter he puts the second Chorus round through the jaw emitter housing at the angle the NeoCortex gives him — not center-mass, not dramatic, the smallest aperture that ends the problem — and the stun system dies mid-cycle with a sound like a word that didn't finish. The momentum carries the chassis into his shin anyway. He steps over it, left hand dropping to asphalt as he catches his balance, palm flat against the wet ground for a fraction of a second, then rising again.

Rook fires from the structure entrance. Two rounds, tight group, the heavy pistol indexed at the lower bio-battery because someone told him where the hardware lives. Kyle reads them both late — the NeoCortex flags the latency, 0.07 seconds behind the predictive window, which means the bio-battery's passive draw is climbing and the processing has a ceiling tonight that it didn't have last month — and he moves through the gap in a way that is not evasion, exactly. More like the gap was already there and he located it. Rook adjusts. Kyle is already running.

The Augmentation Signature Read has had Rook mapped since the parking structure — augmented legs, Tier 3, interface points at both hips, BCI at the right temple, no cardiac. Kyle does not need the chrome to fail. The chrome will keep working until the man inside it stops working, and the man inside it has a femoral artery on the inside of each augmented leg where the prosthetic's intake bonded to the original tissue. Tier 3 chrome left the major vessels intact because Tier 3 chrome was advertised as *flesh-compatible*, which meant the CorpoNation that sold it had calculated, accurately, that intact vessels were a feature their customers would pay for. Kyle takes the last two steps inside the barrel and feels the slide press against his ribs as Rook fires, the round going into concrete below his foot, the muzzle blast burning through his shirt in a clean circle that he will find tomorrow and understand as the measurement of how close he cut it. He does not catalog the burn. He catalogs the inside of Rook's left thigh.

Silence comes in a precise arc — low, angled inward, the cut traveling across the interior of the thigh at the line the femoral followed below the chrome intake. The edge passed through the gap the way the edge always passed through gaps. Rook's left leg, which had been bearing his weight in firing stance, did not collapse from the cut — Tier 3 chrome did not need blood to function — but Rook himself did, because the cut had opened the femoral and Rook had between forty and ninety seconds depending on which textbook you trusted, and Kyle did not need ninety. Rook's eyes did not go blank. They went confused. His augmented legs were still trying to hold him; his body was no longer cooperating with the augmentation. He sat down on the concrete the way a building settles, not falling, just descending to the level gravity had been recommending all along. The pistol dropped from a hand that had become uncertain about what it was holding. He looked at the leg. He looked at Kyle. He breathed. The chrome was not the problem; the chrome had been advertised, accurately, as flesh-compatible, and the flesh was the part Kyle had asked for. Rook bled out at the curb in just over a minute. Kyle did not stay for it. He stepped past him. He turned from the structure entrance.

## Part VIII: Walking Toward Mira

The dog is down near the sedan, the jaw emitter dark, the front actuators still and folded in the particular way that means the power cell is drained rather than destroyed — it will cycle back if someone resets it, which is a problem for someone who is not Kyle, tonight, in this rain. Rook is at the curb. Nothing is behind his eyes anymore; the body is doing the body's quiet work of letting the rest of itself follow the blood out. Kyle looks at the place on the asphalt where KT's machete landed. The sound it made when it hit comes back to him with the precision the NeoCortex reserves for things it has cataloged as *significant without yet knowing why*. A machete is not a subtle weapon. Neither is the arm that swung it. Neither is the debt that bought the arm. He looks away from the machete and up at the fire escape.

Three stories. The sound Nines is making has changed register — lower now, less urgent, which is the direction sounds go when the body begins conserving. Two minutes was an estimate. The NeoCortex does not update it. The NeoCortex is running at a processing ceiling it did not have last month and he is aware of this the way you are aware of a tire running soft — not a crisis yet, a number changing in a direction that does not stop changing. He registers Nines. He does not climb. He files Nines in the same drawer where he has been filing things all night, and the drawer is heavier than it was when the night started, and he does not examine what that weight means, and he walks toward Mira.

She has not run. He gives her credit for that the way he gives credit for things that cost something — without warmth, without ceremony, as a fact that belongs in the catalog. The machine pistol is indexed low and slightly left, which is the indexing of someone who has decided not to use it and has not yet decided what to do with their hands. Her BCI is at the left temple. She is breathing through her nose. The orange streetlight is behind her and it catches the rain on her shoulders and the wet asphalt throws the light back up from below and she looks, for 0.3 seconds, like something the city built rather than someone who works for it. He does not catalog this. He lets it pass. The blade is in his right hand. The night is almost over. Nines is making a sound that is getting quieter. He walks toward her because she is the only thing left on this street that is still a question.

## Part IX: Bucktown

"Fourteen of us jumped you in the Bucktown stretch two months ago," Mira says. "You walked out. We lost eight people."

The NeoCortex doesn't flag the statement as new information. It files it as *closure* — the label that fits the folder that has been sitting open for two months, the specific cowardice of not asking why the Bucktown crew let him leave. He looks at her. He does not show the recognition. He has spent the entire walk over wondering whether she would be the one to name it, and now that she has named it the catalog is not particularly relieved. The naming was not the problem. The problem was the two months of not naming.

"This wasn't a hit." Her voice has something in it that is not the tightness from before. He does not have a word for it. "This was a test. I needed to see if the stories were true."

The NeoCortex flags the micro-expressions. Brow symmetry, lip compression, the particular stillness around the eyes that belongs to people who have stopped performing and started saying something. She is not lying. He hates that the hardware is the thing that knows this. He would prefer to know it himself, from the part of him that is not facility-built, but that part does not have the processing speed and tonight the processing speed is the only thing that has been earning its weight. "They're true," he says.

The machine pistol comes up. He reads the shot in the 0.2 seconds before she takes it — the shoulder angle, the wrist rotation, the specific indexing of someone firing into a target they have already decided to keep. He does not dodge. The round hits the subdermal mesh at his left shoulder and deforms against the lattice with an impact like a fist from someone who means it, and the force travels down through his arm and into the hand that holds Silence, and the steel transmits the shock the way steel transmits any shock — straight through, into the floor of his palm, into the small joints of his fingers, into the place where his discipline kept its accounts. He keeps walking. Two meters from her he stops. The blade has not moved from low carry. It is the same matte black blade it was at the start of the night and at the end of it, and the rain runs off it the way rain runs off everything else in this city.

"Drop the weapon," he says.

A half-second where nothing comes. Then her professional register finds its footing, and she drops it. The machine pistol clatters on the wet asphalt the way every other piece of hardware on this street has clattered tonight. The street, by Kyle's rough estimate, was now carrying somewhere north of forty thousand credits of dead and broken cyberware between the dog, KT, Rook, and the rifle on the fire escape. The cross-street had not been worth forty thousand credits of anything five minutes ago. The world was, in this small specific way, fucked at exactly the tempo Kyle had ridden in to it on.

## Part X: Lotus Wants You

"Lotus wants you on payroll." The machine pistol is on the asphalt between them. She does not look at it. "Full protection envelope. Percentage of every contracted route through our territory. A salary the Pivot would not feel." She says the Pivot the way someone says a word they looked up before they needed it. The NeoCortex catalogs the research. He does not show that he catalogs it. "You said no the first time. I'm asking again."

He does not raise the blade. He lets the rain do what it is going to do to his face. Above them Nines is making a sound that is almost not a sound anymore. The dog's actuators were still trying to cycle the broken jaw emitter at irregular intervals, the chassis ticking at the curb, the kind of mechanical noise that did not need anyone to listen to it but happened regardless.

"No," he says.

She nods. The nod arrives a half-beat after a captain who expected that answer would have produced it. He files the half-beat. She does not press. "Freelance roster, then." Her voice has leveled back into professional register and he gives her credit for the speed of the recovery and nothing else. "Lotus contracts routes through our territory that need a clean face on them. You get first refusal. Carrier rates. No Lotus crew on you in the meantime." She says *in the meantime* the way people say phrases that are doing more work than they appear to be doing. He files that too. The wet asphalt between them held the orange streetlight in long irregular pools and the rain came down between them at the same indifferent rate it had come down the entire engagement, and the arrangement she was describing lived in this — two people on a cross-street with a dead woman thirty meters behind them and a man above them spending the last of what the bleed had left him. The math was honest. The freelance roster was the smallest concession this night could end with: his independence formally intact, the audition crews kept off him, the fee structure named plainly, no Lotus brand on anything he did or touched or was. He had spent his life refusing to be anyone's man. The roster was not that. The roster was a door he could close from the inside. What the math did not say — what the math could not say because it did not have access to the full dataset — was that the Syndicate had run fourteen people into him two months ago and lost eight of them and did not stop. Did not stop. He filed the gap. He would not examine it tonight.

Laugh or you'd cry. Kyle laughed quietly, into the inside of his collar, where Mira could not see it. The audition had been an audition. The recruitment was the test result. The test result was: he had passed by killing two of the people who designed the test. Lotus would now compensate him for the two months they had been studying him by hiring him. The world was managed by people who built systems like this and convinced themselves the systems were the only way the work could get done, and Kyle had stopped being the kind of man who argued the point a long time ago. He had become, instead, the kind of man who closed the door from the inside.

"Fixer's name," he says.

"Sable." She says it without hesitation, which means it was ready, which means the name was always part of the offer. "She'll route the contracts."

He nods. The name lands in the place names land. He does not show that it lands. He has known Sable's voice for two months through a cracked terminal, has worked her pipeline cleanly enough that the work has felt routine, and now Lotus is naming her as the routing fixer for his roster spot. The pipeline that has been delivering his contracts is also the pipeline that has been delivering Lotus's. The pipeline has not been advertising this. Sable has not been advertising this. The pipeline has been doing two jobs at once, with one face on, and Kyle is now hearing the second face named for the first time. He files it. He will examine it later. He will examine it carefully. The drawer for tonight's filings is heavy and the bed in 2F is four blocks away and Mira is still standing in the rain waiting for him to walk away from her so the rest of this can be tomorrow's problem.

He looks at the dog, chassis dark near the sedan, front actuators folded in the particular stillness of drained power rather than destroyed mechanism. He looks at Rook, at the curb, with a leg that did not need to be augmented to be the leg that killed him. He looks at the place on the asphalt where KT's machete fell and made a sound he has still not finished cataloging, and the blade is in his right hand and Nines on the fire escape is quiet now in a way that has stopped being about the bleed. He turns. The wet cross-street opens ahead of him toward the Pivot, toward the cold broth he did not finish, toward whatever the morning was going to ask of him, and he walks.

## Part XI: West Erie South

West Erie south. The towers leaned into the cross-street the way they did at every elevation Kyle moved through GLMZ at — the verticality of the city pressing the rain into vertical columns wherever the air had room to move, and at street level the rain was a vertical column of cold percussion through the canyon between The Pivot's block and the residential lattice across from it. His left shoulder registered the bruise in slow pulses, the subdermal mesh having done what it was built to do, which was stop the round and hand the force to the tissue underneath, which was not built for anything in particular. He cataloged it without examining it. The rain had thinned to mist and the mist caught the streetlight in ways that were not interesting and he was grateful for that.

The bike was still where he had left it behind the pharmacy on Ohio. He walked past the alley on his way north, not stopping. He had walked into the cross-street tonight and he would walk out of it. The bike was for the morning. The morning was the morning. The discipline did not have a category for *ride home after killing two people who arranged a six-position ambush to recruit you* and Kyle had decided, somewhere in the last block, that the discipline was correct not to have one.

Mrs. Chen's stall is dark. The fold-down counter is latched. The small propane burner under the left unit is cold — he knows this from three feet away because he knows the smell of it running and this is not that smell. She opens at four. He has two hours on her. He walks past and does not slow down and does not look at the counter where the broth he did not finish is not waiting for him.
