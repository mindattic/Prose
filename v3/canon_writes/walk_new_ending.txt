She stopped at the door. She did not produce a key. She did not unlock anything. She turned, instead, with the same composed efficiency she had walked the route with, and for the first time since the noodle stall she let the hood fall back.

Kyle had not expected to know the face. He knew the face. The fixer he had worked with for two months through a cracked terminal had a voice that did not need a face attached to it — Sable was an arrangement of consonants and judgments and contract sourcing, not a person, and the not-personness had been the entire shape of the working relationship. The face cancelled the not-personness in a single second. He filed the cancellation without immediately knowing where to file it. *Sable.* The name landed in his interior catalogue with a weight he had not budgeted for.

She watched him take her in. She did not smile. She had not pulled the hood back for greeting; she had pulled it back to make sure he understood that she was choosing, in real time, to give him the data of what she looked like. The data was a transaction. The transaction was a deposit on something she was about to ask him to spend.

"Tomorrow," she said. "This address, this time, the room behind this door. Shielded. Bring nothing electronic you can leave behind. Your implant comes with you because your implant cannot be detached, but anything else you carry stays in your apartment. The conversation we are going to have cannot happen in a room that can hear it."

He read the wording. *Shielded* did most of the work. The kind of shielding she meant was the kind that cost money she did not casually spend; the kind that you arranged in advance for a single conversation you did not want recorded. The hum in his chest, the low electromagnetic signature that lived in the implant's background like a second heartbeat, was suddenly something he was aware of for the first time in years. He had not known it was there because there had been no reason to notice it. There was, now, a reason.

"Tomorrow," he said.

She held him a second longer. The rain came down between them at the same indifferent rate it had come down the entire walk. Then she nodded, pulled the hood back up, and stepped past him into the dark of the block without unlocking the building behind her. She had never been going inside tonight. The building had been the agreement point, not the meeting point. The walk had been the offer, and the offer had been accepted, and the building was where the offer would be consummated tomorrow if Kyle showed up, and Kyle was going to show up.

She was gone in three steps. The Gray Zone's bad rain swallowed the tan coat the way the Gray Zone swallowed most things at this hour.

Kyle stood at the door for a count of ten. The brass-gasketed lock he could see through the window beside the threshold was the tell he had assumed it would be. He did not test the door. He turned. He walked back the way they had come — east-northeast in reverse, through the recycler vent's hot industrial breath and past the third-block security drone on its standard sweep and past the awnings that had not stopped dripping in the eleven minutes the walk had taken in the other direction and the further eleven it would take him in this one. He did not run the route past the array. He let it sit. He walked it.

The bike was where he had left it, two blocks south of the noodle stall, the plate still the one he had ridden down on, the cover still beaded with rain. He started it. He rode the long way home through tier crossings he did not strictly need to make, because the long way home was where Kyle did most of his thinking, and tonight was a night he had something to think about.
