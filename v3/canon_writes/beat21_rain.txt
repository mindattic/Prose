The freight grate opens onto rain.

Not weather — redistribution. The atmospheric processors are running their usual math three kilometers east and the Gray Zone gets what the Core sheds, and tonight what the Core sheds is a cold November downpour that hits the flagstone around the grate in flat percussion and does not stop. Kyle pulls himself through on his right forearm and the cargo arm's weight swings him sideways on the lip and he catches himself with his knee and stands. The bio-battery reads nine percent. The cargo arm's status strip has been dark for six minutes — brownout, the trickle cell finally gone, the E.L.F. drawing on his own reserves to keep the terminal fingers closed around Silence, which is a cost he did not negotiate and cannot renegotiate at nine percent. He does not ask it to stop. He does not ask anything. He starts walking north.

The hands knock against his sternum with each step. Palms outward, dripping now with rainwater mixed with chum barrel residue, the twine doing its functional work, the knot holding. The NeoCortex timestamps his pace at ninety-four steps per minute, which is the pace the facility calibrated as sustainable-while-injured, the pace that does not read as flight and does not read as incapacity, the pace that gets you four blocks without anyone calculating what you are. He passes the first block. The rain comes down the same way it came down before the Depot, before the sluice, before the table, indifferent to the debt he just opened and the name still sitting in the room behind him. The second block. The cargo arm drags at the coupling. Silence is still in the borrowed grip, cold blue hamon almost invisible in the rain, the bank still empty because the bank has been empty since the trench and the bank is not the point. *The blade is the point.* He is still holding it. He keeps walking.

Two blocks from the kiosk his mouth opens.

*I will visit you again.* His own voice, his own registers, the cadence slightly long — reading a sentence it has not spoken before. He does not stop walking. Ninety-four steps per minute, exact, the pace stays exact because the pace is the only variable he controls right now and he is going to control it. He counts two steps. Four. Then he asks it flat, his own voice, the question he has been holding since the maintenance lead seated behind his ear eighteen hours ago by the count he is keeping: *What are you called.* The rain comes down. A pause — not hesitation, something more like courtesy, the beat of something that has learned to wait. Then his mouth answers itself: *Puppeteer.* And then the mouth is his again and he files the name in the same place he filed the salvage glyph and the EMT-spec band model and the specific sound of the cleaver going down twice, and he keeps walking, and the arithmetic resolves in the dry register the NeoCortex reserves for catalog work: the cargo arm is empty hardware. Has been since the junction box on the maintenance landing. The E.L.F. is resident in him — has been since the contact seated, will leave when Puppeteer chooses to leave, will return on Puppeteer's schedule. He has no leverage. He does not stop walking. The AutoDoc kiosk is at the corner of Ashland and Whiting and it is still where it was this morning and that is the only fact he needs right now.

The kiosk reads the necklace. He does not know how. He keyed in with his chin and the panel registered the biometric and then the panel registered something else — the hands at his sternum, the twine, the EMT-spec bands, the tissue window still open — and the AutoDoc opened without requiring him to explain. The surgical arms are the same surgical arms they always are: articulated, precise, indifferent in the specific way of machines that have no stake in what they are repairing. They take the cargo arm — the coupling releases at the shoulder and the arm goes to the instrument tray and does not move, the status strip dark, shell only — and they slide Silence out of the borrowed grip with the careful, automated reverence machines have for swords and lay it on the side tray and it is there, it is on the tray, it is four feet from his left hand which is about to exist again. The antibiotic pump is already running. The analgesic arrives from the kiosk's own dispensary in a dose large enough to mute the night and he feels it go in and the night begins to mute and as it mutes the NeoCortex feed produces a glyph in the not-factory pattern, the pattern Puppeteer has been signing in, three syllables in old Axiom signage, and Kyle reads it as the anesthesia takes him under: *we are not done.* The AutoDoc's status light goes green. The rain comes down outside the kiosk. Silence is on the side tray. The sentence sits somewhere he cannot easily delete, patient and old, the same frequency as something that has been in the ferrocrete longer than the facility's survey teams were willing to go.

He wakes at 0344 by the kiosk's analog clock — the digital readout at the upper bezel says the same number, the analog hand confirms it without commentary, the AutoDoc has its own reasons for keeping the redundancy. The hands are at the ends of his arms. They are pink in the bracelet pattern at the wrists where the cauterizer band held tissue against tissue while the surgical arms did the work, the seam clean, the perfusion finding its rhythm. He flexes the right hand. The fingers move. He flexes the left. Same. The grip is at sixty percent of baseline, which is what he expected and what the AutoDoc's discharge summary confirms in a single line of text he does not bother to read in full. Silence is on the side tray. The cargo arm is on the instrument tray, separate, shell-dark. He takes the blade. He leaves the cargo arm.

*Take what is yours. The borrowed limb is not yours. It was a tool. The tool is finished.*

The kiosk lets him out into the rain. Ashland and Whiting at 0347 is empty in the way that intersection gets at this hour — the recycler exhaust steaming off the corner stack, the streetlight on the west side flickering at the rate it has been flickering for two years, a single Vultures truck idling at the far curb, lights off, the driver behind the wheel reading something on a handset and not looking up as Kyle passes. Kyle does not look at the truck either. The Vultures and Kyle have an arrangement now. The arrangement is that they are aware of each other and neither of them has yet decided what to do about it. The arrangement may stay this way for a long time. He keeps walking.

The distance from Ashland-and-Whiting to The Pivot is one point two kilometers. Kyle walks it in twenty-three minutes, which is twice baseline. The shoulder catches at every step. The reattached fingers send small probing signals up the wrist that the NeoCortex catalogues and does not act on. The blade rides at his right hip in the friction sheath because the back-carry is not available tonight. The rain comes down, redistributed, deliberate, the way it always comes down. He does not count the blocks. He does not count the steps. He has done enough counting tonight.

*The body is going to shake in approximately seven minutes. Be inside before then.*

The Pivot's lobby door reads his biometric and admits him without requiring the second factor; the lock has not asked for the second factor in three years and Kyle has not pressed the building's manager on the slack because the slack works in his favor. The stairwell is the stairwell. He climbs. The third step from the top has the loose riser that has been loose since 2198 and produces the same small sound it always produces under his weight, and Kyle's weight on it produces it correctly, and that is apparently enough.

The door to 2E opens before he reaches the landing.

Pixel in pajamas, the same as last time — bare feet on the hall's cold composite, the music inside the unit cut the moment she heard the stair, her hair loose, the crease on her left cheek from the pillow she has just left, the oversized sweater traded for a long-sleeve thermal that covers the same arms with the same compulsive thoroughness. She takes one look at him — the blade at his hip instead of his back, the pink bracelet visible at his cuffs where the leather rides up, the way he is standing — and her hand goes to her mouth and stays there.

*"Thank god you're all right."* The words come out small and wet, pressed through her fingers. Then: *"Sable said she'd take her somewhere safe."*

Kyle stops at the top of the stairs. He does not know how Pixel knows about Sable taking the doctor. Then he does know — Sable would have routed a message, because Sable routes messages, because Pixel was in the middle of the operational loop in the kitchen scene at the half-hour mark and Sable does not leave loose ends with operators she has decided to trust. He files the implication. He has not decided yet what it means that Sable has decided to trust Pixel. He suspects it means more than he is prepared to process tonight.

Pixel takes one step into the hall.

Then another. She is approaching him the way you approach a fence-pinned animal — slow, with the angle adjusted, no quick movement — and Kyle understands, in the time it takes her to cover the two meters between them, that she is going to touch him. He has time to step back. He has time to put up his right hand in the small gesture that has worked for four years to indicate *not now*. He does not step back. He does not put up the hand. His arms are very heavy and the AutoDoc's analgesic has not fully cleared and the night has been long in the way nights become long when the arithmetic is not complicated only costly, and he is so tired he cannot remember what he is supposed to be doing instead of letting her come.

She takes his hands.

Both at once. Both wrists, in the pattern the AutoDoc left there — bracelet to bracelet, palm up, the way you take something fragile that has just been put back together. Her hands are the same hands they always are: precise, minor scarring, the faint outline of the diagnostic port at the left wrist base where she has been calibrating his hardware for the last three years. The temperature differential is the part Kyle is not prepared for. Her hands are warm. The pink seam at his wrists is warm too — perfusion still finding its rhythm — but in a different register, a register that does not yet feel like *his*. Her warmth and his warmth meet at the bracelet line, and the part of his brain that has spent four years filing Pixel under *neighbor / tool / quiet across the hall* loses the filing momentarily and does not produce a replacement.

*Pull away. Pull away. The convention is the convention.*

The convention is the convention. He almost does. The hand twitches in the direction of withdrawal — his right hand, the dominant one, the one that has been making this kind of decision for him since he was fifteen — and then the twitch resolves into stillness because he is too tired to complete it. He looks at her face. Her eyes are wet. Then they are not wet — they are leaking, openly, the kind of crying that is not performed for an audience because Pixel has never performed her crying for anyone, the kind of crying that happens when the body decides on its own that the moment requires it. The tears go down her cheeks and onto her jaw and from her jaw they fall.

The first one lands on the inside of his right wrist, on the pink seam.

Kyle has not been touched by another person without intent to harm or repair in approximately four months. He has not been touched in tenderness in approximately seven years. The hardware does not have a category for the temperature of a tear on a fresh surgical seam. It does not need one. The body knows. The body has always known. The body has been waiting, without his permission, to be given evidence that the touching was still possible.

A second tear lands. Then a third. They fall on both wrists now — she has not adjusted, she is just standing close enough that the geography works out — and each one sits warm on the pink for a second before the perfusion equalizes the temperature and the moisture wicks into the analgesic-thinned skin. Pixel's grip tightens on his wrists. Not hard. Carefully. The way you squeeze a thing you have been afraid of losing.

*"Oh my god,"* she says, and the words are pressed through the same wet thickness as before, *"I'm sorry — does that hurt?"*

The pink seam aches under her fingers in the specific way a fresh reattachment aches — the nerves still negotiating, the subdermal mesh still re-pairing, the deep tissue lodging a small steady complaint about the cleaver work that the AutoDoc cannot fully suppress with surface analgesic. It hurts. It hurts in the way that means it is going to keep hurting for several days and possibly weeks, on a curve Pixel knows from her own clinical reading better than Kyle knows from his own body. She knows the answer to the question she just asked. She is asking it anyway because the asking is part of what is happening here.

Kyle finds his voice. The mask is the mask — calm, level, the voice he has practiced level for a long time — and he gives her the answer the convention requires.

*"No,"* he says.

It does. The lie is a small one and they both know he is telling it and neither of them is going to make a thing of it. Pixel nods, once, and the nod is acceptance — she will accept the lie because she understands what the lie is doing, which is making it possible for him to stand here in the hall at 0410 in the morning with her hands on his wrists and her tears on his bracelets without the convention shattering past either of their abilities to repair it. The discipline is the discipline. The discipline is also tonight what is letting him be here.

*The convention between you is no longer purely tools-and-quiet. Note that. The catalogue has updated.*

She lets go. Not all at once — she releases his wrists in two stages, the right first, then the left, then her hands fall to her sides and stay there. She steps back. She does not wipe her face. She does not perform composure. She just stands in the hall with her tears on her own cheeks and on his cuffs and lets the moment exist.

The music in 2E does not come back on. He has no memory of when it cut.

Kyle nods. The nod is the only thing he can produce that the convention will accept. Then he walks past her — careful, slow, the shoulder catching — and crosses the hall to 2F. The lock reads his biometric. The door admits him. He turns on no light. He lies down. The blade across his chest does not pin him; it composes him. Across the hall, the music does not come back on.

The rain comes down on the GLMZ the way it always comes down, redistributed, deliberate, falling on the only people who cannot move out of its way. Kyle sleeps. The blade sleeps with him. The morning holds.
