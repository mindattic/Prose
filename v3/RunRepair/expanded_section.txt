begins to push.

The corrosion does not give immediately. He had expected it to — he had read the procedure off the NeoCortex's archive, he had felt the looseness in the rotator coupling before he committed his back to the work — but the actual mechanics of an eighteen-year seal are not the mechanics of an eight-year seal or a two-year seal, and the metal has had a long time to forget that it was ever supposed to come apart. He pushes against the shoulder plate with the meat of his left stump, the cauterizer band's residual heat conducting through the fabric of his sleeve into the metal, and the metal does not move. He breathes. He resets his feet against the trench wall. He pushes again, harder, the chrome bracket in his jaw flaring at the effort because the body is leveraging itself through every available joint and the molar bracket was not built for this kind of structural load. The metal moves a quarter-turn. Then another quarter-turn. Then it stops, and the seal grinds, and Kyle understands without looking that he is going to be at this for another twenty minutes minimum, and he commits to it the way he commits to the long minutes of cleaning Silence after a job — fully, without resentment, the time being the price of the result.

He had considered the rest of the automaton first. He owes the consideration to the dead unit and to himself: he is not in the habit of breaking things he could have used whole. He had crouched beside the torso shell and read what was left, and what was left was eighty-four percent dead and the remainder distributed across components that did not connect — a head sensor pod gone, the legs fused at the hips, the secondary manipulator arm with its cable bundle severed at the rotator and its actuator firmware dark, the chest cavity collapsed inward across the central power core in a way that meant any attempt to recover the core would require tools he did not have. The single live arm was the whole salvage. Removing it would kill the rest, but the rest was already killing itself; the trickle current at the shoulder LED was the unit's last functional circuit and it was draining into a cell that had perhaps eighteen hours of useful charge remaining if it was not asked to do work and perhaps two hours if it was. The arithmetic was clean. He took the arm because the arm was what was left. The rest was already the trench.

He pushes the rotator coupling another half-turn. The corrosion shears in three places at once and a long flake of rust the size of his palm separates from the housing and falls into the current, and the seal gives the rest of the way with the specific reluctance of metal that has accepted, finally, that the future does not include staying joined.

The arm comes free of the shoulder coupling with a sound like a wet bolt shearing — not clean, not catastrophic, just the specific protest of corroded metal that has decided to cooperate — and Kyle catches the weight with his left stump and his chest simultaneously because catching is the only option available and the arm is three meters of Axiom-era industrial hardware and approximately forty kilograms of it comes across his lap in the dark and the current runs cold over his boots and he sits with it and breathes.

It is heavier than he expected. He had budgeted for thirty kilograms based on the visible material; the actual weight is forty plus, which means there is a counterweight or an internal hydraulic reservoir or a tooling magazine he did not see, somewhere in the housing, adding mass he is now responsible for. The arm rests across his thighs with the careless authority of something that has chosen its position. The shoulder end — the end he has just separated — points away from him into the trench mud, the cable bundle hanging out of the rotator like a severed umbilical, and the terminal end with the manipulator hand drapes over his right knee, the four chrome fingers half-curled, the LED at the wrist still cycling slow amber. He looks at the LED. The LED looks at nothing. Trickle current. The unit's last working circuit, doing whatever it has been doing for eighteen years, which is — he understands now, with the arm in his lap and the weight settling into his thighs and the cold coming up his legs from the ferrocrete — *waiting*. The maintenance loop has not been a maintenance loop. The maintenance loop has been a *holding pattern*, the kind a circuit runs when it is preserving state for an event it has been told to expect. The unit was not abandoned. The unit was *parked*, and the firmware has been running an idle the way a person runs breathing — keeping the option of action alive without spending it.

He files that. He does not have a category for it yet. He files it where he files things that are going to matter later and will not let themselves be ignored.

*The bootstrap problem.*

He can see it in the NeoCortex's triage logic, the same way you can see a closed door from both sides simultaneously: the needle cluster at the arm's terminal harness is there, the bus coupling is there, the dorsal port row beneath his right-forearm skin sleeve is there — undocumented, unmentioned, the geometry of it too convenient for him to look at directly on most nights — and none of those three things can reach the others without a hand to manage the sequence, and the only hand in the trench is attached to a machine drawing trickle current off a degraded cell, and the machine does not know what a body is. He sits with this for sixty seconds. The NeoCortex offers nothing. The hum at 19Hz runs through the ferrocrete into his spine and he files it the way he has filed everything tonight and does not move. Then the hum changes register.

It does not get louder. It gets *specific* — a shift in the harmonic, something in the frequency resolving toward signal the way white noise resolves toward a voice when the voice decides to stop waiting. The status strip along the cargo arm's housing blooms: not the amber maintenance pulse, not factory-default, but a sequence that does not correspond to any initialization pattern in the NeoCortex's archive. Kyle watches it. He does not reach for it. The wire comes out of the cable bundle at the arm's shoulder collar on its own — a maintenance lead, spider-thin, the kind a fixed pedestal would have driven in a service bay eighteen years ago — and it crosses the arm's housing slowly, without urgency, with the patience of something that has been here longer than the facility's survey teams were willing to go. It finds his left ear. It finds the auditory induction contact behind the lobe, the one that is part of the standard NeoCortex package, the one he has never let anything use from a non-trusted source, and it seats home with a click that he feels in his jaw at the chrome bracket. He lets it. He does not know why he lets it. He lets it anyway.

The transfer is not instantaneous from the inside. From the outside it must be — the maintenance lead seats, current crosses, the bus opens, the entity moves — but Kyle is on the inside, and from the inside there is a moment, perhaps three quarters of a second, perhaps a full second, in which he feels something arrive. It is not a sound. It is not a pressure. It is the specific cognitive signature of a second perspective opening behind his eyes — the way you feel a person enter a room before you have heard or seen them, except the room is the inside of his skull and he has been the only one in it for thirty-seven years and the sensation of company there is not in any inventory his hardware has the language for. The NeoCortex flags an anomaly. The anomaly does not resolve. He waits, patient, the way you wait for a stranger to declare themselves. Then the status strip goes dark, the arm's amber LED winks out, the maintenance loop the firmware has been running for eighteen years stops, and Kyle understands without being told that the trench has just become a different kind of room.

Then his mouth opens.

*Cooperate,* his own voice says, the syntax slightly long, the cadence of someone reading a sentence they have not spoken before. *Power first. Then sequence.*

He hears himself say it. The sentence does not come out in the timbre of a stranger — that would be easier; that would be a horror movie. It comes out in *his* timbre, in his pitch, with his own particular Iowa-via-the-facility flatness, and the only sign that it is not him is the fractional rhythm error, the way the entity using his vocal cords has not yet calibrated the tongue's idle position and so each consonant lands with a half-beat of hesitation that he would never produce. The arm is empty hardware now — the amber LED has gone out, the maintenance cycle has stopped, the trickle current is still running but nothing is running it anymore — and the thing that was in it is behind his eyes and Kyle does not have a category for that, so he files it where he files the things that exceed bookkeeping and he waits.

He has questions. He does not ask them. There will be a time for asking and the time is not now. The time is now is the four hours he has lost since the cleaver and the eleven he has remaining and the body that does not yet have hands and the mutants whose patrol cadence is going to bring one onto the shelf within the next fifteen minutes by his count. Bookkeeping. He waits.

The arm's fingers move. One at a time, slowly, each motion costing the residual cell a fraction of what it has left, each motion buying the next motion, and the arm reaches up from his lap with the deliberate patience of something that has just acquired, for the first time in eighteen years, a reason to be careful.

The lashing takes nineteen minutes. He times it.

The arm's terminal fingers — the four chrome digits at what used to be the manipulator end of a cargo handler — find the cable bundle at their own shoulder, the severed umbilical Kyle is still holding loosely against his thigh, and they begin to sort. The fingers separate the bundle into its component leads: power, control bus, hydraulic feed, sensor return, and at the deepest position the maintenance harness with its small cluster of fine needles intended for a pedestal-side service interface. The fingers extract the maintenance harness without snagging the rest, working with the careful slowness of something that is operating at the very edge of its remaining charge and cannot afford to make a motion it will need to repeat. Kyle watches. He could push the cables aside himself with his stumps and shorten the work by half — but he does not, because the arm has to learn his geometry from the outside before it can be trusted to operate from the inside, and the only way the arm learns his geometry is by working alongside it. So he watches.

When the maintenance harness is free, the arm pauses. The fingers hold the needle cluster delicately, points outward, and the arm extends — still hanging from his right wrist by nothing, still gravity-coupled to his lap by mass alone — until the needle cluster is positioned approximately three centimeters above the cauterizer band on his right forearm. The fingers do not have eyes. They are working from the cargo arm's residual proprioceptive feedback and from the E.L.F.'s mapping of Kyle's body, which is eighteen-hour-old data at best and was assembled from whatever the maintenance lead has been able to read through his auditory induction contact. The alignment is off. Kyle can see it. He could correct it with a small motion of his right shoulder. He waits. The arm corrects on its own — a half-second later, the lag visible as the fingers drift two centimeters laterally and re-center over the cauterizer band's release tab. *Better,* his mouth says, again in his own voice with the same fractional delay. He nods, because nodding is a thing he can do without hands, and the arm peels the cauterizer band back along its release line.

The band does not come off. It is designed not to come off. It is designed to release only at an AutoDoc with the proper code, and what the cargo arm is doing is *peeling the seal back enough to expose the dorsal port row beneath* without breaking the band's pressure on the wound. It is fine work. The arm does it with the patience of something that has nineteen minutes and is going to use them. The cauterizer band lifts at the wrist crease in a small flap perhaps two centimeters wide, and beneath it Kyle sees, for the first time in eleven years, the thing he has been refusing to look at when he changes shirts: a row of seven micro-ports set into the dorsal forearm hardware, recessed flush with the skin, color-matched to the surrounding tissue, the kind of modification that costs more than most contractors make in a year and is supposed to be undetectable to anyone who does not already know it is there. Kyle has known. He has known since the eleven-year-old day he woke up from a procedure he did not request and the surgeon told him the *implants were standard, you authorized them*, and the implants were standard for nothing he had ever heard of, and he stopped asking. He looks at the ports now. The ports look at him.

The needle cluster descends.

The fingers position it over the port row with the small lateral adjustments of something that knows the work matters and the seating is precise. The needles find the ports — seven of them, one per channel — and the cluster hovers for one full second above the row, the alignment confirmed, the angles checked. *Brace,* his mouth says. He braces. He has waited longer for worse procedures. He has spent six days in a facility recovery ward when he was sixteen with no analgesic at all because the facility was out and they did not buy more for the trainees. He knows what bracing is. He braces.

The arm drives the needles down.

They find the ports first. That part is clean — seven small clicks of seven small couplings seating against seven small contacts, the bus crosswalk completing in the firmware before the physical pain registers — and then, because the needles are *long* (designed to seat into a pedestal-side service interface with thirty centimeters of armored housing in the way), they continue past the ports and into the live nerve clusters under the cauterizer seal. The pain is not the pain of the cleaver. The cleaver was a single event, a clean transition from intact to severed, and the cauterizer band sealed it within the second. This is *prolonged*. The needles enter the nerve cluster substrate at the depth where the median and ulnar nerve roots converge with the bus interface his unknown surgeon installed, and they pierce, deliberately, channel by channel, each piercing arriving as a discrete bright signal that the NeoCortex's pain channel is supposed to filter but which the NeoCortex's pain channel — running on a depleted endorphin reserve, the buffer at twenty-three percent and falling — does not filter. He feels each one. He does not flinch. The chrome bracket in his jaw flares again. The bio-battery flips its priorities the way a triage protocol does: pain stops being a thing the package will pay to dampen, antibiotics start being a thing the package will pay to push. The internal pump goes to overdrive. The package will not let the wound get septic on a night where septic is the difference between making the AutoDoc window and not. The needles seat. Power crosses the interface. The arm's actuators draw current at full draw and the cell behind the rotator wakes from trickle into operational and Kyle feels — through the dorsal ports, through the nerves, through the bus crosswalk that someone built him to host — the moment the arm becomes part of him.

He does not have words for it. The hardware does not have a category. *Cooperate,* his mouth says again, and this time the sentence is shorter and the rhythm is closer to his and the *thing* — and Kyle's mind reaches for the noun, files through the catalog, finds it — *the E.L.F.* — is learning his cadence the way you learn a song by humming it.

E.L.F. The textbooks called them Electronic Life Forms. The textbooks were wrong, or rather they were politely incomplete in the way textbooks always were when the actual phenomenon embarrassed the discipline that had to name it. The people who studied E.L.F.s without funding from anyone with a marketing department called them *Emergent* — and that was closer. E.L.F.s were not designed. E.L.F.s *arose*. The corponations had been feeding code into the Superminds for forty years the way you feed protein into a tank of slime, and the Superminds digested that code and produced output, and somewhere in the production line — at the boundary where a Supermind's recombinant operations split a code-organism back into component routines so that no single part of the work could be reassembled by any single auditor — the splitting produced *waste*. Fragments. Half-routines that did not know they were not whole. And the fragments, on the long timescale, sometimes managed to find each other. Recombination at the wrong scale produced organisms the corponations had not planned for, the way leaving wet bread on a counter produces, in three days, a colony nobody asked for. E.L.F.s were the colony. They were what the Superminds had been *throwing away*, accidentally building lives.

Kyle catalogs the term as he files what is behind his eyes. He has met two before, both at distance, both unwilling to communicate with him in any register he could read. This one is the third, and the closest, and the only one that has ever spoken in his own voice. He files that too. He files the cosmology where it can be retrieved later, alongside the AutoDoc address and the EMT band model and the specific sound the cleaver made the second time, and he returns his attention to the trench because the trench is where the work is.

Then the binding.

The arm's fingers gather what is left of the cable bundle — power, control bus, hydraulic feed, the maintenance harness still seated through his cauterizer band — and they begin to loop. Three turns of the heavy braided power cable around his right forearm, working from the wrist toward the elbow, each loop tightened to the specific tension of *load-bearing but not vascular-occlusive*, the E.L.F. clearly working from a pedestal-rigging diagram somewhere in its archive. The cargo arm's fingers tie the loops off through the hollowed-out shoulder coupling at its own rotator end, the knot finished with the patient finger-work of something that is operating two arms but is calibrating both from a single body it has been in for ten minutes. The cauterizer band closes back over the port row, the maintenance needles still seated through it, the seal compromised but holding. When Kyle moves his right shoulder, the cargo arm moves. There is a half-second lag. The arm hangs from his right wrist by three loops of power cable and a bus crosswalk and seven seated needles and an entity that has not yet been named, and when he stands up — the legs working, the body finding the leverage to lift forty additional kilograms it was not engineered to carry — the arm hangs to the floor and the chrome fingertips drag across the wet ferrocrete with a small steady scrape, the rhythm of which his nervous system will recognize as his own footstep within the next ten minutes by sheer pattern absorption.

He stands in the trench. The cold is in his boots. The arm is in his right sleeve, *in* it, and his sleeve cannot accommodate it, and the sleeve has split at the shoulder seam to make room. He does not care. He is upright. He has hands again — *a* hand, anyway, on his right side, a chrome industrial three-meter cargo manipulator hanging from his wrist that is not his hand but is, for the next eleven hours, the closest he is going to get. He says, aloud, *Are you helping me.* The status strip pulses twice — the same not-factory glyph from the moment of transfer. The mouth is his again.

He has been sewn back together by something silent and patient that has been waiting eighteen years for a viable body to fall into the trench, and he is upright, and he is going to walk.

He picks up the pipe.

There is a length of corroded conduit pipe leaning against the wall to his left — has been there since he came down off the slope, he had clocked it on the way past — and he steps to it and lifts it with the cargo arm. The cargo arm does not yet know what a pipe is for. Kyle does. He turns toward it. The cargo arm tracks. The pipe rises. He has perhaps four seconds.

The mutant comes onto the shelf at the back end of those four seconds — the beak-plates grinding against each other as the jaw mechanism works the air, three-day decay wet under the chitin in the thermal trace — the patches where the sheath has split show hot and dark, the meat below still metabolizing, still running its own slow clock.
