The road through Calyx ran high enough that the pines gave out and only lichen-crusted rock held the wind, and they had been three weeks on it by the time Esvane let the fire burn past dark for the first time since Ocipheus.

"You're thinking about the crest again," Petra said, not looking up from the knife she was stropping. She'd stopped asking him what he was thinking about somewhere around the second week; she'd started telling him instead, and she was usually right.

"I'm thinking that a House with a crest like this one," Wren said, "spent seven centuries deciding what shape its crest should be, and never once decided what to do with the man wearing it."

Esvane made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "Your cousin decided plenty. He decided to let it get taken in the first place, and he decided to let two generations forget where it went, and now he's decided to send his own blood to fetch it back rather than spend a coin of his own risk on the job. That's not nothing. That's a whole policy."

"You say his name like a curse."

"I say his name like a client," Esvane said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the only reason I get paid."

Petra's stropping hand had gone still. "Calyx never paid me anything," she said, to the fire, not to either of them. "I do this because the alternative was staying somewhere I'd already decided I wasn't going to die."

Nobody asked her to finish that. Wren had learned, by then, that the crew kept exactly two kinds of silence — the kind that meant a question would be answered if you were patient, and the kind that meant it wouldn't be answered ever, by anyone, and asking again only spent trust he'd need later. He let this one be the second kind.

"For what it's worth," he said instead, "I don't do it for the House either. I do it because he's the only family who ever wrote my name down anywhere that mattered, and I would like, once, to be the reason he got to keep something instead of the reason he lost it."

Esvane looked at him for a long moment across the fire, the flat, assessing look she gave a plan when she was checking whether it still held weight. "That's a worse reason than mine," she said. "It'll get you killed faster."

"So will yours."

"Mine's already gotten me paid twice," she said, and that ended it — not because the argument was won, but because she'd decided the conversation had used up its allotted portion of the night.

It found them on the fifth night after that, in the col between two black ridges where the wind couldn't decide which direction to blow and so did both at once.

Esvane heard it before either of them did — a wet clicking under the wind, arrhythmic, patient, the sound of something counting instead of breathing — and had the fire stamped to embers with the flat of her boot before Wren had finished turning his head toward it.

"Up," she said. "Now. Rocks, not trees."

He'd never seen a sinterspawn scout up close, only the corpses of the ones the road crews burned at crossroads as a warning nobody needed twice. This one came low along the scree, quick as water finding a crack, faster than its size should have allowed, and the pale plating along its spine caught the last of the coals' light and gave nothing back.

Petra put two crossbow bolts into it before it closed the last ten meters, and neither one turned it. It was Esvane, not the case, she moved to shield when the thing finally came into the firelight's reach — a half-second decision Wren watched her make and would spend a long time afterward not knowing what to do with, because it was the first time in three weeks he'd seen her spend herself on a person instead of on the job.

Wren's knife found the soft joint behind the foreleg on the second pass, drilled into his hands by Loera until his hands no longer needed him to think about it, and the thing went down convulsing, and then went down still, and none of them moved for a ten-count after that just to be sure the stillness was going to hold.

Esvane checked the case first. Then she checked Petra. Then, last, she checked him, and something crossed her face when she found him unmarked that he couldn't afford to name, because naming it would have cost him something he didn't have to spend that morning.

"That's twice this month the case has nearly gone into something's stomach," she said, once her breathing had settled. "Next time we don't wait for daylight to move. Next time we don't stop for fires at all."

She meant it as a lesson. He heard it, even then, as the first draft of a harder sentence, one she would finish four mornings later, at a river he hadn't seen yet.

They had two days on the pursuit and both of them knew it wouldn't hold.

Esvane said as much on the descent from the ridge, not stopping to say it, the case riding tight against her chest exactly as it had ridden since Ocipheus and would keep riding, he understood without being told, until it was no longer hers to carry. "If it comes down to it," she said, "the box goes to Calyx. That's the job. That was always the job."

"And if it comes down to it," Wren said, because he had learned by then which questions were worth the breath, "what's my job?"

She didn't answer that one. Petra, a half-step behind her, didn't either, though something crossed her face that might have been the beginning of an answer she'd decided not to finish.

It came down to it on the fourth morning, at a river crossing with one narrow ford and no cover on either bank worth the name. He heard the column before he saw it — too many boots for a border patrol, moving with a purpose that wasn't looking for anything so much as already knowing where it was. Esvane read it in the same half-second he did.

"Go," she said. Not to him. To Petra, and to the case.

He understood — a half-beat too late, same as most things that year — that she had not been asking whether to split up. She had already decided it, probably days ago, banked against the morning it would be needed — a spare line coiled and ready long before the first one frays. He was the piece of the plan that could be spent. The case was not.

"Esvane -"

"You'll manage," she said, which was not the same as an apology and was not offered as one. She was already moving, Petra a stride behind her, the case between them like something with its own gravity, pulling the whole shape of the crew toward the far bank and away from him.

He could have run. He chose instead the two seconds it took to be sure the column's attention had found him and not them, because some habits outlasted the reasons a man had first learned them for, and this one — buy the others their exit, whatever it costs the one who stays — was older in him than House Calyx, older than Loera, older than anything he'd been taught on purpose.

They did not look back. He did not expect them to. That was, he would think later, very nearly the whole of what he needed to know about the difference between a crew and whatever this was going to become instead.

The water closed over Esvane's boots and Petra's, and then the tree line, and then there was only the column closing the last distance, and a small dark shape at its front already reading him with the same patient, precise attention it turned on everything — already deciding how much of a fight this was actually going to be.

Not much of one, as it turned out. He had one knife and a fold that cost more than he had left to spend that morning, and the outcome settled itself before he had to.

He did not raise his hands. He also did not run. He had already decided, standing in the mud with the river taking the last of his crew away from him, that he was done making that particular choice for other people's reasons.

Wren walked with his hands bound in front of him, which he had decided, three days into it, was worse than bound behind. Bound behind, a man could tell himself the rope was an insult aimed at his hands. Bound in front, he had to watch them the whole day, tied, useless, trailing him around like an accusation he couldn't set down.

Doyle walked a half-step behind him and to the left, close enough to close the gap in under a second, far enough that Wren's shoulder blades had stopped waiting for the touch somewhere around day two. He didn't talk. He nudged, once, when the trail forked and Wren's feet started toward the wrong half of it — two fingers, brief, between the shoulder blades, the exact minimum force the correction required and not one ounce more.

"You could just tell me which way," Wren said, the third time it happened.

"Could," Doyle said. Flat. He did not elaborate, and Wren had stopped expecting him to.

Orim had said nothing to him directly in three days, and Lyra had noticed. He watched the boy the way he watched weather that hadn't decided yet what it meant to do — not unkindly, exactly, but without once setting the watching down. When Wren had offered, on the second night, to make himself useful — a fire built faster, a line better tied, anything to earn the food he was eating — Orim had said only, "That is exactly what I would expect you to say," and gone back to his own fire, and that had been the whole of the conversation.

Lyra understood the instinct. A man who had spent a working lifetime reading what people wanted him to believe did not stop reading it just because the boy in question had a reasonably sympathetic face and a story that hung together. Especially not then.

Eleven days on the highland road, and her knees knew every one of them. Orim's joints answered the steeper grades with a sound she'd decided, as a professional courtesy, not to mention. Doyle walked without complaint because Doyle walked everything without complaint. By day eight Lyra had stopped finding that reassuring or irritating. It simply was.

The deadline was not a comfort to think about, so she measured instead. Senne's column had been three days behind at Verlaine. Eleven days of highland passes since, and she hadn't counted on the gap holding. She'd been right not to count on it.

The path down to Sal Vento smelled of the sea two hundred paces before anyone saw it — salt, limestone, and under both the cold of rock decades of spray never fully dried. Cut steps, not worn track. Risers sized for load, treads wide enough for two abreast. Somebody had built this to last. She approved of it on principle — honest work always earned that from her.

"Ship in the lower harbor," Doyle said. Flat. Just information — that was how the shell delivered everything now, no distinction in it between the urgent and the idle.

"Liturgy registration?" Lyra asked.

"Wrong marks. Somebody painted over the hull registry and did decent work, but the paint doesn't match under the ultraviolet band." A beat. "Reads Balthier."

The Balthier. Lyra had heard that name once, in a procurement review the year before the crossing — an Ocipheus Station reconnaissance vessel off the Northern Route roster, flagged administrative loss, never chased down. Seventeen years she'd trusted the Station's paperwork meant something. The trust had gone somewhere else, without much feeling left to spend on it.

They took the stone table outside the harbor tavern to wait on Orim, and that was where the trouble started - not with them, at first. A dockhand caught an elbow he decided he hadn't earned, his drink went down a stranger's front, and the stranger came up already swinging. In a port this size a spilled drink was never about the drink. It was about who'd bumped whom on purpose, and who was watching closely enough to have arranged it.

Lyra put her back to the wall on reflex - seventeen years of drill did that whether she asked it to or not - and watched two men become four, and an argument turn into a proper scrap: short, ugly, nobody's technique, elbows and table legs, one man's face meeting stone hard enough that he sat down without meaning to. She took a wrist reaching for Doyle's collar, turned it the way the manual said, put its owner down without breaking anything. Doyle dropped a second man into a barrel, unhurried about it. Four down or fled inside the time it took to describe, nobody dead - the correct outcome for a fight that hadn't been about anything to begin with.

That was when Tavar came through the wreckage of it, unhurried, and sat down across from her like the last two minutes hadn't happened.

She'd confirmed his documents two hours past, at this table: Loera operative, no rank she recognized as command. Not a captain. No ship of his own. He'd given her four things in order of most to least verifiable — name, House, the ship's name, and his codes for the mooring watch's rotation — the way a man gives information to someone he's decided is worth the risk of being wrong about.

"That wasn't staged for you," he said, meaning the fight, "but I watched how you handled it."

"You already knew the ship was here?" Lyra asked.

"I knew several things were here. I came to find out which ones you needed." He set a folded chart on the table — the sea cave south of the harbor mouth, Balthier moored inside it, out of every sightline but his own, and beside it the watch's rotation, hour by hour, in his own hand. "This is what it costs," he said, and named a price.

She paid it without arguing the number. He told her the rest on the walk down.

Declan had known cold with a job to do before. Drift cold, the kind that lived a hundred feet under Unaka ridge and didn't care whose shift it was. This one was worse. Sea cold, at the tail end of night — the kind that had quit announcing itself hours ago and just kept coming.

He waited behind the limestone shelf with Orim, out of the wind but not out of the cold, because cold didn't need wind to find a man, same as gas didn't need a draft to find the low place in a shaft. Above them, on the overhang, Lyra had been at the scope the better part of an hour. He'd timed it without meaning to. Fifty-eight minutes. He'd learned to count on watch a long time before he learned anything else useful, and some habits didn't leave when the man they'd belonged to did.

Orim scried from a crouch, fingertips together at his sternum, eyes gone soft and elsewhere. He didn't use the Rod. Hadn't since the gorge. Declan didn't ask why. A man learned to leave some things in another man's coat pocket, same as you left a fella's dead brother out of the talk on shift. Ninety seconds. Declan counted them anyway — the old habit from counting the interval between a whistle and a shell landing — not because the number mattered, but because counting was a way of being somewhere other than afraid.

Orim's eyes opened.

"Two soldiers," he said. "The one at the gangway foot is seated, rifle across the knees, watching the gate side of the dock, not the water. The one at the forward hatch is upright, checking the mooring lines along the starboard rail. He'll finish that and go still. Call it four minutes."

He paused. "The hold is clear. I didn't go past it. There's something between me and the space behind the forward bulkhead I didn't want to push through."

He said it flatly: observed fact, not an invitation to make more of it.

"Engine housing," Lyra said. "Residual heat, maybe. Liturgy apparatus reads strange even asleep."

"Possibly." He didn't sound convinced, and didn't push it either. He sat down on the shelf — not a lowering, a sitting his body had decided on before he'd been consulted — and looked at the backs of his hands, breathing the deliberate breathing of a man remembering what deliberate breathing was for.

Declan ran a thumb over the bayonet catch at his belt, once, twice. Test the door before you open it — an old rule, older than this body, older than the last one. He'd learned it the hard way in the monster corridor three days back, when the bolt jammed and the blade was the only tool left in his hands fast enough to matter. A man checked the tools he trusted the hard way. He didn't trust any other kind.

"Two," Lyra said. "Gangway and forward hatch. Quiet."

He looked at the cave mouth like open ground before a push, reading it for the places it would kill him if he'd read it wrong. "Aye," he said. Then, because the moment wanted more than one word and he chose to give her more: "I go first. You after."
