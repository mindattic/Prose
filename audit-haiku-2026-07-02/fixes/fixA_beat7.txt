The firmware Vox had pulled from the gray market was old enough that the package still listed a developer name — not a CorpoNation, not a subsidiary, a person, a handle, six characters and a dot. The kind of thing somebody shipped out of a bedroom in 2199 because the quarterly mainline updates had stopped covering a class of hardware that wasn't dying fast enough to be worth discontinuing and not current enough to be worth patching. The firmware did exactly what it had done then: it ran depreciated 4.1 broadcast protocol, the slow kind, the kind that had been officially retired when the municipal mesh moved to a unified handshake standard and the whole GLMZ stopped speaking the old tongue simultaneously. Every relay on the Network had been retooled. Every CorpoNation scanner, every Arcturus Civil Security sweep, every piece of automated traffic analysis had been taught to look for what came after. Nobody had thought to keep watching for what came before. You didn't guard a door that had been bricked over for twenty-two years.

Vox spent forty minutes in the hauler running channel tests while Rook was inside getting the briefing from Ohara. Not forty minutes of active work — forty minutes of the old firmware saying things to the city's gutted back-channel infrastructure and the city answering, slowly, in the creaking dialect of hardware that nobody maintained and nobody had gotten around to decommissioning. The municipal relays still forwarded the old protocol packets. They just did it last, after all the current traffic, the way post offices forward mail to addresses that have stopped existing — out of habit, because the redirect rule never got cleared, because the human being whose job it was to clear it had retired and the automated audit was configured to flag only active anomalies, and a packet format that hadn't moved in twenty-two years didn't look like an anomaly. It looked like background radiation.

The four-channel rig she'd built across the past six weeks ran clean on it. Slower than Rook liked — she'd said so, twice, in the way Rook said things twice when she was deciding whether to push — and Vox hadn't argued the point, because arguing it would have required explaining that the slowness was load-bearing. PEREGRINE's signals apparatus was fast, current, scanning for exactly the kind of traffic a competent crew would generate on modern protocols. Slow was the thing it wasn't configured to chase.

Vox reached across the console and reseated the fourth channel's coupling by hand. The old firmware wanted physical contact, no handshake, the way hardware talked before it learned to trust anything. The hauler's dead dash threw a green ghost of the readout across her knuckles, and she held two fingers on the seam until the packet cleared, then let go.

The fourth channel she'd rebuilt from scratch, from a firmware kernel that predated the unified handshake by two years, ran at a third of the throughput of the old sixth and was in practice invisible to anything PEREGRINE was currently watching. It wasn't a gap she'd found. It was a gap the city had built for itself and forgotten it had — the same gap it had built in every district where the maintenance budget had stopped and the infrastructure had not. The GLMZ ran on deferred decisions. She was running on one now.

Rook came out of Ohara's building twenty-two minutes after she went in. She came out with the set of someone who had counted exits on the way out the same way she'd counted them on the way in, found the same number, and wasn't reassured by the consistency. Vox said nothing. Rook stopped at the hauler's side panel and didn't get in immediately, standing in the rain with one hand on the wet metal of the roof, and the pause was the audit: Rook running numbers behind her face, assigning cost to things that had costs, finding the column that didn't balance and running it again.

"Vox," she said, not a question.

"The 4.1 mesh is live. Four channels. The old-trunk relay in Z4 is still forwarding." Vox paused. "PEREGRINE will be using current-standard sweep. They won't be listening for us on a protocol that was officially retired when they were still bidding on their first contract."

Rook absorbed that the way she absorbed operational data — committed, compared against known positions, not confirmed aloud. She looked at the panel of the hauler, at her own hand on the wet roof, and for a moment was very still in the rain.

"How long does the ghost last once they start actively hunting?" she said.

"Two days. Maybe three if they don't know what they're looking for." Vox let that sit for a beat. "They won't know what they're looking for. Nobody uses this anymore."

"Nobody," Rook said, flat.

"Nobody who isn't running on something that broke before the standard changed," Vox said. Which was her, and Rook knew it, and neither of them said what that meant. The rust that made you invisible was still rust. It still cost you the sixth channel and the throughput and whatever the bandwidth loss meant for the things you couldn't see as fast on four as you'd once seen on six. The hauler sat in the rain with its lights off and the old protocol packets moving slow and patient through the city's forgotten back channels, invisible to everything looking for something current.

Rook got in. Vox closed the mesh, and held it, and the four channels breathed their quiet antique hum against everything the city was saying at full volume in languages Stave's board would never scan for, because the languages were older than the board.
