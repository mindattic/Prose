Morning came as a degree of difference in the dark, not a sunrise — the substation had no east wall to give them one, just the slow lift of the rain from sideways to vertical, which meant the storm had moved off the higher tiers and they were getting it straight down now, their share. The place took shape around that change in the rain the way a room does when your eyes stop fighting it: a long low hall of dead transformer bays, the big iron coffins gutted decades back and left to rust in rows, and between them a floor of grated steel that had drunk so much oil over so many years it gave the air a taste, a flat metal sweetness on the back of the tongue that Vox had stopped noticing on the walk in and noticed again now that there was nothing to do but wait. The light was the gray that isn't a color so much as the absence of the night's, leaking down through the open roofline, and it lay on everything without falling on anything — no shadows, no edges, just the bays going from black to charcoal and the puddles between them going from black to the dull pewter of standing water that hadn't moved in hours.

Stave's people were gone by then. They'd left the cabinet breathing its bought lie on the fourth channel and a clean seam where the dead-not-dead door read live to anyone counting from the outside, and that was the whole of what they'd left, because Stave sold doors, not entrances.

Boiler walked the floor twice with Scout's crawlers trailing him like something he'd been assigned to feed, reading the wall's near face for the place Ohara had papered eighteen months ago. He found it where the log said it would be — a hairline you'd have to know was there, sealed and resealed, a pale ridge of compound a shade off the wall's own gray. He didn't trust the log. He trusted his hand. He laid his palm flat against the patch and held it there past the point of reading it, the way he held a column he'd decided not to like, and his palm came away wrong: not cold the way wet steel is cold, but cold the way the inside of a thing is cold, a draw against the skin instead of a chill on it.

"It's pulling heat," he said, to no one, to the wall. "Year and a half, and nobody put a hand on it." He wiped the palm on his coat without seeming to decide to.

Ohara came in last and ungraciously, the way people come to the thing they paid for and then resent paying. She stopped two steps inside the door and stood there, and Rook watched her not look at the wall — watched her give the gutted bays a long survey they didn't need, the ceiling, the rain, anything but the one face of one wall she'd sealed with her own compound eighteen months ago and walked away from believing sealed meant closed. There was a read on her Rook had been waiting for and now had: not the read of a hostage and not the read of a client, but the read of a builder come to inspect her own work and finding it occupied. Ohara had made this room. Not the substation — the partition behind the patch, the locked place with no door she'd remembered keeping. She'd poured the walls and set the seal and pocketed whatever passed for a key, and she'd done it well enough that her own hand couldn't find the way back in. Now the only way to stand inside the thing she'd built was to let a stranger pick the lock and pay a body to climb through after — and Rook watched that land on her in the doorway, not as fear, fear would have been cleaner, but as the flat humiliation of a person who has to buy a ticket into a room she owns. When her eyes finally went to the patch they went fast and came off fast, a thing touched and dropped. Lace put her against the cabinet and told her where to keep her mouth, and she kept it, and the keeping of it was the most honest thing she'd done since the table.

Rook set up out at the seam in the floor where PEREGRINE's vector came in low and patient on Scout's read — not a dig anymore, Stave had been right, just a crew positioning to be standing under the wall when it went, their approach the slow confident line of people who'd bought a clock and trusted it. Rook crouched at the floor seam and set two cold lamps on the grate, one and then the other, squaring them by eye to the line the read had drawn, and the small green of them was the only color in the hall that the rain hadn't washed to gray.

She stayed crouched when the lamps were set, one hand flat on the grating, and let the cold come up through her palm. The same cold Boiler's hand had read off the patch. The same cold Vox was about to go into. *If she doesn't come back —* Rook stopped that before it finished. Counted exits instead. The load corridor at the north end. The main bay door, compromised by PEREGRINE's approach vector. The roofline, forty feet and no rope. *Three ways out, one of them good.* She ran the count until her breathing settled, and then she ran it again, because counting was the only honest thing she could do for someone who was about to go somewhere she couldn't follow.

Vox sat against the wall with the patch and let the channels go down one at a time the way you turn off lights leaving a house — the crew's chatter first, then the cabinet's bought hum, then Stave's open line they'd left in the door, last. With each one the hall got quieter and the rain got louder by the same amount, until the straight-down drum of it on the dead transformers was the whole sound of the world and her own pulse was the second thing under it, and she made herself stop counting that too.

Lace crouched in front of Vox.

"The log said eleven days." Lace's voice was low, only for the two of them, under the rain. "I read nine and I went in nine. I was two days wrong, and I let you carry the two." She didn't say sorry. She'd already learned this morning what her words were worth when she sat on them, and she wasn't going to spend one now where it would only buy her the feeling of having spent it. She reached up instead and laid two fingers flat against the patch beside Vox's head, in the cold, the bare pads of them on compound a year and a half dead, so the two of them would be reading the same thing off the same wall.

Her fingers were steady. Vox marked that the way she marked everything — it came in as data before it came in as anything else — and only after did she understand what the steadiness was for. It was the apology Lace had standing to make. Sorry was a word, and Lace had just been shown what her words were worth when she held them; she'd sat on a number and let it read as a smaller number, and no sentence she could build now would unsit it. But she could refuse to let Vox be the only hand on the failing wall. She could put her own skin against the same cold and keep it there — pay in the open, this time, in the one currency the wall would accept, with her hand where everyone could see it shouldn't be. It cost her nothing the crew could see and everything Vox could feel, which was the exact inversion of the eleven days.

Vox felt the small warmth of Lace's fingers leave the steel almost as fast as it arrived, the patch taking it the way it took everything, drawing the heat off two living fingertips at the same patient rate it had been drawing it off the wall for eighteen months. The patch read warmth and took it. That was all the patch did, and it didn't care the warmth was an apology. The two of them knelt there a moment longer with their hands a span apart on the one surface in the room that was actively eating the heat off them, and neither moved, and the not-moving was the closest the two of them came to even.

Then Rook said the only thing Rook said all morning, which was the distance, in meters, and let the number be the warning.

Then Vox put her own hand on the seam, the silent room on the far side of it, and went in after the only signal that was left.

Vox went quiet. On the floor the lamps held their distance, and the rain came straight down now, and her breathing slowed and stopped being a thing the crew could hear. Across the hall, Rook didn't move from her position at the floor seam, one hand still flat against the grating, palm down the way she'd put it at the start. She hadn't moved it. She wouldn't move it until Vox came back out.
