The site had to be in Z2.

Not for security — for the hardware. Stave had explained this the way they explained everything, flat and instrumented, with the patience of someone who had stopped expecting the explanation to change the situation. The Atlas-grade partition was running on firmware that assumed a specific grid frequency, the kind of precision that the Loop ran to within a hundredth of a cycle and that the outer zones approximated by feel. Take the rig into Z5 and the frequency drift would register as noise on every read. Take it into the Gray Zone and the grid was dirty enough that the partition's own hardware might misread the delta between the lock and the lock-state, which wasn't a safe thing for hardware in the process of being opened. Z2 was the only zone inside GLMZ that had the infrastructure integrity and the absence of commercial monitoring to make the procedure run clean.

Stave told them this in the transformer room with the copper smell in the air, and then Scout said: "The building that's been scheduled for demolition. The one Boiler's been in."

Boiler looked at the ceiling the way he looked at ceilings when the ceiling was giving him information the person beneath it wasn't. "You were in a condemned building when I found you," Rook said.

"I've been in and out of it for four months," he said. "The grid line running to it got cut in 2219 when the demo order went active, and then the demo order got delayed and the grid line stayed cut and the substation kept supplying it anyway, because the substation didn't know the order went active and the demo order didn't know the grid line had been cut and nobody in the relevant offices had been in contact with the other relevant offices since approximately the year the building was condemned." He paused. "The grid frequency at that building is Loop-standard. Has been since it was built, because it was built when this was a Loop-adjacent block. The frequency hasn't been touched in six years because nobody has maintained the building in six years. There's no drift."

The silence in the transformer room was the silence of a crew working a position they hadn't expected to come easy.

"The room on the second level," Scout said.

"Has adequate structural load for Stave's rig and everything it needs to run," Boiler said. "I checked. It was the first thing I checked, actually. I check structural load before I check anything else. The floor's old timber, pre-poured, and it's in better condition than the rest of the building because it's the only room that was dry." He touched the column beside him — the diagnostic gesture, the thumb along the load path. "The whole third floor has been wet since 2222 and the second has some damage at the junction with the stairwell, but the room on the west side of the second is intact. The window's sealed. The plumbing doesn't touch it." He looked at Rook. "It's been waiting there in a building nobody was going to get around to demolishing. I thought that was relevant."

Rook looked at him, and then at Scout, and then at the rest of the crew in the copper room with the bus-duct desk.

"Why were you in the building?" she asked.

Boiler considered this the way he considered everything structural — not whether to answer, but what the honest weight of the answer was before he carried it. "I wanted somewhere to go," he said. "After the Lake Platform. Somewhere that was still standing for a reason that had nothing to do with anyone deciding it should stand. I found this building standing for exactly no reason at all. That seemed right." He looked at the ceiling of the transformer room, which wasn't his building and hadn't been chosen for him. "I'll pack the kit before we leave."

Stave looked at the floor. "The room will need prep," they said. "Isolation. I need two surfaces level to within three millimeters because the interface has a tolerance that the hardware doesn't advertise and the manual doesn't warn you about, and if the surface shifts during the procedure it will register as patient movement and we will have a problem." They folded their hands on the bus-duct desk. "I will need forty minutes in the room before Ohara is brought in. The prep is not optional."

"You'll have forty minutes," Rook said.

"I'll also need someone who can hold the south stairwell access. The one at the junction where the floor is compromised — nobody can use it, but somebody can come down through the third floor if they know the third floor is accessible from the roof. I'll need someone who knows when a footstep is a footstep and when it's the building." Stave paused. "Not the same skill."

Scout's hands were flat on her knees. "Tell me where the stairwell is," she said. She didn't say she would hold it or that she was good at it. She asked where it was. The distinction was something Rook had always liked about Scout.

"I'll draw it," Boiler said. He produced a pen from his pocket the way Boiler produced everything — without performance, as a thing that was simply in its proper location and had been retrieved. He drew a floor plan on the side of an equipment manifest that Stave found under the desk, and it wasn't a rough sketch but a dimensioned plan, because Boiler didn't produce approximate things even under time pressure. He had been walking that building for four months. He gave it to Scout and she studied it for twenty seconds, which was the exact amount of time she needed, and then she gave it back. She didn't take it. She had it.

Lace had been quiet in the room for twenty minutes, which was Lace's version of very active. She was building something in her head — the approach, the cover, the story the building needed to tell to anyone who saw it occupied for an evening and asked questions afterward. Her eyes had done the room three times since they arrived and she hadn't asked a single question about the floor plan, which meant she had already built the floor plan herself off the structural tells.

"The story," she said finally, "is utility reconnection. The block's on the municipal maintenance schedule and they're running three months behind and the condemned building is the lowest-priority item on the list. I can put us in the scheduling system as of today. By the time anyone cross-references the demo status against the utility status we'll have been gone for forty-eight hours." She looked at Stave. "I'll need the building's utility reference ID."

Stave gave it to her without hesitation, which meant they had already memorized it, which told Rook something about how long Stave had been circling the specific gravity of this problem. Some preparations take months. The ones that take months are the ones where the person doing the preparing has been waiting for someone to arrive and make the preparation usable.

"Three hours," Rook said. "Lace, you're first in. Boiler with the kit. Scout takes the stairwell. Vox, I want Ohara's approach route covered from Z2 to the building with no signal visible on the outside of our channel." She looked at Stave. "Can you be ready in three hours?"

"I have been ready," Stave said, and it was the first thing they had said in the transformer room that had any feel to it, "for two years."
