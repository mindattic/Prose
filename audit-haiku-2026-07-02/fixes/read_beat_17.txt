Inside the wall was the loud room first, and it arrived as pressure before it arrived as anything else — a weight that set behind her eyes and sank into her jaw, eighteen months of the patch's own complaint pitched at a frequency she felt in her molars more than heard. The cold came next, not a temperature but a direction, a steady pull through a seam that read live and wasn't, the wall lying to its own near face the way it had lied to Ohara's log. Her body was somewhere behind her — knees on the substation grit, hand flat on the patch, Lace's two fingers a hand-span from her own — and she made herself feel the floor through her kneecaps once, a tether, before she let it go and went past the loud room into the quiet, the silent room where the only signal was the one she carried.

She started reading the wall the way you read a held breath: where it was thin, where it was thinnest, where the knee Stave wouldn't price had already bent. She found the bend. It was behind her, not ahead. They were past it.

The first sign that the outside had stopped being patient reached her body before it reached the reading. Two floors up, where the substation's old service deck ran out over the transformer bays, something put its weight onto grating that hadn't carried weight in years — a single boot-fall that traveled the structure down through the dead bus and into the patch and arrived under her palm as a tick, the kind of tick a building makes settling, except buildings settle slower than this and only once. Then a door somewhere above opened the wrong way, against a frame swollen with a year of straight-down rain, and the screech of it came down the steel as a long thin scrape she felt in the bones of her hand and not in any ear she still had down on the floor. Boots on grating. A door forced. The vibration of people who had decided the building was already theirs.

She didn't lose the reading to it. She did lose, for the length of one breath she wasn't taking, the conviction that the reading was the only clock in the room.

Then Rook's knock reached her — not through any channel, through the steel itself, one boot to the cabinet's frame, flat and deliberate, the way you knock on a door you know someone is behind when you need them to know you're still out there. She waited for the second one. It came late, a half-beat late, long enough that she had time to wonder if there'd be a second at all, and the lateness was its own information — Rook taking the time to be sure of the meters before sending them, because sending the wrong number into a wall someone was living inside was worse than sending it slow. The second knock landed. She counted the gap between them and read the meters out of it. Closing. PEREGRINE's slow confident line had stopped being slow.

She had the reading half-built — the thin places mapped, the order she'd have to take them in to walk the failure backward instead of forward — and half-built was a sentence with no verb. To finish it she needed the cabinet's bought hum on the fourth channel, the reference tone Stave had sold them, the clean false-live she was supposed to read the real wall against: a known lie to measure the unknown one by, the one steady thing in the room that was steady on purpose.

She reached for it the way you reach for a wall in the dark — not looking, just trusting the hand to find the load-bearing thing where the load-bearing thing had always been — and her hand closed on air. The hum was gone. Not faded, not drifting; gone between one reach and the next, a step taken onto a stair that wasn't there, the whole weight of the reading already committed to a surface that had stopped existing under it.

The vertigo was real and physical even down in the silent room where she had no body to feel it with. Her sense of the wall tilted, the mapped thin places sliding loose from their bearings because she'd plotted every one against the reference and the reference was what had just walked out from under her. Stave's cabinet had stopped breathing its lie — brownout, or a hand on a switch fifty meters out, or the wall itself finally pulling more than the line could feed — and for a stretch she couldn't have priced she was inside a wall she could no longer measure against anything but herself.

She made herself stop reaching for the thing that was gone. That was the whole of the recalibration, and it was harder than it sounded: not finding a new instrument but quitting the dead one, prying her attention off the silence where the tone had been and putting it back on the only signal still arriving — the cold's steady pull, the patch's held breath, the eighteen months of complaint that had never once gone quiet because it couldn't. She'd gone past the loud room on the way in because the silent room was where the clean answer lived; now the clean answer was the part that had failed her, and the dirty one was all that was left, and the dirty one had never stopped talking.

Up through the steel the boots found a rhythm. Below it, a hand-span from hers, Lace's two fingers were still flat against the patch — she could feel them, faint and warm, a heat the wall was already working to claim. Lace didn't know the tone was gone. Lace didn't know PEREGRINE had the meters. Lace knew only what she'd agreed to know: that she was the body on the outside of the wall that held its position so the body on the inside had something to navigate by. The two fingers stayed where they were, steady, offering the only thing on offer.

Vox let them be what they were. A tether. Not a rescue.

She decided to keep going. There was no moment to it, no swell — just the small mean count of someone who has lost the safe way and tallied what the unsafe way costs and found the number survivable, or near enough that the difference was academic at this range. She had a wall and herself and a clock she could no longer see the hands on. She turned back into the loud room.

*Survivable, probably. Except probably was priced against an instrument that just went dark, and now it's just me and a wall, both of us lying about how much time is left.*
