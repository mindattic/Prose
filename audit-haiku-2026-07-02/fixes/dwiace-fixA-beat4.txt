The buzzer sounded once at nine, which was the hour Rennick had given them, which meant they were the kind of people who arrived exactly when they were told and resented every minute of it. He'd made the call the night before. Not the other way around. The name had come up on a list he'd had no business connecting to a North Shore family, and he'd connected it anyway, and then he'd dialed a number in Evanston at an hour no stranger's voice is welcome and told the man who answered where to be and when. Not asked. You don't ask a father down three tiers into the Gray Zone; you give him a time and let the thing that's already in him do the rest.

His optical overlay flagged the street-level camera out of habit more than need: a man, late fifties, silver-haired, the build of someone who used to be athletic and hadn't quite admitted the tense had changed. Behind him, a woman a year or two younger, arms crossed at her sternum. Not cold. Rennick noted the distinction.

Below, the private hire idled at the curb with its hazards on, the driver not getting out - which told him they'd instructed the driver to wait, which told him they still believed they were the ones deciding how long this would take. They'd learn otherwise on the stairs. Everyone did.

"Right on my time," he said, to the room.
