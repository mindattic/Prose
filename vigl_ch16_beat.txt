The gorge told them before Orim did.

It had taken three hours to come down off the Forge Hearth ridge, through the last of the volcanic scrub, into country that had given up being anything in particular a long time before any of them were born — not farmland, not wilderness with a name, only the unclaimed fall of scrub and dry wash between the highlands and the coast. No House had ever bothered to survey it. This was not, in Orim's long experience of unclaimed ground, because no one had wanted it. It was because everyone who mattered had already read it, once, a hundred years or more ago, and had moved on without writing down what they'd found.

Lyra's shoulder had opened two hours back, under the corridor pack — not enough for the White Tincture, a shallow furrow the pauldron had mostly caught, stiffened now in the cold as shallow wounds always did. She had stopped mentioning it. He had stopped waiting for her to. He kept it with the rest of what he was carrying, unweighted, alongside the temperature of the wind and the age of the scrub and the number of days remaining before Sal Vento stopped waiting for anyone. Information. Not yet a decision.

The wash narrowed into a gorge at the tree line, and the tree line had no birds in it.

He read the absence before he could have said what he was reading. Two hours of descent, and the scrub had carried the ordinary noise of scrub — insects, a carrion bird working something dead in the middle distance, wind doing what wind did to dry brush. Then the gorge mouth, and a silence arriving whole, the way pressure changes before sound does, so that a man attuned to thresholds felt it in his ears before he had located anything to call it fear.

"Orim."

He had already stopped. He stood at the gorge's edge with his boots in the last of the scrub and his eyes on the particular ten meters of ground ahead of him, reading it with the same unhurried thoroughness he gave every threshold before he permitted his own feet to cross it — a man reading a letter he suspects will change what he does with the rest of his day, he had told Lyra once, does not open it standing up.

"Bones," he said. "Old ones and new ones. No pattern I'd call a den, exactly — more a larder that outgrew its own management." He crouched, touched something in the dust neither of the others could see from where they stood, and did not pick it up. "Deer-analogue, most of it. Some of what came through the corridor behind us, if the marrow's any indication — the pack we crossed two hours ago wasn't only fleeing north. Something back here was thinning it from the south."

"How old, the recent kills?" she asked.

"Recent enough that the flies haven't finished arguing about which parts to file a claim on." He straightened, slower than the answer should have cost a man his age, and looked up the gorge — a hundred meters of dry channel walled by rock the color of a bruise, narrowing before it opened again into whatever waited past the bend. "This is the fast way to the coast road. The long way costs the rest of the day and puts us on the ridge after dark, which is a cost I'd rather not add to what the last ridge already took out of us." He did not say why. The last ridge had been the fire-scry, and none of them had discussed it since, and none of them needed to.

"And the short way?" she asked.

"The short way has whatever emptied that larder still inside it, most likely. I can't scry past the bend — my range doesn't reach, and something in that direction reads to me the way a held breath reads. Which is not information. It is the absence of information in a specific shape." He had learned, across fifty years of reading thresholds, that this particular absence was itself worth more than most of what passed for information, and had also learned that saying so changed nothing about which road they were about to walk.

Doyle had come up on the other side without a sound, which by now was simply how Doyle moved, rifle slung and hands loose, the black optic reading the gorge walls with the same flat attention he gave every approach. There was a great deal the man could have said about what he felt standing at that mouth. He offered one word of it.

"Bad," he said.

"Yes," Lyra said.

Two words were the whole of what Doyle had ever needed to give, and the whole of what Lyra had ever given back, and Orim had watched enough men decide enough things to have concluded, long before either of these two were born, that the shortest exchanges were usually the honest ones. They went in anyway. There had never really been a question, not in any sense that made asking it worth the breath. Three days had already turned suspicious once this week, and Sal Vento would not wait for a party that took the long way around every hard thing between here and the coast.

The gorge widened past the bend into a bowl of open ground the size of a tourney field, walled on three sides by the same bruise-colored rock, floored in dust and old bone and the flattened wreckage of scrub that something considerably heavier than deer had been walking over for longer than a season. At the bowl's far side, where the rock face broke into a shadowed overhang deep enough to be its own weather, something was already moving before any of them had finished understanding the scale of the space they had walked into.

Orim gave it three seconds of looking before deciding what it was not. That was the discipline: never name a thing before you had watched it long enough to be wrong about it once, in your own head, quietly, where the wrongness cost nothing.

It came out from under the overhang the way a landslide comes out from under a ridge — not fast, not at first, mass announcing itself before speed did, four legs and then, wrongly, a fifth point of contact where a tail should have simply trailed and instead planted, gripped, pushed. Fourteen feet at the shoulder would have been generous for the harrower Ardea had once described to Lyra secondhand from a garrison report. This cleared that mark before its head came fully into the light, and kept coming — twenty feet, easily, at the ridge of its back, a bulk that had no business moving on legs at all, closer in outline to a war engine built with a mandate for excessive tonnage than to anything that had ever needed to survive on its own four feet in open country.

The hide was not grey-brown like the corridor pack's. It was plated — overlapping segments the color and apparent hardness of split slate, a natural armor that should not have existed on anything that also needed to breathe, to bend, to hunt. Where the plates met at shoulder and hip they showed dark seams, wet-looking, that did not match the dry matte of the plates themselves. The head, swinging toward them, carried no eyes Orim could find — only two rows of shallow pits along the brow ridge, opening and closing independently of one another, each one flaring a faint heat-shimmer at the edges like a coal drawing breath.

It had no need to see them. It was already reading the warmth coming off their bodies from sixty meters out.

The sound arrived before the charge did — not a roar, nothing so simple. A frequency that came up through the ground into the soles of the boots and into the back teeth at the same moment, low enough that for a full second it read as the gorge itself settling, some geological complaint the rock was lodging against its own weight. Then it kept going, sustained, and understanding arrived in Orim a beat or two before he said anything about it: this was breath. This was simply what the creature sounded like at rest, before it had decided to do anything at all.

He had gone very still. He recognized the stillness in himself as he recognized it in anyone standing at a threshold — not fear, exactly, though fifty years had never quite taught him the difference from the inside, only how to keep either of them out of his hands until there was work for his hands to do.

"Catalogue reads nothing," he said, quietly, to himself as much as to her. "Fifty years, and I have nothing in fifty years for this."

Whatever ran behind Doyle's oval receptor did not wait to be asked. It never did, and Orim had long since stopped needing to see the words themselves; he read the same page in the half-second tilt of that black housing, arriving, as he almost always did, at the same conclusions in an older and slower language. This, roughly, was what the instrument gave the younger man, and what Orim had already begun assembling for himself out of bone-age and breath-frequency and the particular wrongness of a fifth ground-contact point:

Mass beyond any calibrated field reference — eleven to fourteen tons, forward-loaded. A gait that made no tactical sense, sustained ground pressure inconsistent with the speed it was producing. A hide of laminate plate, non-metallic, calcite in its nature, with a single failure point at the cervico-dorsal junction that opened on the forced exhale and nowhere else. And, cross-referenced against an archive Orim himself had never once needed to consult, because he had written the marginal hand into a different ledger sixty years before Doyle's receptor had ever existed to read it back to him: a thing out of Sphere-119, unrecorded under any common name, with one prior notation from the Year 873 that said only that it was not to be summoned, and not to be catalogued twice.

He did not share the second half of that sentence. There would be time for it later, if there was a later, and no version of saying it now would change what any of them did in the next ten seconds.

Doyle absorbed the readout the same instant it arrived, unbidden as always — a half-second's stillness, the optic tilting a fraction, and then he was already moving, unslinging the rifle, already doing the only thing the readout had ever been for.

"There," he said, and touched two fingers to the base of his own throat, then pointed at the beast's shoulder, low, where the neck met the ridge of its back. "When it — " He made a short exhaling sound, touched the spot again. "Opens."

"When it breathes?" Lyra asked.

He nodded once.

It charged.

The distance from the overhang to where they stood had looked, a moment before, like forty meters of open ground and margin. It closed in under three seconds — mass translating into speed at a rate Orim watched the girl's body answer before her mind could have finished working it out. She had already moved: shield up, feet set, taking the angle rather than the center of the charge, the way a man takes a battering ram he cannot stop by standing directly in front of it.

The impact traveled through the Vigil Shield and into her arm and shoulder and down through her braced leg into the ground. Orim watched the resin-hardened face of it flex under a load it had never been built to absorb in a single blow, watched her ride the deflection sideways rather than fight it, letting the shield's angle bleed the force into a glancing slide instead of a dead stop that would have taken her arm out of its socket. It was the lesson six weeks of his own flat insistence had put into her hands, whether she credited him with it or not, and he watched her use it now the way a man watches a tool he built perform exactly as designed.

She came up already reading the next problem, which was the reach. The head came around on a neck longer than the body's proportions should have allowed — a whip-crack correction faster than the charge that had produced it — and she went inside it rather than away, close enough that the jaw's arc passed over her instead of through her.

"Doyle!" she called, not turning, the shield still between her and whatever correction was coming next. "The angle!"

He had already found it. Low and wide against the gorge wall's shadow, the position his old war had built into him before any of them had met him — find the ground that gives you the shot, hold it, let the tank absorb what the shot needs time to set up. Orim had watched this same discipline in exactly one other man in his life, a man dead forty years now, and had never once told Doyle so, because some comparisons were not gifts.

He did not fire. Not yet. The seam only opened on the exhale, and the beast was drawing breath for another charge, plates drawn tight, and firing into closed armor would have cost him one of five rounds for nothing. Orim had watched him hold this same wait on three prior occasions and understood, without needing the sentence spoken aloud, that this was the same patience that had kept a boy alive in a hedgerow long enough to become the man crouched in this gorge — patience that cost nothing until the moment it cost everything.

The beast reared onto its hind legs, higher than its mass should have allowed — Orim saw the correction and said nothing — and the exhale came out of it in a visible cloud of cold breath, the seam at its shoulder opening, wet-dark, for perhaps half a second.

Doyle fired.

The round went where he'd aimed it. The beast's whole body convulsed sideways, a shudder running the length of the plated spine like a wave through water, and the sound it made afterward was not the low breathing frequency any longer — something higher, worse, a note that cracked at the edges the way a voice cracks when asked to do something it was never built to do.

It was not enough. Orim understood that in the same breath he understood the shot had landed clean. A single round through a seam the width of two fingers was a wound, not a kill, on something that size, and what the creature did instead of dying was turn everything it had left toward the thing that had hurt it.

It came down off its hind legs directly onto her.

She got the shield up. She did not get the angle — there had been no time to choose one, only time to choose between the shield and nothing — and the forelimb that came past the shield's edge caught her across the ribs and side. Not the full weight of the blow. Enough. Orim watched her go down without having decided to go down, watched a hand come up to brace against the dust and come away red before her eyes had found the wound. Three claw marks, he could see even from where he stood, torn rather than punctured — the half-plate had turned the worst of it, taken the deepest one on the hip rather than the gut. Not a fact he was glad of. Only one more kept for later, if there proved to be a later.

She tested whether she could stand while the beast reared again for what would be, with the same flat clarity that governed most of what Orim allowed himself to notice, the blow that answered that question for her.

"Down!" Doyle's voice, louder than Orim had heard him manage in six weeks, and she went down and sideways rather than up, and the second round went into the same seam, wider now from the first wound, and the scream cracked further, and under all of it, over all of it, there was Orim's own voice, though he would not have been able to say, afterward, at what point exactly he had decided to use it.

"Stand clear," he said. Not loud. The tone of a man who had said it before and understood exactly what it cost the people who needed to hear it.

He crossed the open ground toward the wounded thing rather than away from it, which was not what forty years of doctrine would have told a sensible man to do, and he was aware, distantly — the same removed awareness he had of most things about himself while he was doing them — that he no longer entirely counted as a sensible man where the Rod was concerned, and had not for some years.

The mineral tip was still faintly warm from the Forge Hearth. He had wrapped it in his own coat two hours before and had not asked himself when it would cool, because there had not been time, and now there was less. A thin skin of soot had settled into the cord wrapping at the grip since the ridge — the residue of a housing asked to work hard, and recently — and he read that soot as he read everything else that morning: as a fact about how thin his margin had grown, offered to no one, weighed by no one but himself.

He did not stop at forty meters. Forty meters was the clearance he had held for fifty years between himself and whatever he chose to let through the world's own skin for the Terrible Births — his own word for it, coined the first time a scry stopped being sight and turned to killing instead. He crossed inside it — inside twenty, inside ten — close enough that the beast's next convulsion, its head swinging low, searching by heat for the smaller warm thing now standing well inside its reach, nearly caught him before Doyle's third round, fired wide of the seam on purpose, took it in the brow-pit instead and bought him the half-second he needed.

This was not a cold pierce, whatever the forty-meter clearance and the two settled breaths otherwise demanded of him. He had already read what the beast carried in it before the readout ever confirmed the name — a marginal hand, a hundred years dead, warning against summoning the thing twice — and a creature that had crossed the membrane once already to be standing in this gorge at all did not offer virgin ground the way granite or dry scrub did. Its wound was aperture-adjacent before Doyle's rounds had ever opened it further, a place where the world's skin had already thinned by the creature's own passage through it once before. He was not pressing into a threshold cold. He was pressing into one that had, in some sense he had no clean word for, already been crossed — and he understood, distantly, that this was very likely the only reason what he was about to do would work at all.

He put the mineral tip into the wound. Not near it. Into it — the ragged opening Doyle's two rounds had made in the plate, wet and dark and still widening with every convulsion — and he pressed the Rod's tip against the raw edge of it like a key into a lock he had never before had the nerve to try.

He held there. Motionless, the Rod's tip resting against the wound's raw edge, looking at nothing but that wound — not at Lyra, not at Doyle, not at the dying thing's swinging head — the way a man looks at a door he built with his own two hands and has kept shut for fifty years, deciding, in no hurry now that the deciding could no longer be avoided, whether he still meant what he had built it to mean.

"Not at a living thing," he said, to no one, to the beast, to fifty years of his own rule. "I said I never would."

He pulled the trigger.

He had watched the Fire Scry two hours before — the exhalation of a Sphere-23 volcano crossing a hundred meters of open air to burn six men off a road — and had understood, long before that, watching a hundred openings before it, that the Rod did not create anything. It only parted the skin and stood back from whatever pressure on the far side did with the opening.

What came through this time had nowhere to go but directly and immediately into the beast's own body, because the aperture was the wound and the wound was the door, and he had aimed a piece of the world's own skin — small, wet, alive in his two hands — straight into eleven tons of living tissue at a range of perhaps four inches.

For half a breath the raw edge of the wound went a color that had nothing to do with the beast at all — warm amber-gold, wet and clinging, the color of light through old honey, the world's own liquor pressing at the tear like water at a thumb held over a spout. Then the gold went under it. Water came first — not a stream, not a spray, a wall of it, black and cold in a way that had nothing to do with any water he had ever put a hand in, carrying the mineral smell of somewhere far underground and far from any sun. A Sphere-08 lake, at a depth that had spent geological time compressing itself into a pressure no membrane on Entos's surface had ever been built to stand adjacent to. He knew it with the same certainty as the age of the scrub and the wrongness of the fifth ground-contact point — a fact arrived at before the words for it, and not one he intended to say aloud that day, or perhaps any day. It did not spray outward through the wound. It went in, because the wound was the only opening the pressure differential had been offered, and eleven tons of body cavity that had never once needed to withstand deep-lake hydrostatic load absorbed, in under two seconds, considerably more of it than a body was built to hold.

The sound it made afterward had no name that Orim had ever come across in fifty years of collecting names for things, and he did not attempt to give it one now, on the grounds that trying would have been dishonest. It was not the cracked scream from before. It was something wetter, and shorter, and then it was nothing at all, because the plated ridge of its back had stopped moving in the way that meant the thing driving it had stopped being able to.

It did not fall so much as stop arguing with the ground it had been standing on, and went down all at once. The impact threw dust and old bone fragments into the air in a ring around the point of contact, and somewhere under the plates something further gave way with a sound like wet canvas, and cold lake water the color of iron ran out of the wound and pooled black in the dust, carrying with it things that had no business existing in a gorge three hours' walk from the sea. None of the three of them looked at it very long.

Orim sat down where he was standing. Not carefully — his legs had simply finished the conversation without consulting him — and he registered this about himself with the same flat, unhurried attention he gave to everything else that morning — a fact, arriving late, that he had spent more than he had been keeping track of spending.

The Rod's tip had gone dark — the same dead-coal dark that comes once the last heat has bled out of a thing — no glow left at all, not even the low thermal answer it had carried after the Fire Scry — and this time he did not wrap it in his coat. He set it down in the dust beside him and looked at his own two hands as though they belonged to someone he had recently been introduced to.

Doyle reached her first. He had two words for what he found — the torn plate, the blood already soaking the lower edge of her half-plate, more of it than the eye expected a body to be carrying around loose — and he used the correct one, which by now was understood among the three of them as the mark of how seriously he was taking a situation.

"Bad," he said again, and this time it was not a description of the gorge.

"Grey Tincture," she said. "Left side of the kit. Not the White — the White's for bleeding I don't have time to stop properly, and I have time." She did not, entirely, but Orim saw, from where he sat, that saying it seemed to make it closer to true for her, and that there was a whole practice of triage that ran on exactly that logic.

Doyle found the vial without fumbling, which told Orim something about how many times the young man had watched her work that kit on other people, and set it aside without a second look. She talked him through the rest in short sentences — pressure here, not there, the tincture goes on the cloth first, never directly into an open wound unless you want to learn why the manual says not to — and somewhere in the middle of it Orim heard her say, distantly, as if through a wall: "That's a lot of blood to be carrying around outside a person?"

Doyle did not answer that. He kept working.

Orim did not move from where he sat until the worst of the bleeding had slowed under Doyle's hands and her own steadying breath, and when he did move it was only to reach out and set one hand, briefly, on the cold dust beside the dead thing's ruined shoulder — not a benediction, not a farewell, nothing he would have put a word to even for himself. He took the hand back. He offered no explanation, and neither of them asked him for one; they had both learned better by now.

"I have pointed that Rod at six men on a road," he said finally, to the gorge in general, "and slept afterward, because they had chosen the wrong side of a war I did not start. I have summoned forty-five seconds of some creature's whole existence into a dockyard corridor and turned away so I would not have to watch what I had done to it, because the alternative was watching my own party die instead, and I could live with that arithmetic." He looked at his hands again. "I have never done this. I told myself, the first time I built the courage to open anything at all, that whatever else the Rod became for, it would never become that."

"It killed the thing that was killing us," Lyra said, because it was true, and because the alternative to saying the true thing was saying something merely comforting, and she had learned, across six weeks, that he had never once asked her for the second kind.

"Yes," he said. "That is also true." He picked the Rod back up out of the dust, turned it once in his hands, and did not look at it again after that. "Both things are true. I find that this does not make either of them lighter."

They did not linger in the bowl. There was nothing to loot from a carcass that large that any of them wanted to carry, and the black pool spreading from the wound had already begun drawing the kind of insects that fed on things that should not exist in this world's ecology, working it with the same patient indifference they brought to anything else dead. Doyle checked his remaining rounds — two — and said nothing about the count, which by now Orim understood meant the count troubled him more than a comment would have helped.

Lyra walked the rest of the gorge with one hand pressed against her side under the half-plate, upright because he needed her upright and because sitting down to properly feel the wound was a luxury the schedule did not currently extend to her. Somewhere in the last hundred meters before the gorge opened back onto the coastal scrub, Orim understood — without being able to say when exactly he had arrived at it — that the three of them had just fought as a single mechanism rather than as three people standing near each other: the shield taking what could be taken, the rifle finding what could be found, the last resort spent only when the first two had done everything they could do and still fell short. Nobody had called the positions aloud. Nobody had needed to. He set that fact aside too — one more thing that seemed likely to matter again before this was over — and did not say it aloud either, on the grounds that it kept better unspoken.

The scrub gave way to the smell of salt an hour later, faint at first, then unmistakable — the same smell that had reached him two hundred meters out from every coastal town in fifty years of walking toward one, promising a port and a bed and, if the Avandrey's schedule held, a reason to keep moving before the wound in Lyra's side finished deciding how much it intended to hurt in the morning.

"Sal Vento," he said, mostly to the horizon. He'd been saying it at intervals since the ridge. "If the weather holds."

"It'll hold," Lyra said.

He did not correct her, though weather, like most things he had spent a long life reading, would do what it intended to do regardless of what any of them hoped from it, and he had long since stopped considering that worth saying out loud either.
